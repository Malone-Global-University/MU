The English Reformation

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1500, every parish in England belonged to the Western Church under the pope. By 1603, every parish belonged to a national church governed by the Crown, worshipping in English, and officially Protestant. Between those dates lay four reigns, at least four distinct religious settlements, and a long, uneven struggle over what the changes meant to the people who lived through them.

Many English parishes preserved churchwardens' account books, the working records of the lay officials who maintained the church building and its furnishings. Read across the sixteenth century, such books can trace the shifting life of a single community: payments for lights before images, for vestments, and for a rood loft; later, payments for taking such things down, for a Bible in English, for a communion table, for whitewash over wall paintings; then, in some places, payments to bring images and altars back; then, once more, payments to remove them. Not every parish kept such records, and the surviving ones are unevenly distributed, but where they exist they show something that narratives of kings and statutes can obscure. The Reformation in England was not a single event decided in London. It was a sequence of instructions from the Crown that ordinary parishioners had to implement, resist, evade, or embrace, often at considerable cost, and often more than once.

This chapter traces that sequence from the late medieval English church through the death of Elizabeth I in 1603. It begins with an unusual feature that distinguishes the English case from the German and Swiss Reformations examined in Chapters 62 and 63. In Germany and Switzerland, preachers and city councils challenged inherited doctrine and ecclesiastical order from within a Latin church. In England, the decisive first break was a matter of jurisdiction. Between 1529 and 1534, Henry VIII and Parliament repudiated the pope's authority over the English church and declared the king its supreme head. That legal revolution was not, at first, a doctrinal one. For more than a decade afterward, the English church retained the Mass, the veneration of saints in much of its practice, and a good deal of traditional theology, even as monasteries were dissolved and the Bible was placed in parish churches in English.

Protestant doctrine and worship followed unevenly. Under Edward VI, advisers influenced by continental reformers produced the prayer books of 1549 and 1552 and an official confession of faith. Under Mary I, the Catholic church was restored, papal authority was reinstated, and several hundred Protestants were burned for heresy. Under Elizabeth I, Parliament and Crown assembled a settlement in 1559 that combined a Protestant doctrinal position, an English liturgy, retained bishops, and royal governance. The Elizabethan church satisfied neither committed Catholics nor the more zealous Protestants who wanted further reform, and the history of the later sixteenth century is in large part the story of those two groups, recusants and Puritans, and of the majority of parishioners who stood somewhere between them.

The chapter's governing question is how a monarchy's break with papal authority developed into a durable Protestant establishment, despite repeated reversals, popular resistance, and continuing religious division. The answer offered here avoids two common simplifications. One is the older view that a corrupt late medieval church collapsed almost of its own weight and that Protestantism was welcomed as liberation. The other is the sometimes oversimplified opposite, that a vibrant traditional religion was destroyed by force with little real support for change. Historians now generally agree that both pictures need modification, and that the outcome in 1603 was neither predetermined nor simply imposed. It emerged from negotiation between the Crown, Parliament, clergy, gentry, urban communities, and parish congregations over four or five generations.

The chapter relies on several kinds of evidence. Statutes, royal injunctions, proclamations, and the records of Convocation and Parliament reveal what the Crown intended. Prayer books, catechisms, sermons, and printed Bibles show what authorities wished people to hear and say. Visitation articles and diocesan court records show enforcement, and churchwardens' accounts, wills, and parish inventories show what happened locally. The narratives of martyrs, printed by Protestants and by Catholics, show how contending communities remembered their sufferings, and state papers, ambassadors' dispatches, and private letters reveal political calculation. Each source type reflects a particular vantage point, and much of the discussion that follows asks whose perspective is recorded and whose is not.

This chapter occupies a particular position within Part X and Volume III. Chapter 61 examined the late medieval church and the demand for reform, and Chapters 62 and 63 followed the Lutheran, Reformed, and radical movements. Chapter 65 turns to Catholic renewal and the Council of Trent, and Chapter 66 to religious war in France and the Netherlands. The English story intersected with each of these, and it also helped to shape the confessional states and overseas empires examined in later chapters. It remains, however, a distinct pattern: a Reformation that began with the Crown, was reversed by the Crown, and was finally secured by the Crown, while its meaning remained contested among the English people themselves.

Historical Context

Chapter 61 examined the church that Tudor England inherited, and only the essentials need be recalled here. The English church formed part of Latin Christendom. It was divided into two provinces, Canterbury and York, and into dioceses and some nine thousand parishes, each served by a priest and maintained by the parishioners through the work of elected churchwardens. Bishops held lands, sat in the House of Lords, and presided over church courts that dealt with matters ranging from probate and marriage to heresy. The monasteries and friaries held a great deal of land and exercised patronage over many parish livings. Appeals in the most important cases could be taken to Rome. The pope was also the source of dispensations, including the dispensations that permitted marriages within degrees of kinship prohibited by canon law.

Political conditions were as important as religious ones. The Tudor dynasty rested on Henry VII's victory at Bosworth in 1485, after decades of dynastic conflict, and its security depended on the production of a male heir. Henry VIII succeeded in 1509 at the age of seventeen and married Catherine of Aragon, the widow of his elder brother Arthur, with the benefit of a papal dispensation. Their only surviving child was a daughter, Mary, born in 1516. The European setting mattered as well. Catherine's nephew Charles V, whose position is examined in Chapter 75, ruled Spain and the Habsburg lands, and after his troops sacked Rome in 1527 the pope was in practice under imperial pressure. Meanwhile, as Chapter 62 explained, Luther's challenge had divided the German lands. In 1521 Henry himself had published a defense of the seven sacraments against Luther, for which the pope gave him the title Defender of the Faith.

England also had its own heterodox tradition. Lollardy, a movement associated with the fourteenth century teaching of John Wyclif, survived in small, secretive networks, particularly in parts of the southeast, the Chilterns, and the Thames valley, and was periodically prosecuted. Its significance is debated: it clearly preserved criticism of images, pilgrimage, and the Mass, and it favored vernacular scripture, but it was small and probably cannot by itself explain the later rapid reception of Protestant ideas. Anticlericalism, a hostility to clerical wealth, legal privileges, and fees, was also real, though scholars differ about how deep it ran and whether it was directed at the institution of the church or at specific abuses. These problems are taken up again in Sections 64.1 and 64.25 and in the Historical Debate.

A note on dates is necessary. England used the Julian calendar throughout this period, ten days behind the Gregorian calendar that Catholic Europe began adopting in 1582, and England did not adopt it until 1752. In England the legal year also began on 25 March, so that a date such as 1 March 1603 would have been recorded under the year 1602 by contemporaries. This chapter uses the Julian calendar with years beginning on 1 January, and gives Elizabeth's death as 24 March 1603, a date that contemporary English documents might give as 24 March 1602. Regnal years are also used in the titles of statutes, so that the Act of Supremacy of 1534 is cited by lawyers as 26 Henry VIII, chapter 1.

Finally, readers should keep in mind that the word "Protestant" did not at first describe a stable English identity, and that the adjective "Anglican" is anachronistic for most of the sixteenth century. Contemporaries spoke of the Church of England, the "new learning," "gospellers," "papists," and later "Puritans." The chapter uses those terms where historically appropriate and explains them when they first appear.

Section 64.1 · Division I: The English Church and the Break with Rome

The English Church on the Eve of the Reformation

Parish religion lay at the center of late medieval English life. A parish church was a communal building as well as a place of worship, and the nave was maintained by parishioners, often through gifts, bequests, church ales, and the organized activity of guilds and confraternities. Worship was in Latin, but the church was filled with images, painted walls, stained glass, and objects that gave laypeople a visual and ritual vocabulary: the rood, a carved crucifix often set on a screen between nave and chancel; images of saints to whom candles were offered; processions at Rogationtide and Corpus Christi; and the Mass at the altar, where the consecrated host was believed to become, through transubstantiation, the body of Christ. Wills, which usually began with a religious preamble and left money for Masses, lights, and the repair of churches, show how many testators expected to be helped after death by the prayers of the living. Belief in purgatory supported a substantial economy of chantries, obits, and gilds.

The clergy formed a large and varied body. Secular priests staffed the parishes, many of them modestly educated and poorly paid. Monks, nuns, canons, and friars lived in several hundred religious houses, some very wealthy, some small and struggling. Bishops combined pastoral and administrative duties with political roles. Church courts exercised wide jurisdiction over wills, marriage, tithe disputes, and moral offences, and complaints about fees and delays were common. Clergy enjoyed certain legal privileges, including benefit of clergy in criminal cases, and disputes over the boundary between church and secular jurisdiction recurred. The Hunne affair of 1514 to 1515, in which a London merchant died in the bishop's prison after refusing to pay a mortuary fee and became the subject of a major dispute between church and common law authorities, shows how these tensions could erupt, though it should not be treated as typical.

Historians have long disagreed about the health of this religious system. An older view, shaped largely by Protestant tradition and later by A. G. Dickens's influential work, stressed corruption, ignorance, and anticlericalism as preparing the ground for reform. From the 1980s, revisionist historians including J. J. Scarisbrick and Eamon Duffy argued that the evidence, especially from churchwardens' accounts, wills, and printed devotional books, showed a vigorous, popular, and adaptable religion, with lay piety expanding rather than withering, and that the Reformation was therefore something largely done to the English people. More recent scholarship has found a middle ground. Late medieval religion was vital, but vitality is not the same as immunity to criticism. Reformers could find real targets in clerical wealth, pluralism, uneven pastoral standards, and the claim of papal jurisdiction, and Lollard and humanist critiques provided intellectual resources. What was lacking in 1500 was not criticism or devotion, but a political circumstance in which the structure itself could be challenged. That circumstance came from the Crown.

Section 64.2

Humanism, Evangelical Reform, and the English Bible

Renaissance humanism, discussed in Chapters 57 and 59, reached England through scholars such as John Colet, whose lectures and sermons stressed the plain meaning of Scripture, and Thomas More, whose Utopia (1516) used satire to criticize social and political abuses. Erasmus visited England several times and taught at Cambridge, and his Greek New Testament of 1516 and its Latin translation provided scholars with a tool for questioning the Latin Vulgate. Humanists generally sought moral and educational reform within the existing church, and More became a determined opponent of Protestantism, but their emphasis on returning to scriptural sources provided a vocabulary that evangelical reformers adapted to different ends.

Evangelical ideas arrived in the 1520s, in part through printed books from Germany and the Low Countries. A small circle at Cambridge, associated with the White Horse tavern and including future reformers such as Thomas Bilney, Hugh Latimer, and Robert Barnes, discussed Luther's writings. Barnes was tried for heresy in 1526. Meanwhile William Tyndale, a Gloucestershire-born scholar who concluded that the English could not understand Scripture without a vernacular text, left England and published an English New Testament, printed at Worms in 1526, and smuggled into England through merchant networks in the following years. Tyndale's translation, based on the Greek, was prohibited, and the authorities, including Bishop Tunstall of London and Thomas More, tried to seize and burn copies. Tyndale continued to translate parts of the Old Testament and to write polemical works. He was arrested in the Low Countries and executed in 1536.

The suppression of vernacular scripture reveals a major element of early English reform. The issue was not simply doctrinal. Access to the Bible in English raised the question of who could interpret Scripture and whether lay readers could do so without clerical guidance, which in turn bore directly on clerical authority. Evidence for evangelical readers in the 1520s and early 1530s suggests small and scattered networks, concentrated in London, parts of East Anglia, Essex, Kent, and university circles, and included merchants, artisans, clergy, and women as well as scholars. Printing, discussed in Chapter 55, made the trade in books possible, and the smuggling of books followed existing commercial channels. Authorities prosecuted a relatively small number of cases, and the movement should not be exaggerated, but its existence meant that when the Crown broke with Rome, reformers were ready with texts, arguments, and networks, and some of them gained influence at court.

Section 64.3

Henry VIII, Catherine of Aragon, and the Royal Marriage Crisis

By the mid 1520s, Henry's marriage had produced no surviving son, and the king, then in his thirties, became convinced that his union with Catherine was invalid. His argument drew on the biblical prohibition of marrying a brother's wife in Leviticus and held that the papal dispensation permitting the marriage had been defective. The question was not trivial in dynastic terms. England had no settled tradition of female rule, and the memory of civil war in the fifteenth century made the succession a matter of urgent political importance. The king's growing attachment to Anne Boleyn, a lady of the court who was reportedly unwilling to become merely a mistress, intensified the matter, but historians generally agree that the primary driver was dynastic insecurity, even if personal feeling shaped its timing and course.

The matter should have been resolved by papal annulment, and annulments were not unusual for rulers. In 1527 Henry instructed Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, his lord chancellor and papal legate, to seek one. The difficulty was political. Pope Clement VII was effectively under the power of Charles V, Catherine's nephew, after the sack of Rome in May 1527, and could not afford to antagonize him. A legatine court sat at Blackfriars in London in 1529, with Wolsey and Cardinal Campeggio presiding, but the case was transferred to Rome before a judgment was given. Wolsey fell from power in autumn 1529, was charged under the statute of praemunire, which penalized the assertion of foreign jurisdiction in England, and died in November 1530 while traveling to face further charges.

Catherine herself resisted the annulment with considerable firmness, insisting that her first marriage to Arthur had never been consummated and that her marriage to Henry was valid. She had support among English clergy, in the court, and among ordinary people in London, and the Spanish ambassador described the popularity of her cause. Thomas More and Bishop Fisher were among her prominent defenders. Anne Boleyn's supporters included reform-minded clergy and courtiers, and Thomas Cranmer, a Cambridge theologian who suggested that the universities of Europe be consulted on the question, entered the king's service. It is important not to read the later outcome backward. In 1529 there was no plan for a break with Rome. Henry's immediate objective was to compel the pope to grant his request, and the idea of royal supremacy developed under pressure, as a legal and theological justification emerged for doing without Rome.

Section 64.4

The Reformation Parliament and the Break with Rome, 1529–1534

Parliament, summoned in November 1529 and meeting in several sessions until 1536, is known as the Reformation Parliament. Its legislation was initially directed at the clergy, whom critics accused of abusing their jurisdiction and fees. The Commons presented a complaint, the Supplication against the Ordinaries, in 1532, which criticized the church courts. In 1531 the clergy of Canterbury, threatened with a charge of praemunire for having recognized Wolsey's legatine authority, paid a very large fine and accepted a title for the king as supreme head of the church with the qualification "so far as the law of Christ allows." In 1532, the clergy's Submission acknowledged that Convocation would not make new canons without royal assent, and Thomas More resigned as chancellor, an act that signaled his opposition. Thomas Cromwell, a lawyer who had served Wolsey, rose rapidly as the king's principal adviser and, from 1532 to 1533, became the organizer of the parliamentary program.

In 1533 the legislative break was completed. The Act in Restraint of Appeals, drafted with Cromwell's influence, declared that England was an empire, meaning a polity with a single supreme head with full jurisdiction, and prohibited appeals to Rome in matters of wills, marriage, and tithes. William Warham, the archbishop of Canterbury, had died in 1532, and Thomas Cranmer was appointed in 1533, with papal bulls still obtained, and consecrated in March. In May, Cranmer's court declared Henry's marriage to Catherine invalid and Henry's marriage to Anne, secretly celebrated earlier in the year, valid. Elizabeth was born in September 1533. The Act of Succession of 1534 made Anne's children the heirs and required an oath, to be sworn by subjects, accepting the new succession. Later in 1534, Parliament abolished payments to Rome, and the Act of Supremacy declared that the king was supreme head of the Church of England.

The break was therefore the result of a series of steps rather than a single decision, and its justification drew on historical and legal claims. The statutes asserted that English kings had always held imperial authority, citing, among other arguments, selective readings of medieval history and the example of Constantine. The claim also built on the view that Convocation and Parliament together represented the whole English realm, so that parliamentary statute could determine matters previously reserved to church authority. This was an important constitutional development. The claim that the king in Parliament could legislate on the church placed a very wide range of subjects in the hands of the legislature, a principle with long consequences that Chapter 77 will take up. It is equally important that none of the statutes mentioned a change in doctrine. The Mass, the sacraments, the veneration of the saints in most traditional forms, and the doctrine of purgatory were not altered in 1534.

Cause and Effect

The Break with Rome, 1529–1534

Long-term causes

Medieval disputes over church and royal jurisdiction, including the statutes of praemunire; a tradition of anticlerical criticism; Lollard and humanist critiques; the rise of printing and vernacular religious literature; and the growth of the Tudor state's administrative capacity.

Short-term causes

Henry's lack of a male heir; the annulment request of 1527; the pope's political dependence on Charles V; Wolsey's failure; Anne Boleyn's rise; the influence of Cromwell and Cranmer; and parliamentary and clerical pressure.

Event or transformation

Between 1529 and 1534, statutes ended papal jurisdiction, established royal supremacy, annulled the first marriage, and required an oath of allegiance to the new succession.

Immediate effects

Executions of those who refused the oaths; new treason law; royal control over appointments and church revenue; and the creation of an administrative apparatus under Cromwell.

Long-term consequences: The establishment of a national church under royal governance, a precedent for parliamentary authority over religion, and the subsequent attachment of the English church's identity to the monarchy.

Historical complexity: The break cannot be explained by Henry's marriage alone, since the marriage crisis became decisive only because jurisdictional, political, and intellectual conditions made a break thinkable. Nor was it the product of a mass Protestant movement, since evangelicals in 1529 were few. The outcome emerged from the interaction of dynastic need, international politics, legal argument, and the opportunism and conviction of particular advisers.

Section 64.5

Royal Supremacy, Thomas Cromwell, and Resistance to the New Order

The Act of Supremacy of 1534 did not give Henry the power to administer the sacraments or define doctrine independently of any counsel, but it did give him authority to visit, reform, and correct the church, and Cromwell, appointed vicegerent in spirituals in 1535, put this authority to administrative use. The Treasons Act of 1534, in force from February 1535, made it treasonable to deny the king's titles, so that words alone could now be treated as treason. Clergy were required to preach against papal authority and to remove the pope's name from service books. Oaths were administered to office-holders, clergy, and religious communities, and the government gathered information about opposition through informers and local officials.

The most prominent resisters were John Fisher, bishop of Rochester, and Thomas More. More was prepared to accept that Parliament could settle the succession, and he kept his views on the supremacy to himself, but both men refused an oath whose wording appeared to repudiate papal primacy and to condemn the first marriage. Both were imprisoned in the Tower, and after the Treasons Act they were tried and executed in June and July 1535. More's silence under questioning, followed by his declaration at trial, has been analyzed at length, and legal historians continue to debate the strength of the case against him. Their executions shocked European opinion, and Erasmus and other humanists lamented More's death. A group of Carthusian monks of London who refused the oath were executed in 1535 with particular severity, and religious houses that resisted faced pressure from visitors.

Resistance took other forms as well. Some clergy continued to pray for the pope or to preach ambiguously, some laypeople expressed disapproval of the new queen, and Catherine's supporters, including Princess Mary, who was declared illegitimate, remained a focus of opposition until Catherine's death in January 1536. Anne Boleyn herself fell from favor and was executed in May 1536 on charges of adultery and treason, after which Henry married Jane Seymour, who bore the future Edward VI in October 1537. At the same time, the new order attracted real support. Many clergy accepted royal supremacy, in part from fear but in part from conviction that a Christian prince had authority over the church, and Protestants such as Cranmer and Latimer saw in supremacy an opening for reform. The effect of the early 1530s was to create a church whose head was a king who regarded himself as defender of traditional doctrine, but whose principal ministers included reformers.

Historical Vocabulary
Royal Supremacy

Royal supremacy was the doctrine, enacted in statute in 1534, that the English Crown held supreme authority over the church within the realm, replacing the jurisdiction of the pope. Henry VIII was styled supreme head, a title that Elizabeth I replaced in 1559 with supreme governor, in part to avoid offense to those who held that only Christ could be head of the church, and to avoid implying a claim to priestly functions. Supremacy did not mean that the monarch preached or administered sacraments. It meant that the monarch, acting with Parliament and the clergy, controlled appointments, doctrine, law, and discipline in the church.

It is important not to equate supremacy with Protestantism. Henry VIII asserted supremacy while retaining the Mass, and Catholics under Mary I temporarily repudiated supremacy while keeping the Crown's powers over most practical church matters. Modern readers should also distinguish supremacy from the later idea of an "established church," which implies a settled legal relationship that took shape gradually.

It also shows that the break was effected by the king in Parliament, with the participation of clergy in Convocation, and so contributed to a constitutional principle. Section 64.6 · Division II: Henry VIII and the Transformation of Religious Institutions

Thomas Cromwell, Royal Visitations, and the Dissolution of the Monasteries

The dissolution was prepared by an inquiry into the church's wealth. In 1535, royal commissioners compiled the Valor Ecclesiasticus, a survey of the incomes of bishoprics, cathedral chapters, parishes, and religious houses, which gave the Crown, for the first time, a systematic picture of ecclesiastical resources. It survives as a major source for historians. In the same period, Cromwell's commissioners, among them Richard Layton, Thomas Legh, and John ap Rice, visited monasteries, gathered reports on discipline and morals, and issued injunctions. Their reports alleged laxity, superstition, and sexual misconduct in some houses. These reports were written by men with an interest in justifying the policy, and their reliability is disputed. Modern historians generally treat them as selective and often exaggerated, though not as pure invention, and note that other evidence, including episcopal visitations, suggests that many houses were reasonably well regulated.

The first stage came with the Act for the Suppression of the Lesser Monasteries in 1536, which dissolved houses with an annual income below two hundred pounds, justifying the measure by reference to the alleged corruption of small houses and the possibility of moving religious to larger ones. A court of augmentations was established to administer the confiscated property. The northern rising of 1536 and 1537, discussed in Section 64.8, interrupted the process briefly, but afterward the pace increased. Abbots and communities of larger houses were pressed to surrender voluntarily, with pensions offered to heads and members, and those who resisted, such as the abbots of Glastonbury, Reading, and Colchester, were tried and executed for treason in 1539. In 1539 a further statute confirmed the Crown's title to surrendered houses. The friaries had largely been closed by 1538, and the last major abbey, Waltham in Essex, surrendered in March 1540.

Altogether some eight hundred religious houses, large and small, were closed over about four years, along with major shrines. The shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury was dismantled in 1538, and the cult of the saint was attacked, as were other shrines and relics, including some thought to be fraudulent, such as the so-called Rood of Grace at Boxley, which was exposed as mechanical. Cromwell's role was central, but the policy also reflected royal need for revenue and the interests of reforming advisers. Notably, the dissolution did not require a change in doctrine, and the king insisted on the retention of Masses and prayers for the dead in his own chapels and, in modified form, in the foundation of new cathedrals. The new institutions supported by the Crown included six new dioceses created out of former monastic revenues, as well as colleges, which suggests that the policy was not simply plunder.

Section 64.7

Monastic Wealth, Land Redistribution, and Social Consequences

The scale of the transfer of property was large. Religious houses held a very substantial share of English land, estimated by scholars at roughly a quarter or more of landed wealth, and much of it passed to the Crown. The Crown at first retained substantial revenues, but its expensive wars with France and Scotland in the 1540s, and later military and financial needs under Edward, led to large sales of land. Purchasers included nobles, courtiers, lawyers, merchants, and gentry, as well as some town corporations and individuals of modest means. A market in former monastic land developed, and many families built their position on it. The result was not a revolution in the social structure, since the established landed elite benefited most, but it did enlarge and consolidate the gentry, and created a body of property owners with a material interest in resisting any return to monastic ownership. This interest helps to explain why even Mary's regime could not restore the property, a problem discussed in Section 64.16.

The consequences for the former religious were varied. Heads of houses and senior monks received pensions, in some cases generous, while ordinary monks and nuns received smaller sums, and many monks became parish clergy. Nuns, who had fewer options, received small pensions and in some cases lived together or returned to their families, and the broader loss of female religious life was significant in a society that offered few institutional roles for unmarried women. Friars, who lacked endowments, were often left with little. Estimates of the numbers affected vary, but several thousand men and women had lived in religious houses, along with servants, tenants, and local suppliers whose livelihoods depended on them. Many former religious remained in their localities, and some maintained links with Catholic networks through the following decades.

The social functions of the houses are harder to measure. Monasteries provided hospitality to travelers, alms to the poor, and in some cases schooling, medical care, and employment. Historians debate how extensive these functions were, since the charitable giving of many houses was modest in relation to their income, and nuns, monks, and friars varied greatly in their engagement with their communities. Monastic libraries were dispersed, and many manuscripts were destroyed or sold as waste, a major loss to learning that contemporaries such as the antiquary John Leland lamented. Parish churches that had been appropriated to monasteries passed to lay impropriators who collected tithes without necessarily providing for the clergy, a legacy that left a mark on parish finances. The dissolution also removed a major structure of intercession: the prayers of religious communities for founders and benefactors ceased. Whether this was experienced as liberation or loss depended on the observer, a point explored in the Multiple Perspectives feature below.

Section 64.8

Popular Resistance and the Pilgrimage of Grace

Opposition to the dissolution and the wider religious changes produced the largest rebellion of the Tudor period. In October 1536 a rising began in Lincolnshire, triggered in part by rumors that parish churches would be stripped of their valuables, along with fears of new taxation and the arrival of the commissioners who were suppressing the lesser monasteries. It collapsed within about two weeks, but the movement spread to Yorkshire, where Robert Aske, a lawyer from a gentry family, became a leader. Aske and his followers marched under the banner of the Five Wounds of Christ, called themselves pilgrims, and swore an oath emphasizing the restoration of the church, the preservation of the commonwealth, and the removal of evil counselors, a phrase understood as aimed at Cromwell and Cranmer. Tens of thousands joined, and the rebels took York and Pontefract Castle. In December, at Pontefract, their representatives drafted articles that called for restoration of papal authority, the return of Mary to the succession, the restoration of monasteries, and the punishment of heretics and their supposed patrons.

The royal response combined negotiation and force. The Duke of Norfolk, with a smaller army than the rebels, negotiated, and he made assurances of pardon and a parliament in the north. Henry issued a pardon, and the rebels dispersed. The promised parliament never met. In January and February 1537 a further, smaller rising under Sir Francis Bigod in Yorkshire and Cumberland gave the government the pretext to arrest the leaders and execute them. Aske was hanged in chains at York in July 1537, and the total number executed is generally estimated at about two hundred or more. Martial law was applied across the region, and the government afterward reconstituted the Council of the North as a stronger instrument of royal authority.

Historians have long debated the nature of the Pilgrimage. It was a religious movement, in that its oaths and its banners expressed attachment to the traditional church, but also a movement with economic and political grievances, including taxation, enclosure, the loss of local patronage, and anxiety about the growing power of the Crown's ministers. Gentry and nobles, some of whom joined under pressure, played prominent roles, but commons were also deeply involved. The geography is significant. The north was poorer, more remote from London, and had a high concentration of monastic houses relative to population. The Pilgrimage also reveals a limit on Henry's power and shows that religious policy could be enforced only with the cooperation of local elites. At the same time, it did not become a national uprising, and southern England, with many reform-minded communities, remained largely quiet, which indicates that regional differences in religious allegiance were already present.

Section 64.9

Doctrine under Henry VIII: Reform and Religious Conservatism

The doctrine of the English church in the later 1530s and 1540s moved first in a reformist direction and then in a conservative one, reflecting shifts in court politics and in the king's own judgments. The Ten Articles of 1536, issued under royal authority after debate in Convocation, endorsed only three sacraments, baptism, penance, and the Eucharist, as essential, while retaining belief in the Real Presence, and they criticized abuses connected with images, saints, and purgatory without wholly rejecting them. In the next year, the Bishops' Book, a longer exposition, reflected a mixture of traditional and reforming views. The royal injunctions of 1536 and 1538 instructed clergy to teach in English the Lord's Prayer, the Creed, and the Ten Commandments, to remove abuses associated with pilgrimage and images, and from 1538 to place an English Bible in every parish church.

Henry intervened personally in doctrine, and he held that the Eucharist involved the real presence of Christ and that clerical celibacy was a requirement for priests. After the fall of Cromwell, who was arrested in June 1540 and executed in July after the collapse of Henry's brief marriage to Anne of Cleves, conservatives such as Bishop Stephen Gardiner and the Duke of Norfolk gained influence. The Act of Six Articles of 1539, passed before Cromwell's fall, reaffirmed transubstantiation, communion in one kind for laypeople, clerical celibacy, vows of chastity, private Masses, and auricular confession, and imposed severe penalties on those who denied them, including burning for denial of transubstantiation. The King's Book of 1543 restated doctrine in a conservative direction. In 1543 the Act for the Advancement of True Religion restricted who might read the English Bible, reflecting fears about unregulated lay interpretation, and barred women below the gentry and many ordinary men from reading it.

The result was an English church that was neither Protestant nor wholly Catholic. It rejected the pope, it dissolved monasteries, it had a vernacular Bible and a few English devotional texts, and the English Litany of 1544, largely Cranmer's work, appeared as the first officially authorized service in English. Yet it retained the Mass, clerical celibacy, and prayers for the dead. The persecution of religious dissent continued in both directions. Protestants such as Anne Askew, a gentlewoman who was tortured and burned in 1546 for denying transubstantiation, were executed alongside those who denied royal supremacy. The king's religion thus produced conflict on both flanks, and it left a legacy of ambiguity that his successors would resolve in very different ways.

Section 64.10

English Scripture, Thomas Cranmer, and Henry VIII's Religious Legacy

The printing of the Bible in English proceeded under royal sponsorship after 1535. Miles Coverdale's translation, partly based on Tyndale's work and on German and Latin versions, appeared in 1535. Matthew's Bible of 1537, which incorporated more of Tyndale's translation, was licensed by the king, and the Great Bible of 1539, edited by Coverdale with Cromwell's backing, was the version ordered to be placed in churches. Its large size and its title page, which depicted the king handing the Word of God to clergy and laity, who respond with cries of "Vivat Rex," expressed the ideal that the supremacy and Scripture belonged together. Reading aloud in church, group discussion, and, in some places, disputes among parishioners about interpretation, sometimes alarmed the authorities. Printing, together with a rapid expansion of the trade in religious books, made Scripture newly present in parish life.

Thomas Cranmer, archbishop of Canterbury from 1533, was a central figure in these developments. A scholar and a cautious administrator, he had been influenced by Lutheran ideas and by humanist learning and held to a doctrine of the Eucharist that gradually shifted away from Real Presence, though he concealed this until Edward's reign. He relied on Henry's protection, which saved him from conservatives who tried to bring him down in 1543 and 1546. Cranmer's skill as a writer of English prose, evident in the Litany and later in the prayer books, left a lasting mark on English religious language. His position illustrates the difficulty of generalizing about reformers: he was both a loyal servant of an authoritarian king and a convinced evangelical who worked patiently to alter the church from within.

When Henry died on 28 January 1547, the religious position of the realm was unresolved. The king's will, made under the terms of the Third Succession Act of 1544, named Edward as heir, followed by Mary and then Elizabeth, and set up a council of regency. Henry had reduced papal authority to a nullity in England and dissolved the monasteries, and he had also preserved the Mass and punished Protestant dissent. The body of the clergy, many parish communities, and the political elite contained both reformers and conservatives. In the previous decade, a new generation of educated clergy and laity had been influenced by Scripture in English, and the king's advisers in his last years, including those associated with the young prince's household, were mostly reformers. This balance of forces made rapid Protestant change possible after 1547, and it is this possibility, rather than any settled Protestant church, that Henry bequeathed.

Multiple Perspectives

The Dissolution of the Monasteries

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives, synthesizing patterns that historians have identified in many sources. They are not quotations from any single named person.

From the perspective of a monk or nun: Dissolution meant the end of a vocation and a community, often offered as a pension but experienced as dispossession. Some religious accepted the change, particularly where they had sympathy for reform or no practical alternative, while others resisted and suffered execution. Women had far fewer later opportunities than men.
From the perspective of a royal official or an evangelical reformer: The houses represented wealth and privilege tied to a foreign authority and, for reformers, to doctrines of merit and intercession they regarded as false. For officials, dissolution also offered revenue, and the process presented administrative and legal challenges they were committed to managing.
From the perspective of a gentry or merchant purchaser: The sale of land offered a chance to acquire estates, rents, and tithes, and often to consolidate holdings near existing property. Purchasers varied in religious conviction, but many developed a stake in the new order that did not depend on their theology.
From the perspective of a poor tenant or local dependent in a northern community: The monastery might be a landlord, an employer, a source of alms, and a center of local identity. Its loss could mean changed rents, lost work, and the weakening of local religious life, and it helps to explain the support the Pilgrimage drew among the commons.
Section 64.11 · Division III: Edward VI and the Protestant Reformation

The Accession of Edward VI and the Protestant Regency

Edward VI succeeded at the age of nine, and a minority government administered the realm. Edward Seymour, the king's maternal uncle, was made Duke of Somerset and Lord Protector, with powers that exceeded those that Henry's will had authorized. Somerset was an evangelical sympathizer who supported reform, and he gathered around him Cranmer and a number of reformers, as well as foreign Protestants who began arriving in England, including Peter Martyr Vermigli and Martin Bucer, who were later given university positions. The new government, unlike Henry's, was committed to a Protestant direction, and the young king's tutors, who included John Cheke, were reformers who encouraged his own evangelical convictions.

Somerset's regime was challenged by the rebellions of 1549, by wars with Scotland and France, and by financial strain, and in October 1549 he was removed by a coup led by John Dudley, Earl of Warwick and later Duke of Northumberland. Northumberland, who became the dominant figure in the council, was more pragmatic than doctrinaire, but he allied himself with the reformers and encouraged a more decisive move toward Protestantism, particularly after he had secured power. His policies included the elevation of reforming bishops, among them Nicholas Ridley in London and John Hooper in Gloucester, and the replacement of conservative bishops such as Gardiner of Winchester and Bonner of London, who were imprisoned or deprived. Historians differ about Northumberland's own beliefs, but there is little doubt that his government carried through the more radical measures of 1550 to 1553.

A boy king, for whom the council exercised authority, presented a new constitutional problem, since royal supremacy now meant supremacy exercised by a council acting in the name of a child. Protestants argued that the king, as a godly prince, was a new Josiah, a biblical king who destroyed idols, a comparison that appeared in Cranmer's coronation address and in reformist sermons. Critics later argued that the changes were less the work of the king than of councillors who profited from them. The question of how far the Edwardian reformation reflected conviction or opportunism remains discussed, and it is likely that both played a part.

Section 64.12

Church Reform, Royal Injunctions, and the Destruction of Traditional Worship

The royal injunctions of 1547 and the royal visitation that carried them out introduced a major change in parish life. Parishes were instructed to remove images that had been the object of pilgrimage or offerings, to suspend processions, to stop the use of candles before images, and to acquire an English Bible and the Paraphrases of Erasmus, along with a book of Homilies, official sermons written by Cranmer and others, to be read where no licensed preacher was available. The legislation permitted communion in both kinds in English, and Parliament repealed the Six Articles and the heresy laws and made clerical marriage lawful, though the latter followed in 1549. Practices such as the use of candles on Candlemas and palms on Palm Sunday were abolished, and in the following years the removal of images and altars became widespread.

The Chantries Act of 1547 dissolved the chantries, colleges, and guilds that supported priests to pray for the dead, and vested their lands and goods in the Crown, partly because the doctrine of purgatory on which they depended had been undermined by Protestant teaching and partly to raise revenue. The effects were large. Thousands of chantry priests lost their positions, and the endowments that supported many local schools, hospitals, and almshouses were affected, though in some places they were refounded. Guilds and fraternities that maintained religious and social life lost their property, and parishes sold the plate and vestments, sometimes in anticipation of confiscation or to keep proceeds. Churchwardens' accounts reveal many such sales and a mixture of compliance and negotiation.

The enforcement of change depended on bishops, visitors, churchwardens, and gentry, and the pace varied. London, the southeast, and East Anglia moved quickly, and many parishes in the north and west were slower and more reluctant. Some parishes removed images zealously, some hid them, and others kept them for reuse. Iconoclasm, the destruction of religious images, was undertaken by officially sanctioned action as well as by popular zeal, and the borderline between the two is not always clear. In some towns, reformers preached against images with success, while elsewhere the removal was a bureaucratic act of compliance. The result was a transformation of the interior of the parish church, with walls whitewashed, rood lofts dismantled, and altars replaced by communion tables, and with it a change in what worship looked and sounded like.

Section 64.13

The First Book of Common Prayer and the Rebellions of 1549

Parliament passed the first Act of Uniformity in January 1549, requiring the use of a single English-language order of service in all churches from Whitsunday, 9 June. The Book of Common Prayer, compiled largely by Cranmer with a committee of bishops and theologians, replaced the multiple Latin service books of the medieval church. It condensed the daily offices into Morning and Evening Prayer, provided English forms for baptism, marriage, burial, and the Eucharist, and used Cranmer's reworking of earlier materials, including the Litany and elements of the Sarum rite. The 1549 book retained much that was familiar, including vestments for the celebrant, the altar, and ceremonies such as the sign of the cross, and it described the communion in language that conservatives, notably Gardiner, argued could be read as compatible with the Real Presence. Reformers regarded it as a stage toward further change.

In the summer of 1549, two major rebellions occurred. In Devon and Cornwall, rebels objected to the new service, in particular to the English language, which in Cornwall excluded many Cornish speakers who knew little English, and demanded the restoration of the Mass, the Six Articles, and images. They besieged Exeter and were defeated in August by royal forces including foreign mercenaries, with heavy losses. In Norfolk, Kett's Rebellion, led by the landowner and tanner Robert Kett, drew thousands to a camp at Mousehold Heath near Norwich, where they put forward demands, principally concerned with enclosures, rents, and the conduct of lords, and also touching on the quality of the clergy. They took the city for a time before being crushed by an army under the Earl of Warwick, and Kett was executed.

The two rebellions should be distinguished. The western rising was explicitly religious in its articles, though economic and local concerns also played a role, and it provides vivid evidence for popular attachment to the Mass and traditional ritual, and for the importance of language. Kett's rising was primarily agrarian, directed at enclosure and landlord abuse, though its leaders were not hostile to reform and used Protestant language. Taken together, they placed Somerset's government under strain, contributed to his fall, and demonstrated that the pace of change and the social tensions of the time could interact. Historians use them as evidence for both popular resistance to reform and for the limits of generalizing about popular religion, since in the same year other communities complied or even welcomed the change.

Section 64.14

The Second Prayer Book, Protestant Doctrine, and the Crisis of 1553

Under Northumberland, reform moved decisively in a Reformed direction, influenced by continental theology as discussed in Chapter 63. Cranmer, who had moved to a symbolic or spiritual understanding of the Eucharist, debated with Bucer and Vermigli and engaged with Zurich and Strasbourg reformers, and the political context allowed him to act. Bishop Hooper's objections to vestments, which prompted a controversy in 1550, anticipated later Puritan arguments. A revised ordinal in 1550 dropped some ceremonial features of ordination, and in 1550 and 1551, altars were replaced with tables in many dioceses, with Ridley's visitation in London as an example. John Knox, who had been a galley slave in French captivity, preached in England and influenced a late revision of the book.

The Second Act of Uniformity of 1552 introduced a revised Book of Common Prayer, which came into use in November 1552. It removed vestments for the celebrant, replacing them with the surplice, eliminated the language and structure that had resembled the Mass, placed the communion at a table in the body of the church, altered the words of administration to emphasize remembrance and faith, and omitted prayers for the dead. The Black Rubric, added late after objections from Knox, explained that kneeling to receive communion did not imply adoration of the elements. The change involved an important shift. The 1549 book had left room for traditional readings, and the 1552 book unambiguously excluded them. In 1553 the king issued, by royal authority and without formal approval from Convocation, the Forty-Two Articles, a confession of faith that affirmed justification by faith and denied transubstantiation, but they were not yet in force when Edward died.

Edward fell ill in early 1553 and died on 6 July, at the age of fifteen. Northumberland, concerned about a Catholic succession under Mary, persuaded the king to draw up a document, the "Devise for the Succession," which excluded his half-sisters, regarded as illegitimate under the statutes, and named Lady Jane Grey, a Protestant great-granddaughter of Henry VII, through his younger daughter, who had recently married Northumberland's son. Jane was proclaimed queen on 10 July, but Mary raised support in East Anglia and the Thames valley, and the council, including many who had signed the Devise, abandoned Northumberland within days. Jane's reign lasted barely nine days. The episode shows the fragility of the Edwardian reformation, which had depended on a minor monarch and a minority government, and which had not, in the space of six years, secured the commitment of most of the population.

Section 64.15 · Division IV: Mary I and the Catholic Restoration

Mary I's Accession and the Reversal of Protestant Reform

Mary's success in July 1553 was due to a combination of legitimacy, regional support, and the weakness of her opponents. As the daughter of Henry VIII and under his will the next heir after Edward, she commanded broad loyalty, and the failure of the Jane Grey coup demonstrated how widely the legal succession was accepted. Her supporters included Catholics, but also many Protestants who put the dynastic claim first. This fact has some importance for interpretation: the popular response to her accession did not show that England was predominantly Catholic, and it did not show that it was predominantly Protestant either. It showed that the principle of legitimate succession was strong.

Mary was a convinced Catholic who regarded the Edwardian changes as heretical and the break with Rome as an offence against her mother's memory and against the church. She moved quickly to restore Catholic worship, and the Mass was celebrated in many churches before it was legally restored. Protestant bishops, including Cranmer, Ridley, Latimer, and Hooper, were imprisoned, and conservative bishops such as Gardiner, who became lord chancellor, and Bonner were restored. Parliament, in its autumn 1553 session, repealed the Edwardian religious legislation, returning the church to the state at the end of Henry's reign, while still leaving the royal supremacy formally in place, a stage that reflected Mary's reluctance to accept the title but also political caution.

Mary faced serious challenges. Her gender raised doubts, since no queen had ruled in her own right, and she needed a husband. Her choice of Philip of Spain, the son of Charles V, was unpopular among many English subjects and politicians, who feared Spanish domination, and it provoked Wyatt's Rebellion in January 1554, which was suppressed after marching to the edge of London. The rebellion led to the execution of Jane Grey and her husband, who had been in the Tower. The marriage went ahead in July 1554, under a treaty that limited Philip's powers in England. The choice of Philip created a strong link between Catholicism and foreign influence in the minds of many English Protestants, an association that would affect English politics for generations. The relation of the restoration to the wider Habsburg system is examined in Chapter 75.

Section 64.16

Reconciliation with Rome and Cardinal Reginald Pole

The restoration of papal authority required the return of Cardinal Reginald Pole, a grandson of the Duke of Clarence and thus of royal blood, who had lived in exile, rejected Henry VIII's supremacy, and become a figure of the Catholic reform movement in Italy, discussed in Chapter 65. Pole arrived in November 1554, and Parliament petitioned for reconciliation. Pole absolved the realm on 30 November, and in early 1555 Parliament repealed the anti-papal legislation, including the Act of Supremacy. The reconciliation was a major symbolic moment, at which Parliament and the nobility knelt to receive absolution. But it was achieved on one important condition. The pope, Julius III, agreed that holders of former monastic property, which had passed into many hands, would not be required to restore it.

Pole, who became archbishop of Canterbury after Cranmer's deprivation in 1556, pursued an agenda of institutional and spiritual renewal. His legatine synod of 1555 and 1556 issued decrees that emphasized the residence of bishops and clergy, better education of priests, regular preaching, and catechesis, and it ordered the publication of a book of homilies for use when no preacher was available. Bishops were appointed in accordance with these aims. Efforts were made to improve clerical training and to prepare a program of seminary-style formation, which anticipated the reforms of the Council of Trent, though the plan was not realized fully before Mary's death. A few monasteries were refounded, including Westminster Abbey and houses of friars, and Catholic devotional practice began to be rebuilt, with churchwardens' accounts recording the purchase of vestments, rood images, and service books, in many parishes at considerable expense.

The restoration confronted practical difficulties. The loss of monastic lands could not be reversed, and the Crown's finances were weak. The supply of educated priests was limited, and many married clergy who had taken advantage of Edward's legislation were deprived, with estimates of the proportion varying by diocese. In some places, married priests were required to separate from their wives and sometimes restored after penance. The pace of reconstruction also varied by region and parish. In many places, restoration was swift, and parishioners found the resources to rebuild what had been taken down. In some places there was passive resistance, and in a few places, evidence of obstruction or slow compliance. The overall picture is of a program with real energy and real obstacles, and of a church that needed time that it did not have.

Section 64.17

Marian Persecution, Protestant Martyrdom, and Religious Exile

In December 1554 and January 1555, Parliament revived the medieval heresy laws, and burnings began in February 1555 with John Rogers, a prominent Protestant who had edited Matthew's Bible. In the following years, roughly 280 men and women were executed for heresy, most in the southeast, especially London, Essex, Kent, and Sussex, with a lower number in other regions. Among them were prominent churchmen, including Hooper, Ridley, and Latimer, burned in 1555, and Cranmer, burned at Oxford in March 1556 after a series of recantations followed by a final repudiation of them. But most of the executed were ordinary people: artisans, tradespeople, laborers, and a significant number of women. Their numbers and social composition suggest the extent of evangelical belief at the parish level in the southeast by the 1550s.

The motivations of the persecution are debated. Mary and Pole regarded heresy as a spiritual and social poison and believed that the example of public punishment would deter and persuade, and many continental regimes burned heretics at the time, so that the practice was not unique. Bishop Bonner's role in London was prominent, and Gardiner, who died in November 1555, seems to have been less enthusiastic. Public reaction was mixed. Some witnesses were hostile to the victims, and others sympathized, with reports of crowds expressing sorrow or support for the condemned. Evidence on the effectiveness of the policy is similarly mixed. It does appear to have strengthened the Protestant cause in some localities by creating martyrs, and the decision to maintain the policy over three years suggests that the regime saw no alternative.

Many Protestants fled abroad. Roughly eight hundred left, a number that includes clergy, gentry, scholars, and merchants, and gathered in cities including Frankfurt, Strasbourg, Zurich, Emden, and Geneva. In Frankfurt, disputes erupted over whether to use the 1552 prayer book or a Genevan model, foreshadowing later Puritan divisions. In Geneva, a congregation under John Knox and others produced the Geneva Bible, first published in 1560, with its influential marginal notes. Exile enabled English Protestants to experience Reformed churches directly, and many returned in 1558 with a commitment to further reform. Others stayed in England and met secretly, in London and elsewhere, in underground congregations that preserved their practice. The experience gave later Protestantism a resource of memory, in which the Marian burnings became an emblem of English Protestant identity, preserved above all in John Foxe's Acts and Monuments, first published in English in 1563, which presented the victims as heirs of a true church persecuted by a false one.

Section 64.18

The Achievements, Limitations, and Legacy of Mary's Restoration

Recent scholarship has revised an older picture of Mary's reign as a doomed and bloody interlude. Historians such as Eamon Duffy, David Loades, and others have shown that the restoration was well planned and in several respects effective, with a reasonably coherent preaching and catechetical program, and substantial lay support in many localities. The speed with which many parishes re-equipped themselves is evidence of real commitment, and the reconstruction of ceremony could build on memory and habit. The reign also involved administrative and financial reforms, and Pole's synod anticipated measures that were later regarded as characteristic of the Catholic Reformation.

Nonetheless, the limits are evident. The reign was short, five years, and it did not produce an heir. Mary's marriage was childless, and her husband was often absent, which meant that the succession of Elizabeth, whom Mary regarded with suspicion as a Protestant sympathizer and as the daughter of Anne Boleyn, was probable. The war with France, entered in 1557 at Philip's urging, led to the loss of Calais in January 1558, a blow to prestige, and it increased resentment of the Spanish connection. Harvest failures and an influenza epidemic added to hardship in 1556 to 1558. The persecution damaged the cause it was meant to defend, and the Protestant exiles and underground congregations formed a ready body of support for a change of regime.

Mary and Pole died within hours of each other on 17 November 1558. The consequence was that the restoration, which had depended on the personal commitment of the queen and archbishop, ended abruptly. This outcome was not inevitable in the sense that the restoration was bound to fail, since a longer reign or a Catholic successor might have consolidated it. But the contingency of the death should not be treated as the sole cause of the outcome. Elizabeth, unlike Mary, took the throne without a foreign husband, with a hostile France and an uncertain Spain, and with a nobility and gentry that included many with a stake in the property settlement of the previous decades. Each of these conditions favored a different path. The Marian experience nonetheless left a legacy for English Catholicism, since many of the priests, and some of the ideas and practices that sustained it later, came from the restored church.

Section 64.19 · Division V: Elizabeth I and the Construction of a Protestant Establishment

Elizabeth I and the Religious Settlement of 1559

Elizabeth was twenty-five when she became queen on 17 November 1558. She had conformed outwardly to Catholic worship under Mary, but her upbringing, her education by humanist tutors, and her political position as the daughter of Anne Boleyn all pointed toward a Protestant settlement. Her chief secretary, William Cecil, was an experienced reformer, and her advisers included others who had held office under Edward. The queen was cautious, and her instinct for order, her dislike of religious controversy, and her sense of the dangers presented by Catholic powers, particularly Spain and France, affected the form of the settlement. The first Parliament of her reign met in January 1559, and the process was neither smooth nor simple.

The Crown originally proposed a single bill to restore the supremacy and the 1549 or 1552 prayer book, but it met resistance in the House of Lords from Marian bishops and from conservative peers, and the Convocation of the clergy rejected the changes. After a disputation at Westminster in March and with changes in Parliament's membership, the government divided the legislation. The Act of Supremacy, passed in the spring of 1559, restored royal authority over the church, but its title for the queen was Supreme Governor, and it required an oath from clergy and office-holders. The Act of Uniformity imposed a revised Book of Common Prayer from the feast of St. John the Baptist, 24 June 1559, and was carried in the Lords by a very narrow margin. The revised book was based on the 1552 book but altered it in significant ways, and these are discussed in the primary source feature below.

Enforcement began with a royal visitation in the summer of 1559. Commissioners went through the dioceses and administered the oath, and the Injunctions of 1559 regulated church practice, including the removal of images, the preaching of sermons, and the conduct of the clergy. Nearly all of the Marian bishops refused the oath of supremacy and were deprived, which created an opportunity to fill the bench with reformers, though several of the new bishops were reluctant to accept the more conservative aspects of the settlement. Matthew Parker, a Cambridge scholar and former chaplain to Anne Boleyn, was consecrated as archbishop of Canterbury in December 1559. By contrast, most parish clergy, of whom only a small proportion refused the oath, conformed, and many continued in post. The achievement of 1559 was therefore the creation of a legal framework that most of the clergy and laity were prepared to accept, but it was not yet a Protestant England in the sense of widely shared belief.

Section 64.20

The Thirty-Nine Articles, Episcopal Government, and Anglican Doctrine

The doctrine of the Elizabethan church was set out in the Thirty-Nine Articles, a revision of Cranmer's Forty-Two Articles adopted by Convocation in 1563 and, in a slightly revised Latin and English form, in 1571, when Parliament required clergy to subscribe to those articles concerning the faith and sacraments. The Articles affirmed the authority of Scripture, justification by faith, the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, and the sufficiency of the Scriptures for salvation, and they rejected transubstantiation, purgatory, the sacrifice of the Mass, and papal supremacy. On the doctrine of predestination, the Articles gave a moderately Reformed account without excluding some variety of interpretation. The Homilies, first issued under Edward and expanded in 1563, together with Bishop John Jewel's Apology of the Church of England (1562) and Alexander Nowell's catechism, provided additional doctrinal texts.

The church retained bishops, cathedrals, a hierarchy of courts, and a liturgy with some continuity of form, and its defenders argued that these were consistent with Scripture and primitive Christianity. This position is sometimes called the via media, or middle way, though historians caution that the phrase can mislead. It was not a theology of moderation between Rome and Geneva in a modern sense. In doctrine, the Elizabethan church was predominantly Reformed, and its defenders such as Jewel regarded Rome as in error. Its distinctive feature was its structure and its retention of some ceremonies. The fullest intellectual defense of the settlement came near the end of the reign in Richard Hooker's Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, whose first books appeared in 1593 and which argued that church government and ceremonies were matters on which reason, tradition, and Scripture could together guide a Christian commonwealth.

The term "Anglican" for this religious identity developed later, and care is needed in applying it to the sixteenth century. Within the church, differences existed in theology and practice. Many bishops and clergy were committed Calvinists in doctrine, while others, such as some cathedral clergy, valued ceremony and sacramental ideas that led later to controversy. The queen herself held views that were more conservative on ceremony, on clerical marriage, which she disliked, and on preaching, which she thought was sometimes excessive. The result was a church that held within it a spectrum of opinion, bound by common legal obligations and prayer book, and the management of this diversity was a central problem of the reign.

Section 64.21

Catholic Survival, Recusancy, and Religious Persecution

In the early years of the reign, many Catholics attended parish services while hoping for a change, and the government was relatively restrained in its enforcement. The situation altered in the late 1560s. In 1568 Mary, Queen of Scots, fled to England, creating a Catholic claimant to the English throne, and in 1569 the Northern Rising, led by the earls of Northumberland and Westmorland, attempted to restore Catholic worship and was crushed with many executions. In 1570, Pope Pius V's bull Regnans in Excelsis declared Elizabeth excommunicated and deposed, which made Catholic loyalty seem to many officials to be a question of treason. The Catholic response was divided, since many Catholics were loyal subjects who did not support deposition, but the bull made their position harder.

From 1574, priests trained at the English college founded at Douai by William Allen began to arrive in England, and from 1580 they were joined by Jesuits, including Edmund Campion and Robert Persons. These missionaries sustained the Catholic community through the celebration of Mass and the sacraments, usually in the households of gentry, where priests could be hidden in concealed spaces. Women played a very large role, since they organized household devotion, sheltered priests, and passed on the faith to children, and some were fined or imprisoned for it. The government responded with a series of penal laws. The Act of 1581 raised the fine for recusancy, the refusal to attend church, to twenty pounds a month, and made it treasonable to reconcile anyone to Rome. The Act of 1585 made it treason for a priest ordained abroad to be in England. After the Babington plot of 1586, which led to the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots in February 1587, and the Spanish Armada of 1588, a Catholic invasion seemed an immediate danger, and enforcement hardened.

Roughly 180 Catholics, priests and lay helpers, were executed under Elizabeth, some in barbarous fashion as traitors, while others died in prison or were fined into poverty. The authorities insisted that the victims died as traitors, not as heretics, and the Catholic community held that they died for their faith. The number of recusants is hard to assess, because many Catholics attended the parish church to avoid penalties, a practice that critics called "church papistry." It is generally estimated that Catholics formed a small but persistent minority, stronger in Lancashire, Yorkshire, parts of the Midlands, and the gentry households of the south, and often under the protection of local landowners. English Catholicism thus survived, but changed from a majority religion to a minority community, organized around households and missionary priests rather than parishes and ceremonies. A disagreement between secular priests and Jesuits about strategy and loyalty marked the later years.

Historical Vocabulary
Recusancy

Recusancy derives from the Latin recusare, to refuse. In Elizabethan law a recusant was someone who refused to attend the services of the established church, in breach of the Act of Uniformity. The term came to refer particularly to Roman Catholics who refused to attend, though Protestant dissenters could in theory also be prosecuted. Recusancy was punishable by fines, which rose over time, and by other civil disabilities.

Recusancy should be distinguished from the broader Catholic population. Many Catholics attended the parish church to avoid penalties and were called church papists, and some held Catholic beliefs but did not clearly identify with the missionary community. Modern historians therefore treat recusancy as a legal category that captures only part of English Catholic life. The term also did not carry the sense of principled dissent that it later acquired, since contemporaries regarded it as a failure to meet a legal and civic obligation.

Section 64.22

Puritanism, Presbyterian Reform, and Protestant Nonconformity

Some returning exiles and other reformers regarded the Elizabethan settlement as incomplete. They objected to the survival of vestments, to kneeling at communion, to the sign of the cross in baptism, to the apparent laxity of discipline, and to the failure to provide a learned preaching ministry. In the 1560s, the Vestiarian controversy led to the suspension of clergy who refused to wear the surplice and to the Advertisements of 1566, issued by Parker to enforce uniformity. Opponents called the critics Puritans, a term of abuse, and they called themselves godly or the faithful. The movement was a spectrum, from those who objected to particular ceremonies to those who sought the abolition of bishops.

In the early 1570s, a more radical program emerged. In 1572, the Admonition to the Parliament denounced the episcopal structure and called for a church governed by elders and ministers on a presbyterian model, as in Geneva and Scotland, and Thomas Cartwright, a Cambridge professor, gave academic support to the position. The government, led by Archbishop John Whitgift after 1583, rejected the program, and Parliament, in which Puritan sympathizers sat, was warned by the queen not to meddle in matters of religion. A network of ministers developed informal gatherings to study Scripture and discuss practice, known as prophesyings, which the queen regarded with suspicion. When Archbishop Edmund Grindal refused to suppress them in 1577, Elizabeth suspended him from his functions. The Marprelate tracts of 1588 and 1589, satires against the bishops, led to a crackdown.

A smaller group, the separatists, concluded that the Church of England was beyond reform and set up independent congregations, such as that of Robert Browne in the early 1580s. Some of these leaders, including Henry Barrow and John Greenwood, were executed in 1593 under legislation passed that year. The legislation reflected an important point about the Elizabethan regime: it treated Protestant separatism and Catholic recusancy as threats to order. Yet most Puritans remained within the church and sought to reform it from inside, building on opportunities for preaching, lectureships, and patronage, and many gained influence in parishes, universities, and the gentry. The tension between conformity and further reform, unresolved at the queen's death, would shape English politics for the next century, as Chapter 77 examines.

Historical Vocabulary
Puritan

Puritan was a contemporary term, often hostile, for Protestants who wished to purify the English church of what they regarded as remnants of Catholicism in ceremony, structure, and discipline. It covered a wide range, from those who accepted bishops but sought a more preaching-centered ministry to those who sought a presbyterian system, and, at the margin, those who separated from the church. Puritans shared a Reformed theology with many bishops, and the differences within the church were often about practice and discipline rather than doctrine.

The word should not be used as a modern synonym for strictness or prudishness. Puritans were not defined by a distinctive creed separate from the church, and historians debate how sharply to distinguish them from other zealous Protestants. The label is useful, but it needs careful use.

Section 64.23

The Reformation in Everyday Life: Parishes, Families, Women, and Schools

The reception of the Reformation depended on what happened in parishes. Official policy required attendance at services, regular preaching or reading of homilies, instruction in the catechism, and the maintenance of church fabric by churchwardens. Visitations by bishops and archdeacons recorded what was done, and parishioners could be presented to the church courts for absences, for sexual offences, or for failing to receive communion. Churchwardens' accounts, preserved for a minority of parishes, record the replacement of rood lofts by royal arms and of Latin service books by English prayer books, bibles, and psalters. Whitewash and texts from Scripture were painted over images, and pews gradually replaced open nave space, so that the physical experience of worship altered, with a stronger emphasis on hearing the sermon.

Religious instruction was supported by printed material. The prayer book itself provided a short catechism, and Nowell's longer catechism of the 1570s was widely used by teachers and clergy. The psalms, translated into English meter, were sung in church, and the Geneva Bible became the principal household Bible, with the Bishops' Bible of 1568 as the official church version. Literacy, still limited, was increased through parish clergy, schoolmasters, and the grammar school foundations of the period, though the claim that Edward VI founded many schools has been qualified by historians who note that many were refoundations of earlier institutions. Preaching became more important, but the shortage of trained preachers meant that many parishes depended on homilies and curates of limited learning, and the quality of the clergy improved gradually, with more university graduates by the end of the century.

The Reformation altered household life and gender experience in complex ways. The legalization of clerical marriage created a new category of clerical family, regarded with suspicion by the queen and by traditionalists, but increasingly normal by the late sixteenth century. Protestant teaching placed emphasis on the household as a place of religious education, with fathers and mothers responsible for prayer, Bible reading, and catechizing children, and women, though excluded from clerical office, could be prominent as readers, patrons, and martyrs. The loss of convents removed a vocational option, and the end of purgatory and the cult of saints altered rituals of death, mourning, and memory, such as bequests for prayers. Poor relief, previously aided by monasteries, guilds, and almsgiving linked with prayer for the dead, shifted toward parish and civic responsibility, and a series of statutes, culminating in the poor laws of 1598 and 1601, established a national system of parish rates. Belief in magic, witchcraft, and providence was not removed, and Protestant preachers sometimes sought to replace older practices such as blessing and charms with prayer and providence, with mixed success. Evidence for the beliefs of ordinary people is difficult, but wills, with their changing preambles, suggest a gradual transition from traditional to Protestant formulae across the later sixteenth century, uneven by region and class.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reading a Parish's Reformation

Churchwardens' accounts offer one of the best windows on how national policy was experienced locally. A parish that paid for removing its images in 1548, for a new service book in 1549, for taking down an altar in 1550, for reinstating an altar and rood in 1554, and for pulling them down again in 1559, left a sequence of expenses that records not only the instructions of the Crown but the choices and costs of ordinary people. Some accounts show prompt compliance, some foot-dragging, and some deliberate concealment of goods that might be needed again. Others show that parishioners spent substantial sums on restoration under Mary.

Such records have limits. They survive unevenly, and they record expenses rather than belief. A parish that obeyed may have done so out of conviction, fear, or habit, and a parish that delayed may have been reluctant, poor, or simply slow. Historians combine accounts with wills, visitation records, and court cases to build a more complete picture, and they remain cautious in generalizing from a small number of well-documented communities to the whole realm.

Section 64.24

Wales, Ireland, and the Uneven Geography of Tudor Religious Change

The Tudor monarchy ruled several territories with different languages, legal systems, and religious traditions. Wales had been integrated with England by the Acts of 1536 and 1543, which extended English law and administration and made English the language of the courts, while the great majority of the population spoke Welsh. The Welsh reception of Protestantism depended in large part on language. An Act of 1563 required the translation of the Bible and prayer book into Welsh, with a deadline of 1566 that was missed by a year, and the Welsh New Testament and prayer book appeared in that year, largely the work of William Salesbury, Richard Davies, bishop of St Davids, and Thomas Huet. In 1588, William Morgan, later a bishop, published the complete Welsh Bible. Historians regard these translations as critical for the survival of written Welsh and for the acceptance of Protestantism, since the church could now use the language of the people. Some Catholic resistance persisted, as in parts of the Marches and north Wales, but the Welsh church by 1600 was largely Protestant in practice.

Ireland followed a different course. Henry VIII was declared king of Ireland by the Irish Parliament in 1541, and the Irish Parliament had passed a supremacy act in 1536, but the Crown's authority was limited, effectively confined to the Pale around Dublin and a few areas, and the Gaelic and Old English lords largely controlled the rest. Edwardian reform made little progress, and Mary restored Catholicism with little difficulty. Elizabeth's Irish Parliament of 1560 passed acts of supremacy and uniformity, but the church had few Protestant clergy who could preach in Irish, the translations were slow, the New Testament in Irish was not printed until 1602 to 1603, and enforcement was limited outside the Pale. Instead, the arrival of Jesuits and other Catholic clergy from the 1560s, the strengthening of ties with continental Catholicism, and the military conflicts of the period, including the Desmond rebellions of the 1570s and 1580s and the Nine Years' War from 1594 to 1603, linked the Catholic religion to resistance to English conquest and to a developing Irish identity. Protestantism became associated with English settlers and the Crown, while the majority of the Irish population, both Gaelic and Old English, remained Catholic. Trinity College Dublin, founded in 1592, was one of the Crown's efforts to train a Protestant clergy.

Scotland, a separate kingdom, underwent its own Reformation, which is mentioned here only for comparison. In 1560, the Scottish Parliament, meeting amid civil conflict and with English military support, abolished papal authority and adopted a Reformed confession, and the kirk developed a presbyterian structure under the influence of John Knox and later Andrew Melville. Unlike England, where the Reformation was directed by the monarch, in Scotland the movement was led by a Protestant aristocracy and ministry, and the Catholic monarch, Mary, Queen of Scots, was forced out in 1567. The contrast sharpens the question of how far the Crown's role was decisive in England and shows that the "English" Reformation was only one of several in the British Isles, a point that becomes important for the political crises of the seventeenth century.

Section 64.25

The English Reformation by 1603: Achievement, Division, and Unfinished Transformation

By 1603, England had been a Protestant kingdom for much of a generation. The Church of England had a distinctive legal and liturgical identity, and the Crown's authority over it was established. A generation of clergy and laity had grown up knowing no other form of worship than the prayer book. The Bible in English, the Book of Common Prayer, and the calendar of the national church formed a shared religious culture, and Protestant identification with the nation, symbolized in the memory of the Marian martyrs and of the defeat of the Armada, was strong. For these reasons, historians often argue that by the end of Elizabeth's reign, a majority of the population had become, in a broad sense, Protestant, though the process was gradual and the question of how deep it went remains debated.

The settlement had not removed division. Catholics, perhaps a small minority, remained, sustained by priests and households, and legally and socially disadvantaged. Puritans pressed for further reform, and on the queen's death, the Millenary Petition of 1603, which asked the new king for changes, showed that the problem remained. The settlement did not create religious toleration. The Elizabethan government never intended to permit freedom of conscience, and it saw uniformity as essential to political stability. It distinguished, in a way that later historians have sometimes found artificial, between outward conformity, which it enforced, and inward belief, which it said it did not seek to investigate, though in practice the distinction was hard to keep. Ideas of toleration emerged only later, as examined in Chapters 82 and 140, and in connection with the conflicts of the seventeenth century.

The Reformation also had consequences beyond religion. It enlarged royal and parliamentary authority, enriched a new class of landowners, changed the provision of education and poor relief, affected literacy and print culture, and altered ideas about gender, family, and the household. It shaped English overseas expansion, as examined in Chapters 72 and 73, in which Protestant identity and anti-Catholic sentiment played a role, and it set the terms for the religious conflicts of the Stuart period. The Reformation was therefore neither a short event nor a finished one. The process continued, as later generations reinterpreted and contested the Tudor inheritance, and the Elizabethan settlement was both the product of unusual circumstances and a framework within which later developments occurred.

Historical Debate

Was the English Reformation Imposed, Embraced, or Resisted?

The question: To what extent was the English Reformation a revolution from above, driven by the Crown and a political elite, and to what extent did it grow from below, in response to popular religious change, or meet with resistance that shaped its outcomes?

Interpretation A: A Reformation from below that was welcomed: A. G. Dickens, in a study that dominated postwar scholarship, argued that Lollardy, anticlericalism, and early evangelical networks created a ready audience for reform, so that Protestantism spread because many people found it persuasive, particularly in towns and the southeast.
Interpretation B: A traditional religion forcibly dismantled: Revisionist historians, notably J. J. Scarisbrick and Eamon Duffy, emphasized the strength and adaptability of late medieval religion, and argued that the Reformation was imposed by a state that destroyed popular religious practices, which many people defended, as shown by rebellions and by the speed with which parishes restored Catholic worship under Mary.
Interpretation C: A negotiated and uneven process: More recent scholarship, including work by Christopher Haigh, Diarmaid MacCulloch, Patrick Collinson, Ethan Shagan, and Alec Ryrie, emphasizes variation by region, social group, and time, and suggests that most people neither enthusiastically embraced nor wholly rejected change, but adapted, conformed, negotiated with authority, and gradually shifted over decades, with Protestantism winning acceptance in part through the long Elizabethan period of stable rule and preaching, and in part through generational change.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on the same sets of sources, including churchwardens' accounts, wills, visitation records, court cases, rebellion articles, printed books, and martyrologies, but read them differently. Accounts that show spending on images in the 1530s can be read as evidence of vitality, and accounts showing prompt removals can be read as compliance or conviction. Evidence of heresy prosecutions can show how widespread early evangelicals were or how isolated.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they understand the role of the state, the nature of popular religion, and the causes of religious change, and it shapes how the Tudor period is remembered, whether as liberation from superstition or as a destructive episode. It also bears on broader questions about whether religious change can be imposed by authority.

  • The language of worship, from Latin to English, and the central role of Scripture and preaching
  • The interior of the church, with altars, images, and rood lofts replaced by a table, texts, and royal arms
  • The abolition of monasteries, chantries, purgatory, and many saints' cults, and the legalization of clerical marriage
  • The status of Catholicism, from the religion of the realm to a minority community

What Stayed the Same

  • The parish as the unit of religious life, with its churchwardens, buildings, and rhythms of baptism, marriage, and burial
  • The structure of dioceses, bishops, cathedrals, and church courts
  • The expectation that membership in the community meant membership in the church, enforced by law
  • The social hierarchy of gentry patrons and local officials who managed religious change

Who experienced change most directly? Former monks and nuns, chantry priests, married clergy, Catholic gentry, and the southeastern urban Protestants experienced change most directly. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural parishioners experienced continuity in the rhythms of parish life even as their worship changed. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Reformation was both a deep institutional rupture and a process in which much of the inherited framework survived.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the English Reformation from the late medieval church to the death of Elizabeth I in 1603, and it has argued that the Reformation was a sequence of contested transformations rather than a single event. Its governing question was how a monarchy's break with papal authority developed into a durable Protestant establishment despite reversals, resistance, and continuing division. The answer depends on distinguishing three processes: legal separation from Rome, doctrinal and liturgical Protestantization, and the longer conversion of parish communities.

The late medieval English church was a vital and widely supported institution, though it faced criticism from Lollards, humanists, and anticlerical voices. Evangelical ideas, carried by printed books and Tyndale's English New Testament, reached small networks in the 1520s. The Reformation as a political event began not with these groups but with the Crown, when Henry VIII's need for a legitimate male heir and the pope's dependence on Charles V turned an annulment request into a jurisdictional crisis.

Between 1529 and 1534, the Reformation Parliament replaced papal jurisdiction with royal supremacy, and the Act of Supremacy and the Treasons Act enforced the change through oaths and the threat of execution, as the fates of More and Fisher showed. This was a legal revolution, yet it did not alter the Mass or the principal doctrines. The distinction between rejecting papal authority and adopting Protestant doctrine runs through the whole chapter.

Under Thomas Cromwell, the Crown surveyed church wealth, visited religious houses, and dissolved the monasteries between 1536 and 1540, transferring a large share of landed property and changing local religious, social, and charitable life. The Pilgrimage of Grace showed that resistance could be broad, but it also showed regional limits. Henry's doctrinal position oscillated, producing the English Bible and Litany alongside the Six Articles and the King's Book, and he left a church that was neither fully Protestant nor Catholic.

Under Edward VI, the minority government advanced Protestantism through injunctions, the dissolution of the chantries, the removal of images, and the prayer books of 1549 and 1552, and doctrine moved in a Reformed direction. The rebellions of 1549 revealed popular resistance and agrarian grievance. The reformation's dependence on a boy king was exposed in 1553, when the attempt to place Lady Jane Grey on the throne collapsed.

Mary I restored Catholicism and papal authority in 1553 and 1554 with substantial lay support in many places and with a serious program of renewal under Cardinal Pole. The burning of roughly 280 Protestants, the exile of several hundred more, and the Spanish marriage damaged her cause and gave Protestants an enduring memory of martyrdom, preserved in Foxe's book. Her restoration was ended by her death in 1558, but it left a legacy for later English Catholicism.

Elizabeth I's settlement of 1559 created a Protestant church with a revised prayer book, royal governance, retained bishops, and the doctrinal definition of the Thirty-Nine Articles. It was a compromise, accepted by most of the clergy and laity, but not by committed Catholics, who became a recusant minority sustained by missionary priests and household worship, nor by Puritans, who sought further reform. Penal laws against Catholics and separatists show that the regime sought uniformity, not toleration.

The Reformation affected the Tudor realms unevenly. Welsh-language scripture helped establish Protestantism in Wales, while in Ireland the Reformation was weakly implemented and became associated with English conquest. Scotland's separate Reformation had a different, presbyterian character. Everyday life changed in parish worship, household religion, gender roles, education, and poor relief, but unevenly, and historians continue to debate whether the Reformation was imposed, embraced, or negotiated. By 1603, England was broadly Protestant and the Crown's authority over the church was secure, but the question of how far reform should go, and how far dissent should be tolerated, remained open.

Key Terms

Royal supremacy
The doctrine, enacted in 1534, that the Crown held supreme authority over the English church, replacing papal jurisdiction.
Praemunire
Medieval statutes penalizing the assertion of foreign jurisdiction in England, used against Wolsey and the clergy in 1529 to 1531.
Annulment
A declaration that a marriage had never been valid, which Henry VIII sought from the pope and eventually obtained from Cranmer's court in 1533.
Convocation
The assembly of the clergy of each English province, which debated doctrine and taxation and whose role changed under royal supremacy.
Act in Restraint of Appeals
The 1533 statute declaring England an empire and ending appeals to Rome in key cases.
Treasons Act (1534)
The statute making denial of the king's titles, including supreme head, a treasonable offence.
Valor Ecclesiasticus
The 1535 survey of church incomes that supplied the information for the dissolution.
Visitation
An official inspection of clergy, religious houses, or parishes, used by bishops and by the Crown to enforce policy.
Dissolution of the monasteries
The closure of religious houses between 1536 and 1540, with transfer of their property to the Crown.
Chantry
An endowment to pay a priest to pray for the dead, abolished in 1547 along with associated guilds and colleges.
Pilgrimage of Grace
The northern rising of 1536 to 1537 against religious change, taxation, and the dissolution.
Act of Six Articles
The 1539 statute reaffirming traditional doctrines such as transubstantiation and clerical celibacy under severe penalties.
Great Bible
The 1539 English translation, edited by Coverdale, ordered to be placed in parish churches.
Transubstantiation
The Catholic doctrine that the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ in the Mass, rejected by Protestant reformers.
Injunctions
Royal or episcopal orders regulating church practice, such as those of 1547 and 1559.
Act of Uniformity
A statute requiring use of a single prayer book, passed in 1549, 1552, and 1559.
Book of Common Prayer
The English liturgy compiled chiefly by Cranmer, issued in 1549 and 1552 and revised in 1559.
Prayer Book Rebellion
The 1549 rising in Devon and Cornwall against the English liturgy.
Iconoclasm
The removal or destruction of religious images, officially sanctioned under Edward VI.
Heresy
Belief or teaching judged by church authority to contradict true doctrine, punishable by burning under Henry and Mary.
Marian exiles
Protestants who left England under Mary, some of whom returned with Reformed ideas and a commitment to further reform.
Martyrology
A collection of accounts of those who died for their faith, such as Foxe's Acts and Monuments.
Supreme Governor
The title adopted by Elizabeth I in 1559 for her authority over the church.
Thirty-Nine Articles
The doctrinal statement of the Church of England, adopted in 1563 and enforced by subscription in 1571.
Via media
A phrase, later associated with Anglicanism, for a middle way; applied to the Elizabethan church with caution.
Recusancy
Refusal to attend the services of the established church, chiefly associated with Catholics.
Church papist
A Catholic who attended the parish church to avoid penalties while retaining Catholic belief.
Seminary priest
A priest trained abroad, for example at Douai, to serve the Catholic mission in England.
Puritan
A contemporary term for Protestants seeking further purification of the English church.
Presbyterianism
A form of church government by ministers and elders rather than bishops, advocated by some Elizabethan reformers.
Separatist
A Protestant who left the established church to form independent congregations.
Catechism
A manual of religious instruction, usually in question and answer form.

Chapter Reflection

The English Reformation matters because it shows, in an unusually clear form, how religious change could be directed by a state and yet remain beyond the full control of any ruler. A king's dynastic problem produced a constitutional revolution that placed the church under statute, and four successive governments used that authority to impose four different settlements. Each change had to be carried out by parish officials and accepted, evaded, or resisted by communities, and the eventual outcome emerged from this interaction between command and response across several generations.

The chapter also contributes to the argument of Part X and of Volume III. Alongside the German, Swiss, French, and Dutch cases, the English experience shows that the Reformation took no single form. In England the Crown led, the church retained bishops and much of its structure, and the break with Rome became bound up with national identity, the authority of Parliament, and the consolidation of the early modern state examined in Chapter 74. At the same time, the Reformation was part of an international movement, in which English reformers drew on Zurich, Strasbourg, and Geneva, and English Catholics drew on Rome, Douai, and Spain, a connection that Chapters 65 and 66 develop.

The consequences extended beyond the sixteenth century. The unresolved status of Catholics and Puritans, the relationship between Crown and Parliament in religious matters, and the contested identity of the national church became central problems of the seventeenth century, and the Protestant memory of the Marian martyrs and the Armada shaped English political culture for generations. The Reformation also altered the landscape of property, education, poor relief, and family life, and it affected the terms of English expansion overseas, as later chapters show. The Elizabethan settlement endured, but it was never the inevitable outcome of what had come before.

Finally, the chapter invites students to hold together two things: the real power of the state to reshape institutions, and the equally real limits on its power to change belief. Chapter 65 now turns from England to the Catholic Church's own program of reform and renewal, which responded to the Protestant challenge and to the longer demand for reform examined in Chapter 61, and which shaped the confessional landscape of the whole continent.
