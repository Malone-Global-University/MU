The Avignon Papacy, Great Schism, and Religious Reform

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

For nearly seventy years the head of Latin Christendom governed from a fortified palace on the Rhône rather than from Rome, and for nearly forty years after that, two and eventually three men claimed to be the one legitimate successor of Saint Peter. The institution that had claimed to be the final court of appeal for all western Christians found that it could not settle, by its own procedures, the question of who the pope was.

Visitors to Avignon today still encounter the Palais des Papes, a vast complex of stone towers, chapels, audience halls, and storerooms rising above the river. It was built mainly in the middle decades of the fourteenth century for a papal court that had not planned to stay. The building is a useful starting point because it shows both sides of the story this chapter tells. Its scale and organization testify to a confident, well administered, wealthy institution that managed benefices, petitions, legal cases, diplomacy, and finance across the whole of Latin Christendom. Yet its fortifications, and the very fact that the pope resided there, also testify to the insecurity of papal power in Italy and to political pressures that medieval popes could not ignore.

Earlier chapters of this Part traced the demographic, political, and social shocks of the fourteenth century: the famines and plague examined in Chapter 50, the dynastic warfare of Chapter 51, and the revolts and social tensions of Chapter 52. This chapter turns to a different kind of crisis, one of institutions and ideas. The papacy sat at the head of the only church to which nearly all western Europeans belonged, and its government touched wills, marriages, taxation, university appointments, monastic discipline, and the filling of thousands of church offices. When that government became divided against itself, the consequences reached parish priests, cathedral chapters, kings, university masters, and ordinary believers who wanted to know which sacraments and which authorities could be trusted.

Two intertwined stories run through the chapter. The first is the breakdown and reconstruction of papal authority: the clash between Boniface VIII and Philip IV of France, the move to Avignon, the administrative growth of the papal court, the return to Rome, the disputed election of 1378, the rival obediences, and the long search for a solution that ended at the Council of Constance in 1417. The second is the emergence of competing ideas about how the church should be governed and reformed, including the conciliar argument that a general council might act when popes could not, the critique of clerical wealth and authority associated with John Wyclif in England, and the reform movement of Jan Hus in Bohemia that led to one of the most violent religious conflicts of the fifteenth century.

The evidence for these developments is unusually rich in some respects and unusually partisan in others. Papal registers, chancery records, financial accounts, and petitions to the Curia survive in large numbers and allow historians to reconstruct how the Avignon administration worked. Council proceedings, university treatises, canon law commentaries, sermons, chronicles, and letters preserve the arguments of the reformers and the lawyers. At the same time, nearly every narrative source was written by someone who belonged to one obedience or another, or who condemned the entire papal system, so historians must read with attention to loyalty, polemic, and purpose.

This chapter treats the crisis as a crisis of governance and legitimacy within Latin Christendom. Neither the Avignon papacy nor the schism concerned a doctrinal quarrel between competing creeds; the rival popes shared the same sacraments and the same theology and disputed a succession. The chapter also stays within its assigned boundaries. Wyclif, Lollardy, Hus, and the Hussites appear here because they illuminate the contest over ecclesiastical authority in the conciliar age, while the broader inheritance of clerical criticism, popular piety, and humanist reform in the decades before Luther belongs to Chapter 61, and the political worlds of Burgundy, the Baltic, and Poland-Lithuania are developed in Chapter 54.

The chapter concludes by explaining a paradox. By 1449 the western church again had one pope, and papal monarchy had survived the conciliar challenge. Yet the institutional repair left unanswered many of the questions that the crisis had made urgent: how clerical wealth should be justified, how church offices should be filled, who could discipline a pope, and how pastoral care could be improved. The restoration of unity was real, and so was the unfinished work of reform.

Historical Context

The papacy of the late thirteenth century stood at the end of two centuries of institutional growth examined in Part VII. The reform movement of the eleventh century, discussed in Chapter 44, had asserted the independence of the church from lay control and the primacy of the bishop of Rome within it. Over the following generations popes had developed a body of canon law, a system of courts, a corps of legates and judges delegate, and a financial apparatus capable of collecting revenue across Latin Christendom. Universities, treated in Chapter 48, trained the lawyers and theologians who staffed this system. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 had required annual confession and communion and had given the church a program of pastoral reform, while the new mendicant orders, the Franciscans and Dominicans, carried preaching and confession into towns across Europe.

This was also a church deeply embedded in property and politics. Bishops and abbots administered estates, held courts, and often served kings as councillors. Cathedral chapters, collegiate churches, and parishes were sustained by benefices, endowed offices that provided income to their holders, and the right to appoint to benefices, called patronage, was held by popes, kings, nobles, monasteries, and towns. The same system that supported worship, learning, and charity also created opportunities for absenteeism, the accumulation of several offices by one person, and the use of church revenues to reward service to rulers. Criticism of clerical wealth and ambition was therefore old by 1300, and it came from within the church as often as from outside it.

Politically, the papacy had defeated the Hohenstaufen emperors in the thirteenth century only by relying on French and Angevin power, and its position in Italy remained fragile. The Papal States were a patchwork of cities, lordships, and noble clans, and Rome itself was dominated by rival baronial families, notably the Colonna and the Orsini. Meanwhile, monarchies in France and England, examined in Chapter 45 and Chapter 51, were building more effective systems of taxation and royal justice. Rulers who needed revenue for war looked at the wealth of the clergy, and the question of whether kings could tax the church without papal consent became a leading constitutional issue between secular and ecclesiastical authority.

The evidence for this chapter comes from several kinds of source. The papal registers, the Vatican archives of financial accounts, petitions to the Curia, and chancery formularies reveal how the administration of the church operated day to day. Council acts, university treatises, canonist commentaries, and sermons record the argument over authority. Chronicles, letters, and polemical poems, including the writings of Petrarch and Catherine of Siena, preserve contemporary perceptions. Historians must distinguish routine administrative evidence, which often shows an effective and responsive system, from polemical sources, which emphasize luxury, simony, and neglect. The label “Great Schism” itself, like “Babylonian Captivity,” is a retrospective term whose meaning shifted over time, and the chapter treats such labels as part of the historical record rather than as neutral descriptions.

Section 53.1

53.1 The Western Church around 1300: Authority, Wealth, and the Problem of Reform

Any account of the Avignon papacy and the schism must begin with strength rather than decline. Around 1300 the bishop of Rome claimed a plenitude of power over the universal church, and the machinery that supported the claim was formidable. Canon law, systematized in Gratian’s twelfth century collection and in the papal decretals gathered in the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, gave the Curia a body of rules and procedures that bishops, abbots, and secular rulers could invoke and had to answer. Appeals flowed to Rome, legates traveled to kingdoms, and papal judges delegate heard cases far from the papal court. The papacy had also become a major financial institution, drawing revenue from the Papal States, from tribute, from fees, and from periodic taxes on the clergy levied for crusade and other purposes.

This authority rested on the practical usefulness of the papal court. Litigants sought papal judgments because they were more widely enforceable than local ones, clergy sought dispensations and provisions, and rulers sought privileges, absolutions, and arbitration. The universities of Paris, Bologna, Oxford, and elsewhere supplied jurists and theologians who elaborated papal powers, and the mendicant orders, answerable directly to Rome, gave the papacy a trained corps of preachers and inquisitors independent of local bishops. The system also produced strains. Bishops resented papal interference in appointments, cathedral chapters defended their right of election, and rulers disliked foreign tax collectors and the removal of money from their kingdoms.

Calls for reform were therefore part of the ordinary life of the church long before Avignon. Councils such as Lyon in 1274 had legislated against simony, pluralism, and clerical neglect, and writers inside and outside the hierarchy criticized wealth and worldliness. Historians distinguish carefully between criticism of churchmen, which was common and often conventional, and rejection of the church itself, which was rare. Throughout this chapter the movements examined, from conciliar theory to Lollardy to Hussitism, arose in a society in which almost nobody imagined a Christianity without a church, and the debates concerned how that church should be governed, funded, and held to account.

Section 53.2

53.2 Boniface VIII, Philip IV, and the Crisis of Papal Monarchy

Boniface VIII, a canon lawyer of the Caetani family elected in 1294 after the unprecedented resignation of Celestine V, held an exalted view of papal authority and was prepared to defend it. The immediate conflict arose over taxation. As England and France prepared for war, both kings taxed their clergy heavily, and in February 1296 Boniface issued the bull Clericis laicos, forbidding laypeople to tax the clergy and clergy to pay without papal permission. Philip IV’s government responded by prohibiting the export of money from France, which struck directly at papal revenue. Boniface retreated, issuing clarifications that allowed voluntary contributions in emergencies, and the crisis cooled.

It returned in the early 1300s over the prosecution of a French bishop and over royal jurisdiction. In November 1302 Boniface issued Unam sanctam, a statement of the unity of the church and the subordination of temporal power to spiritual authority in matters of salvation. Historians continue to debate how novel its claims were and how far it was aimed at Philip specifically, but the political result was a confrontation. Royal councillors, among them Guillaume de Nogaret, accused Boniface of heresy and illegitimacy and called for a general council to judge him. In September 1303 Nogaret and Sciarra Colonna, whose family was an enemy of the Caetani, led an armed group to Anagni, where the pope was staying, and seized him. Local townspeople freed him within days, but Boniface died in Rome in October 1303.

Anagni has often been portrayed as the moment when the medieval papacy fell. That reading is too simple. Boniface was not destroyed by French arms, and papal jurisdiction in most of Europe continued unimpaired. What changed was the political environment: it had become clear that a determined monarchy could resist, humiliate, and pursue a pope, and that the papacy could not rely on the old assumption that violence against a pontiff would provoke universal retribution. His short lived successor Benedict XI sought to reduce tensions, and the conclave after his death in 1304 ran for nearly a year before turning to an outsider, the archbishop of Bordeaux, who became Clement V.

Section 53.3

53.3 Clement V and the Papal Move to Avignon

Bertrand de Got, elected in June 1305 at Perugia, was a Gascon who had served as archbishop of Bordeaux, a subject of the English king in his duchy of Gascony and a figure acceptable to both the French and the Italian cardinals. He was crowned at Lyon in November 1305 and did not go to Rome. Conditions in Italy were disorderly, with baronial factions in Rome, war among cities, and little secure papal control in the Papal States. He also needed to deal with Philip IV, who pressed for a posthumous trial of Boniface VIII and for action against the Order of the Temple. The Templars were arrested across France in October 1307, and after investigation and political pressure Clement suppressed the order at the Council of Vienne in 1311 to 1312, a decision shaped by royal pressure and by evidence that historians have long debated.

The papal court moved around southern France for several years before settling in Avignon in 1309. The choice was practical. Avignon was a prosperous city on the Rhône, near the papal territory of the Comtat Venaissin, in a position that gave convenient access to the sea and to routes toward Italy, Iberia, and the north, and it lay outside the kingdom of France, within the lands of the Angevin count of Provence. Clement did not announce a permanent move, and in fact most contemporaries expected the court to return. Only gradually, as successive popes built palaces, expanded the administration, and encountered renewed disorder in Italy, did Avignon become the established seat.

It is therefore misleading to describe the move as a French kidnapping or the Avignon popes as prisoners. Clement V and his successors made choices in response to a combination of danger in Italy, financial and administrative convenience, personal ties, and pressure from the French crown, and they repeatedly considered returning. The residence outside Rome mattered because the pope was also bishop of Rome, and because the city and its churches depended on papal presence for revenue and prestige. But the move was an extended improvisation that hardened into a settled arrangement, not a single decision with a clear purpose.

Section 53.4

53.4 Governing the Church from Avignon

Under John XXII (1316 to 1334), Benedict XII (1334 to 1342), and Clement VI (1342 to 1352), Avignon became one of the most highly organized administrative centers in Europe. The papal chancery issued and registered letters in enormous numbers. The Apostolic Camera managed revenue and expenditure under its chamberlain. The Rota, the court of auditors, heard cases, and the Apostolic Penitentiary dealt with confessions and dispensations reserved to the pope. Supplications from clergy and laypeople across Europe arrived in bulk, were summarized by clerks, and were granted or rejected, and the surviving registers allow historians to see ordinary petitioners, from a poor cleric seeking a benefice to a nobleman seeking a marriage dispensation, engaging with the papal court.

The popes also acted as diplomats and political arbiters. They attempted to mediate between England and France in the early phase of the Hundred Years’ War, discussed in Chapter 51, tried to organize crusading expeditions, negotiated with the Holy Roman emperors, and intervened in the politics of Naples and northern Italy. Theological controversy continued at the papal court as well. John XXII became entangled in the dispute with radical Franciscans over apostolic poverty and in a protracted conflict with Louis IV of Bavaria, which helped produce political writing such as Marsilius of Padua’s Defensor pacis (1324), a text that argued for the subordination of the clergy to secular authority and the sovereignty of the community.

The Palais des Papes, begun under Benedict XII and extended under Clement VI, provides physical evidence of this administrative expansion, but its construction reflects more than comfort. It housed the officials, records, treasury, and ceremonies of an international government. Seen in this light, Avignon was not a mere interlude. It is the period in which the central administration of the western church became more bureaucratic, better documented, and more fully integrated with canon law, a development that contemporaries admired and criticized in equal measure.

Section 53.5

53.5 Papal Finance, Benefices, and the Growth of Criticism

Running this administration was costly, and the Avignon popes developed an elaborate system of revenue. Annates, usually one year’s income of a benefice, were demanded from clergy appointed to certain offices. Common services were payments by bishops and abbots on confirmation. The papacy took the income of vacant benefices in some cases, collected tithes and special taxes for crusade, and charged fees for documents, dispensations, and legal services. These payments sustained the court, funded diplomacy and warfare in Italy, and paid for the buildings at Avignon, and many were regularized under John XXII and Benedict XII.

The system of provisions and reservations was closely linked to this financial structure. Rather than allowing local patrons or electors to choose every benefice holder, the papacy increasingly reserved appointment to particular offices, or issued provisions naming candidates in advance. Papal provision could reward scholars, serve as a mechanism of patronage for cardinals and kings, and give the popes influence in dioceses across Europe. It also meant that benefices could be held by absentees who never visited their churches, that one clergyman could hold several offices, and that petitioners competed at the papal court for lucrative positions, a practice that critics described as the sale of spiritual things.

Historians must be careful here. Financial demands on the church were not in themselves signs of corruption, since governments of all kinds required revenue, and many provisions went to well qualified candidates. The accusations of simony, the buying and selling of spiritual offices, were often directed at practices that were legal under contemporary canon law but looked mercenary to critics. At the same time, the sheer scale of papal collection, the visible wealth of cardinals and curial officials, and the long wait of poor clergy for preferment fed resentment. Kings, cathedral chapters, and universities, each with their own interest in appointments, formed a broad base of criticism that later shaped both reform movements and attacks on papal power.

Historical Vocabulary
Benefice and Provision

A benefice was an ecclesiastical office endowed with property or revenue, such as a parish, canonry, abbacy, or bishopric, which provided income to the person who held it in return for religious duties. A provision was the act by which a patron, and increasingly the pope, appointed a person to a benefice, often before a vacancy occurred or in place of the usual local selection. A reservation was a papal claim to fill particular offices.

These terms matter because the system of benefices connected spiritual work, income, family strategy, royal service, and papal power. A modern reader may find it strange that a clergyman could hold an income from a church he never visited. In the fourteenth century this arrangement was an accepted way to pay scholars, officials, and clerical administrators, and the argument was over its limits. Critics did not usually deny that churchmen needed support; they attacked pluralism, absenteeism, and the commercial atmosphere of the papal court.

Section 53.6

53.6 Avignon, France, Italy, and the Politics of Papal Independence

All seven Avignon popes came from the south of France, and a large majority of the cardinals they created were French or Occitan, so contemporaries, especially in Italy and England, were quick to see the papacy as a French institution. The residence in a city that was within the French cultural sphere and close to the French kingdom increased that perception, as did the fact that French kings frequently asked for tithes, privileges, and support in war. English and imperial opponents suspected the popes of favoring France in the Hundred Years’ War and in their dispute with Louis of Bavaria.

Yet the evidence does not support the claim that the popes were puppets. John XXII and Benedict XII pursued their own priorities, often resisting French requests, and Clement VI tried hard to mediate between England and France. The popes needed French money and protection but also needed to avoid being identified as the instrument of a single power, because their authority depended on claiming to be the father of all Christians. Papal diplomacy aimed at preserving this independence, and the Curia was a multinational body that included Italians, English, Germans, Spaniards, and others in its lower ranks.

Italy remained central to papal strategy. The popes claimed sovereignty over the Papal States and spent heavily to recover them. Cardinal Gil Albornoz, in the 1350s and 1360s, reconquered much of the region and issued constitutions for the Papal States that remained in effect for centuries. In 1348 Clement VI bought the city of Avignon from Joanna I of Naples, the Angevin countess of Provence, for 80,000 florins, giving the papacy full control of the city for the first time. It is important to distinguish this from the Comtat Venaissin, a nearby territory that had been papal property since 1274. The popes thus held a small territorial base in southern France that was independent of the king of France, but that base did not make them French agents and did not resolve the problem of Rome.

Section 53.7

53.7 “Babylonian Captivity”: Reputation, Polemic, and Reform Criticism

The phrase “Babylonian Captivity” compares the Avignon residence to the biblical exile of the Israelites in Babylon. Its best known literary source is the Italian poet and scholar Petrarch, who lived near Avignon for years and wrote letters denouncing the court as a place of luxury, intrigue, and moral decay, calling it a western Babylon. His denunciations are vivid and widely quoted, but they reflect a particular perspective. Petrarch was an Italian humanist hoping for a revival of Rome, he depended on patronage in the very court he criticized, and his rhetoric was heavily shaped by classical and biblical models. Other writers, including the Swedish visionary Bridget of Sweden and the Italian mystic Catherine of Siena, attacked the papal residence for spiritual reasons, calling for the pope to return to his see and to reform his court.

These criticisms drew on a deep sense of Rome’s importance as the city of Peter and Paul, of the martyrs, and of the great basilicas, where the pope had a particular duty as its bishop. They also drew on the sense that wealth and ceremony at Avignon contradicted the ideals of apostolic poverty, a tension made sharper by the Franciscan poverty controversy. For Italians in particular the absence of the papacy meant lost pilgrims, lost revenue, and a loss of prestige for Rome.

The label should therefore be handled as a hostile interpretation, not a neutral description. It passed from Italian and reform polemic into later Protestant and national historical writing, which found it useful for arguments about papal corruption, and it long dominated narratives of the period. Modern scholarship tends to read Avignon more complexly, as a period in which the administrative achievements and the criticisms were both real, and in which contemporaries disagreed about what the residence meant. Students should treat “Babylonian Captivity” as evidence of how critics framed the papacy, rather than as a straightforward summary of the facts.

Historical Debate

Was Avignon a Period of Decline or of Administrative Consolidation?

The question: Did the residence of the papacy at Avignon weaken the institution, as later critics asserted, or did it strengthen papal government in ways that historians should recognize as achievement?

Interpretation A: Avignon as decline. This older and still influential view emphasizes the absence from Rome, the French dominance of the college of cardinals, the growth of fiscal demands, and the loss of moral prestige. In this account the burdens of the Curia and the visible wealth of the court prepared the way for the schism and for later anticlericalism.
Interpretation B: Avignon as consolidation. Many modern historians stress the efficiency of the Avignon administration, its sophisticated use of records, its judicial and diplomatic reach, and its responsiveness to petitioners. In this view the era reveals a papacy at the height of its administrative capacity, and the criticism of finance and patronage reflects the burdens of an effective central government more than moral collapse.

Evidence: The two readings use different sources. Interpretation A leans on polemical letters, sermons, and later chronicles. Interpretation B relies on registers, accounts, and petitions that show administration at work. The two kinds of evidence are not necessarily contradictory.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one explains the schism. If Avignon was simply a decline, the schism seems a predictable outcome. If it was consolidation, the schism looks more contingent, a political and procedural collapse in 1378 that was not inevitable from the earlier administration.

Section 53.8

53.8 Returning the Papacy to Rome

The idea of returning to Rome was never entirely abandoned. After the Albornoz campaigns made parts of the Papal States more secure, Urban V (1362 to 1370) entered Rome in October 1367, spent time there and in nearby towns, and welcomed the Byzantine emperor John V Palaiologos, who sought western aid against the Ottoman advance examined in Chapter 41. But disorder, the opposition of many French cardinals, and renewed warfare led Urban to return to Avignon in 1370, where he died the same year. His example showed that the return was possible but difficult.

Gregory XI (1370 to 1378), nephew of Clement VI and also a French cardinal, eventually carried it through. Pressure came from several directions: Bridget of Sweden, who had died in 1373, remained a prophetic voice, and Catherine of Siena, a Dominican tertiary from Siena, wrote letters to the pope urging return and reform and traveled to Avignon in 1376. At the same time the political situation in Italy had turned against the papacy. The legates governing the Papal States had provoked widespread resentment, and from 1375 Florence led a league of cities in rebellion in what became known as the War of the Eight Saints, after the Florentine magistrates who directed it. Gregory concluded that only his presence could restore control.

He left Avignon in September 1376 and arrived in Rome in January 1377. Catherine deserves recognition as a prominent advocate and as an intermediary in the conflict with Florence, but historians no longer credit her with single handedly persuading the pope. His decision was shaped by military and diplomatic pressures, by the long standing wishes of many Italians and some cardinals, and by his own sense of duty to Rome. He died in March 1378, barely a year after the return, with the Papal States still unsettled and the college of cardinals divided between French and Italian members. The conclave that followed would meet under the most difficult circumstances.

Section 53.9

53.9 The Election Crisis of 1378 and the Beginning of the Great Schism

When Gregory XI died, sixteen cardinals were present in Rome, a majority of them French or Occitan, and they were divided into factions. Rome’s citizens, anxious that the papacy would leave again, crowded around the Vatican demanding a Roman or at least an Italian pope. In the conclave that opened in April 1378 the cardinals chose Bartolomeo Prignano, the archbishop of Bari, an Italian official of the Curia who was not a cardinal. He took the name Urban VI. Rioting and pressure surrounded the election, and sources report that during the confusion the cardinals presented an elderly Roman cardinal to the crowd as the new pope before Prignano’s election was made known. The cardinals then confirmed the choice, enthroned Urban, and for some weeks dealt with him as pope.

Urban VI proved a difficult ruler. He denounced cardinals publicly, announced reforms of luxury, threatened to create many new cardinals to dilute French influence, and quarreled with powerful supporters. By the summer most of the cardinals had left Rome. In August 1378, at Anagni, they declared the election void because it had been conducted under duress, and on 20 September at Fondi they elected Robert of Geneva, a cardinal related to many ruling houses, who took the name Clement VII. By 1379 Clement had established himself at Avignon, while Urban remained in Rome, and each pope had his own cardinals, officials, and revenue.

The dispute concerned the legitimacy of an election, not doctrine. Both sides accepted the same church, creed, and sacraments, and both appealed to canon law on whether a conclave under pressure could be valid. If the cardinals had acted freely in April, Urban was pope and Clement an antipope. If fear had undermined the election, Urban’s claim failed, and Clement was properly chosen. No court existed that could settle the question, because the ordinary arbiter of such disputes was the pope himself. The cardinals who made the case were the same ones who had first accepted Urban, and their change of mind made them look opportunistic to some observers, while their claim of fear appeared credible to others. Historians continue to find the evidence ambiguous, and modern scholarship generally avoids simply declaring one side fraudulent.

Section 53.10

53.10 A Divided Christendom: Rival Obediences, 1378 to 1409

Christendom divided into rival obediences, the groups of rulers, dioceses, orders, and universities that recognized one pope or the other. The Roman obedience under Urban VI and his successors, Boniface IX (1389 to 1404), Innocent VII (1404 to 1406), and Gregory XII (1406 to 1415), included most of Italy, the German lands and the empire under Wenceslas and later rulers, England, Hungary, Poland, the Scandinavian kingdoms, and Flanders. The Avignon obedience under Clement VII (1378 to 1394) and Benedict XIII (1394 to 1423, though effectively abandoned by most later) was recognized by France, Scotland, Savoy, parts of the Angevin Neapolitan world, and eventually the Iberian kingdoms of Castile, Aragon, and Navarre.

These alignments were not simple. Political alliance often determined loyalty: England, at war with France, supported Rome, while Scotland, allied with France, followed Avignon. Castile and Aragon delayed before declaring for Clement, and Italian states changed sides as their interests shifted. Within a kingdom the division could be sharp, with bishops, universities, and religious orders choosing different popes. Naples, for example, saw dynastic conflict shape papal loyalty, and the Holy Roman Empire contained princes and cities of both allegiances. Obediences were therefore political and ecclesiastical coalitions that changed over time rather than fixed blocks, and some regions, such as the borderlands of Savoy and the Rhineland, could move between them.

The schism also revealed the extent to which papal authority depended on secular support. Neither pope could enforce his claims without kings, princes, and cities willing to host and finance him. When French support for Avignon cooled in the 1390s, the Avignon pope Benedict XIII was besieged in his palace, and for a time France withdrew obedience from him altogether. Attempts at negotiation, proposed meetings, and arbitration repeatedly failed, in part because each pope believed he was the legitimate pontiff and could not resign without admitting doubt, and in part because rulers who benefited from the divided church had little incentive to hasten its end.

Historical Vocabulary
Obedience

In this period an obedience meant the body of rulers, clergy, and communities that recognized a particular claimant as pope. To “withdraw obedience” was to cease acknowledging a pope’s authority, as France did from Benedict XIII for several years. The term is useful because it avoids prejudging which claimant was legitimate, whereas the later word antipope reflects the verdict of whichever line of popes was eventually recognized. This textbook uses “claimant” and “obedience” in narrative prose and does not label rival popes “fake.”

Section 53.11

53.11 What Did the Schism Mean for Clergy and Laypeople?

For the hierarchy the schism meant doubled appointments. When a bishopric or abbey became vacant, rival popes might each name a candidate, and the two obediences could produce rival bishops in the same diocese, as happened in parts of France, the Empire, and Italy. Religious orders divided, in some cases with separate Roman and Avignon superiors. Rulers used the situation to bargain: kings could threaten to switch obedience unless a pope conceded privileges, and many secured greater control over appointments and taxation in exchange for support.

Taxation was another burden. Each pope needed revenue to maintain his court and to fight for recognition, and each demanded annates, tithes, and special levies from clergy in his obedience, often with the support of a ruler who took a share. Clergy complained that they were paying more than ever to support a divided church, and in some regions rulers and councils limited payments or redirected them. Such conflicts strengthened the tendency of kings to regulate the church in their own territories, a theme that returns in the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges and in the concordats examined at the end of this chapter.

Lay believers faced a more spiritual problem. If a priest was appointed by the wrong pope, were his sacraments valid? Theologians generally answered that sacraments administered in good faith within a recognized church remained effective, and that ignorance of the true pope did not invalidate ordinary pastoral acts, which kept the system functioning. But anxiety was real, particularly where bishops were disputed or excommunications were issued by one side against the other. Writers such as the Catalan Dominican Vincent Ferrer, who at first supported Avignon, preached both reform and repentance and the legitimacy of the church’s sacraments amid the confusion. Most communities made local accommodations, following their bishop or prince, praying for the pope their ruler recognized, and waiting for a settlement.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under Two Popes

For most villagers the schism did not alter the rhythm of baptisms, marriages, masses, and burials. The parish priest said the canon of the mass in which the pope was named, and the name chosen depended on the obedience of the region. Travel and trade could take a person from one obedience to another, so a merchant from England might hear a different pope named in a church in Aragon or Scotland. The evidence for lay reaction is limited and mostly indirect, drawn from preachers, chroniclers, and the surviving records of disputes over local benefices and taxation.

The effects were most concrete for clergy seeking positions, for students and masters who needed papal letters, and for people involved in litigation that required papal confirmation. Pilgrimage and indulgence systems were also affected, since rival popes proclaimed jubilees and indulgences that competed for pilgrims and offerings. The result was not only confusion but a growing sense that spiritual benefits had become entangled in politics, a feeling that nourished calls for reform.

Section 53.12

53.12 Wyclif, Lollardy, and the Question of Church Authority

John Wyclif (c. 1330 to 1384) was an Oxford theologian whose reputation as a philosopher and theologian rested on a long academic career. In the 1370s he developed a theory of dominion, arguing that lordship and property depended on divine grace, and that a church that failed in its spiritual mission could be reformed and even deprived of temporal possessions by secular rulers. His writings attracted the interest of political figures, including John of Gaunt, who were hostile to the wealth of the clergy and to papal taxation. In 1377 Gregory XI condemned a series of propositions drawn from his works, though Wyclif was protected for a time by his patrons and by Oxford.

Wyclif’s ideas developed in the context of English politics and Oxford debate and were not simply a response to the schism, which began only in 1378. The schism, however, gave him new reasons to question papal claims. He came to argue that the true church consisted of those predestined to salvation, that scripture was the supreme authority, and that popes and clergy were subject to correction when they departed from it. Late in his career he challenged the doctrine of transubstantiation in favor of a view that the bread and wine remained in substance while becoming Christ’s body sacramentally, a position that alienated many allies. In 1382 an ecclesiastical council in London condemned a set of his propositions, and he was forced from Oxford, retiring to Lutterworth, where he died at the end of 1384.

The movement known as Lollardy drew on his ideas, but it was not a unified party. Its followers included clergy, gentry, artisans, and women, who circulated vernacular scripture, notably the English Bible translations associated with Wyclif’s circle, and criticized clerical wealth, images, pilgrimages, and some sacramental teaching. The English crown responded with the statute De heretico comburendo (1401), which provided for burning relapsed heretics, and with the failed Oldcastle rising of 1414. Lollardy persisted underground for generations. Historians caution against treating Wyclif as a Protestant born too early. His thought was deeply scholastic and medieval, his concerns were not identical to those of sixteenth century reformers, and the later claim that he was the “Morning Star of the Reformation” belongs to Protestant historical memory more than to fourteenth century evidence.

Section 53.13

53.13 Jan Hus and Reform in Bohemia

Bohemia was a rich and politically important kingdom of the Luxembourg dynasty. Prague, enlarged under Charles IV, had become an imperial capital, and Charles University, founded in 1348, drew students from across central Europe. The city had also developed a lively reform tradition before Hus, with preachers such as Jan Milíč of Kroměříž and Matěj of Janov criticizing clerical worldliness and urging frequent communion. The kingdom contained both Czech and German speaking communities, and tensions between the two shaped university and city politics, so the reform movement cannot be reduced to imported ideas.

Jan Hus (c. 1370 to 1415), a Czech master of arts and later theologian at Prague, became rector of the university and, from 1402, preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel, where he preached in Czech to large audiences. He criticized clerical abuses, simony, and the moral failings of priests, and defended the authority of scripture. He knew Wyclif’s writings, which had reached Prague in part through connections between Bohemia and England after the marriage of Anne of Bohemia to Richard II, and he defended some of Wyclif’s propositions as orthodox. But Hus did not simply copy Wyclif. He rejected Wyclif’s view of the Eucharist, accepted transubstantiation, and developed his own conception of the church in works such as De ecclesia, drawing also on Bohemian reformers and the Augustinian tradition.

In 1409 the Decree of Kutná Hora, issued by King Wenceslas IV, gave the Czech nation in the university three votes against one for the other nations, prompting many German masters and students to depart, some founding the University of Leipzig. Hus’s opposition to papal crusade indulgences issued by the Pisan pope John XXIII, and his conflict with the archbishop of Prague, led to excommunication and a papal interdict on Prague, and he left the city for a time. By 1414 he was ready to defend his views before a general council, a decision that placed him in the center of the era’s greatest ecclesiastical assembly.

Section 53.14

53.14 Conciliarism: Could a Council Stand above a Pope?

The schism raised a practical and theoretical question: who could judge a pope? Canon law offered some precedents. Medieval canonists had discussed cases in which a pope might be judged, notably for heresy, and had developed ideas about corporate bodies and representation that could be applied to the church. Twelfth and thirteenth century jurists had argued that the cardinals and the church as a whole retained some residual authority if the pope fell into error. Such arguments had mostly remained academic, but the crisis gave them urgency.

Three solutions were proposed. The via cessionis asked both claimants to resign. The via compromissi would submit the dispute to arbiters. The via concilii, the council route, held that a general council representing the whole church could resolve the matter, even without papal summons. The University of Paris, under theologians such as Pierre d’Ailly and later Jean Gerson, became a major center of conciliar thought, and canonists such as Francesco Zabarella supplied legal arguments. Earlier treatises of 1380 and 1381, associated with Conrad of Gelnhausen and Henry of Langenstein, had already argued that a council could act in an emergency.

Conciliarism was not a single doctrine. Some thinkers regarded councils as an extraordinary remedy for a particular emergency, while others argued that the church’s highest authority lay permanently in a council, with the pope as its chief executive officer. Councils were also not elected parliaments. Participation was limited to prelates, doctors of theology and law, and representatives of rulers and universities, and at Constance voting by nation gave heavy weight to political units. Conciliarism should therefore not be described as medieval democracy. It was an argument about corporate authority within a hierarchical church, and its supporters aimed to restore unity and reform, not to create popular sovereignty.

Historical Vocabulary
Conciliarism

Conciliarism is the modern name for a set of ideas holding that a general council of the church possessed authority that could, at least in certain circumstances, be exercised even against or above the pope. The word was coined much later; contemporaries argued concrete cases through legal and theological reasoning. In practice conciliarism ranged from a moderate belief that a council could act when the papacy failed to a more radical claim that a council was the supreme governing body.

The term matters because it describes how churchmen tried to solve the schism within the church’s own tradition. Students should avoid equating it with modern constitutionalism or democracy: the council was a body of clerical and political elites, and conciliar thinkers largely defended the hierarchy.

Section 53.15

53.15 The Council of Pisa and the Three-Pope Crisis, 1409

By the first years of the fifteenth century the ways out of the schism had been tried and had failed. The via cessionis had won wide support after the University of Paris consulted its masters in 1394, but neither line of popes would give way. France withdrew its obedience from Benedict XIII in 1398, restored it in 1403 after he escaped from confinement in Avignon, and in 1408 again declared neutrality. In 1407 Gregory XII of Rome and Benedict XIII agreed in principle to meet at Savona, and each then found reasons not to come. When Gregory created new cardinals in 1408, in breach of his own promises, a group of his cardinals left him, and several of Benedict’s cardinals abandoned him as well. Meeting at Livorno in the summer of 1408, the dissident cardinals of both obediences called a general council to open at Pisa in March 1409.

This was not an improvised act. Conciliar arguments had been developing since the treatises of 1380 and 1381 and had been refined by Pierre d’Ailly, Jean Gerson, and canonists in Italy and France. They held that when the papal office could not be filled by agreement and each claimant refused to yield, the church as a whole, acting through cardinals, prelates, and learned delegates, could assemble without papal summons to preserve its unity. Pisa put that argument into practice. Roughly a hundred bishops, many proxies, abbots, delegates from universities and cathedral chapters, and ambassadors of kings and princes took part, and the council declared in June 1409 that Gregory XII and Benedict XIII were notorious schismatics, deposed them, and elected Peter Philarges, archbishop of Milan and a Franciscan born on Crete, as Alexander V.

Pisa had real strengths. It drew on a broad coalition of France, England, Bohemia, much of the Rhineland, Florence, and Louis II of Anjou, and it commanded the respect of the universities. But a council that deposed popes without any agreement among the powers could not enforce its verdict. Gregory XII kept the support of Ladislas of Naples, the Malatesta lords of Rimini, and parts of Germany, while Benedict XIII retained Aragon, Castile for a time, and Scotland. Alexander V, whose bulls favoring the mendicant orders angered the University of Paris, died in May 1410 and was succeeded by Baldassare Cossa, a former legate at Bologna and a skilled political and military manager, who became John XXIII. The result was three claimants, each with an obedience, not one. Pisa shows that conciliar theory was necessary to break the stalemate but not sufficient, because a council’s authority depended on the willingness of rulers to act on it.

The failure also redirected the strategy. John XXIII, driven from Rome by Ladislas of Naples in 1413, needed a protector and turned to Sigismund, who had been elected king of the Romans in 1410 and again in 1411 and was determined to be remembered as the restorer of unity. Sigismund pressed for a council on imperial soil, and at the end of 1413 John agreed to summon one to meet at Constance. That decision, taken under pressure and without any intention of surrendering his claim, shaped the whole course of the next council.

Cause and Effect

Why the Schism Lasted so Long

Long-term causes

A strongly centralized papal monarchy with no recognized procedure for judging a disputed election; resentment of Curial finance and patronage; and the dependence of both the papacy and secular rulers on a church that was also a major property holder.

Short-term causes

The 1378 conclave, held under pressure from the Roman crowd; Urban VI’s harsh conduct; the cardinals’ repudiation; and the willingness of rulers, especially in France, to recognize Clement VII.

Immediate effects

Two papal courts, doubled appointments, mutual excommunications, and divided religious orders and universities across Latin Christendom.

Long-term consequences

The rise of conciliar theory, increased royal control of local churches, a new urgency in demands for reform, and a legacy of distrust in papal government.

Historical complexity: The schism endured because several things were true at once: each claimant had a plausible legal case, each had political backers who gained from his survival, no accepted authority could judge between them, and resignation seemed to concede illegitimacy. The Council of Pisa shows how even a remedy backed by many rulers could deepen the problem when it ignored these realities.

Section 53.16

53.16 The Council of Constance, 1414 to 1418

The Council of Constance opened on 5 November 1414 in an imperial city on Lake Constance that had to house cardinals, bishops, abbots, university delegates, royal and princely ambassadors, lawyers, and the merchants, innkeepers, and entertainers who followed them. Contemporary chroniclers, notably Ulrich Richental, gave very large figures for visitors, but these are impressionistic and historians treat them with caution. The authorities were also unusually diverse: the council attracted the leading minds of the age, including d’Ailly, Gerson, and Zabarella, as well as delegates from Poland and Lithuania, Hungary, Scandinavia, and the Iberian kingdoms.

Sigismund played a decisive role. He arrived at Christmas 1414, presided over ceremonies, protected the assembly, and used his political leverage to keep the pope in the city and to broker support among rulers. Early in 1415 the council made a crucial procedural decision. Instead of voting by head, which would have favored the numerous Italian prelates and John XXIII’s clients, it organized voting by nations, Italian, French, German, and English, with a Spanish nation added after the Iberian rulers abandoned Benedict XIII. Questions were prepared in each nation and then settled in general sessions. This gave rulers and universities a larger voice, but it also made the council dependent on political blocs. It was an institutional design for emergency, not a representative assembly in a modern sense.

When John XXIII fled to Schaffhausen under the protection of Duke Frederick of Austria on 20 March 1415, the council might have dissolved. Instead the fifth session of 6 April issued Haec Sancta, which asserted the council’s authority, and Sigismund forced Frederick to submit. John was deposed on 29 May 1415 and later accepted his fate. Gregory XII agreed to resign in July 1415, but only after Carlo Malatesta, acting for him, convened and authorized the council in his name, which preserved Gregory’s claim to legitimacy while allowing the assembly to act with papal sanction. This unusual legal solution mattered because it gave the council a path to a settlement that the Roman obedience could accept.

Benedict XIII was harder. Sigismund traveled to Perpignan and Narbonne in 1415 and 1416, and in December 1415 the kingdoms of the Iberian obedience agreed to withdraw obedience from him. Benedict retreated to the fortress of Peñíscola, where he continued to claim the papacy until his death. Constance declared him deposed in July 1417. A last dispute then delayed the election: Sigismund, the Germans, and the English wanted reform of the church before a new pope was chosen, while the French, many Italians, and the cardinals preferred to elect first. The compromise of October 1417 included the decree Frequens, requiring regular councils at fixed intervals, and a limited set of reform decrees, with other matters postponed to negotiation with the next pope. An electoral body of the cardinals and thirty delegates, six from each nation, then chose Oddone Colonna on 11 November 1417 as Martin V.

The council’s reform program was less far reaching than many delegates had hoped. Martin V negotiated separate concordats in 1418 with the German, French, English, and Spanish nations, which addressed some matters of appointments, annates, and papal revenue but were limited in term and scope, and the Curia kept much of its financial and administrative machinery. Constance also condemned Wyclif’s teachings, and in 1428 Martin V’s authorities had his remains exhumed and burned. The council therefore secured the end of the schism, expressed conciliar ideas in Haec Sancta and Frequens, and left the question of reform largely open.

Section 53.17

53.17 Hus, the Hussites, and the Limits of Conciliar Unity

Hus left Prague in October 1414 and traveled to Constance relying on a safe-conduct from Sigismund, dated 18 October after he had already set out. The document protected his journey, although historians have debated the precise extent of that protection and whether it covered his stay at Constance and his return. It did not clearly grant immunity from ecclesiastical trial, and neither the council nor Sigismund treated it as doing so. Hus was arrested in late November 1414 and held in harsh conditions. Between 5 and 8 June 1415 he was heard publicly, asked to recant a list of propositions drawn or extracted from his writings, and said that he could not abjure what he had not held. He was condemned as a heretic, degraded from the priesthood, and burned on 6 July 1415. His associate Jerome of Prague was executed on 30 May 1416.

The executions provoked outrage in Bohemia and Moravia. A protest issued on 2 September 1415 ultimately bore the seals of 452 members of the Bohemian and Moravian nobility, most of them knights and lower nobles. Three days later, a smaller group formed a league to defend reform preaching and resist measures against the movement. Yet Hus’s death did not by itself create the Hussite movement. Reform preaching, resentment of clerical wealth, Czech and German tension, and the royal and noble politics of the Luxembourg kingdom all shaped what followed. The practice of communion in both kinds, bread and wine, for the laity spread quickly in Prague after 1414, and it gave the moderate party the name Utraquists. Open conflict began when Wenceslas IV died in August 1419 shortly after the First Defenestration of Prague on 30 July, in which a crowd of reformers threw town councillors from the New Town hall. His half brother Sigismund claimed the crown, and many Bohemians refused to accept as king a man they held responsible for Hus’s death.

Martin V proclaimed a crusade against the Bohemians in March 1420, and between then and 1431 repeated expeditions, drawing recruits from Germany, Hungary, and elsewhere, entered the kingdom. They failed. Jan Žižka, a gifted commander, led the radical Táborites from the new town of Tábor and developed tactics built around fortified wagons, firearms, and disciplined peasant and townsmen soldiers. In July 1420 Sigismund’s forces were defeated at Vítkov Hill outside Prague. The Four Articles of Prague of 1420 gave the movement a common program of communion in both kinds, free preaching, limits on clerical property and secular power, and punishment of public sins. They united groups that differed sharply. Prague Utraquists, nobles, and burghers favored a reformed church that preserved much traditional practice, while Táborites, Orebites, and Orphans held more radical views of the sacraments, authority, and social life, and some were apocalyptic. After Žižka’s death in 1424 the field armies under Prokop the Great carried the fight into Silesia, Saxony, Franconia, and Hungary in raids that left a deep imprint on neighboring societies.

The crusades’ failure and the heavy cost of war led the Council of Basel to invite the Hussites to negotiate in 1433. Divisions among them proved decisive. In May 1434 at Lipany a coalition of Utraquist nobles and Prague burghers defeated the Táborite and Orphan field armies, and Prokop was killed. Talks produced an accord with the Bohemian diet in 1433, and a modified version, the Compactata, was promulgated at Jihlava in July 1436, when Sigismund was finally accepted as king. The Compactata permitted communion in both kinds under defined conditions and established a negotiated Utraquist settlement in Bohemia. The papacy never formally accepted the Compactata, which left the settlement constitutionally and ecclesiastically ambiguous, and in 1462 Pius II declared it void. Constance had ended a papal schism, but it also helped provoke a religious war that outlasted the council and produced a church that remained half inside and half outside Latin Christendom’s ordinary structures.

Section 53.18

53.18 Basel, Papal Recovery, and the Unfinished Problem of Reform

Martin V returned to Rome in 1420 and began the long work of rebuilding the city and the papal state. He honored Frequens by convening a council at Pavia in 1423, which moved to Siena because of plague and soon dissolved with little accomplished. In accordance with the decree, he also convoked another general council to meet at Basel in 1431, appointing Cardinal Giuliano Cesarini to preside, but he died in February 1431 before it was underway. His successor, Eugenius IV, a Venetian born Gabriele Condulmer, soon attempted to dissolve the council in December 1431 and summon it to Bologna. The delegates refused, reaffirmed the principle of Haec Sancta in February 1432, and carried on. After a period of bitter conflict Eugenius reversed himself in December 1433 and recognized the council, though on terms that left the underlying question unresolved.

Basel was a real second conciliar crisis. It negotiated with the Hussites, issued reform decrees abolishing annates and limiting reservations and papal provisions, and asserted a claim to govern church finance and appointments. Eugenius, driven from Rome by a revolt in 1434, lived in exile at Florence and saw his revenues threatened. The break came over the reunion of the Greek church, the subject of the Byzantine emperor John VIII Palaiologos’s search for western help against the Ottomans, treated in Chapter 41. When Basel’s majority and Eugenius disagreed about where to meet the Greek delegation, a minority led by Cesarini left for Ferrara, and in 1438 the pope moved his council there and then to Florence. The union with the Greeks proclaimed there in July 1439 was soon repudiated in the East, but it raised Eugenius’s standing.

The rump council at Basel became more radical. In 1439 it declared Eugenius deposed and elected Amadeus VIII, former duke of Savoy and then a hermit at Ripaille, as Felix V. The council also found that rulers did not follow it. France issued the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges in 1438, which adopted selected conciliar decrees as French law, showing how kings could use conciliar reform to strengthen royal control. The German electors adopted neutrality, and the Concordat of Vienna of 1448 between Frederick III and the new pope Nicholas V restored significant papal rights while securing royal influence over German churches. Thinkers who had served Basel, including Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini, later rejoined the papal side. Eugenius died in February 1447 and Nicholas V was elected a month later. Felix V abdicated in April 1449, and the council dissolved at Lausanne.

By the mid fifteenth century the papacy had recovered institutionally, and in 1460 Pius II’s bull Execrabilis condemned appeals from the pope to a future council. Yet the recovery rested on bargains with princes and on a stronger Italian political base, and the underlying demands persisted: how to discipline clergy and pay for pastoral care, how to limit pluralism and absenteeism, how much wealth the church should hold, who could hold a pope accountable, and how laypeople should take part in worship. The unity restored in 1417 and confirmed in 1449 was real, but it did not answer these questions. Chapter 61 returns to this inheritance from the perspective of the generation before Luther.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Papal Authority before and after the Schism

What Changed

  • The papacy came to depend more openly on bargains with rulers, expressed in concordats and national settlements
  • The idea that a council could act in an emergency became a recognized, though contested, part of church thought
  • Papal moral prestige suffered, and criticism of Curial finance, residence, and patronage became more widespread
  • Bohemia developed a lasting church tradition, the Utraquist church, outside the usual Latin pattern

What Stayed the Same

  • The pope remained the head of Latin Christendom, with a central court, canon law, and a system of benefices
  • Most clergy and laypeople continued to practice the same sacramental religion throughout the crisis
  • Rulers continued to seek influence over appointments, taxation, and church property
  • Reform remained a general aspiration that was difficult to implement

Who experienced change most directly: Bishops, cardinals, university masters, and rulers experienced the constitutional struggle most directly, while Bohemians, followers of Wyclif, and clergy whose offices were disputed felt its consequences personally. Most parishioners saw greater continuity in daily worship.

Why the pattern matters: The mix of institutional recovery and unresolved reform explains why religious criticism continued into the sixteenth century.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the institutional and intellectual crisis of western Christendom between the conflict of Boniface VIII with Philip IV and the collapse of the Council of Basel. Its central argument is that the papacy’s strengths, a powerful central administration, a unified body of canon law, and a claim to universal authority, coexisted with weaknesses, among them dependence on secular rulers, vulnerability in Italy, and the lack of an agreed procedure for judging a pope.

The conflict between Boniface VIII and Philip IV over taxation and jurisdiction culminated in the attack at Anagni in 1303. Anagni did not destroy the medieval papacy, but it showed that a determined monarchy could resist papal claims and that papal power rested on political conditions as well as doctrine.

The move of the papal court to Avignon beginning in 1309 was a response to disorder in Italy and political pressure, not a single act of French captivity. In the following decades the popes created one of Europe’s most sophisticated administrations, with a chancery, a financial system, courts, and a diplomatic reach that touched every kingdom. The same development generated criticism of papal finance, provisions, and the wealth of the court, and writers such as Petrarch popularized the image of a “Babylonian Captivity.”

The return of Gregory XI to Rome in 1377 was driven by Italian politics and by advocates such as Catherine of Siena, but it did not secure the papacy. His death in 1378 led to an election under pressure in Rome and the choice of Urban VI. The cardinals’ later repudiation of that election and the choice of Clement VII created the Great Schism, a dispute over legitimacy that left no doctrinal difference between the two sides but divided Latin Christendom into rival obediences.

The schism affected bishops, religious orders, universities, rulers, and ordinary believers, who faced doubled appointments, heavier taxes, and uncertainty. Rulers used the crisis to bargain, and obediences changed with political circumstances. The schism lasted because each claimant had a plausible case, because rulers benefited, and because no accepted authority could decide between them.

In the same period, challenges to clerical authority arose in England and Bohemia. John Wyclif, building on Oxford scholasticism and English politics, questioned clerical dominion and papal authority, and Lollardy carried related ideas among clergy and laypeople. Jan Hus, working in the distinct Bohemian setting of Prague, drew on Wyclif and on local reformers but developed his own positions. Neither man should be seen as a Protestant before Protestantism.

Conciliarism emerged as an attempt to solve the schism within the church’s own legal tradition. It was a diverse set of ideas, not a democratic program, and it depended on the cooperation of rulers. The Council of Pisa in 1409 had the support of many cardinals and rulers but increased the number of claimants to three.

The Council of Constance restored unity by securing the resignation or deposition of rival claimants and electing Martin V in 1417. Its decree Haec Sancta and the decree Frequens expressed conciliar principles, though their lasting constitutional force was disputed. The same council condemned Hus, whose execution in 1415 contributed to the outbreak of the Hussite wars, a reminder that resolving one crisis could deepen another.

The conflict between Eugenius IV and the Council of Basel, the election of Felix V, and the eventual collapse of the conciliar challenge confirmed papal recovery. Concordats and national settlements, such as the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges and the Concordat of Vienna, showed that rulers were able to turn the crisis to their advantage. The papacy ended the period united but more dependent on bargains with princes and on Italian politics.

The restoration of institutional unity did not answer demands for reform of clerical discipline, church wealth, pastoral care, and accountability. These concerns carried forward into the sixteenth century and are examined from another perspective in Chapter 61, while the next chapter, Chapter 54, turns from the church to the wider political and commercial worlds of late medieval Europe.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the central institution of Latin Christendom under strain, not from one collapse but from a series of tests: a clash with a taxing monarchy, a long residence outside Rome, a disputed election, and a decades long division. Each test revealed how much papal authority depended on law, administration, and political consent, and how little provision existed for judging a pope within the system itself.

The chapter also reveals two histories that moved together. The institutional repair of the papacy and the intellectual search for alternatives, from conciliar theory to Wyclif’s critique to Hus’s preaching, were part of the same crisis. They show how late medieval people used inherited legal, theological, and scholastic tools to think about authority, and how those tools produced very different results in Paris, Oxford, Prague, and Constance.

Its consequences extended across regions. Princes gained influence over local churches, Bohemia developed a distinctive religious settlement, and the papacy recovered by bargaining with rulers and entering more deeply into Italian politics. These patterns connect to the political worlds examined in Chapter 54, to the clerical and devotional conditions of Chapter 61, and to the confessional conflicts of Part X, though none of those later developments was predetermined by what happened between 1303 and 1449.

For Volume II, the chapter marks a late stage in the story of Christendom. Latin Christendom had been a powerful framework for institutions, law, learning, and devotion, and the schism did not end that framework. It showed that the unity of the church depended on negotiated authority, that reform was easier to demand than to organize, and that the restoration of institutional unity left the central questions of religious life still open.
