The Late Medieval Church and the Demand for Reform

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Around 1500, western Christians worshipped in tens of thousands of parish churches, endowed chantries and hospitals, joined pilgrimages and confraternities, and argued fiercely about how their clergy should live and how their Church should be governed. Devotion and criticism were not opposites. Both flowed from the same conviction that the Church mattered enormously for salvation.

Consider a parish church in a market town in the decades around 1500, a type of building found from Portugal to Poland. Its nave is the largest gathering space in the community. On Sundays and feast days the parishioners hear Mass said in Latin by a priest whose back is turned to them, watch for the moment when the consecrated host is raised, and listen, if a sermon is given, to instruction in the vernacular. Painted walls, carved screens, side altars maintained by guilds and families, and lamps burning for the dead surround them. Churchwardens or similar lay officers keep accounts for candles, repairs, vestments, and bells. Nothing in this scene is exceptional, and that is its historical importance: the late medieval Church was woven into birth, marriage, work, law, charity, memory, and death for nearly everyone in western and central Europe.

Yet this same Church was also the object of sustained complaint. Reformers inside it denounced clergy who held several offices and lived far from their flocks. Rulers disputed with popes over appointments, courts, and taxes. Satirists mocked greedy friars and corrupt pardoners. Theologians quarreled over the authority of popes, councils, scripture, and tradition. Two movements, one in England and one in Bohemia, were condemned as heresy, and one of their leaders, Jan Hus, was burned at the Council of Constance in 1415. Meanwhile scholars of the Renaissance, examined in Chapters 57 and 59, were editing the Bible and the early Fathers of the Church with new philological tools and asking what Christian renewal might require.

This chapter opens Part X by asking how these facts fit together. The central historical question is how a late medieval Church characterized by widespread participation, institutional strength, and repeated efforts at renewal could also generate criticism and conflicts over authority that contributed to the religious upheavals of the sixteenth century. The answer developed here is neither that the Church was simply corrupt and awaiting a Luther, nor that it was so healthy that the Reformation requires no explanation. It is that vitality, grievance, and reform coexisted, that the people involved wanted different things, and that no single path from these conditions to the division of western Christianity was predetermined.

The chapter is organized in four movements. Sections 61.1 through 61.7 establish the institutions and sacramental system of the Latin Church: papal and episcopal government, relations with secular rulers, the parish, the sacraments, penance and purgatory, and indulgences. Sections 61.8 through 61.13 turn to religious society and internal criticism: saints and pilgrimage, women's religious lives, church finance, clerical misconduct and anticlericalism, monastic renewal, and theological debate. Sections 61.14 through 61.18 examine earlier reform movements and competing visions of authority: conciliarism, Wyclif and the Lollards, Hus and Bohemian reform, the Devotio Moderna, and Christian humanism. Section 61.19 synthesizes these strands and closes on the threshold of 1517.

The chapter concerns the western Latin Church, governed in principle by the bishop of Rome. As Chapter 40 explained, the Orthodox churches of Byzantium, the Balkans, and Rus had by this time long been institutionally distinct from Rome, and the Council of Florence's union with the Greeks in 1439 proved short lived. Latin Christendom also contained Jewish and, in Iberia, Muslim communities, whose position under Christian rulers shaped how the Church understood its own boundaries. The chapter therefore speaks of the Latin Church, western Christendom, or the western Church, and does not treat any of these as a synonym for all of Europe.

Historians reconstruct this world from varied evidence. Bishops' registers and visitation records show what inspectors found in parishes and monasteries. Church accounts and wills reveal what lay people paid for and prayed for. Sermons, confessors' manuals, and catechetical texts show what clergy taught. Papal bulls, council decrees, and diocesan statutes record the formal law. Heresy trials preserve the voices of dissenters, though through the filter of inquisitors. Satires, devotional treatises, and humanist letters register criticism and aspiration. Each source type has limits, and the chapter pays attention to who produced each source and for what purpose.

This chapter prepares the ground for Chapter 62, which examines how Martin Luther's challenge to the preaching of indulgences in 1517 became a wider contest over theology, authority, and the unity of the western Church. It deliberately stops short of that story. Its task is to show what that challenge was challenging, and what other reformers had already attempted, so that the sixteenth century appears as a historical development rather than a sudden break.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the structure of the late medieval Latin Church, including the papacy, bishops, parish clergy, religious orders, and church courts.
  2. Explain how the sacramental system, especially the Mass, confession, and penance, framed ordinary religious life and beliefs about salvation.
  3. Explain accurately what an indulgence was understood to be, how it related to penance and purgatory, and why its administration provoked criticism before 1517.
  4. Analyze the relationship between church and secular authority, including patronage, jurisdiction, and taxation.
  5. Interpret evidence for lay religious practice, including pilgrimage, saints' cults, confraternities, and the religious lives of women.
  6. Distinguish among documented clerical problems, satirical generalization, institutional criticism, and demands for fundamental doctrinal change.
  7. Compare conciliar, Observant, Wycliffite, Hussite, devotional, and humanist programs of reform in goals, participants, and methods.
  8. Trace the Western Schism's consequences for debates over papal and conciliar authority between 1414 and 1517.
  9. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the late medieval Church as declining or as religiously vital.
  10. Assess a primary source, John Colet's 1512 sermon, as evidence of reform from within the clergy.
  11. Contextualize the Church of about 1517 as a set of conditions that made conflict possible but did not make the Reformation inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How can historians explain a Church that was both intensely central to ordinary life and the subject of persistent criticism?
  2. What is the difference between criticizing clergy, criticizing church institutions, and challenging church doctrine, and why does it matter for understanding the Reformation?
  3. How did the theology of penance, purgatory, and indulgences shape lay devotion, and how did it create opportunities for abuse?
  4. In what ways did secular rulers shape the Church, and how did that relationship change the meaning of reform?
  5. Why did the Western Schism leave the problem of church government unresolved even after unity was restored?
  6. What evidence allows historians to judge the quality of parish clergy and the vitality of lay religion, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  7. Why should Wyclif, Hus, the Observants, the Devotio Moderna, and the humanists be treated as distinct movements and not as stages on a single path to Protestantism?
  8. What did contemporaries mean when they demanded reform, and who disagreed about its meaning?

Historical Context

The late medieval Church inherited a long institutional history. As Chapters 23 through 26 showed, Christian communities in the Roman world developed bishops, councils, creeds, and monasticism. As Chapters 31 and 35 showed, the Carolingians and their successors extended Latin Christianity across northern and central Europe, and as Chapter 44 showed, the eleventh century reform papacy asserted the independence of clergy from lay control and the superiority of spiritual authority. The thirteenth century then produced what historians often regard as the high point of papal government, the legal systematization of canon law, the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215, and the mendicant orders of Franciscans and Dominicans, whose preaching and confession reached into towns across the continent.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries tested these institutions. Famine and the Black Death, examined in Chapter 50, killed a large share of the population in many regions, disrupted parish staffing, and intensified concern about death and the afterlife. The Hundred Years' War, examined in Chapter 51, and the growth of fiscal states strengthened rulers who wanted greater control over clerical wealth and appointments. As Chapter 53 explained, the papacy resided at Avignon from 1309 to 1377 and then split during the Western Schism from 1378 to 1417, when rival popes claimed obedience from different realms. These crises did not destroy belief, but they damaged the prestige of papal leadership and made the question of who could correct a failing church government urgent.

Intellectual and cultural change also mattered. Universities, described in Chapter 48, had produced a large body of trained theologians and canon lawyers, and by the fifteenth century they were centers of disagreement as well as learning. Urban prosperity, discussed in Chapter 43, created lay communities able to endow chapels, hospitals, and preaching posts, and literate laity wanted more access to devotional texts. The Renaissance, examined in Part IX, supplied tools of textual criticism and a rhetoric of return to sources. By the late fifteenth century printing, discussed in Chapters 55 and 60, multiplied devotional manuals, indulgence letters, Bibles, and polemic, and made it easier for criticism and reform proposals to circulate.

Several kinds of source are especially important. Canon law and council decrees show what the institution claimed and attempted. Episcopal registers and visitation records show how it actually functioned in dioceses, though inspectors recorded problems more often than ordinary routine. Wills, churchwardens' accounts, and confraternity statutes show lay giving and belief. Heresy trial records preserve Lollard and other dissenting views as reported by hostile examiners. Satire and sermon literature express criticism but often rely on stock figures. Historians increasingly read these sources together, avoiding both the older habit of treating hostile polemic as neutral description and the opposite habit of treating the absence of revolt as proof of contentment.

Finally, readers should keep in mind a methodological caution that recurs through this chapter. Because the Reformation happened, it is tempting to read every earlier complaint as a step toward it. This is a teleological habit. Most late medieval critics of clerical behavior, of papal finance, or of scholastic theology did not intend to leave the Church, and many were conventionally devout. The historian's task is to ask what those people wanted at the time, and then to ask how, in particular circumstances after 1517, some of their arguments were combined with new ones in ways they could not have foreseen.

Section 61.1

The Late Medieval Church: Religious Vitality and the Problem of Reform

Any account of the Church around 1500 must begin by separating three things that are easily confused. The first is religious participation: attendance at Mass, confession, pilgrimage, giving, membership in confraternities, and private prayer. The second is institutional criticism: complaints about clerical behavior, church finance, jurisdiction, or papal government. The third is a demand for reform, meaning a proposal to change something. These three coexisted and often involved the same people. A merchant could give generously to a chantry, grumble about the fees charged by the bishop's court, and support a preacher who demanded stricter discipline of the clergy, without any contradiction. Historians who treat criticism as evidence of declining belief, or participation as evidence of satisfaction, misread the situation.

Interpretations of the late medieval Church have shifted markedly. Older Protestant narratives portrayed it as corrupt and superstitious and the Reformation as a recovery of true Christianity, while older Catholic apologetic sometimes treated the same period as an age of faith disturbed by the ambition of reformers. Johan Huizinga's influential study of the fifteenth century, first published in 1919, emphasized an anxious and sometimes overripe intensity of piety. From the 1960s, historians such as Bernd Moeller argued for German religion in particular that intense religiosity, not indifference, formed the background of the Reformation. In the 1980s and 1990s, work by John Bossy, Eamon Duffy, and others showed how deeply traditional practices structured community life in places such as England. Section 61.19 returns to this debate. For now the essential point is that scholars today generally reject the simple corruption narrative while still recognizing real problems and real demands for change.

The chapter's chronological focus is roughly 1350 to 1517, with earlier foundations introduced when necessary. Geography matters as well. Reform and criticism took different forms in Italian city states, the Valois and Habsburg lands, England, the Holy Roman Empire, Iberia, Bohemia, and the Baltic and Polish lands, where Latin Christianity reached its eastern edge. In Iberia, Christian rulers governed Jewish and Muslim populations, and royal pressure for religious uniformity, including the Spanish Inquisition established from 1478 and the expulsion of Jews from the Spanish crowns in 1492, shaped how reform and orthodoxy were understood there. Everywhere, Christendom was not a society of Christians alone, and the Church's claims about unity coexisted with the daily reality of religious minorities, whose treatment ranged from regulated coexistence to violence and expulsion.

Section 61.2

The Papacy, Bishops, and the Government of the Church

The Latin Church was a legal and administrative organization as well as a community of worship. At its head stood the pope, regarded as the successor of Peter and the supreme judge of canon law. He was assisted by the College of Cardinals, who also elected him, and by the Roman Curia, a set of offices that handled petitions, lawsuits, dispensations, appointments, and revenue. Canon law, built from Gratian's twelfth century collection and later papal decretals, provided the rules for marriage, benefices, wills, clerical discipline, and procedure in church courts. Appeals from across Europe flowed to Rome, and papal letters could grant exemptions, appoint clergy to vacant benefices, or authorize indulgences. This centralization, much of it developed after Chapter 44's reform era, gave the papacy great reach and also made it a target of complaint about delay, cost, and interference in local affairs.

Below the pope were roughly several hundred dioceses, very unequal in size and wealth. Italy had many small ones, while England and the German lands had fewer, larger ones. A bishop ordained priests, confirmed, consecrated churches, held synods, presided over a court, and conducted visitations of parishes and religious houses. He governed with archdeacons, a vicar general, and the cathedral chapter, a body of canons with its own property and privileges. Bishops varied enormously. Some were conscientious pastors, others royal administrators, noble younger sons, or absentee holders who delegated duties to deputies. In the Holy Roman Empire several archbishops and bishops, such as the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne, were also territorial princes and imperial electors, which tied church office to secular politics in ways found less often in western Europe.

The fifteenth and early sixteenth century papacy was also an Italian territorial power. After the return from Avignon, popes ruled the Papal States, fought and negotiated with Italian city states and foreign monarchs, and patronized artists and architects, as discussed in Chapter 58. Pontiffs such as Sixtus IV, Alexander VI, Julius II, and Leo X combined spiritual office with dynastic ambition, nepotism, warfare, and building projects, including the new Saint Peter's Basilica begun under Julius II in 1506. Contemporary critics, including humanists and some cardinals, complained that this worldliness obscured the pope's spiritual duties. Historians should note, however, that this was only one aspect of papal government. The Curia also administered justice, regulated orders, negotiated with rulers, and issued reform legislation, and many European Christians had little direct contact with the papal court at all.

Section 61.3

Church and State: Jurisdiction, Patronage, and Political Authority

The relationship between church and secular rulers was a continuous negotiation, not a simple opposition. Churches held land and jurisdiction, and clergy claimed privileges, including trial in church courts under the principle often called benefit of clergy. Church courts also handled matrimonial cases, wills, tithes, defamation, and moral offenses affecting laity as well as clergy, giving the Church routine authority over everyday disputes. Rulers wanted revenue from church wealth, a say in the choice of bishops and abbots, and access to educated clerical administrators. Popes, in turn, wanted to protect church liberty, collect revenues, and fill benefices through papal provisions. Because bishops and abbots were often royal councillors and large landholders, church office was entangled with political office.

Rulers built various legal barriers against papal intervention. In England, the Statutes of Provisors of 1351 and Praemunire of 1353 restricted papal appointments and appeals to Rome without royal consent, though kings often negotiated informal compromises. In France, the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges in 1438 asserted limits on papal taxation and appointments and drew on conciliar decisions from Basel. In 1516 King Francis I and Pope Leo X replaced it with the Concordat of Bologna, which gave the crown the right to nominate to bishoprics and major abbacies while the pope retained confirmation and certain revenues. The settlement made the French monarchy a powerful steward of the French church and reduced pressure for reform to come from other directions. Spain's monarchs also obtained extensive patronage rights, particularly over the church in conquered Granada and the Atlantic territories, and in the early sixteenth century Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros combined royal service with campaigns to improve clergy education and monastic observance.

In the Holy Roman Empire, church and state were interwoven differently. Territorial princes and city councils increasingly oversaw church matters in their lands, funding preachers, regulating clergy, and sometimes restricting church courts. Representatives of the German estates compiled lists of grievances against Rome, called gravamina, from the mid fifteenth century, complaining of papal taxation, appointments to German benefices, and litigation at the Curia. These documents show institutional criticism flowing through legitimate political channels, usually without any call to leave the Church. The Concordat of Vienna of 1448 between the emperor and the pope settled some of these issues but did not end the complaints. As rulers acquired greater influence over churches in their territories, they also acquired an interest in controlling reform, a factor that would matter greatly when religious division arrived, as Chapters 62 through 66 show.

Section 61.4

Parish Churches, Priests, and Local Religious Life

The parish was the ordinary setting of Christian life. Parishioners were baptized there, heard Mass on Sundays and holy days, were married at or near the church door, received the last rites, and were buried in or near the churchyard. England alone had roughly nine thousand parishes around 1500, and comparable local structures existed across Latin Europe, though patterns differed. Parishes in northern Italian cities, Scandinavian villages, Iberian towns, and Baltic estates varied in size, wealth, and the density of other religious institutions. The parish church was a communal building, often maintained by lay funds, with accounts kept by lay wardens or similar officers. Lay people thus managed much of the material side of religion, from bells and candles to repairs, which gave them real influence over local worship.

Parish clergy occupied different positions. A rector or parish priest might hold a benefice with a secure income from tithes and land. A vicar might serve a parish whose tithes had been appropriated by a monastery or other institution, leaving him a smaller portion. Assistant priests, called curates or by other names, were hired and often poorly paid. In many towns a large number of chantry priests, endowed to say Masses for the dead, formed a clerical group with fewer pastoral duties. Formal university education was rare among parish priests. Many learned through apprenticeship to an older priest, by attending a cathedral or town school, or through service manuals. Evidence from visitation records suggests that clerical learning varied greatly, but also that bishops and archdeacons expected a priest to know how to say Mass, hear confession, and teach the basic elements of faith.

Instruction was a recognized duty. Church legislation required priests to teach the Lord's Prayer, the Creed, the Ten Commandments, the seven deadly sins, works of mercy, and the sacraments, and numerous manuals in Latin and the vernacular helped them do so. Preaching by friars and by endowed town preachers supplemented parish instruction, and sermons ranged from learned to dramatic and popular. Images in wall paintings, stained glass, and carved screens conveyed biblical stories and moral lessons, and religious drama associated with feasts such as Corpus Christi presented sacred history to large audiences. Historians generally reject the older view that medieval laity were uniformly ignorant of Christian teaching. Their knowledge was transmitted largely through hearing, seeing, and ritual participation instead of reading, and its depth varied by region, wealth, and access to preachers.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religious Life in a Late Medieval Parish

The religious year organized time. Advent and Lent were seasons of preparation and fasting, Easter was the high point of confession and communion, and saints' days, local patronal feasts, and processions marked other moments. On Sundays the priest said Mass while parishioners stood or knelt in the nave, often without seating, and the elevation of the consecrated host was the focus of attention. Lay people gave bread to be blessed and shared, paid for candles before images, and contributed to the repair fund. Families paid fees for marriages, funerals, and burial, and sometimes bequeathed money for Masses, torches, or doles for the poor.

Women and men participated differently but both prominently. Women were churched after childbirth, managed household devotion, tended lights, and joined parish sisterhoods, while men served as wardens and members of guild altars. Children learned prayers at home and from priests. Occasional conflict was normal: parishioners disputed seating, tithes, or the conduct of a priest. Such disputes should not be read as hostility to religion. They show that the parish was important enough to fight over. Many parishioners also maintained links with the dead through all-souls observances, bede rolls reading out names of benefactors, and masses bought for loved ones, which gave parish religion a strong sense of continuity between living and dead.

Section 61.5

Sacraments, the Mass, and the Religious Understanding of Salvation

Latin Christian teaching, as systematized by twelfth and thirteenth century theologians and confirmed by later councils, recognized seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, anointing of the sick, holy orders, and matrimony. Each was understood as an outward sign that conveyed grace, instituted by Christ and administered through the Church. Baptism, normally given to infants, removed original sin and incorporated the person into the Christian community. Confirmation, performed by a bishop, strengthened the baptized. Anointing of the sick, or extreme unction, prepared the dying. Marriage, which in canon law depended on the consent of the parties, was blessed by the Church and regulated by church courts. Holy orders conferred priesthood on men, giving them the power to consecrate the Eucharist and to absolve sins. This sacramental structure meant that salvation was understood to be mediated through a visible, ordered Church, which explains why disputes over clerical authority were also disputes about access to grace.

Historical Vocabulary
Sacrament, Penance, and Absolution

A sacrament was a rite understood by Latin Christians to convey divine grace through an outward sign, such as water in baptism or bread and wine in the Eucharist. Penance was both a sacrament and a process: the penitent felt sorrow for sin (contrition), confessed to a priest, received absolution, the priest's declaration of forgiveness on God's behalf, and then performed assigned acts of satisfaction such as prayer, fasting, or almsgiving. The vocabulary matters because Protestant critics later questioned elements of this system, but in 1500 these terms described a coherent theology that most western Christians shared. Modern English "penance" often suggests only a punishment, whereas the medieval concept included inner repentance, spiritual healing, and reconciliation with the community.

The Eucharist stood at the center of worship. The Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 used the term transubstantiation to describe the teaching that, at consecration, the substance of bread and wine became the body and blood of Christ while the outward appearances remained. The Mass was also understood as a sacrificial act in which Christ's sacrifice was made present, and the elevation of the host, accompanied by bells, was a moment of intense reverence. Lay communion was infrequent, often once a year at Easter in line with Lateran IV, and the laity usually received only the consecrated bread, while the chalice was reserved for the priest. Many laity instead participated by watching, praying, and sometimes seeking to see the host, a practice sometimes called ocular communion. The feast of Corpus Christi, extended to the universal Church in the early fourteenth century, brought the sacrament out into the streets in processions organized by clergy, town councils, and guilds.

Historians caution against reading medieval sacramental theology backward through later Protestant debates. Late medieval theologians disagreed about many matters, including how grace worked, what role human cooperation played, and how the sacraments related to divine promise. These disagreements are discussed further in Section 61.13. What most western Christians agreed on was that the priest, ordained in a line stretching back to the apostles, was necessary for the sacraments of Eucharist and penance, and that the Church's sacramental system was the ordinary means of salvation. This is why criticism of unworthy priests had a particular edge: if the priest mediated grace, his character mattered to the people who depended on him, though official teaching held that the validity of a sacrament did not depend on the moral worth of the minister.

Section 61.6

Confession, Penance, Purgatory, and Prayers for the Dead

Canon 21 of the Fourth Lateran Council required every Christian who had reached the age of discretion to confess privately to their own priest at least once a year and to receive communion at Easter. This created a vast pastoral system. Priests needed training in questioning penitents about sins, and a large literature of confessors' manuals, summae for confessors, and vernacular guides to examination of conscience appeared. The traditional steps were contrition, confession, absolution, and satisfaction. Theologians argued about the relative weight of each, especially whether sorrow arising from love of God was required or whether sorrow arising from fear of punishment, combined with absolution, sufficed. The practice had real pastoral importance, offering comfort, moral guidance, and a way to reconcile with the community, but it could also be burdensome, especially for scrupulous individuals who feared forgetting a sin.

The doctrine of purgatory developed within this penitential framework. Western theologians taught that souls of the faithful who died in a state of grace but without having fully satisfied for their sins underwent purification after death before entering heaven. Medieval councils, including Lyons in 1274 and Florence in 1439, affirmed the teaching in forms acceptable to Latin theologians, though the Greeks at Florence disputed aspects of its description. Purgatory was not hell, and souls there were assured of salvation, but their suffering could be shortened by the prayers, alms, and Masses of the living. Because of this, relationships between the living and the dead became a major concern of lay and clerical religion. Historians such as Jacques Le Goff have argued that the idea of purgatory as a distinct place crystallized in the twelfth century, while others emphasize older roots in early Christian prayer for the dead.

The practical consequences were enormous. People left bequests for Masses on the day of burial, at one month and at the anniversary, and for perpetual prayers. Wealthier donors endowed chantries, chapels with priests whose main duty was to pray for the founder. Guilds and confraternities provided burial and prayers for members. Books of Hours and treatises on dying well, the ars moriendi, encouraged individuals to prepare for death, and images of the dance of death reminded viewers of mortality. The Black Death, discussed in Chapter 50, increased these concerns in many regions. Historians debate how much fear and anxiety shaped this religion. Some, such as Jean Delumeau, emphasized a culture of fear, while others argue that late medieval piety was as often consoling, communal, and charitable. What is clear is that the system generated substantial lay investment in the Church, and that anything touching it, including the sale or administration of spiritual benefits, would strike deeply felt concerns.

Section 61.7

Indulgences: Theology, Devotion, Money, and Controversy

Indulgences are among the most misunderstood features of late medieval Christianity, and an accurate account is essential to understanding Chapter 62. In Latin Christian teaching, an indulgence did not forgive sin and did not purchase a license to sin. Sin carried both guilt, removed by contrition, confession, and absolution, and a temporal punishment that remained to be satisfied, either by penitential works in this life or by purification in purgatory. An indulgence was the Church's remission, by its authority, of some or all of that remaining temporal punishment for a penitent whose guilt had been forgiven. The theological basis was the treasury of merits: the idea that the superabundant merits of Christ and the saints formed a spiritual store that the Church, through the pope and in certain circumstances bishops, could apply to the faithful. Pope Clement VI's bull Unigenitus of 1343 gave a formal statement of this doctrine.

Historical Vocabulary
Indulgence and Temporal Punishment

An indulgence was a remission, granted by church authority, of the temporal punishment due for sins whose guilt had already been forgiven. A partial indulgence remitted a stated portion of that punishment, often expressed in days or years of ancient penitential practice, while a plenary indulgence remitted all of it. Neither the guilt of sin nor eternal punishment was removed by an indulgence itself. The common modern phrase "selling forgiveness" therefore misdescribes the official doctrine, though it captures how some critics, and some preachers' rhetoric, made indulgences sound. Understanding the difference between official theology and popular perception is essential to reading the controversies that follow.

Indulgences had grown from earlier penitential practice. Early medieval penance could involve long periods of fasting, and in the eleventh and twelfth centuries popes began offering remission of penance for participation in crusades, as Chapter 47 explained, and later for pilgrimage, charitable works, and prayers at specific altars. In 1300 Boniface VIII proclaimed a Jubilee in Rome, offering a plenary indulgence to pilgrims who visited its basilicas, and later popes repeated and shortened the interval. By the fifteenth century, indulgences were attached to many causes: church building, hospitals, defense against the Ottomans after 1453, bridge repair, and cathedral funds. Certain individuals also purchased letters of confession allowing them to choose a confessor and receive plenary remission at the hour of death. In 1476 Sixtus IV extended indulgences to souls in purgatory, to be applied by way of intercession, not by direct jurisdiction, a distinction contemporary theologians debated.

Money entered in several ways. Official teaching required contrition and confession for an indulgence to be effective, and a donation was understood as an act of charity connected to the cause, not as a price. In practice, however, indulgence campaigns were organized by commissioned preachers and financial agents, and the proceeds were shared among church authorities, rulers, and banking houses. Preachers sometimes spoke in ways that blurred the doctrine, and lay people could understand contribution as the decisive step. Contemporaries noticed. The Prague preacher Jan Hus protested an indulgence campaign in 1412. Scholars such as Johann Ruchrat of Wesel and the Spanish theologian Pedro Martínez de Osma questioned aspects of indulgence teaching in the later fifteenth century, and Osma's views on confession and indulgences were condemned in 1479. Some humanists ridiculed indulgence selling, and German estates complained of money flowing to Rome.

Three points follow. First, criticism of indulgences was not new in 1517, and it came from within the Church as well as outside. Second, most critics distinguished between the doctrine and its administration, and many objected primarily to abuses, to financial arrangements, or to claims that went beyond what theology allowed. Third, indulgences remained popular with lay people, who valued them as a way to help the dead and to receive spiritual benefit through charity and devotion. The particular campaign for the rebuilding of Saint Peter's, which began under Julius II in 1506 and was later preached in German lands, and Luther's response to it belong to Chapter 62. Here it is enough to emphasize that the doctrine was neither obscure nor marginal, and that its fundraising uses had long been controversial.

Section 61.8

Popular Religion: Saints, Relics, Pilgrimage, and Confraternities

Late medieval Christians turned to the saints as intercessors, protectors, and models. The Virgin Mary received intense devotion through prayers, feast days, images, and the developing Rosary, and confraternities devoted to the Rosary spread in the later fifteenth century. Saints with specific associations, such as Saint Anne, Saint Sebastian for plague, and Saint Barbara for sudden death, were invoked for particular needs. Shrines and relics, physical remains or objects connected to saints, attracted donations and pilgrims. Major pilgrimage destinations included Rome, Jerusalem, Santiago de Compostela, Canterbury, Cologne, Aachen, and many regional centers, and local shrines drew visitors from nearby villages. Pilgrimage combined devotion, penance, sociability, commerce, and sometimes tourism, and hospitals and inns grew up around popular shrines.

Lay confraternities, called by many names including guilds, scuole, and brotherhoods, organized religious life around shared activities. Members attended Masses, maintained lights or altars, accompanied funerals, supported poor members, and held communal meals. Some confraternities were organized by trade, others by neighborhood or shared devotion, and some admitted women. Confraternities of flagellants and of penitents practiced communal discipline, while charitable ones ran hospitals or distributed dowries. They gave laypeople a role in organizing devotion that was independent of, though overseen by, the clergy. Charitable foundations, bequests, and civic processions linked religion to urban government in cities from Florence and Venice to Bruges and Cologne, as discussed in Chapter 43.

Popular religion was neither uniform nor free from criticism. Church authorities sometimes tried to regulate cults they considered dubious. The pilgrimage to Wilsnack in Brandenburg, based on the reported survival of bleeding hosts after a fire, was criticized by Prague theologians associated with Hus's circle in the early fifteenth century and by later reformers including Nicholas of Cusa, who as papal legate in 1451 sought to restrict it, yet it remained popular for decades. Preachers condemned superstition, magical uses of sacred objects, and exaggerated claims. At the same time, historians warn against using the word superstition as a simple label for what elites disliked. The same practice could be accepted in one place and challenged in another, and many learned clergy shared in devotion to saints and relics. Regional variation was large: Mediterranean cities favored processions and confraternities, German towns supported endowed preacherships and elaborate altarpieces, and English parishes maintained guild lights and church ales.

Section 61.9

Women, Households, and Religious Participation

Women were excluded from ordination and from most formal church offices, but they were deeply involved in religious life. In households, mothers and female servants often taught children prayers and basic teaching. In parishes, women attended Mass, joined sisterhoods, maintained lights, left bequests, and sometimes served as patrons. In towns, women were prominent as donors and pilgrims. Convents provided another path, offering spiritual life, education, and, for families, a means of providing for daughters, though not all nuns entered voluntarily, and aristocratic houses had strong class character. Convent life was also subject to reform efforts, including attempts to enforce stricter enclosure, which some communities welcomed and others resisted.

Some women gained wide authority as mystics, visionaries, and spiritual writers. Birgitta of Sweden, who died in 1373 and was canonized in 1391, founded the Order of the Savior whose houses included both nuns and monks, and her Revelations circulated widely. Catherine of Siena, a Dominican tertiary who died in 1380, wrote letters to popes and rulers urging peace and reform and was canonized in 1461. Julian of Norwich composed reflections on her visions, and Margery Kempe dictated a spiritual autobiography showing both devotion and conflict with clerical and lay critics. Colette of Corbie reformed Poor Clare houses in the early fifteenth century. Beguines, women living in semi religious communities without full vows, were suspect to some authorities and were condemned in some forms in 1311 and 1312, although many communities survived. Women's spiritual writing usually claimed authority through divine revelation and humility, since formal learning and public teaching were closed to them, which shows both the opportunities and limits of their position.

For historians, women's religion illustrates a general point: ecclesiastical authority was male and clerical, but religious activity was shared more widely. Evidence for women's piety is uneven. Many accounts were written or edited by male confessors, and wills, accounts, and parish records are more informative about wealthy women than poor ones. Even so, the evidence demonstrates that women's devotion, charity, and spiritual leadership formed an important part of late medieval religion, and that concerns about spiritual authority outside clerical channels, whether in mystical visions or lay communities, sometimes provoked suspicion and sometimes admiration.

Section 61.10

Church Property, Benefices, and Ecclesiastical Finances

The Church was a major economic institution. Its income came from endowed lands, tithes, fees, offerings, and legacies. A benefice was an ecclesiastical office with an attached income, such as a parish, canonry, or bishopric, and it was regarded as a form of property as well as a pastoral charge. The tithe, a tenth of agricultural produce or income, supported parish and other clergy, though in many places it had been appropriated by monasteries, cathedral chapters, or lay owners, leaving the resident priest with less. Parishes also received fees for burials, marriages, and masses, and fees were a frequent source of conflict. Estimates of the proportion of land held by churches vary widely by region and are uncertain, but in many areas it was substantial, and in the prince bishoprics of the Holy Roman Empire it was the basis of territorial states.

The papacy developed a complex fiscal system. The Apostolic Camera collected revenue from annates, the first year's income of certain benefices, from fees for appointments and dispensations, from the sale of offices in the Curia, and from taxes on clergy levied for crusades and other causes. Papal provisions, the appointment of candidates to benefices outside the normal process, brought fees and also patronage. Cardinals often held multiple benefices and sometimes foreign sees. Banking houses such as the Medici and later the Fugger served as papal creditors and collectors, linking church finance to commercial networks discussed in Chapter 43. These arrangements allowed the papacy to function across Europe, but they also made it vulnerable to the accusation that spiritual offices were bought and sold.

Three practices drew sustained criticism. Pluralism was the holding of more than one benefice by the same person, often for income. Absenteeism or non residence meant that a holder of a pastoral office lived elsewhere, leaving duties to a poorly paid deputy. Simony, named for Simon Magus in the Acts of the Apostles, was the buying or selling of spiritual offices or benefits, forbidden by canon law. These were real and documented problems, and councils repeatedly legislated against them, yet dispensations allowed exceptions and enforcement was uneven. Historians stress that they flowed partly from the way benefices functioned as sources of income to support educated clergy, administrators, and scholars, not only from personal greed. The system worked adequately for many but also generated resentment among those who bore its costs and among those who believed pastoral care should be primary.

Section 61.11

Clerical Abuses, Anticlericalism, and Criticism from Within

Criticism of the clergy was abundant in the late Middle Ages, and the historian must distinguish several kinds. Documented problems included pluralism, absenteeism, simony, inadequate education, neglect of church buildings, failure to reside, and sexual misconduct, including clerical concubinage, which was widespread enough in some regions that bishops regularly fined it. Visitation records often noted such failings. However, they also show that most parishes were reported in order, that bishops and archdeacons took inspection seriously, and that inspectors recorded exceptions more than routine. A second kind of criticism appears in satire and literature: Boccaccio's and Chaucer's portraits of friars and pardoners, Sebastian Brant's Ship of Fools of 1494, and the later Praise of Folly by Erasmus. These works use stock figures and exaggerate, so they are better evidence of what audiences found comprehensible and amusing than of statistical frequency.

A third kind of criticism was reform preaching. Preachers such as Geiler von Kaysersberg at Strasbourg condemned clerical vice while remaining loyal to the Church. Observant friars denounced lax colleagues, and Girolamo Savonarola in Florence denounced corruption in the Church and the papacy until he was excommunicated and executed in 1498. Bishops and councils, including the Fifth Lateran Council, issued reform decrees. Lay estates compiled gravamina. These critics shared an assumption that the Church was necessary for salvation and that its personnel should conform to its ideals. This is sometimes distinguished from anticlericalism, a term historians use for hostility to clergy as a group or to clerical privilege. Historians debate how widespread such hostility was, how far it targeted clerical privilege instead of religion, and whether it reflected rivalry between lay and clerical authority in towns, where councils sought to tax or regulate clergy.

The relationship between criticism and the Reformation is complicated. The Reformation did draw on anticlerical sentiment, and early Protestant pamphlets used satire against priests and monks. But complaints about clerical behavior existed in all centuries of the Middle Ages and had not produced separation. What changed after 1517 was that some critics began questioning the priesthood's sacramental role and the legal and theological structure that supported clerical privilege, which converted complaint into a fundamental challenge. A caution applies: historians should avoid describing the clergy in general as ignorant, immoral, or idle. Large numbers served diligently, and many lay people sought clergy's help in life and death.

Clergy, Finance, and Reform: Four Viewpoints around 1500

Section 61.12

Monastic Renewal and the Observant Reform Movements

Religious orders were central to western Christianity, and from the fourteenth century many generated internal reform movements. The term Observant refers to groups within an order who sought stricter observance of its original rule, usually emphasizing common life, poverty, enclosure for nuns, liturgical discipline, and study. Among Benedictines, new congregations arose that linked houses under shared discipline, including Santa Giustina at Padua after 1419, the Melk reform in Austria, and the Bursfeld Congregation in Germany from the 1430s. Among Franciscans, Observant leaders such as Bernardino of Siena and John of Capistrano preached widely and attracted large followings. Dominican Observants, Carthusians, and Poor Clares under Colette of Corbie likewise sought renewed discipline. These movements often received support from princes, city councils, and wealthy patrons, who saw reformed houses as spiritual and civic assets.

Observant reform was often contested. Within orders, Observants and the so called Conventuals disputed property, authority, and identity. Reformers sometimes imposed changes from outside, and communities that valued existing customs resisted. Nuns' houses faced pressure over enclosure and visitation by male superiors. In Italy, the Dominican Savonarola led the reformed San Marco convent in Florence and used the pulpit to address politics and morals before his fall. In 1517 Leo X formally divided the Franciscans into an Observant branch and a Conventual branch, showing both the strength of the movement and the failure to preserve unity. Observant friars were also prominent preachers in towns and in indulgence campaigns, which illustrates that reform and papal fundraising could involve the same personnel.

The significance for the chapter is twofold. First, Observance shows that reform took place before Protestantism and largely within existing institutions, relying on rule, discipline, and renewed devotion. Second, it demonstrates that reform required cooperation with authorities and often failed to alter structures beyond particular communities. Historians therefore describe fifteenth century reform as extensive but uneven, a patchwork of local successes. This is important for understanding the claim by later reformers that the Church could not reform itself: that claim was a judgment, shaped by after the fact experience and polemic, about a record that included both real achievement and real limits.

Section 61.13

Universities, Scholastic Theology, and Debates over Religious Authority

As Chapter 48 explained, universities trained theologians and lawyers through disputation, lecture, and commentary, most often on the Bible and on Peter Lombard's twelfth century Sentences. By the fifteenth century this tradition was internally diverse. Schools associated with Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, and William of Ockham taught different approaches to knowledge, language, and God's relation to the world, and universities often supported several positions side by side. The terms via antiqua and via moderna described broad orientations to logic and metaphysics, and teachers such as Gabriel Biel at Tübingen taught modern approaches while others, including Augustinian theologians, emphasized divine grace more strongly. New universities at Prague (1348), Vienna, Heidelberg, Cologne, Louvain, Tübingen, Alcalá, and Wittenberg (1502) expanded the clerical learned class.

Debates over grace and human effort were genuine and unresolved. Theologians asked how far human beings could prepare for grace and what role merit played in salvation. Some stressed divine initiative, others the cooperation of the will, and bishops and councils defined limits against extreme positions. Historians caution that the difference between these positions cannot be mapped neatly onto later Protestant and Catholic divisions, and that Luther's later positions on justification, discussed in Chapter 62, cannot be assigned to whole schools of medieval theology. Mysticism, represented by Meister Eckhart, Johannes Tauler, and later vernacular writers, also offered paths of inner devotion that sometimes provoked suspicion but influenced lay and monastic piety. Scripture remained the foundation of theological study, accompanied by the Fathers and glosses, and vernacular Bibles existed in several languages, including a number of printed German Bibles before 1517.

Questions of authority were equally open. Theologians agreed that Scripture, tradition, councils, and the pope all had roles, but they disagreed about their relationship. Could a pope err in matters of faith? Did a general council rank above the pope in some circumstances? Who could interpret Scripture authoritatively? Conciliar theorists, papalists, canon lawyers, and Scripture centered reformers answered differently. The late medieval university thus supplied reformers of many kinds with arguments, and it supplied the Church with defenders. This pluralism is an important reason that reform could not be reduced to a single program.

Section 61.14

The Great Schism, Conciliarism, and the Struggle to Reform Church Government

Chapter 53 described the Western Schism, which began in 1378 when two rival colleges of cardinals elected rival popes, Urban VI in Rome and Clement VII at Avignon. European rulers aligned according to politics. For nearly four decades Christians owed obedience to competing claimants, each excommunicating the other's supporters. The Council of Pisa in 1409 attempted to resolve the crisis by deposing both popes and electing a third, which produced three lines. The crisis forced theologians and canonists to ask a question that earlier thinkers had rarely needed to address: if the papacy itself failed to function, who had authority to restore order? Thinkers such as Jean Gerson, Pierre d'Ailly, and Francesco Zabarella argued that the universal Church, represented by a general council, possessed authority to act in emergencies and to correct a pope who persisted in scandal.

Historical Vocabulary
Conciliarism

Conciliarism is the name historians give to a family of theories holding that a general council of the Church has authority, at least in certain circumstances, to judge or correct a pope and to direct church government. Versions ranged from moderate positions allowing councils to act only when the papacy failed, to stronger claims that councils held supreme authority. Conciliarism was not modern parliamentary democracy, though some thinkers used analogies from corporate and representative law, and it was never established as permanent Catholic doctrine. Later papal and Catholic teaching rejected the strong form, so historians treat the status of its decrees as contested.

The Council of Constance, which met from 1414 to 1418, became the great test. It secured the resignation or deposition of the rival claimants and elected Martin V in 1417, ending the Schism. In April 1415 it issued the decree Haec Sancta, asserting that a general council derived its authority directly from Christ and that all, including the pope, were bound to obey it in matters of faith, the ending of the Schism, and general reform. In October 1417 it issued Frequens, requiring councils at regular intervals. The council also condemned Wyclif's writings and tried and executed Jan Hus, topics taken up in Sections 61.15 and 61.16. The meaning and authority of Haec Sancta remained disputed afterward: later defenders of papal primacy argued it applied only to the emergency of the Schism, and subsequent popes resisted its general application.

The Council of Basel, which opened in 1431, pushed conciliar claims further, passing reform legislation on benefices, annates, and appeals, and clashing with Pope Eugenius IV, who moved a rival council to Ferrara and then Florence, where in 1439 union with the Greeks was proclaimed in the face of Ottoman pressure, as Chapter 41 described. Basel's radical wing elected an antipope in 1439, which cost it support, and the council dissolved by 1449. Rulers used its legislation selectively, as in the French Pragmatic Sanction of 1438. In 1460 Pope Pius II issued Execrabilis, condemning appeals from the pope to a future council, and the later fifteenth century saw popes consolidate authority. The Fifth Lateran Council, opened in 1512 amid conflict with France, issued decrees on bishops, preaching, and the control of printing, but many critics found its reforms inadequate. The legacy was that the idea of a council remained a live remedy for church disorder, and critics, including Luther in his early disputes, later appealed to one.

Section 61.15

John Wyclif, Lollardy, and the Challenge to Ecclesiastical Authority

John Wyclif, an Oxford theologian who died in 1384, began as a respected philosopher and became a controversial critic of the Church. His central arguments developed in the 1370s and early 1380s amid disputes over papal taxation, royal relations with Rome, and clerical wealth. He argued that Scripture was the supreme authority for Christian belief, that the Church should be poor and that secular rulers could take away the temporal endowments of clergy who abused them, and that the papacy lacked divine sanction for its claims to political power. He also criticized the doctrine of transubstantiation as then taught, proposing that the substance of bread remained after consecration. These ideas, and his insistence that salvation depended on divine predestination and not on membership in the visible institution, put him at odds with leading theologians and bishops. A council at London in 1382 condemned a number of propositions drawn from his work, and he retired to the parish of Lutterworth, where he died, having kept his post but lost university support.

Wyclif's followers, called Lollards by opponents, spread his ideas beyond the university. Early adherents at Oxford produced sermons, tracts, and the Wycliffite Bible translations, which rendered the Latin Vulgate into English in the 1380s and 1390s and circulated widely in manuscript. Lollard teaching varied but often emphasized Scripture, criticized clerical wealth, rejected pilgrimages and images, questioned the Mass, and doubted the efficacy of indulgences and prayers for the dead. Some gentry and knights offered protection, and a petition known as the Twelve Conclusions was posted in 1395. The state responded with force. The statute De heretico comburendo of 1401 authorized the burning of people convicted of heresy who refused to abjure, as well as those who relapsed after previously abjuring, Archbishop Thomas Arundel's Constitutions of 1409 restricted unlicensed vernacular translation and preaching, and the failed uprising led by Sir John Oldcastle in 1414 strengthened official suspicion. Later, Constance condemned Wyclif's teachings and ordered his remains removed from consecrated ground.

Lollardy did not disappear. Heresy trial records from the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, in regions such as Kent, the Chilterns, the Midlands, and East Anglia, reveal small groups of artisans, women, and traders reading vernacular scriptural texts and discussing doctrine in households. These sources come from inquisitors and must be read with care, but they show persistent dissent. Historians disagree about how much Lollardy contributed to the English Reformation after 1530. Some see continuity of belief, others emphasize its small scale and the sharp differences between Lollard and Protestant theology, and some see an indirect influence through vernacular scripture and anticlerical argument. It would be misleading to describe Wyclif as simply a precursor of Protestantism. His ideas arose in fourteenth century conditions, his supporters differed, and his influence on Luther was not a straight line. His importance for this chapter lies in showing that serious challenges to church authority, based on Scripture and critique of clerical wealth, had arisen well before 1517, and that church and state responded with coordinated repression.

Section 61.16

Jan Hus, Bohemian Reform, and the Hussite Legacy

Bohemia, part of the lands of the Bohemian crown within the Holy Roman Empire, had strong ties to English and German intellectual currents. Anne of Bohemia's marriage to Richard II of England in 1382 contributed to exchanges between Prague and Oxford, and the University of Prague, founded in 1348, was a major center. Reform preaching had flourished in Prague, and Jan Hus, a master of the university who began preaching in 1402 at the Bethlehem Chapel, became its most influential voice. He preached in Czech, called for moral renewal of clergy, emphasized the authority of Scripture, and praised some of Wyclif's philosophical and critical writings while disagreeing with other points. Disputes in Prague mixed theology with politics and with rivalry between Czech and German masters at the university, but these should not be read as modern nationalism, since the conflict involved voting rights, loyalty to rival popes, and academic power as well.

Hus came into conflict with the archbishop of Prague and then with the papacy. After he protested an indulgence campaign in 1412, he was excommunicated and Prague was placed under interdict while he remained there. In 1414 he travelled to the Council of Constance under a safe conduct issued by King Sigismund, to defend himself. He was arrested, tried, and condemned for heresy, and was burned on 6 July 1415. His associate Jerome of Prague was burned in 1416. Historians dispute how much responsibility Sigismund bore for the breach of the safe conduct and how far Hus held the positions attributed to him; Hus insisted he did not teach some condemned propositions, and the council relied on extracts and on legal argument that safe conducts could not protect a suspected heretic.

His execution provoked outrage in Bohemia and produced a movement that outlived him. Hussite leaders, backed by nobles and townspeople, formulated the Four Articles of Prague in 1420: free preaching of the word of God, communion in both kinds for lay people, limits on clerical secular power and wealth, and punishment of public sins. The Utraquists, named for communion sub utraque specie, were the moderate wing, while the Taborites, based at the new town of Tábor, were more radical. Crusades against the Hussites, called by popes and Sigismund between 1420 and the early 1430s, were repeatedly defeated by innovative Hussite forces, and Bohemia faced years of war. The Compacts of Basel in 1436 recognized Utraquist communion within an agreement with the council, and an Utraquist church persisted in Bohemia until the seventeenth century. A further group, the Unity of the Brethren, formed in 1457 and emphasized simple discipline. Hussitism shows that reform could generate stable institutional divisions within western Christendom, but it also shows how varied reform outcomes were: the Hussite church kept bishops and many traditional forms, Taborites sought more radical change, and neither was identical to Lutheranism. This is why Hus should not be called simply an early Protestant.

Section 61.17

The Devotio Moderna, Vernacular Devotion, and Personal Religious Renewal

A different kind of reform arose in the Low Countries and the Rhineland. Geert Groote, a learned deacon from Deventer who died in 1384, preached repentance and gathered followers who lived in shared households. After his death, groups known as the Brothers and Sisters of the Common Life formed, living communally under informal discipline, without taking permanent vows, while supporting themselves by copying books, teaching, or crafts. Florens Radewijns organized the brothers at Deventer, and in 1387 a house of Augustinian canons was founded at Windesheim, which became head of a congregation of reformed houses. The movement, called the Devotio Moderna or modern devotion, spread across the Low Countries and into German lands.

Its characteristic practices stressed personal prayer, meditation on the life of Christ, regular self examination, humility, and the imitation of Christ's example, aided by reading in Latin and the vernacular. Devotional manuals, such as the Imitation of Christ, traditionally attributed to Thomas à Kempis, who lived at Mount Saint Agnes near Zwolle and died in 1471, became extraordinarily popular, with very many editions in the age of print. The movement valued practical discipline over speculative theology. The movement supported education in several ways, including manuscript production, student lodging, supervision, spiritual guidance, and, in some communities, direct involvement in teaching, while schools themselves were often existing municipal institutions. Some students, including the young Erasmus, encountered the movement's influence at Deventer and elsewhere. The movement drew some suspicion from mendicant critics who thought its communities resembled unofficial religious orders, but it was defended at Constance by Jean Gerson and others and remained within the Church.

The Devotio Moderna is significant for several reasons. It shows that reform could be quiet and pedagogical, working through households, schools, and the copying and reading of texts. It indicates a rising lay and clerical appetite for vernacular and individual devotion, though this occurred within, and often reinforced, the sacramental system. And it influenced Christian humanists and some Catholic reformers later, who valued inward piety and moral formation. Historians should avoid presenting it as a mere preparation for Protestantism. Its adherents attended Mass, confessed, venerated saints, and obeyed church authority, and many were uninterested in doctrinal controversy.

Section 61.18

Christian Humanism and Reform within the Church

As Chapters 57 and 59 explained, humanists emphasized the study of ancient languages and texts and the idea of returning to sources, ad fontes. Applied to Christianity, this meant renewed study of the Greek New Testament, the early Fathers, and the Hebrew Bible, and critical attention to the Latin Vulgate. Lorenzo Valla's philological analysis in the 1440s, including his demonstration that the Donation of Constantine was a forgery, showed what such methods could do. In England John Colet lectured on Paul's letters at Oxford in the 1490s and became dean of Saint Paul's in 1505, where he founded a school. Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples in Paris published commentary on the Pauline epistles in 1512. Cardinal Cisneros sponsored the Complutensian Polyglot Bible at Alcalá, printed from 1514 and issued later. Erasmus of Rotterdam published a handbook of Christian living, the Enchiridion, in 1503, the satire Praise of Folly in 1511, and in 1516 his Greek New Testament with a new Latin translation, edited at Basel.

Humanist reformers criticized what they considered empty ceremony, excessive reliance on scholastic argument, superstitious uses of relics and pilgrimage, and the worldliness of some clergy and monks. They advocated education, preaching based on Scripture, and an inner Christianity sometimes called the philosophy of Christ. They also differed from one another. Colet defended clerical authority while attacking clerical vice. Erasmus mocked abuses but defended the Church's unity and later opposed Luther. Lefèvre's work on grace and Scripture suggested later more radical directions for some followers. Humanism also had a darker side in the Reuchlin affair of 1510 to 1520, when Johannes Pfefferkorn, a convert from Judaism, proposed destroying Jewish books, and the humanist Johannes Reuchlin opposed him, exposing tensions among scholars, theologians, and Jewish communities. This episode reminds readers that reform and tolerance were not the same thing.

Humanist reform was primarily educated, elite, and textual, and its effect depended on patrons such as bishops, princes, and scholars, who could support editions and schools. Printing, discussed in Chapter 60, amplified it by circulating editions and pamphlets rapidly. Yet humanist reform remained aimed at renewing the Church from within, and many humanists would be unsettled by the later Reformation. For students, Colet's sermon of 1512 offers a case study of this kind of reform.

Translations and later printings require attention to textual transmission and should not automatically be treated as verbatim records of the spoken sermon.

Context: Convocation, the clerical assembly of the province of Canterbury, met under Archbishop William Warham while England was preparing for war with France. Colet, known for biblical lectures and his school at Saint Paul's, was invited to preach. His text was drawn from Paul's letter to the Romans, advising Christians not to be conformed to the world but to be transformed by renewal of the mind. He addressed senior clergy gathered to conduct business, not lay critics or heretics.

Paraphrase of Colet's sermon Colet told the assembled clergy that the Church's greatest need was reform of its own conduct. He warned that pride, covetousness, worldly ambition, and carnal living among bishops and priests had corrupted the Church more than any outside enemy could, and he urged them to resist conformity to the values of the world and to enforce existing canon law more strictly. He insisted that the priesthood was a high and holy calling and that the dignity of the priestly office made lax behavior all the more serious. This is a summary of themes and not a translation of any single passage.

Analysis: The sermon is valuable as evidence that criticism of clerical worldliness could be voiced at the center of the English church establishment, before Luther, and in language of renewal. It also shows the limits of such criticism. Colet did not reject the priesthood, the sacraments, or the authority of church law, and he asked for enforcement of rules already in force. As a single sermon by a prominent scholar to a clerical audience, it cannot show how ordinary lay people or parish priests felt, and it does not tell us how much the clergy changed afterward. Later printed versions may have been shaped by editors and by the controversies of the 1520s and 1530s, which is a reason to compare any translation with its textual history.

Section 61.19

The Church on the Eve of 1517: Competing Reforms and Unresolved Questions

By 1517 western Christianity contained strong sacramental and devotional institutions that most people valued. It had deeply established systems of clerical and ecclesiastical authority, with canon law, courts, and benefices woven into political and economic life. It contained real enthusiasm for renewal, visible in Observant houses, lay devotional communities, humanist scholarship, and conciliar proposals. It also contained critics who disagreed about what should change: some wanted stricter clerical discipline, others reduced papal finance, stronger councils, better preaching, more access to Scripture, or reconsideration of the basis of ecclesiastical authority. Rulers, meanwhile, increasingly shaped church affairs through concordats, patronage, and control of local institutions. The Fifth Lateran Council had just closed in March 1517 with decrees that satisfied some and disappointed others, leaving many questions open.

Historical Debate

Was the Late Medieval Church Declining or Experiencing Religious Renewal?

The question: Did the Church around 1500 suffer from institutional and spiritual decay that explains the Reformation, or was it a vigorous religious world whose vitality and conflicts formed the background to division?

Interpretation A: Institutional problems and spiritual anxiety weakened the Church: Some historians, building on older Protestant and some liberal traditions and on Huizinga's view of an anxious age, emphasize abuses such as pluralism, simony, and clerical ignorance, the damage done by the Schism, and a piety preoccupied with fear of death and purgatory. In this view, accumulated grievances and tension between lay expectations and clerical performance made reform movements receptive audiences.
Interpretation B: Religious vitality and reform were widespread: Other historians argue that lay devotion, confraternities, endowments, pilgrimage, and parish participation were thriving, that reform efforts were numerous and often effective locally, and that criticism reflected high expectations. Work by Moeller, Bossy, Duffy, and others supports the view that the Reformation challenged a living religion and not a dying one, and that the problem must be explained by particular theological and political developments after 1517.

Evidence: Both interpretations use visitation records, wills, church accounts, sermons, council decrees, and satirical literature. They differ in how they weigh evidence of abuse against evidence of participation, and whether criticism indicates alienation or engagement. Regional studies suggest wide variation, which complicates any single verdict. Many historians now combine the interpretations, describing a religiously active society whose institutions faced real strains.

Why it matters: If decline explains the Reformation, it appears a natural response to failure. If vitality is emphasized, historians must explain why a strong religious culture fractured, directing attention to theology, politics, communication, and contingency.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Medieval Reform Traditions and the Conditions Preceding the Reformation

What Changed

  • Papal prestige was damaged by Avignon and the Schism, and rulers gained leverage through concordats and patronage
  • Heretical movements in England and Bohemia showed that Scripture and criticism of church wealth could ground organized dissent
  • Printing and humanist scholarship widened circulation of devotional, biblical, and critical texts
  • Lay literacy, urban wealth, and endowed preaching increased lay participation in religious life

What Stayed the Same

  • The sacramental system, the Mass, confession, and belief in purgatory structured religious life for most Christians
  • Canon law, benefices, church courts, and tithes remained part of local and royal economies
  • Complaints about clerical conduct and cycles of monastic reform had existed throughout the Middle Ages
  • The Church's claim to be the necessary mediator of salvation remained widely accepted

Who experienced change most directly? Bohemian and English dissenters, rulers negotiating with Rome, and urban lay people who gained devotional and educational opportunities experienced the sharpest shifts. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural parishioners and parish clergy lived within familiar rhythms of worship and obligation. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Reformation emerged from a mixture of long standing structures and recent pressures, and that continuity in belief coexisted with changes in politics and communication.

Cause and Effect

How Institutional, Financial, Theological, Political, and Devotional Tensions Interacted

Long-term causes

Centralized papal administration and fiscal demands; benefice based clerical income; the penitential and purgatorial system; scholastic diversity; and the long history of reform movements and heresy.

Short-term causes

The Schism and the incomplete settlement of conciliar questions; princely and royal bargains with Rome; the spread of printing and humanist criticism; and large indulgence campaigns tied to church building and war.

Event or transformation

By 1517 the Church combined strong devotional life with unresolved questions about authority, finance, and reform, setting conditions in which a dispute over indulgences could escalate.

Immediate effects

Reform proposals competed, rulers sought influence over church affairs, and the question of which authority could define true reform became pressing, as examined in Chapter 62.

Long-term consequences

The conditions described here shaped the contest between Protestant, Reformed, and Catholic movements discussed in Chapters 62 through 67, and the confessional states of Part XII.

Historical complexity

No single tension produced the Reformation. Comparable tensions had existed earlier without rupture, and the outcome depended on particular theological claims, political support, and communication.

The unresolved questions of 1517 can be summarized as questions of authority, access, and money. Who had authority to define doctrine and correct abuses: pope, council, bishops, princes, or Scripture interpreted by scholars? How should lay people gain access to grace and to Scripture, and what role should the clergy play? How should church wealth be gathered, used, and governed, and by whom? Different parties answered these questions differently, which is why reform meant different things to different people. Chapter 62 turns to how one of them, Martin Luther, came to answer them in ways that transformed the debate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the western Latin Church in the period from about 1350 to 1517, with the aim of establishing what the Reformation would challenge and what reform efforts had already been attempted. Its central argument is that late medieval Christianity combined widespread religious participation, strong institutions, and persistent criticism, and that reform meant different things to different people. The Church was neither simply decadent nor free of serious problems, and the Reformation cannot be explained either as an inevitable response to decay or as an unforeseeable disruption of a contented society.

The institutional framework was extensive. The papacy and Curia exercised legal and fiscal authority through canon law, appeals, provisions, and revenues. Bishops, chapters, and archdeacons governed dioceses, and in parts of the Holy Roman Empire bishops were also princes. Parish priests, chantry priests, and religious orders provided worship, instruction, and prayer. Church courts regulated marriage, wills, and morals, and rulers bargained with Rome over appointments, taxation, and jurisdiction through statutes, the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges, the Concordat of Bologna, and patronage arrangements in Spain and the Empire.

The sacramental system gave this structure its meaning. Baptism, the Mass, confession, penance, and the last rites framed life and death, and the Fourth Lateran Council had made annual confession and Easter communion obligatory. Purgatory and prayers for the dead generated enormous investment in chantries, confraternities, and Masses. This religious world was neither uniformly ignorant nor uniformly fearful. It was communal, charitable, devotional, and sometimes anxious, and its instruction passed through preaching, images, drama, and ritual as well as texts.

Indulgences stood at the intersection of devotion, theology, and finance. Properly understood, they remitted temporal punishment for sins whose guilt had been forgiven, drawing on the treasury of merits, and they were not forgiveness for payment. Yet their association with fundraising campaigns, commissioned preachers, and papal revenue made their administration controversial, and critics from Hus to Osma and German estates had objected before 1517. Distinguishing doctrine from administration is essential for understanding why the controversy of 1517 mattered.

Popular religion and women's religious lives illustrate the breadth of participation. Saints, relics, pilgrimage, confraternities, and household devotion gave lay people active roles, and women as donors, mystics, nuns, and teachers shaped devotional life despite exclusion from ordained ministry. Church property and finance, meanwhile, created real tensions. Benefices, tithes, annates, fees, and the sale of offices sustained the institution, but pluralism, absenteeism, and simony were persistent targets of criticism.

Criticism took many forms. Documented abuses, satirical stereotypes, reform preaching, lay grievances, and anticlericalism need to be distinguished, since most critics sought improvement within the Church. Observant movements renewed monastic and mendicant life but remained patchy, and their internal conflicts show how difficult reform could be. University theology was diverse and unresolved on grace, merit, and authority, and it cannot be read through later Protestant categories.

Questions of church government were sharpened by the Western Schism. Conciliar theorists argued that councils could correct failing popes, Constance issued Haec Sancta and Frequens, and Basel pressed claims further before papal authority recovered. The status of these decrees remained disputed, and the legacy was an enduring idea that a council might remedy disorder, even though popes and many rulers preferred more limited arrangements.

Two movements were condemned. Wyclif's critique of endowment, papal authority, and eucharistic teaching led to condemnation and to Lollard communities that persisted underground. Hus's preaching and execution produced the Hussite movements, whose Utraquist church endured. Neither can simply be called early Protestant, since each arose in specific medieval conditions and developed distinct features. Meanwhile the Devotio Moderna offered a quieter reform of inward devotion and education, and Christian humanists advocated renewal through biblical learning, criticism, and preaching, as John Colet's 1512 sermon illustrates.

Taken together, these developments show that the Church on the eve of 1517 contained strong institutions, genuine enthusiasm for renewal, competing reform programs, and rulers with growing interests in ecclesiastical affairs. The table of reform currents emphasized how different their goals and relations to church institutions were. These conditions made conflict possible, but they did not make the Reformation inevitable, and the following chapters show how particular events, ideas, and political decisions after 1517 turned dispute into division.

Key Terms

Canon law
The body of church law, based on Gratian's collection and papal decretals, governing clergy, benefices, marriage, wills, and procedure in church courts.
Roman Curia
The set of papal offices, courts, and financial agencies through which the pope administered the Latin Church.
Benefice
An ecclesiastical office, such as a parish or canonry, with attached income, treated in law as a form of property as well as a pastoral charge.
Pluralism
The holding of more than one benefice by one person, often criticized when it led to neglect of pastoral duties.
Absenteeism
Non residence of a cleric holding a pastoral office, leaving duties to a deputy.
Simony
The buying or selling of spiritual offices or benefits, forbidden by canon law and named for Simon Magus.
Annates
Payments to the papacy of a benefice's first year of income, one of several papal revenues resented in places such as the German lands.
Tithe
A tenth of produce or income owed to support the Church, often appropriated by institutions other than the resident priest.
Sacrament
A rite understood to convey grace through an outward sign; Latin theology counted seven.
Transubstantiation
The doctrine, affirmed in 1215, that the substance of bread and wine becomes Christ's body and blood while outward appearances remain.
Penance
The sacrament and process of contrition, confession, absolution, and satisfaction by which sinners were reconciled to God and the community.
Absolution
The priest's declaration of forgiveness of sins on God's behalf.
Purgatory
The state of purification after death for the faithful who died in grace but had not fully satisfied for their sins, helped by prayers and Masses of the living.
Indulgence
A remission by church authority of temporal punishment due for sins already forgiven, partial or plenary.
Treasury of merits
The theological idea that the merits of Christ and the saints form a spiritual store the Church may apply to the faithful.
Chantry
An endowment paying a priest to say Masses for the founder's soul and others, often at an altar or chapel in a church.
Confraternity
A lay association for worship, charity, burial, and prayers for the dead, organized by trade, neighborhood, or devotion.
Anticlericalism
Hostility to clergy as a group or to clerical privilege, a term whose scope and extent in the period remains debated.
Observants
Reform parties within religious orders seeking stricter observance of the original rule.
Conciliarism
Theories that a general council may judge or correct a pope and direct church government, at least in emergencies.
Haec Sancta
The 1415 decree of the Council of Constance asserting conciliar authority, whose permanent meaning remained contested.
Frequens
The 1417 decree of Constance requiring regular general councils.
Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges
A 1438 French royal ordinance limiting papal taxation and appointments, drawing on decrees from Basel.
Concordat of Bologna
The 1516 agreement between Leo X and Francis I giving the French crown nomination to bishoprics and major abbacies.
Gravamina
Lists of grievances compiled by German estates against papal taxation, appointments, and litigation.
Lollardy
The English dissenting movement inspired by Wyclif, criticizing church wealth and practices and valuing vernacular Scripture.
Utraquism
The Bohemian practice and church tradition of communion in both kinds for lay people, from sub utraque specie.
Devotio Moderna
A late medieval devotional movement from the Low Countries emphasizing inner piety, common life, and imitation of Christ.
Christian humanism
Reform oriented scholarship applying philology and classical learning to Scripture, the Fathers, education, and church criticism.
Ad fontes
The humanist slogan "to the sources," calling for study of original texts in their languages.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the Reformation cannot be understood without the world it challenged. The late medieval Church was a vast institution with legal authority, economic power, and a sacramental system that most people regarded as essential. It was also the home of widespread devotion, charitable giving, and communal life. Recognizing this prevents the Reformation from being treated as a simple rejection of an obviously failing system, and it helps students see why religious change was so contested, so emotionally charged, and so tied to politics and society.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern in European history: reform and authority developed together. Councils, orders, preachers, scholars, and rulers all claimed to restore order, and their disagreements concerned who had the right to define it. These issues of jurisdiction, finance, and legitimacy connect this chapter to the earlier investiture conflict of Chapter 44, the papal crisis of Chapter 53, the humanism of Chapters 57 and 59, and the growth of state power that Part XII later examines. They also prepare readers for the confessional conflicts of Chapters 62 through 67, in which the question of which Church or churches held legitimate authority became a matter of war and statecraft.

For Volume III, the chapter shows that the transformations of Renaissance, Reformation, empire, and state formation were intertwined. Humanist scholarship, printing, urban prosperity, and princely ambition provided the means by which religious debate could spread and acquire political force. At the same time, continuities endured: many beliefs about the sacraments, the dead, and the community of the faithful survived the divisions of the sixteenth century in changed forms, and Catholic reform after Trent, discussed in Chapter 65, drew on earlier Observant, devotional, and humanist currents examined here.

Chapter 62 now turns to Martin Luther and the German Reformation. Students should approach it with the questions this chapter has raised: what exactly was challenged, who agreed and disagreed, and why did this challenge, unlike earlier ones, produce a lasting division of western Christianity? Understanding the diversity of late medieval reform is the best protection against the assumption that the outcome was foreordained.
