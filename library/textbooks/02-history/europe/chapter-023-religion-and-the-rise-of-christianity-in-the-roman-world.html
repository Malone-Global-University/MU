Religion and the Rise of Christianity in the Roman World

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Around the year 112 CE, a Roman governor in northern Asia Minor wrote to the emperor Trajan asking, in effect, what he was supposed to do about a religious group he found strange and troubling but could not clearly define as a crime. The exchange survives, and it captures, better than any later summary could, the genuine puzzlement with which the Roman imperial system first confronted the movement that would eventually be called Christianity.

The Roman Empire that Chapter 22 described, prosperous, administratively complex, and religiously varied across its many provinces, was not a place where a single accepted religion bound all subjects together in shared belief. It was, instead, a world crowded with gods, temples, festivals, oracles, household shrines, philosophical schools, and civic ceremonies, in which the vast majority of people took for granted that many different communities honored many different divine powers, each according to its own customs. Into this crowded religious landscape, during the reign of the emperor Augustus and his successors, a small movement emerged among Jewish communities in Roman Judea, centered on a Galilean teacher named Jesus of Nazareth, who was executed by Roman authority and whose followers soon proclaimed, with striking conviction, that he had been raised from the dead.

This chapter tells the story of what happened next, not as a triumphant march from persecution to empire, and not as an inevitable unfolding of a single unchanging doctrine, but as a genuinely open historical process. Followers of Jesus, most of them at first observant Jews, carried a message about him from Jerusalem to the port cities, market towns, and provincial capitals of the eastern Mediterranean, and eventually to Rome itself. Along the way, they debated fiercely among themselves what this new movement required of Gentile converts, argued over the continuing authority of Jewish law, developed characteristic patterns of communal worship and mutual support, produced letters and narratives that later communities would treasure and copy, and gradually built forms of local leadership that, over generations, hardened into recognizable institutions.

None of this occurred in isolation from the wider Roman world. Christian communities met in ordinary households, traveled along Roman roads and sea lanes, used the Greek language that bound the eastern provinces together, and depended on networks of patronage, kinship, and urban association that Roman society had already created for other purposes. At the same time, Christian refusal to honor the traditional gods, and Christian rejection of the sacrifices that bound most Roman communities together, marked the movement as unusual, and at moments dangerous, in the eyes of neighbors and magistrates alike. Roman authorities did not generally understand Christianity as a coherent enemy to be hunted down systematically. For most of the period this chapter examines, persecution was local, sporadic, and often triggered by denunciation from neighbors rather than by any settled imperial policy. Only gradually, and only by the middle of the third century, did the scale of Christian growth begin to draw sustained imperial attention.

This chapter also insists on a further point of historical honesty. Judaism was not merely a backdrop against which Christianity emerged; it was, and remained, a vibrant, internally diverse religious tradition in its own right, with its own institutions, its own responses to Roman rule, and its own experience of catastrophe when the Jerusalem Temple was destroyed in 70 CE. The relationship between the earliest followers of Jesus and the wider Jewish world was not a single clean break at a single moment. It was a long, uneven, and regionally variable process, in which some Jewish communities continued to include followers of Jesus for generations, even as other Christian communities, increasingly composed of Gentile converts, drew sharper boundaries between themselves and the synagogue.

By the chapter's chronological endpoint, around the middle of the third century, Christianity had become geographically extensive, present in cities from Gaul and Rome to North Africa, Egypt, Syria, and Asia Minor, with more limited evidence suggesting Christian presence in Britain, and institutionally more organized, with bishops, clergy, and networks of correspondence linking distant communities. It remained, however, a minority religion within an empire whose population overwhelmingly continued to honor the traditional gods, the imperial cult, and countless local and regional cults. The relationship between the Christian movement and the Roman state remained unresolved, poised on the threshold of a much larger confrontation and, eventually, a stunning reversal that later chapters will examine in their own right.

Understanding this long, contested, and genuinely uncertain process, rather than reading later Christian dominance backward into its earliest centuries, is the task of this chapter. Doing so requires historical method rather than theological argument: attention to evidence, sensitivity to the diversity of ancient religious life, and care to distinguish what can be established historically from what belongs to the realm of faith and later tradition.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the character of Roman civic and household religion as a system emphasizing ritual practice, communal obligation, and reciprocity, without assuming that belief about the gods was therefore absent or unimportant.
  2. Explain the religious diversity of the Roman Empire, including regional cults, mystery religions, philosophical schools, and the imperial cult.
  3. Describe Second Temple Judaism, its institutions, and its internal diversity under Roman rule.
  4. Distinguish historically established facts about Jesus of Nazareth from later theological claims, while explaining the significance of the early resurrection proclamation for the movement's development.
  5. Trace Paul's missionary activity and explain his significance without treating him as the sole inventor of Christianity.
  6. Analyze the gradual, regionally uneven process by which Christian communities separated from the wider Jewish world.
  7. Describe the social organization, worship practices, and everyday life of early Christian communities.
  8. Trace the gradual development of church leadership and organization without projecting later medieval institutions backward.
  9. Evaluate multiple factors that contributed to the spread of Christianity, avoiding single-cause explanations.
  10. Explain the intermittent, locally variable nature of Roman persecution of Christians and identify why Christian refusal of sacrifice created friction with civic and imperial authorities.
  11. Assess primary sources, including Pliny's correspondence with Trajan and early martyr narratives, for what they can and cannot establish historically.
  12. Contextualize Christianity's status by the mid-third century as a widespread but still minority religion within a plural empire.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did religious practice actually involve for most inhabitants of the Roman Empire, and how did its emphasis on ritual and communal obligation relate to, rather than simply exclude, belief about the gods?
  2. Why should historians avoid describing everyone who was not a Christian or a Jew as a single group called "pagans"?
  3. How did Judaism's own internal diversity shape the movement that formed around Jesus of Nazareth?
  4. What can historians establish about Jesus, his followers, and his execution, and what belongs instead to the realm of theological claim?
  5. Why is it misleading to describe a single moment when Christianity "separated" from Judaism?
  6. What social, economic, and geographic factors made the spread of Christianity possible, and why does no single explanation suffice?
  7. Why was Roman persecution of Christians intermittent and local for most of this period, rather than continuous and empire-wide?
  8. What kinds of evidence, letters, martyr narratives, administrative correspondence, and archaeology, allow historians to reconstruct early Christian history, and what are the limits of each kind of evidence?

Historical Context

Chapter 22 examined the Roman Empire at its height as a vast, administratively integrated, and culturally diverse political system, held together by roads, provincial government, military force, law, and a shared, though unevenly distributed, Roman citizenship. That empire was also, from one end to the other, a deeply religious world, though not in a sense that would be immediately familiar to a modern reader accustomed to thinking of religion as a private matter of individual belief, distinct from political and civic life. For most inhabitants of the Roman world, religious practice was inseparable from family, neighborhood, city, and empire, and gods were honored not primarily because one believed a particular doctrine about them but because proper reverence maintained the relationship between human communities and divine power on which prosperity, health, and safety depended.

Within this world, Judaism already occupied a distinctive position. Jewish communities, concentrated in Judea and Galilee but scattered in large numbers across the eastern Mediterranean, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and increasingly the city of Rome itself, worshipped a single god without images, maintained a body of scripture and law, and organized communal life around synagogues, the Jerusalem Temple, and distinctive practices including circumcision, dietary law, and Sabbath observance. Roman authorities often recognized Jewish ancestral customs and negotiated local accommodations when civic obligations conflicted with Jewish practice, but these arrangements varied by place and could be contested, renewed, or allowed to lapse rather than forming a single settled, empire-wide policy, and the relationship between Jewish communities and Roman authority in Judea itself remained tense and, at several points examined in Chapter 22, erupted into open revolt.

It was from within this specifically Jewish world, not from a religious vacuum, that the movement associated with Jesus of Nazareth emerged during the reign of the emperor Tiberius, in the early decades of the first century CE. This chapter's task is to explain how that movement developed, spread, and gradually acquired an identity increasingly distinct from, though never entirely disconnected from, its Jewish origins, while remaining attentive throughout to the genuine plurality of religious life across the empire within which this development took place.

Two methodological cautions govern this chapter throughout. First, historians must distinguish carefully between what can be established through ordinary historical method, using documentary, archaeological, and comparative evidence, and claims that belong to the realm of religious faith, such as the resurrection of Jesus. This chapter states clearly what a historian can responsibly conclude, namely that Jesus's followers proclaimed, remarkably early and with striking conviction, that he had been raised from the dead, without asserting or denying the theological claim itself. Second, historians must resist reading later Christian institutional forms, including the developed episcopal hierarchy, the fixed biblical canon, and the eventual alliance between church and empire, backward into the first and second centuries, when Christian communities were considerably more fluid, diverse, and locally varied than later tradition sometimes suggests.

Section 23.1

Religion in the Roman World

To understand why Christianity struck many of its contemporaries as strange, and at moments dangerous, students must first set aside a common modern assumption, namely that religion is chiefly a matter of interior belief, professed through a creed and practiced primarily in private. Roman religion placed especially strong emphasis on ritual practice, communal obligation, and correct performance, without requiring adherence to a single authoritative creed. This did not mean that beliefs about the gods were absent or unimportant, since Romans certainly held ideas and expectations about divine power and its workings, but the organizing principle of Roman religious life was correct communal practice rather than a fixed body of doctrine that individuals were expected to affirm. Roman religion bound together family, neighborhood, city, and empire through an ongoing relationship of reciprocity between human communities and divine powers, a relationship maintained above all through correct ritual performance.

At the level of the household, the paterfamilias, the male head of a Roman household, oversaw daily observances honoring the household gods, the Lares and Penates, along with ancestral spirits, in rituals that reinforced the household's continuity and its members' shared identity. At the level of the city, civic priesthoods, often held by leading citizens as part of their public career rather than as a separate religious vocation, oversaw temples, festivals, and sacrifices performed on behalf of the whole community. Divination, the practice of discerning divine will through the flight of birds, the examination of animal entrails, or other established techniques, played an important role in public decision making, since Romans believed that undertaking major civic or military actions without appropriate religious sanction invited disaster.

Sacrifice lay at the heart of this system. Animal sacrifice was one of the central forms of Roman public ritual, typically involving the slaughter of an animal followed by a shared meal, but Roman sacrificial practice also included offerings such as wine, incense, grain, and other foodstuffs, and not every act of sacrifice required an animal victim. A sacrifice properly performed, according to established formulas, at the correct time, by the correct officiant, maintained the favor of the gods toward the community that offered it. Failure to perform expected sacrifices, or performing them incorrectly, was understood not as a private lapse but as a genuine danger to the whole community, since it disrupted the reciprocal relationship on which collective wellbeing depended. This helps explain, as later sections of this chapter will show, why Christian refusal to participate in sacrifice, whether to traditional gods or to the emperor's genius, could appear to Roman neighbors and magistrates not merely as an eccentric private choice but as a genuine threat to communal welfare.

Roman religious practice also varied considerably by region, reflecting the empire's vast diversity of peoples and traditions. Rome did not generally impose a single uniform religious system on conquered territories. Instead, Roman administrators often identified local deities with Roman equivalents, a practice modern scholars call interpretatio Romana, allowing provincial populations to continue honoring familiar gods under partially Romanized names and forms. This flexibility helped integrate provincial religious life into the wider Roman system without demanding uniformity, and it meant that the empire's religious landscape remained remarkably plural even as its political administration became increasingly integrated, a theme examined further in Section 23.2.

Section 23.2

Gods, Cults, and Religious Diversity across the Empire

Beyond civic and household religion, the Roman Empire hosted an extraordinary range of additional religious traditions, many of which spread widely through trade, migration, and military movement rather than remaining confined to their region of origin. Historians should avoid the common shorthand of calling all of these traditions collectively "paganism," since that term, discussed further in this chapter's first Historical Vocabulary feature, developed its meaning largely through later Christian usage and can obscure the genuine diversity it describes.

Several Greco-Roman cults included initiation or restricted rites, but historians caution against treating "mystery cults" as a single uniform religious type, since the traditions grouped under that label differed substantially from one another in practice and public visibility. Cults associated with Isis and Serapis, and with Cybele, the Anatolian Great Mother, combined initiatory practices with conspicuous public temples and festivals that were visible and, in many cases, central to civic life, and only some participants underwent deeper initiation involving purification and, in certain contexts, hope for a favorable afterlife. Mithraic worship, by contrast, generally took place in small, restricted underground meeting spaces called Mithraea, spread widely among soldiers along military frontiers, and remained considerably more inward-facing and secretive, so that its precise beliefs and rituals are only partially understood today because initiates left few explanatory texts of their own. Healing cults, centered on gods such as Asclepius, drew pilgrims seeking cures at temple complexes that combined religious devotion with practices resembling medical treatment.

The imperial cult deserves particular attention because it directly connects the religious history examined in this chapter to the political history examined in Chapter 22. Imperial cult took different forms across the empire's regions and periods, and historians caution against reducing it to any single formula. Communities might sacrifice on behalf of an emperor's welfare, honor his genius, or guiding spirit, during his lifetime, pair the emperor with the goddess Roma, personifying the Roman state, or, particularly in the Greek-speaking eastern provinces where a tradition of honoring living rulers with cultic honors predated Roman rule, offer cult more directly to a living ruler. At Rome and in much of Italy, formal deification, following a Senate decree, more typically followed an emperor's death. These were genuinely religious practices, involving real ritual obligation, that also expressed civic hierarchy and political loyalty, rather than a uniform demand for literal theological belief in a living emperor's full divinity.

Alongside these traditions, Greek and Roman philosophical schools, including Stoicism, Epicureanism, and various forms of Platonism, offered educated Romans frameworks for understanding the divine, the cosmos, and human ethical life that operated somewhat independently of civic cult, though philosophers rarely rejected traditional religious practice outright. This philosophical culture would later provide some early Christian writers with intellectual tools for articulating Christian belief in terms comprehensible to an educated Greco-Roman audience, a development examined further in Chapter 26.

Historical Vocabulary
Cult, Sacrifice, and Reciprocity

In the historical study of ancient religion, the word cult refers neutrally to a body of religious practice devoted to a particular deity or group of deities, without the negative connotations the word often carries in casual modern usage. A sacrifice was a ritual offering, understood to maintain proper relations between a human community and a divine power; animal sacrifice was central to Roman public ritual, but offerings of wine, incense, grain, and other foodstuffs were also genuine forms of sacrifice. Ancient religion of this kind is often described by historians as functioning through reciprocity, an exchange of honor and offering for favor and protection, organized above all around correct ritual practice and communal obligation rather than around the profession of a fixed creed, though this did not mean that beliefs about the gods were absent or unimportant to participants. Because communal obligation, not creedal belief alone, organized most ancient religious life, a community's or individual's refusal to sacrifice could be understood by contemporaries as a serious breach of communal obligation rather than merely a private religious preference, a point essential to understanding the friction between Christians and their neighbors examined later in this chapter.

Historians should also use caution with the term pagan, or paganism, discussed further in Section 23.11, since the term developed its common meaning largely through later Christian classification of the diverse non-Christian and non-Jewish religious practices described in this section, rather than describing a single unified religion that existed in the minds of its own practitioners.

Section 23.3

Judaism in the Roman World

Judaism, unlike the civic and household cults examined above, was organized around the exclusive worship of a single god, without images, and around a body of scripture, law, and covenantal tradition that Jewish communities understood as binding regardless of where they lived. This section examines Judaism as a subject in its own right, not merely as a preface to Christianity, since the outline governing this chapter requires sustained, independent attention to Jewish religious life under Roman rule.

At the center of Jewish religious life in this period stood the Jerusalem Temple, rebuilt on a grand scale under Herod the Great in the decades before Jesus's birth, where priests conducted sacrifices on behalf of the Jewish people and to which pilgrims traveled from across the Mediterranean world for major festivals including Passover, Pentecost, and the Feast of Tabernacles. Alongside the Temple, synagogues, present both in Judea and, in growing numbers, throughout the Jewish Diaspora, served as centers for prayer, the reading and interpretation of scripture, and communal gathering, functioning as an institution that did not require a single central sanctuary and that would prove historically significant after the Temple's eventual destruction.

Second Temple Judaism, the term historians use for this period of Jewish religious life, was far from monolithic. Historical sources describe several distinct groups and tendencies, including Pharisees, who emphasized the interpretation and application of Jewish law to daily life and whose traditions shared important concerns with forms of Judaism that later developed into rabbinic Judaism, although historians should not assume simple institutional continuity between the two; Sadducees, often associated with the priestly aristocracy and the Temple establishment, who held different views on questions such as resurrection; the Essenes, an ascetic movement associated by many scholars with the community that preserved the Dead Sea Scrolls; and various other groups, including politically militant factions that contributed to the tensions culminating in the Jewish revolts examined in Chapter 22. Ordinary Jewish religious practice included Sabbath observance, dietary law, circumcision of male infants, and regular prayer, practices that marked Jewish communities as distinctive within the wider Roman world and that Roman authorities often recognized and locally accommodated as ancestral custom, though such accommodations varied by place and period rather than forming a single fixed legal exemption.

The Jewish Diaspora, the dispersed Jewish communities living outside Judea, was extensive by the first century CE, with substantial populations in Alexandria, Antioch, Rome, Asia Minor, and Mesopotamia. Diaspora Jewish communities often used Greek rather than Hebrew or Aramaic in daily life and worship, and many relied on the Septuagint, a Greek translation of Hebrew scripture produced in Alexandria beginning in the third century BCE, which would later prove significant for the earliest Christian communities, most of whose members read scripture in Greek. This diaspora infrastructure, of synagogues, Greek-speaking Jewish communities, and established trade and travel networks connecting Jewish populations across the Mediterranean, would provide, as Section 23.5 explains, an essential foundation for the earliest Christian missionary activity, without in any way reducing Judaism to a mere prelude for a different religion.

Section 23.4

Jesus and the Earliest Jesus Movement

Historians approaching Jesus of Nazareth must distinguish carefully between what ordinary historical method can establish and what belongs properly to the realm of theological claim, a distinction this textbook maintains consistently rather than blurring in either direction. What can be stated with reasonable historical confidence, based on early Christian texts read critically alongside limited external references, is the following: Jesus was a Jewish teacher active in Galilee and Judea during the reign of the emperor Tiberius, most likely in the late 20s CE; he gathered a following among ordinary people, including fishermen, tax collectors, and others outside the religious and political establishment; his teaching centered on the coming of the kingdom of God, ethical demands, and controversial interpretations of Jewish law and Temple practice; he was arrested and executed by crucifixion, a distinctly Roman method of execution, under the authority of the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, who held office in Judea from approximately 26 to 36 CE; and, within a short time after his death, his followers began proclaiming, with evident conviction, that he had been raised from the dead and had appeared to them.

This last point deserves careful historical framing. Historians can establish, with considerable confidence, that Jesus's followers very quickly came to believe and publicly proclaim his resurrection, a conviction attested in sources, including material embedded within Paul's letters, that most scholars date to within a few years of Jesus's death. Historical method, however, cannot adjudicate the truth of a claimed miraculous event; it can only establish what people believed, when they began believing it, and what consequences that belief produced. This textbook accordingly states that the resurrection proclamation was foundational to the movement's identity and rapid early development without asserting or denying its literal truth, a matter properly left to religious faith rather than historical scholarship.

The earliest community of Jesus's followers formed in Jerusalem, remaining thoroughly Jewish in practice, continuing to worship at the Temple, observe Jewish law, and understand Jesus's significance within a Jewish framework centered on messianic expectation, the hope for a divinely appointed deliverer of Israel. This Jerusalem community, led in its early years by figures including Peter and, notably, James, described in sources as Jesus's brother, maintained close ties to the wider Jewish community even as it developed a distinctive identity centered on belief in Jesus as the promised Messiah, or Christ, the Greek term from which the word "Christian" eventually derived. It is this thoroughly Jewish origin, rather than a sudden founding of an entirely new and separate religion, that historians emphasize when explaining how the movement began.

Section 23.5

Paul, Mission, and the Expansion beyond Judea

Paul of Tarsus, a Greek-speaking Diaspora Jew and, according to his own account, an initial opponent of the Jesus movement, experienced a transformative encounter that he understood as a direct calling from the risen Jesus, and he subsequently became the movement's most historically significant missionary to Gentile, or non-Jewish, populations. Paul's authentic letters, written to Christian communities he had founded or with which he corresponded, including congregations at Corinth, Thessalonica, Philippi, Galatia, and elsewhere, and eventually addressed to Rome itself, constitute the earliest surviving Christian texts, predating the written Gospels by at least a decade and, in some cases, by several decades.

Historians should resist the older, oversimplified narrative in which Jesus supposedly founded a Jewish-only movement that Paul single-handedly transformed into a universal, Gentile-oriented religion. The evidence instead suggests a messier and more historically interesting picture: debates over whether and how Gentile converts should be incorporated into the movement, and whether they needed to observe Jewish law, including circumcision and dietary rules, were already underway among Jesus's early followers before Paul's most active missionary period, and Paul was one significant voice, though an unusually influential one, within these ongoing debates rather than their sole originator. A meeting described in early Christian sources as occurring in Jerusalem, sometimes called the Apostolic Council, addressed precisely this question. Paul's own account in his letter to the Galatians and the later account in the Acts of the Apostles both preserve evidence of major disputes over Gentile inclusion, although the two accounts differ in their presentation and details. Both support the conclusion that circumcision was not imposed on Paul's Gentile converts, while the exact scope and reception of any Jerusalem agreement remain debated among scholars.

Paul's missionary strategy depended heavily on existing Roman and Jewish Diaspora infrastructure. He traveled along Roman roads and extensive Mediterranean shipping networks connecting major cities of Asia Minor, Greece, and the wider eastern Mediterranean, and, according to the pattern the Acts of the Apostles describes, he characteristically began his activity in a new city at the local synagogue, drawing both Jewish listeners and Gentile "God-fearers," non-Jews who had already been attracted to Jewish monotheism and ethical teaching without undergoing full conversion to Judaism, before establishing separate Christian communities in private households. Antioch, a major city in Roman Syria, served as an important early base for Gentile Christian mission, and it was there, according to early Christian sources, that the term "Christian" was first applied to Jesus's followers by outside observers.

Paul's letters, though occasional documents written to address specific problems in particular communities rather than systematic theological treatises, provide historians with invaluable direct evidence of early Christian communal life, including disputes over leadership, sexual ethics, participation in civic religious meals, spiritual gifts, and the proper conduct of communal worship. They reveal communities that were socially mixed, including free persons, enslaved people, women in various roles, and people of differing economic status, a social texture examined further in Section 23.7.

Section 23.6

Jews and Christians: An Uneven Separation

One of the most persistent oversimplifications in popular understanding of early Christian history holds that Christianity separated cleanly and quickly from Judaism, perhaps at the very moment of Jesus's death or shortly thereafter, producing two distinct religions that then developed independently. Current historical scholarship presents a considerably more complicated picture, generally described as a long, gradual, and regionally uneven process rather than a single decisive break, a question this chapter examines directly in the Historical Debate feature below.

Several developments contributed to an increasing, though never uniform, differentiation between Jewish communities and the growing number of Christian communities across the first and second centuries. The admission of Gentile converts without requiring full observance of Jewish law, discussed in Section 23.5, created communities whose membership and practice diverged increasingly from synagogue norms. The destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE, examined in Chapter 22 as a consequence of the Jewish revolt, removed the central institution of sacrificial worship around which much of Jewish religious life had previously organized, prompting significant transformation within Judaism itself, including the increasing importance of rabbinic interpretation of Torah as a foundation for Jewish religious life in the Temple's absence, while also removing a shared institutional reference point that had connected many early Jewish followers of Jesus to the wider Jewish community.

The Bar Kokhba revolt of 132 to 135 CE, a further major Jewish uprising against Roman rule in Judea, had its own significant consequences for the increasingly distinct trajectories of Jewish and Christian communities, since the revolt's messianic associations and its ultimately catastrophic suppression, including the Roman expulsion of Jews from Jerusalem, further separated the historical experiences of Jewish and Christian populations in the region. At the same time, historians caution against assuming that these events produced an immediate, complete, or universal separation. Sources describe continuing Jewish Christian communities, sometimes labeled by later heresiologists with names such as Ebionites, persisting for generations in some regions, while relations between Jewish and Christian communities in the same city varied considerably depending on local circumstances, personalities, and the relative size and character of each community.

It is equally important to state clearly that later Christian tradition, in some texts, gospels, and subsequent Christian writing, developed increasingly polemical and, at points, historically distorting characterizations of Jewish responsibility for Jesus's death and of Judaism generally, characterizations that later contributed to centuries of Christian antisemitism examined at multiple points in this textbook's later chapters, including discussions of medieval and modern persecution. Historians must read such polemical material critically, recognizing it as evidence of an increasingly competitive and, at times, hostile relationship between developing Christian communities and the Jewish communities from which many of their members had originally come, rather than as a neutral historical account of events.

Section 23.7

Christian Communities and Everyday Life

Early Christian communities met, for most of the period examined in this chapter, in private households rather than in dedicated public buildings, a pattern with significant social consequences that historians increasingly emphasize. A wealthier household member, sometimes a woman, might offer space for the community to gather, effectively becoming a patron whose social standing shaped the community's practical life even as Christian teaching, in principle, treated all baptized members as spiritually equal regardless of social status. This tension between professed spiritual equality and persistent social hierarchy runs throughout the evidence for early Christian community life.

Communal worship centered on shared meals, prayer, the reading of scripture and circulating letters, and two distinctive rituals: baptism, an initiation rite involving water that marked entry into the community, and what later tradition called the Eucharist, a ritual meal commemorating Jesus's last meal with his followers before his death, understood by participants as a means of communal and, in evolving theological terms, spiritual participation in Christ. Roman outsiders, overhearing garbled reports of Christian gatherings that spoke of eating "the body and blood" of their god, sometimes suspected Christians of cannibalism or other scandalous practices, a misunderstanding that contributed to social suspicion examined further in Section 23.11.

Christian communities also organized systems of mutual material support, including care for widows, orphans, the sick, and the imprisoned, funded through voluntary contribution, a practice that historians note bears some resemblance to, while also differing in important ways from, the mutual aid functions of Roman voluntary associations and burial societies that structured much ordinary civic life. Enslaved people appear throughout the evidence for early Christian communities, sometimes as members alongside their enslavers, a fact that did not, in this period, translate into any systematic Christian challenge to the institution of slavery itself, though Christian teaching did, at points, urge enslavers toward gentler treatment and mutual religious respect. Women likewise appear prominently in the sources, as patrons, prophets, teachers in some communities, and, as the Primary Source Window on Perpetua illustrates, as martyrs, even as most Christian communities, like the wider society around them, maintained patriarchal expectations regarding household authority and public leadership.

Family life within Christian households frequently involved religious difference, since conversion often occurred household by household but did not always include every family member, producing documented tensions between Christian converts and non-Christian relatives, a dynamic vividly illustrated in the account of Perpetua's relationship with her father, examined later in this chapter.

Section 23.8

Authority, Texts, and Church Organization

Christian communal leadership developed gradually and unevenly across the period this chapter examines, and historians caution strongly against imagining first-century Christian congregations as miniature versions of the later, considerably more centralized medieval church. Paul's letters describe a range of leadership roles and spiritual functions within early communities, including apostles, understood broadly as authoritative founding missionaries; prophets, who delivered inspired teaching; teachers; and various forms of practical service. Over the course of the first and second centuries, more settled and consistent patterns of local leadership emerged in many communities, typically involving overseers, using the Greek term that gives us "bishop," presbyters or elders, and deacons, who assisted with practical and charitable functions.

By the early second century, sources including the letters attributed to Ignatius of Antioch describe an emerging pattern in which a single bishop presided over each city's Christian community, supported by a council of presbyters and assisted by deacons, a structure that would become increasingly standard, though its adoption and precise form varied by region and proceeded at different speeds in different communities. This textbook treats this development as a genuine historical process of institutionalization rather than as evidence that a single, uniform church structure existed from the movement's earliest days.

Alongside developing leadership structures, Christian communities produced and circulated an expanding body of texts. Paul's letters, as already noted, represent the earliest layer of this literature. The canonical Gospels, narrative accounts of Jesus's life, teaching, death, and resurrection, were composed in the following decades, generally dated by scholars to the period from roughly 70 to 100 CE, drawing on earlier oral tradition and, in some cases, earlier written sources no longer independently preserved. Additional letters, apocalyptic literature, and other writings continued to be produced and circulated among communities throughout the second and third centuries. It is essential for students to understand that the collection of texts later recognized as the New Testament did not exist as a fixed, agreed body of scripture during the period this chapter examines; rather, various communities used various combinations of authoritative texts, and the process of establishing a widely, though still not universally, recognized canon extended well into and beyond the period covered by Chapter 26.

Section 23.9

Conversion and the Spread of Christianity

Why did a small movement originating among Jewish communities in a minor Roman province eventually spread across the Mediterranean world? Historians have proposed many explanations over the past century and a half, and this chapter's central methodological lesson is that no single explanation, offered in isolation, adequately accounts for the pattern of growth that the evidence reveals. Several factors worked together, in combinations that varied by region, period, and social context.

Infrastructure mattered enormously. The Roman road network, extensive Mediterranean shipping networks, the widespread use of Greek as a common language across the eastern provinces, and the existing web of Jewish Diaspora communities and synagogues together provided channels along which Christian missionaries, letters, and traveling believers could move with relative ease compared to earlier periods of Mediterranean history. Urban life mattered as well: the surviving evidence is strongly concentrated in cities, and many of the earliest securely documented communities were urban, where dense populations, existing networks of household and workplace association, and greater exposure to diverse religious and philosophical ideas created conditions favorable to the formation of new religious communities, although Christianity also reached rural populations before the mid-third century, even if the evidence for rural Christian life in this period is considerably thinner than for cities.

Social and familial networks likely mattered more than formal missionary preaching for the ordinary spread of the movement from one household to the next. Conversion in this period was frequently a communal or household matter rather than a purely individual decision, with an entire household, including its enslaved members, sometimes adopting a new religious affiliation following the conversion of its head, though evidence also shows individual family members converting independently, occasionally against the wishes of relatives. Charitable practices, including care for the poor, the sick, and the imprisoned, may have made Christian community membership practically attractive to some, particularly amid the uncertainties of ancient urban life, though historians caution against reducing conversion to simple material calculation.

Popular older explanations deserve particular scrutiny. The claim that Christianity appealed primarily to slaves and the poor oversimplifies evidence showing Christian communities that included people across a range of social and economic positions, including some of considerable wealth and status, particularly from the second century onward. The claim that women were uniquely responsible for converting the ancient world, though reflecting real and significant female participation and leadership in some communities, similarly overstates a single factor at the expense of a fuller picture. The claim that martyrdom automatically produced conversions, though martyr narratives themselves sometimes made this claim rhetorically, is difficult to verify as a general historical mechanism and should be treated as one possible, but unproven, contributing factor rather than a demonstrated cause. The most historically responsible conclusion holds that Christian expansion resulted from the interaction of infrastructure, urban social networks, household and family dynamics, charitable practice, and the movement's distinctive religious claims, operating differently in different times and places, rather than from any single decisive cause.

Section 23.10

Persecution: Local Hostility and Imperial Intervention

Popular understanding of early Christian history often assumes that Rome persecuted Christians continuously and systematically from the reign of Nero until the conversion of Constantine, a picture this chapter explicitly corrects. The historical record instead shows a pattern of intermittent, often localized hostility, punctuated by more serious episodes, rather than a sustained, empire-wide policy of persecution maintained across nearly three centuries.

The earliest clearly attested instance of state violence against Christians occurred at Rome in 64 CE, when the emperor Nero, seeking to deflect suspicion that he himself had caused a devastating fire in the city, blamed and severely punished the Christian community there. This episode, described by the Roman historian Tacitus writing decades later, appears to have been a local response to a specific crisis rather than the beginning of a coordinated general persecution, and there is no clear evidence of a comparable empire-wide campaign in its immediate aftermath.

The correspondence between Pliny the Younger and the emperor Trajan around 112 CE, examined in this chapter's central Primary Source Window, reveals the genuine legal and administrative uncertainty that characterized official Roman treatment of Christians for much of the second century. Pliny himself was uncertain whether the Christian name alone or crimes associated with it should be punished, a legal question modern scholars agree Trajan did not resolve in the abstract. Instead, Trajan instructed Pliny not to seek Christians out actively, to disregard anonymous accusations, and to punish those formally accused before a magistrate and confirmed to be Christian, while pardoning those who denied the accusation and demonstrated compliance by offering sacrifice. The precise legal basis for treating Christian identity itself as punishable therefore remains a matter of continuing scholarly debate rather than a settled question this correspondence definitively answers.

The second century saw several severe, though geographically limited, episodes of persecution, including the martyrdom of the elderly bishop Polycarp at Smyrna and a notably violent persecution at Lyons and Vienne in Roman Gaul, described in a surviving letter from the Christian communities there to churches elsewhere, which recounts mob violence, formal trials, and executions carried out with considerable brutality. These episodes illustrate that persecution, when it occurred, could be intense, but they also confirm its localized and sporadic character, since comparable violence did not occur simultaneously across the whole empire.

By the early third century, individual and locally organized persecutions, including the events surrounding Perpetua's martyrdom at Carthage around 203 CE, continued to occur in various provinces. It was only with the edict of the emperor Decius in 249 to 251 CE, examined further as this chapter's concluding development in Section 23.12, that Roman authorities for the first time implemented a broadly empire-wide measure, requiring inhabitants generally, not Christians specifically, to perform sacrifice and obtain a certificate, or libellus, attesting compliance, a policy that inevitably confronted Christians throughout the empire with a stark and simultaneous choice between sacrifice and legal jeopardy. This chapter treats the Decian measure as a significant escalation in scale rather than as the beginning of continuous persecution, since the more severe and sustained imperial campaign associated with Diocletian belongs to Chapter 25.

Section 23.11

Christianity within a Plural Empire

Understanding why Christians periodically attracted hostility requires setting aside the assumption that Roman society was simply intolerant of religious difference. As Sections 23.1 and 23.2 established, the Roman Empire generally accommodated an extraordinary range of religious traditions, including Judaism's own distinctive exclusive monotheism, without systematic persecution. What made Christianity distinctively troubling to some contemporaries was not religious difference as such but the movement's characteristic and, in the eyes of many outsiders, uniquely uncompromising refusal to participate in the sacrificial system that bound Roman communities together, a refusal rooted in Christian monotheism and in the conviction, inherited in part from Jewish tradition, that honoring other gods constituted a fundamental betrayal of exclusive devotion to the one true God.

Jewish communities shared a comparable exclusive monotheism and a comparable refusal to sacrifice to other gods, and Roman authorities often, though inconsistently and through repeated local negotiation rather than a single settled legal rule, recognized Jewish ancestral custom as a longstanding basis for accommodation, an arrangement examined in Sections 23.3 and the Historical Context above. Judaism did include proselytes, converts from the wider population, and Roman legal treatment of Jewish communities varied considerably by time and place rather than resting on any simple, uniform toleration. Even so, Christianity's active pursuit of converts across the empire's diverse population, combined with its comparatively recent and unfamiliar character in the eyes of many contemporaries, made its refusal of civic religious obligation appear, to some Roman observers, as a more direct and unsettling challenge to the wider social order than the ancestral distinctiveness of Jewish communities typically did.

Social suspicion compounded this religious friction. Christian gatherings, held privately in households and, at points, restricted to members and their guests, invited rumor and misunderstanding, including the misinterpretation of Eucharistic language as evidence of cannibalism, noted in Section 23.7, and suspicion regarding Christians' use of familial language, calling one another "brother" and "sister," which some outsiders misconstrued as evidence of incestuous practice. Educated critics, including the second-century philosopher Celsus, whose arguments survive largely through a later Christian refutation, attacked Christianity on intellectual and social grounds, characterizing it as a superstition appealing to the credulous and socially marginal and criticizing Christian claims about scripture, miracles, and Jesus's resurrection.

At the same time, Christianity did not exist in a state of constant open conflict with the wider religious environment. Christian and non-Christian neighbors coexisted, traded, and interacted in ordinary daily life across the empire for most of the period this chapter examines, and outright persecution, as Section 23.10 explained, remained the exception rather than the constant rule. Understanding Christianity's position within a genuinely plural religious empire, rather than assuming perpetual, uniform hostility, allows students to appreciate both the real friction the movement generated and the ordinary social coexistence that also characterized much of this period.

Section 23.12

A Minority Religion Becomes an Imperial Problem

By the middle years of the third century, roughly two hundred years after the earliest Jerusalem community first proclaimed Jesus's resurrection, Christianity had become geographically extensive and institutionally more coherent than at any earlier point in its history, present in identifiable communities stretching from Gaul and Rome in the west to North Africa, Egypt, Syria, and Asia Minor in the east, with particular strength in major urban centers including Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, and Carthage; limited and difficult-to-verify literary evidence also suggests some Christian presence in Roman Britain by this period, though it cannot be treated as securely established in the way the other regions can. Bishops in these and other cities maintained networks of correspondence, addressing shared concerns over doctrine, discipline, and the treatment of Christians who had lapsed under persecution, a network of communication that gave the movement, for the first time, something resembling a coordinated, though still far from centralized, collective identity across considerable distances.

At the same time, this chapter closes by emphasizing that Christians remained, throughout this period, a minority within an overwhelmingly religiously plural empire. Estimates of the total Christian population by the mid-third century vary considerably among modern scholars and should be treated with appropriate caution given the limited and uneven evidence, but even generous estimates place Christians as a distinct minority relative to the empire's total population, concentrated disproportionately in certain cities and regions rather than evenly distributed across every province. Traditional civic and household religion, the imperial cult, and the wide range of other cults examined in Section 23.2 remained the ordinary religious experience of the great majority of the empire's inhabitants.

The Decian persecution of 249 to 251 CE, discussed in Section 23.10, revealed both Christianity's growing visibility and the continuing precariousness of its position. For the first time, an emperor's religious policy, though not explicitly targeting Christians by name, produced a genuinely empire-wide crisis for Christian communities, forcing individual believers everywhere to choose between sacrifice and the risk of severe legal consequence, and producing, in the aftermath, significant internal Christian controversy over how to treat those who had complied, a controversy that would occupy church leaders for years afterward and that connects directly to the institutional and doctrinal developments examined in Chapter 26.

This chapter therefore closes at a genuine historical threshold. Christianity had become too extensive and too visible to be dismissed any longer as an obscure local sect, yet it remained a minority religion whose long-term relationship with Roman imperial power was entirely unresolved. Chapter 24 now turns away from this religious narrative to examine the broader political, military, and economic crisis that gripped the Roman Empire during the third century, a crisis whose pressures would, in turn, shape the dramatic and unexpected transformation of the relationship between Christianity and the Roman state that Chapter 25 examines under Diocletian and Constantine.

Historical Vocabulary
The "Parting of the Ways"

Historians often use the phrase the parting of the ways to describe the gradual process by which Christian communities and the wider Jewish world came to be understood, by insiders and outsiders alike, as belonging to two distinct religious traditions rather than to a single, internally varied movement. As Section 23.6 explains, current scholarship generally rejects the older assumption that this separation occurred at a single early moment, emphasizing instead a slow, regionally uneven process extending across the first, second, and even into the third centuries, with some communities and individuals occupying ambiguous or mixed positions for a considerable time. Students should treat the phrase as a useful analytical shorthand for a complex historical process, not as a description of a single datable event.

Primary Source Window

Pliny the Younger and the Emperor Trajan on the Christians, c. 112 CE

Creator: Pliny the Younger, Roman governor of Bithynia-Pontus, and the emperor Trajan · Date: c. 112 CE · Type: Official administrative correspondence, Letters, Book 10

Context: As governor of a province in Asia Minor, Pliny encountered accusations against local Christians and, uncertain how Roman law and precedent required him to proceed, wrote directly to the emperor Trajan for guidance, a pattern of provincial governors seeking imperial direction already familiar from Chapter 22's discussion of provincial administration.

Paraphrase of Pliny, Letters 10.96, and Trajan's reply, 10.97 Pliny explained that he had interrogated those accused of being Christians, executing those who persisted in the admission after repeated warning, on the grounds that their obstinacy alone deserved punishment, while releasing those who denied the charge and demonstrated their sincerity by offering wine and incense before an image of the emperor and of the gods, and by cursing Christ. He noted that an anonymous pamphlet had named a number of individuals, some of whom claimed they had never been Christian or had abandoned the faith years earlier, and he explicitly asked Trajan whether the Christian name itself, apart from any specific crime, was punishable. Trajan, in reply, did not answer that question directly. He approved Pliny's general approach, instructed him not to seek Christians out actively, ordered that anonymous accusations be disregarded as setting a poor precedent, and specified that anyone who denied being a Christian and proved it by sacrificing should be pardoned regardless of past suspicion.

Analysis: This exchange is invaluable because it shows, in an unusually direct administrative source, the absence of any settled, comprehensive imperial law specifically criminalizing Christianity as such, alongside a clear willingness to punish those formally accused and found obstinate. It also shows that Trajan sidestepped Pliny's explicit legal question about the Christian name itself, leaving the precise legal basis for punishment genuinely ambiguous, a point modern scholarship continues to debate rather than treat as settled. The exchange reveals the centrality of sacrifice as the practical test of loyalty, and it shows that persecution, where it occurred, generally depended on local accusation rather than active state investigation.

Questions for students:

  1. What test does Trajan specify for determining whether an accused person should be released?
  2. Why does Trajan reject anonymous accusations, and what does this reveal about Roman legal principles?
  3. What does this exchange suggest about how much, or how little, Roman officials generally understood about Christian belief and practice?
  4. Why is this source better evidence for administrative practice in one province at one moment than for a uniform empire-wide policy toward Christians?
Primary Source Window

The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity, c. 203 CE

Creator: A composite text, including material presented as Perpetua's own prison account, later incorporated into a larger martyr narrative by an unnamed editor · Date: c. 203 CE, Carthage, North Africa · Type: Martyr narrative, or passio, partly first-person

Context: Perpetua, a young married woman of respectable status, and Felicity, an enslaved woman, were among a group of catechumens, or converts under instruction, imprisoned and eventually executed at Carthage for refusing to renounce Christianity, during a period of local persecution in Roman North Africa.

Paraphrase of the surviving narrative The text describes Perpetua's repeated confrontations with her father, who pleaded with her to renounce her faith for the sake of her family and her infant son, her imprisonment alongside Felicity and other catechumens, Felicity's own late pregnancy and childbirth shortly before the execution, and the eventual deaths of the group in the amphitheater before a public crowd. The portion presented as Perpetua's own account describes her visions and her determination, framed in the text as evidence of unwavering faith under extreme familial and social pressure.

Analysis: This source is valuable for the social and family dimensions of persecution it reveals, including generational and gendered conflict, the situation of an enslaved co-martyr, and the involvement of a local Roman public spectacle in the execution. Students should also treat it critically as a text shaped by later Christian editing and by the conventions of martyr literature, which characteristically emphasized steadfastness and triumph over suffering; the presence of an embedded first-person voice within a larger edited narrative raises important questions about authorship and reliability that historians must weigh carefully.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this narrative reveal about family conflict produced by religious conversion?
  2. How does the presence of an enslaved co-martyr, Felicity, complicate any simple picture of the social composition of early Christian communities?
  3. Why must historians distinguish between the portion of the text presented as Perpetua's own account and the surrounding editorial framework?
  4. Why do martyr narratives require particularly careful historical evaluation compared to administrative correspondence such as the Pliny-Trajan letters?
Historical Debate

When Did Christianity Separate from Judaism?

The question: Did Christianity separate from Judaism at a single, identifiable early moment, or should historians understand the "parting of the ways" as a long, gradual, and regionally uneven process extending across several centuries?

Interpretation A — An early and relatively rapid separation: Some older scholarship, and some popular accounts, held that the admission of Gentile converts without full observance of Jewish law, combined with the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE, produced a fairly rapid and fairly complete separation between Christian and Jewish communities within a generation or two of Jesus's death.
Interpretation B — A long, gradual, and regionally uneven process: Much current scholarship instead emphasizes evidence for continuing Jewish Christian communities persisting for generations, considerable local variation in how sharply Jewish and Christian identities were distinguished, and a process of mutual self-definition that extended well into the second and even third centuries, differing significantly from region to region.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on early Christian texts, references in later rabbinic literature, and the archaeological and documentary record of specific communities, but they differ in how much weight to give to evidence of continued overlap and ambiguity relative to evidence of increasing distinction and, at times, hostility.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how they narrate the earliest centuries of both Jewish and Christian history, and it cautions against assuming that later, clearly separate religious identities existed from the very beginning of the movement examined in this chapter.

Student task: Identify evidence from this chapter that could support each interpretation, and explain why a single fixed date for "the parting of the ways" may be historically misleading.

Multiple Perspectives

Seeing Christianity Differently: Perspectives across the Roman World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Roman provincial magistrate: Christians who refused to sacrifice appeared not merely eccentric but obstinately disloyal to the civic order, since sacrifice expressed the reciprocal relationship on which communal and imperial wellbeing depended, and repeated refusal after formal warning could not simply be overlooked.
From the perspective of a traditional civic priest: Sacrifice performed correctly and consistently by the whole community maintained the favor of the gods, and any group that publicly and persistently withheld its participation raised a genuine, not merely symbolic, danger to collective welfare.
From the perspective of a Jewish community leader in a Diaspora city: Depending on the place and period, followers of Jesus within the community might be understood as a controversial but internal Jewish faction, or, increasingly over time, as a distinct and sometimes rival group whose Gentile converts and christological claims placed them outside the boundaries of the synagogue community.
From the perspective of a Christian bishop in the mid-third century: The growing network of correspondence among churches offered real support for maintaining unity and discipline, but the Decian crisis raised painful and divisive questions about how to treat believers who had complied with the sacrifice order under threat.
From the perspective of an enslaved Christian woman such as Felicity: Membership in a Christian community offered spiritual fellowship shared with free members of the congregation, but did not, in the world she inhabited, alter her legal status as property or exempt her from the particular vulnerabilities of enslavement, including forced separation from her newborn child before her execution.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Christianity from c. 50 CE to c. 250 CE

What Changed

  • Christian communities expanded from a small circle closely tied to Judea and the wider Jewish world to a geographically extensive network reaching Gaul, North Africa, and beyond, with more limited and less certain evidence suggesting some presence as far as Roman Britain
  • Local leadership developed from fluid, varied roles such as apostles and prophets toward more settled patterns of bishops, presbyters, and deacons in many cities
  • A body of authoritative Christian texts expanded considerably, though without yet forming a single fixed canon
  • Roman imperial attention shifted from sporadic local episodes toward the first genuinely empire-wide coercive measure under Decius

What Stayed the Same

  • Christianity remained a numerical minority within the empire's overall population throughout this entire period
  • Household-based community life, communal meals, and mutual charitable support remained central to Christian practice
  • Persecution, where it occurred, continued to depend heavily on local accusation and circumstance rather than a single centralized policy until the very end of this period
  • Christian communities continued to exist within, and interact with, an overwhelmingly religiously plural Roman world

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real growth and institutional development of Christianity and its continuing minority, locally variable, and religiously embedded position prevents students from assuming that Christian dominance was already underway well before Constantine.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Christianity Spread?

Long-term causes

The Roman road and sea network, the widespread use of Greek across the eastern provinces, and the existing infrastructure of Jewish Diaspora communities and synagogues provided channels along which a new religious movement could travel.

Short-term causes

Individual missionaries such as Paul, household and family networks, and practical charitable activity within particular cities allowed the movement to take root community by community.

Development

Interconnected Christian communities spread across the Mediterranean world, increasingly linked through correspondence among bishops and shared texts.

Immediate effects

New communities formed in additional cities and regions, creating demand for local leadership, teaching, charitable organization, and communication among congregations.

Long-term consequences

A geographically dispersed but increasingly connected Christian movement became socially visible enough to attract sustained debate, institutional development, and eventually broader imperial attention.

Historical complexity

No single explanation, whether appeal to enslaved people, to women, to the poor, or to those facing martyrdom, accounts by itself for this growth; multiple factors operated together and with different weight in different regions and periods.

Historical caution: The very unevenness of the evidence, concentrated in certain well-documented cities and communities, means that historians must be careful not to generalize confidently about regions or social groups for which direct evidence is scarce.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Christian Household Meeting, c. 180 CE

Note: the scene below is a composite historical reconstruction, drawing on evidence discussed throughout this chapter, rather than a description of a specific documented event.

Imagine a modest but comfortable house in a provincial Roman city on an evening near the end of the week. The household's owner, a woman of independent means whose husband has died, has offered her home's largest room for the local Christian community to gather, as she has done for several years. Guests arrive gradually: an artisan and his wife, several of the household's own enslaved members who share the family's faith, a widow supported through the community's charitable fund, a traveling believer carrying a letter from a church in a distant province, and a handful of catechumens preparing for baptism, who attend the teaching and prayers, although participation in the Eucharist is restricted to baptized members.

The gathering opens with prayer and the reading aloud of a letter, perhaps one attributed to Paul or another apostolic figure, followed by teaching from a presbyter and questions from those present. After prayer, bread and a cup of wine mixed with water are offered in thanksgiving and distributed to the baptized members present, a practice of this general kind described by the mid-second-century Christian writer Justin Martyr. Some communities also held other communal meals for mutual support and fellowship, though Eucharistic practice itself varied considerably by time and place and should not be assumed uniform across every community this chapter examines. The gathering's charitable dimension continues alongside worship, as the community collects a contribution toward the care of the sick, the imprisoned, and those in material need. Social distinctions persist even within this setting, since the household's patron retains her practical authority over the physical space and resources involved, and the enslaved members present remain legally enslaved even as they share fully in the community's rituals. As the evening ends, the traveling believer's letter is read once more and copied by hand for circulation to a neighboring community, part of the growing web of correspondence that, as Section 23.8 explains, increasingly connected Christian communities across considerable distances.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how a small Jewish movement centered on Jesus of Nazareth developed, over roughly two centuries, into a geographically extensive, though still minority, religious presence across the Roman Empire. It began by establishing the religious world into which Christianity emerged, a Roman world with especially strong emphasis on civic and household practice, sacrifice, and reciprocity, while still involving beliefs and expectations about divine powers, and populated by an extraordinary range of cults, mystery religions, philosophical schools, and the imperial cult, all coexisting within a generally, though not unconditionally, tolerant religious environment.

The chapter then turned to Second Temple Judaism as a subject of independent historical importance, examining the Jerusalem Temple, synagogues, internal Jewish diversity, and the Diaspora infrastructure that would later prove significant for Christian expansion, without treating Judaism merely as a preface to a different religion. It examined what historians can and cannot establish about Jesus of Nazareth, distinguishing secure historical claims from matters properly belonging to theological faith, and traced how the earliest Jerusalem community, and subsequently Paul of Tarsus and other missionaries, carried the movement's message across the eastern Mediterranean and eventually to Rome itself.

A central theme of the chapter has been the rejection of oversimplified narratives: Christianity did not separate from Judaism at a single early moment but through a long, uneven process extending across centuries; Paul did not single-handedly invent Gentile Christianity but participated in debates already underway; Christian church leadership did not spring into being as a fully formed hierarchy but developed gradually and unevenly; and Roman persecution of Christians was not continuous and empire-wide for three centuries but intermittent, localized, and dependent on particular circumstances until the Decian measure of 249 to 251 CE.

The chapter has also emphasized the everyday, social texture of early Christian life, household worship, shared meals, charitable mutual support, and the participation of women and enslaved people alongside free members of varying status, while acknowledging the persistence of social hierarchy even within communities that professed spiritual equality. It examined why Christianity spread, rejecting single-cause explanations in favor of a combination of infrastructure, urban networks, household dynamics, and charitable practice, and it examined why Christian refusal of sacrifice generated friction with neighbors and magistrates within a religious culture that placed especially strong emphasis on reciprocal ritual obligation and communal practice.

By the chapter's chronological endpoint around the middle of the third century, Christianity had become too extensive to dismiss as an obscure local sect, with communities and increasingly organized leadership present from Gaul and Rome to North Africa, Egypt, Syria, and Asia Minor, and more limited, less securely attested evidence suggesting some presence as far as Roman Britain, yet it remained a minority religion whose relationship with Roman imperial power remained genuinely unresolved. This threshold sets up the developments examined in the following chapters of Part IV: the third-century political and military crisis examined in Chapter 24, the transformation of imperial government and the dramatic reversal in Christianity's relationship with the Roman state under Diocletian and Constantine examined in Chapter 25, and the doctrinal, institutional, and social developments of the maturing church examined in Chapter 26.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Civic cult
Religious practice organized around a city's official priesthoods, temples, and festivals, conducted on behalf of the community as a whole.
Imperial cult
Religious practices honoring emperors, their genius or numen, or the goddess Roma; forms varied substantially by region and could simultaneously express genuine religious devotion, civic hierarchy, and political loyalty.
Sacrifice
A ritual offering, including animal sacrifice as well as wine, incense, grain, and other foodstuffs, understood to maintain proper relations between a community and a divine power.
Reciprocity
The reciprocal exchange of honor and offering for divine favor that organized much ancient religious practice; it emphasized correct ritual and communal obligation without implying that beliefs about the gods were unimportant.
Synagogue
A Jewish communal institution for prayer, scripture reading, and gathering, present both in Judea and throughout the Diaspora.
Diaspora
Jewish communities living outside Judea, dispersed across the Mediterranean world, Mesopotamia, and beyond.
Second Temple Judaism
The term historians use for Jewish religious life centered on the Jerusalem Temple before its destruction in 70 CE, encompassing considerable internal diversity.
Messiah / Christ
A Hebrew and Greek term, respectively, meaning "anointed one," referring in Jewish tradition to a hoped-for divinely appointed deliverer, and applied by Jesus's followers to him.
Apostle
A term meaning "one sent," used for authoritative early missionaries and founding figures of the Christian movement, including Paul.
Gospel
A narrative account of Jesus's life, teaching, death, and resurrection; the four canonical Gospels were composed roughly between 70 and 100 CE.
Ekklesia
The Greek term, meaning "assembly," used by early Christians for their communities, later rendered "church."
Bishop
From the Greek episkopos, "overseer"; a local Christian leader whose singular authority over a city's community became increasingly standard from the early second century onward, though development varied by region.
Presbyter
An elder assisting in local church leadership, often working alongside a bishop and deacons.
Deacon
A Christian community official responsible chiefly for practical and charitable functions.
Baptism
A ritual involving water marking initiation into the Christian community.
Eucharist
A communal ritual meal commemorating Jesus's last meal with his followers, central to early Christian worship.
Catechumen
A convert undergoing instruction in preparation for baptism.
Martyr
From a Greek word meaning "witness," a person who suffered death rather than renounce Christian faith.
Persecution
State or communal action against Christians for their refusal to conform to expected religious practice, generally intermittent and localized before 249 CE.
Apostasy
The formal renunciation of a professed faith, a central concern during and after periods of persecution.
Libellus
A certificate issued during the Decian persecution attesting that an individual had performed the required sacrifice.
Parting of the ways
The historians' term for the long, gradual, and regionally uneven process by which Christian and Jewish communities came to be understood as religiously distinct.
Syncretism
The blending or identification of deities and religious practices from different traditions, common in the religiously plural Roman world.
Mystery cult
An imperfect umbrella term for traditions involving initiation or restricted rites, such as the cults of Isis, Cybele, and Mithras, which differed considerably from one another and should not be treated as a single uniform religious type.
Interpretatio Romana
The Roman practice of identifying local or foreign deities with Roman equivalents, facilitating provincial religious integration.
Pagan / Paganism
A later, largely Christian-derived classification for diverse non-Christian and non-Jewish religious practices; used cautiously by historians.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced a genuinely open historical process, not a predetermined march from persecution to imperial triumph. A small movement centered on a crucified Jewish teacher grew, through the labor of missionaries, the networks of households and cities, and the conviction of ordinary believers, into a minority but geographically extensive religious presence across the Roman Empire, without at any point along the way appearing destined for the dramatic reversal that Chapter 25 will examine.

Understanding this chapter's careful distinctions, between historical evidence and theological claim, between a single dramatic cause and multiple interacting factors, and between later institutional forms and the fluid, varied communities of the first and second centuries, equips students to read the remainder of Part IV with appropriate historical caution. The third-century crisis examined in Chapter 24 will unfold largely independently of the religious developments traced here, yet the political and social pressures of that crisis will help set the stage for the extraordinary transformation in Christianity's relationship to the Roman state that Chapter 25 examines.

Students should also carry forward this chapter's insistence that Judaism remained, throughout this period and beyond, a vital religious tradition in its own right, not merely a stage on the way to a different religion, and that the increasingly distinct trajectories of Jewish and Christian communities emerged through a long and uneven historical process whose later consequences, including centuries of Christian antisemitism, this textbook will examine honestly in subsequent volumes.

Finally, this chapter's account of a religiously plural Roman world, in which Christianity was one distinctive but minority presence among many cults, cautions against assuming that European religious history moved along any single, inevitable path toward Christian dominance. Chapter 25 will show how contingent, unexpected, and historically significant the eventual alliance between Christianity and the Roman state actually was.
