Scandinavia, Poland-Lithuania, Russia, and the Ottoman European Order

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Between 1648 and 1774 the northern and eastern half of Europe was not a quiet margin beside the great western monarchies. It was a crowded political space in which a Lutheran Baltic empire rose and fell, a noble republic with an elected king confronted its neighbors, a Muscovite tsardom became an imperial power, and an Ottoman sultanate that ruled much of southeastern Europe adapted to new rivals on the Danube and the Black Sea.

In the winter of 1657 to 1658, Charles X Gustav of Sweden led an army across the frozen waters of the Little Belt and the Great Belt, the straits that separate the Danish mainland from the islands on which Copenhagen stands. The crossing, which contemporaries regarded as extraordinary and which depended on an unusually severe winter, placed a Swedish army within reach of the Danish capital and forced the Danish crown to accept the Treaty of Roskilde in February 1658. It also illustrates a point that frames this chapter: the political map of northern Europe was not drawn by diplomats alone. It was shaped by ice and weather, by the ability of governments to feed and pay soldiers, by the loyalty of nobles and townspeople, and by the willingness of neighboring rulers to exploit a rival's moment of weakness.

A quarter of a century later, in September 1683, a very different army stood before a very different city. The Ottoman force commanded by the grand vizier Kara Mustafa Pasha had besieged Vienna since July, and its defeat on September 12 by a relief army that included Habsburg imperial troops, contingents from several German principalities, and a Polish-Lithuanian force led personally by King John III Sobieski, opened a long war that reversed Ottoman fortunes in Hungary. Yet the Ottoman Empire that emerged from that war in 1699 was not the empire of imperial historians' caricatures. It retained Belgrade, most of the Balkans, the Danubian principalities as tributaries, and the Crimean Khanate as a vassal, and in 1739 it recovered Belgrade itself. Only in 1774, at Küçük Kaynarca, did it accept a settlement that for the first time gave Russia a durable foothold on the northern Black Sea coast and altered the status of the Crimean Khanate.

This chapter studies the political systems that collided in these episodes. It begins with two Scandinavian monarchies that were not alike: Sweden, which built a Baltic empire in the seventeenth century, passed through royal absolutism under Charles XI, lost its eastern Baltic provinces in the Great Northern War, and then entered an Age of Liberty in which the Riksdag of the Estates held unusual authority; and Denmark-Norway, which adopted a written hereditary absolutism in 1660 to 1665. It turns next to the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, an elective monarchy governed by noble assemblies, whose seventeenth-century wars, eighteenth-century dependence on foreign powers, and partition in 1772 must be explained without treating its fate as foreordained. It then follows Russia from a tsardom shaped by serfdom, Orthodoxy, and frontier expansion into an empire declared in 1721, and finally examines the Ottoman European order, with its provincial administrators, local notables, religious communities, and tributary polities.

The chapter's period, 1648 to 1774, begins with the settlement of Westphalia examined in Chapter 67 and closes with two events that illustrate how the balance of the north and east shifted: the First Partition of Poland-Lithuania in 1772, carried out by Russia, Prussia, and Austria, and the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774. It does not extend to the later partitions, to the Russian annexation of Crimea in 1783, or to the full program of Catherine II, which is reserved for Chapter 84. The comparative history of eighteenth-century wars and alliances belongs to Chapter 80, and Chapter 78 has already examined the Austrian and Prussian states whose growth forms an essential part of the background here.

Historians reconstruct these societies from a very uneven evidentiary base. Swedish administrative records, including land registers, tax rolls, and the proceedings of the Riksdag, are unusually rich. Polish-Lithuanian history rests on sejm and sejmik records, magnate archives, town books, and the writings of the noble political nation, but the voices of peasants and of many Orthodox and Jewish communities appear mostly through others. Russian sources include legal codes, chancery records, chronicles, and foreign accounts, while Ottoman history draws on imperial registers, the proceedings of Islamic courts, chronicles, and diplomatic correspondence, supplemented increasingly by Greek, Armenian, Slavic, and Hebrew sources. Cossack chronicles, treaties, and the letters of hetmans preserve Ukrainian perspectives, but they were often written with later political purposes. This chapter therefore keeps asking whose account survives, whose does not, and which later national narrative may be shaping what historians have inherited.

Within Volume III, this chapter extends the argument of Part XII that European state formation took several forms. France, Britain, the Dutch Republic, Austria, and Prussia were not the only models available. The monarchies, republics, and empires of the north and east combined fiscal and military institutions, noble privilege, religious diversity, and frontier societies in different ways, and their interaction transformed the European system. The chapter's central question is how competing systems of monarchy, noble representation, imperial administration, and regional autonomy shaped this transformation, and why some powers gained strategic influence while others met mounting territorial and political constraints. The answer proposed here rejects both the idea of a single path of modernization and the idea of an inevitable Russian ascent and inevitable Swedish, Polish-Lithuanian, and Ottoman decline.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Compare Swedish royal absolutism after 1680, Danish-Norwegian hereditary absolutism after 1660 to 1665, and the Swedish Age of Liberty as distinct constitutional outcomes.
  2. Explain how the institutions of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth worked, including the elective monarchy, the Sejm, the sejmiks, and the liberum veto, and distinguish noble liberty from general political equality.
  3. Analyze the Khmelnytsky Uprising and the Cossack Hetmanate as the work of Ukrainian political actors, and interpret the contested meaning of the Pereiaslav agreement of 1654.
  4. Trace the Great Northern War from its causes through Narva, Poltava, and the Treaty of Nystad, and identify the territories that actually changed hands.
  5. Assess the foundations of Russian expansion before Peter I and evaluate Peter's administrative, military, religious, and social reforms through their institutional results and social burdens.
  6. Describe the Ottoman European order, including provincial administration, the transformation of the timar system, local notables, religious communities, the Danubian principalities, and the Crimean Khanate.
  7. Interpret the siege of Vienna, the Treaty of Karlowitz, the settlements of 1718 and 1739, and the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca as stages in a changing frontier rather than as a simple sequence of Ottoman defeats.
  8. Distinguish foreign coercion from domestic institutional weakness in explaining the First Partition of 1772, without treating the partition as inevitable.
  9. Compare the Baltic and Black Sea systems in terms of maritime access, state finance, diplomacy, and the changing positions of Sweden, Russia, Poland-Lithuania, the Ottomans, Austria, and Prussia.
  10. Connect political change to the experiences of nobles, peasants, townspeople, Jewish communities, religious dissenters, frontier peoples, and women.
  11. Evaluate primary sources, including laws, treaties, and constitutional documents, by asking who created them, for what purposes, and whose perspectives they omit.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Sweden and Denmark-Norway, neighboring Lutheran monarchies, follow such different constitutional paths between 1660 and 1772?
  2. How did the cost of war shape political institutions, tax systems, and the relationship between rulers and elites in each of the four systems studied here?
  3. In what sense was the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth a serious constitutional alternative to monarchical absolutism, and what were the limits of that alternative?
  4. How did Ukrainian Cossack leaders, the Commonwealth, Muscovy, and the Crimean Khanate each pursue their own objectives in the mid seventeenth century, and why does the meaning of the 1654 agreement remain disputed?
  5. Which of Peter I's changes had deep Muscovite roots, which were genuine innovations, and what did they cost the people who bore them?
  6. How should historians describe the eighteenth century Ottoman Empire, as decline, as adaptation, or as something that resists both labels?
  7. How did the Baltic and the Black Sea function as connected but distinct strategic regions, and why did control of their coasts matter for state finance and diplomacy?
  8. Whose interests did territorial expansion and diplomatic settlement serve, and who bore their costs?

Historical Context

The northern and eastern powers examined here entered 1648 with histories that were already long and different. Sweden, a Lutheran kingdom since the Reformation examined in Chapter 62, had grown from a peripheral northern realm into a major military state during the Thirty Years' War, and the Peace of Westphalia confirmed its possession of Western Pomerania, the port of Wismar, and the former bishoprics of Bremen and Verden, together with a seat in the Imperial Diet. Sweden already held Finland, Estonia, Ingria, the fortress district of Kexholm, and, since 1621, Livonia with Riga, much of it taken from Poland-Lithuania. In 1645 the Treaty of Brömsebro had added Gotland, Ösel, and the Norwegian provinces of Jämtland and Härjedalen, together with Halland for thirty years, at Denmark-Norway's expense. Denmark-Norway, a composite monarchy united under the Oldenburg dynasty, still controlled the Sound, the narrow strait through which Baltic trade passed, and collected tolls there, but it had lost ground in the Baltic and was a weakened rival.

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth had been created by the Union of Lublin in 1569, which joined the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania in a single political community with a common elected king and a common Sejm, while preserving separate laws, treasuries, and armies. Its late medieval origins are treated in Chapter 54. In the first half of the seventeenth century it was one of the largest states in Europe by area, extending from the Baltic coast and Royal Prussia to the Dnieper, and it contained Catholics, Orthodox Christians, Greek Catholics, Protestants, Jews, and Muslim Tatar settlers. To the east, Muscovy had emerged from the Time of Troubles in the early seventeenth century under the new Romanov dynasty, and its tsars were rebuilding central authority. To the south, the Ottoman Empire, whose expansion into southeastern Europe is traced in Chapter 41, ruled the Balkans and the Danubian lands, claimed suzerainty over the Crimean Khanate and the principalities of Moldavia, Wallachia, and Transylvania, and was a participant in the diplomacy of central and eastern Europe.

Three general forces shaped the century that followed. The first was military and fiscal competition of the kind analyzed in Chapter 74: states that could mobilize men, grain, metal, and money for prolonged campaigns gained influence, while those that could not either reformed or became objects of other powers' policy. The second was the uneven weight of noble privilege. In some realms, such as Sweden after 1680 and Denmark-Norway after 1660, crowns curtailed the political power and landed wealth of nobles. In Russia, rulers bound nobles more tightly to state service while expanding their control over peasants. In Poland-Lithuania, nobles protected their liberties against the crown and, ultimately, against efforts to build a more effective state. The third force was the shifting geography of trade and strategic access, as control of Baltic ports and, later, northern Black Sea coasts became a prize.

Calendars. This chapter uses the Gregorian calendar (New Style) unless stated otherwise. Russia used the Julian calendar (Old Style) throughout this period, which ran ten days behind in the seventeenth century and eleven days behind in the eighteenth. Poland-Lithuania had adopted the Gregorian calendar in the sixteenth century. Denmark-Norway adopted it in 1700. Sweden used a variant calendar between 1700 and 1712, returned to the Julian calendar, and adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1753. The Ottoman state used the Islamic lunar Hijri calendar alongside a fiscal solar calendar. The Battle of Poltava, for example, occurred on June 27, 1709 Old Style, which is July 8, 1709 New Style.

The evidence base requires caution at each step. State treaties are essential, but they record the claims of those who negotiated them, and Cossacks, peasants, towns, and many subject communities were frequently absent from the table. Chronicles and memoirs were often composed for patrons or later political causes. Nineteenth and twentieth century national historiographies, whether Swedish, Polish, Ukrainian, Russian, Turkish, Balkan, or Habsburg, have shaped what was preserved, translated, and emphasized, and they sometimes read modern borders and identities back into the period. This chapter follows the principle, set out in the series rules, that later national borders and identities must not be treated as though they governed the political choices of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Section 79.1

Sweden as a Baltic Great Power after 1648

After Westphalia, Sweden occupied a position out of proportion to its population and resources. The kingdom, together with Finland, probably contained well under two million people around 1650, in a harsh northern environment with a short growing season, yet it ruled territory on the southern and eastern shores of the Baltic and in the Holy Roman Empire. Contemporaries and later historians described this as a Baltic empire, and some Swedish statesmen spoke of the sea as a Swedish lake, although the phrase overstates what the crown actually controlled. Sweden's imperial position rested on military reputation, on revenues from conquered ports and tolls, on subsidies from allies such as France, and on a system that allowed the crown to alienate or lease royal lands and revenues to great nobles in exchange for service and credit.

Swedish government at mid century was a partnership among a monarch, a high nobility, and four estates. The Riksdag consisted of the nobility, the clergy, the burghers, and the peasants, the last being a distinctive feature that set Sweden apart from most of Europe, where peasants had no formal political representation. The peasant estate comprised freeholding farmers and crown tenants, not the large servile population found in much of eastern Europe. At the same time, the high nobility dominated the Council of the Realm and the great offices, and the Chancellor Axel Oxenstierna, who had guided Swedish policy through the Thirty Years' War, remained influential until his death in 1654. Queen Christina, who had come of age in 1644, rewarded supporters with land and titles at a pace that alarmed the other estates, and the Riksdag complained repeatedly about the erosion of crown revenues. In 1654 she abdicated, later converted to Catholicism, and lived mostly in Rome, leaving the throne to her cousin Charles X Gustav of the Palatinate-Zweibrücken line.

Charles X Gustav inherited a state with large ambitions and chronic fiscal weakness. His solution, in the tradition of Swedish war making, was to carry war abroad and to make it pay for itself. In July 1655 he invaded the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, whose king, John II Casimir, still claimed the Swedish crown through the Vasa dynasty. Swedish forces advanced with astonishing speed, taking Warsaw and Kraków and exposing the weakness of the Commonwealth's defenses, an episode examined in Section 79.7 from the Commonwealth's point of view. The invasion, however, produced a patriotic revival in Poland-Lithuania and alarmed neighboring powers. Russia declared war on Sweden in 1656, Denmark-Norway moved to exploit Swedish overextension, and the Dutch Republic, anxious about Swedish control of Baltic trade routes, supported Denmark. The Elector of Brandenburg, Frederick William, changed sides more than once, extracting concessions from both Sweden and the Commonwealth, including recognition of his sovereignty in Ducal Prussia.

Charles then turned on Denmark-Norway. In the winter of 1657 to 1658, after Denmark declared war, his army crossed the frozen straits to Zealand, and the Treaty of Roskilde of February 1658 compelled the Danish crown to cede the provinces of Scania, Halland, Blekinge, and Bohuslän, together with the island of Bornholm and the Norwegian region of Trøndelag. These transfers gave Sweden the eastern shore of the Sound and made the Swedish realm geographically continuous across the southern tip of the Scandinavian peninsula. Charles, however, was not content, and in the summer of 1658 he renewed the war and besieged Copenhagen. The Danes held, helped by their navy, by the determination of the capital's burghers, and by Dutch naval intervention that broke the blockade. Charles X Gustav died unexpectedly in February 1660, and his regents negotiated the Treaty of Copenhagen in May 1660, under which Bornholm and Trøndelag returned to Denmark-Norway while Scania and the other eastern provinces remained Swedish. The Treaty of Oliva with Poland-Lithuania followed in the same year and confirmed Swedish possession of Livonia, while John Casimir renounced his claim to the Swedish throne.

The effect of the Northern Wars was to make Sweden larger and more exposed. The acquisition of Scania gave Sweden a long land border with Denmark-Norway that would be tested repeatedly, and the former Danish provinces, whose population spoke Danish dialects, had to be integrated through a deliberate policy of Swedification in law, church, and education. The great powers, led by the Dutch and the English, had shown that they would not allow a single power to dominate both shores of the Sound. Sweden therefore had to maintain an expensive military establishment in several theaters, in the Baltic provinces, in Swedish Pomerania, and against Denmark-Norway, and the crown's regents who governed after 1660 for the child king Charles XI proved unable to finance it without damaging the realm's finances.

Ruling a Baltic empire was an administrative challenge as well as a military one. The Baltic provinces of Livonia and Estonia retained their German-speaking noble corporations, their law, and their Lutheran church, whose privileges Sweden had confirmed when it took the provinces. Riga, the largest port in the eastern Baltic, remained a mercantile city with its own council and trading networks. Finland, which was an integral part of the realm and not a colony, sent representatives to the Riksdag and was governed through Swedish law, though its peasants carried heavy burdens of taxation and recruitment. The Swedish crown also held Swedish Pomerania, Wismar, and Bremen-Verden, whose status as imperial fiefs gave Sweden a vote in the Imperial Diet and a lever in German politics, but also entangled it in imperial disputes. The empire was thus a mosaic of territories with different laws and loyalties rather than a unified state, and it depended on a small population and a relatively poor home economy whose major exports were iron, copper, timber, and naval stores.

Evidence for the Swedish state in this period is abundant. The crown's land registers and accounts, the minutes of the Council and Riksdag, and the parish records that later multiplied under the church law of 1686 allow historians to reconstruct population, taxation, and military recruitment in unusual detail. These sources must still be read with attention to their purpose, since many were created by a state that wished to count resources for war. They say far less about the inner lives of peasants or about the Sámi and Finnish communities in the north, whose experience of the Swedish state is better documented through tax, court, and missionary records than through their own words.

Section 79.2

Charles XI, Swedish Absolutism, and the Reorganization of Royal Power

Charles XI was five years old when his father died, and Sweden was governed by a regency dominated by great nobles, among them Magnus Gabriel De la Gardie, who held the chancellorship. The regency's policy relied on French subsidies and on continued grants of crown revenues to the nobility, and its management of the finances became the subject of mounting criticism. The king assumed personal rule in 1672, and in 1675 Sweden, bound to France by subsidy, invaded Brandenburg. The campaign went badly. The Swedish army was defeated at Fehrbellin in June 1675, and Brandenburg, Denmark-Norway, and the Dutch Republic joined an anti-Swedish coalition in what became the Scanian War of 1675 to 1679. Danish forces invaded Scania, and the war there became a bitter conflict in which local Scanian irregulars, known as the snapphanar, resisted Swedish rule. Charles XI himself led the army at Lund in December 1676, a hard fought battle in which Swedish forces defeated the Danes with heavy losses on both sides, and a further Swedish victory at Landskrona in 1677 secured Scania. Sweden's position was saved less by its own strength than by the diplomacy of Louis XIV, whose victories forced Sweden's enemies to restore most of their conquests in the peace settlements of 1679, a consequence of the wider European war examined in Chapter 76.

The war demonstrated that the Swedish state could not sustain its commitments with the existing arrangement of land, revenue, and noble service. It also produced a moment of political opportunity. Large parts of the nobility, some of whom were blamed for the military failures and for the corruption of the regency, had lost credibility, and the other three estates, the clergy, burghers, and peasants, wanted relief from taxation that the nobility, which held tax exempt land, did not share. The Riksdag of 1680 became the instrument through which Charles XI reorganized royal power. It authorized a reduction (reduktion), the recovery by the crown of lands and revenues that had been alienated to nobles since the 1650s, and subsequent reductions extended this principle further back and into the Baltic provinces. The Riksdag also declared, in effect, that the king was not bound to govern through the Council of the Realm, and in 1693 it formally recognized him as an absolute sovereign responsible to God alone.

The reduction transformed the distribution of land. Historians estimate that by the middle of the seventeenth century the nobility had come to hold roughly two thirds of the land in Sweden proper, including crown lands that had been granted or pledged, and that the reduction reduced this share to a much smaller proportion, while restoring a very large part of the realm's land and income to the crown. These estimates depend on how land is measured and on the definition of what counts as noble land, and Swedish scholars have debated the precise numbers. What is not in doubt is that the reduction ended the policy of financing the state through alienation of its own resources and created a revenue base on which the crown could place a standing military organization. It did not abolish the nobility, which continued to supply most of the officers and senior officials, but it ended its dominance of the Council and its wealth as a political class.

The new revenue underpinned the allotment system (indelningsverket), which assigned the support of regiments to defined districts. Under the system, groups of farms known as rotar jointly maintained a soldier, providing him with a cottage, a plot of land, and a regular supply of goods, while cavalry were supported by larger holdings. Soldiers lived in their communities, trained at set periods, and were mobilized in wartime. The system created a standing army of considerable size at relatively modest cost, tied military organization to the agrarian economy, and gave the crown a reliable source of manpower. It was not an easy burden for peasants, who had to support their soldiers and bear the loss of labor in wartime, but it was largely a negotiated and regulated system, and it was less arbitrary than conscription in many other states.

The reorganization extended to other institutions. The crown placed administration in the hands of collegial boards staffed by professional officials, the church was regulated by the church law of 1686, which also supported parish record keeping and a degree of mass literacy through compulsory catechism examinations, and a reorganized navy based at Karlskrona, the new port founded in the 1680s in Blekinge, provided a southern base. A new legal commission reformed the law code. These measures strengthened the state's capacity to collect revenue and mobilize soldiers, and Charles XI's reign is often treated as the high point of the Swedish military state. The system still had limits. In the 1690s a series of harvest failures, the worst of them in 1695 to 1697, caused a famine that killed a large proportion of the population in Finland, with estimates ranging from roughly a fifth to a third of its inhabitants and lower but still substantial losses in parts of Sweden. This catastrophe, one of the worst in early modern Europe, showed the vulnerability of a northern agrarian society even under a well run state.

Finland and the Baltic provinces experienced these changes differently. The reduction in Livonia, applied from the 1680s, struck at the landholding of the local German-speaking nobility, and it produced a political crisis. Johann Reinhold Patkul, a Livonian nobleman who led the protest, fled Sweden and later became an adviser to Augustus II of Saxony and Poland and to Peter I of Russia, an instance of how the grievances of a Baltic elite could be exploited by Sweden's rivals. In Finland, the reduction was less contentious and the allotment system took hold, producing a Finnish soldiery that fought in most of Sweden's wars. In both regions the consequences of the late seventeenth century were not simply the extension of a central state but a negotiation among crown, local elites, church, and peasants, whose outcome depended on circumstances.

Historical Vocabulary
Scandinavian Absolutism and the Riksdag

Absolutism in this chapter refers to a system in which the monarch claimed sovereign authority not subject to the consent of representative bodies, and in which the monarch, not noble councils, directed taxation, administration, and war. In Sweden after 1680, it was established through the Riksdag's own acts and without abolishing the Riksdag. In Denmark-Norway, it was written into a fundamental law, the King's Law of 1665, and the estates ceased to meet in any significant way. The Riksdag of the Estates was the Swedish assembly of four estates, the nobility, the clergy, the burghers, and the peasants, which voted taxes and advised the crown, and which gained far greater authority after 1719. Neither term maps neatly onto modern concepts. Absolutism did not mean unlimited arbitrary power in practice, since kings depended on officials, the church, law, and local cooperation, and the Riksdag was not a modern parliament elected by all citizens.

Section 79.3

Denmark-Norway, Hereditary Absolutism, and Northern Maritime Competition

Denmark-Norway presented a very different constitutional outcome from Sweden's. It was a composite monarchy in which the kingdoms of Denmark and Norway, together with the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein and Atlantic dependencies including Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and Greenland, were ruled by a single king of the House of Oldenburg. Denmark was the political center, and its capital, Copenhagen, controlled the Sound. Norway, though subordinate in practice, had its own law, its own governor in the form of a royal stattholder, and its own social structure, since it had few great nobles and a peasantry that was largely free.

Before 1660 the Danish monarchy had been bound by a coronation charter (håndfæstning) negotiated with the Council of the Realm (Rigsråd), dominated by great noble families who monopolized the main offices and owned much of the land, and who enjoyed tax exemption. This arrangement had come under strain. Danish defeat in the Thirty Years' War intervention examined in Chapter 67 and the losses to Sweden in 1645 and 1658 discredited the nobility, who had controlled military leadership, and the royal finances were in disarray. The crisis of 1658 to 1660 resolved it. After Roskilde and the siege of Copenhagen, the Danish crown owed an enormous debt to its supporters, and the capital's burghers and the clergy, who had been excluded from power, saw their opportunity.

When an assembly of estates met in Copenhagen in 1660, it became a venue for conflict between the nobility, who wanted to preserve their exemption from taxation, and the clergy and burghers, who rejected a tax burden that fell disproportionately on them. Frederick III, with the backing of influential officials including the Copenhagen mayor Hans Nansen and the bishop Hans Wandal, allied with the non noble estates. Over the autumn the estates, with the nobility outmaneuvered, declared the crown hereditary and handed the king full powers to rewrite the constitution. In October 1660 the king received their oath, and the nobility accepted the outcome, partly under pressure from troops and city militia. The change was a revolution from above, supported by non noble groups, that gave the king the right to determine the form of government.

The King's Law (Kongeloven), signed on November 14, 1665, set the new constitution in writing. It declared the king to be the supreme head on earth, above all human laws and accountable to God alone, and gave him full authority to make and change laws, to appoint and dismiss officials, to declare war and peace, and to levy taxes. It tied the king to the Lutheran confession, specifically the Augsburg Confession, prohibited the division of the realm, provided for hereditary succession and rules of regency, and in effect precluded any role for the estates in government. It was kept secret and not published until 1709, which shows both the king's confidence in the system and a recognition that the underlying political settlement relied on practice more than on public proclamation. It remained the formal basis of Danish government until 1849. Historians sometimes describe it as the most thoroughly written absolutist constitution in Europe, and it contrasts with France, where Louis XIV's authority rested on custom, legal tradition, and theory but not on a single founding document (a subject treated in Chapter 76).

The new system rested on a restructured elite. The Council of the Realm was replaced by collegial boards run by royal appointees, and rank at court was based on a new system of titles, with the creation of counts and barons from loyal families and the admission of educated commoners and foreign administrators, among them Germans, into the service state. Peder Schumacher, ennobled as Griffenfeld, rose from a merchant family to become the chief minister and a principal architect of the system before his fall in 1676. The law codes issued under Christian V, the Danish Law of 1683 and the Norwegian Law of 1687, systematized legal practice in each kingdom, and in Norway the crown strengthened royal administration while allowing the preservation of its own legal tradition. Norwegian officials, merchants, and sawmill owners gained opportunities under absolutism, but the kingdom remained subordinate to Copenhagen in finance and policy.

Denmark-Norway's economy rested on agriculture, the Sound tolls, shipping, fishing, and overseas commerce. The tolls collected at Helsingør, protected by the fortress of Kronborg, brought the crown a major portion of its income, and they were a constant source of conflict with the Dutch, the English, and Sweden, which was exempted from them as a result of earlier treaties. The kingdom exported cattle and grain, the Danish estates producing for the north European market, while Norway sent timber, fish, and iron to the Dutch and British markets, and the great timber and shipping trade made Norwegian ports and sawmills increasingly important. The Danish crown also sought colonies, establishing a presence at Tranquebar in India, forts on the Gold Coast, and the island of St. Thomas in the Caribbean, where it entered the Atlantic slave economy discussed in Chapter 70. These enterprises were modest in scale compared with those of the Dutch and the English, but they involved Danish and Norwegian merchants and sailors in the system of coerced labor and trade.

The social consequences of absolutism varied by region. In Denmark, peasants were bound to estates under the system of adscription introduced in 1733, the stavnsbånd, which tied men of working age to the estate on which they were born and which was a response to the demands of the army and the landowners. In Norway, peasants were mainly freeholders, and the sale of crown estates in the later seventeenth and early eighteenth century increased peasant ownership in some regions. Absolutism thus strengthened the hand of the crown and enlarged the power of the administrative elite, but it left the privileges of the landowning nobility in rural society largely intact, and it did not bring social equality. Competition with Sweden remained a defining feature of Danish policy, and unresolved disputes in Schleswig and Holstein, where the ducal house of Holstein-Gottorp allied with Sweden against the Danish crown, ensured further wars. These tensions, together with the Danish bid in 1700 to restore losses in the Great Northern War, are the subject of the next section.

Section 79.4

The Great Northern War, 1700–1721, and Sweden's Age of Liberty

The Great Northern War arose from the ambitions of several rulers who saw an opportunity in the accession of a very young Swedish king. Charles XII, who became king in 1697 at the age of fifteen, appeared a weak ruler of a vast and resented empire. In 1699 and 1700 a coalition took shape. Augustus II, Elector of Saxony and King of Poland-Lithuania since 1697, wished to take Livonia and strengthen his hereditary position, helped by Patkul's appeal. Frederick IV of Denmark-Norway wished to regain lost territory and to resolve the dispute over Holstein-Gottorp, and Peter I of Russia wished to gain a Baltic outlet and a stronger position among the powers. Each had distinct aims, and the alliance remained unstable throughout the war.

Charles XII struck first. In the summer of 1700, with the support of English and Dutch naval squadrons that preferred to keep the Sound open, he landed an army near Copenhagen and compelled Denmark-Norway to make peace at Travendal in August. He then moved against the Russians who had besieged Narva. On November 30, 1700 (New Style), a Swedish army much smaller than its opponent attacked the Russian camp in a snowstorm and destroyed it, capturing much of the artillery and many officers. The victory at Narva made Charles famous and persuaded him that he could defeat his enemies one by one. He turned south against Augustus II and spent the next six years in Poland-Lithuania, defeating Saxon forces, installing Stanisław Leszczyński as a Swedish backed king in 1704, and finally invading Saxony and forcing Augustus to renounce the Polish crown in the Treaty of Altranstädt of 1706.

Peter I used the years of Swedish absence to rebuild. He reorganized the army along European lines, relying on newly raised regiments, and he secured the Neva region of Ingria, where in May 1703 he founded Saint Petersburg on marshy ground at the Neva's mouth, a fortress and later a capital built with forced labor and at great human cost, as Section 79.11 explains. In 1708, Charles XII invaded Russia, hoping to compel Peter to make peace. He was delayed by the loss of a supply convoy at Lesnaya, harassed by scorched earth tactics, and drawn into Ukraine, where the Cossack hetman Ivan Mazepa, who had grown resentful of Peter's demands, joined him with a small following. Most Cossacks did not follow Mazepa, and the Russian army sacked his capital at Baturyn. On July 8, 1709 (New Style), at Poltava, the Swedish army, weakened by a hard winter, depleted in numbers, and with a wounded king, attacked a well prepared Russian position and was defeated. Charles fled with a few men into the Ottoman Empire, while the remainder of his army surrendered at Perevolochna a few days later.

Poltava did not end the war but it changed its character. Denmark-Norway, Saxony-Poland, and Prussia and Hanover, at first neutral, now joined or exploited the campaign against Sweden. Charles lived for years at Bender in the Ottoman Empire and persuaded the Ottomans to declare war on Russia in 1710, which resulted in the Russian disaster on the Pruth in 1711. He left the Ottoman Empire in 1714 and returned to Swedish Pomerania, where he held out until Stralsund fell in 1715. Russia, in the meantime, conquered Livonia, Estonia, and most of Finland, defeated a Swedish squadron at Gangut in 1714, and raided the Swedish coast. Charles XII died on November 30, 1718 (New Style) while besieging the Norwegian fortress of Fredriksten. The circumstances of his death, from a single shot in the trench, have been debated since, and some contemporaries and later writers speculated that a Swedish hand fired the shot, but no conclusive evidence supports an assassination.

The settlements that ended the war carved up the Swedish empire. Sweden ceded Bremen and Verden to Hanover in 1719, ceded Stettin and part of Pomerania to Prussia in 1720, and agreed in the Peace of Frederiksborg in 1720 to give up its exemption from the Sound tolls and to recognize Danish control of the ducal part of Schleswig. The most consequential agreement was the Treaty of Nystad, concluded on September 10, 1721 (New Style) at Uusikaupunki in Finland. Sweden ceded to Russia Livonia, Estonia, Ingria, the Karelian district of Kexholm, and the islands of Ösel and Dagö, retaining Finland, which Russia had occupied and now returned, and Russia paid two million silver rixdollars. The treaty confirmed the existing privileges of the Baltic German nobility and towns in the ceded provinces and guaranteed Lutheran worship. For Russia, it provided the fruit of Peter's war, a window on the Baltic, access to ports including Riga, Reval (Tallinn), and Narva, and the central position on the sea that its rulers had sought.

Sweden's constitution was recast in the aftermath of Charles XII's death. The king left no children, and the Riksdag, meeting in 1719, elected his sister Ulrika Eleonora as queen on condition that she accept a new constitution, the Instrument of Government of 1719, which sharply limited royal authority and affirmed the decisive role of the estates. When Ulrika Eleonora abdicated in favor of her husband, Frederick of Hesse, who was crowned as Frederick I in 1720, a revised Instrument of Government of 1720 confirmed the new balance. The constitution made the Council responsible to the Riksdag, gave the king only a limited role in the Council's decisions, provided that the Riksdag was to meet regularly, and made the Riksdag's Secret Committee, dominated by the nobility, an important body. The reorganization marked a decisive break with the absolutism of Charles XI and Charles XII.

The era from 1719 to 1772, known as the Age of Liberty (frihetstiden), saw an active political life by the standards of the time. From the 1730s the Riksdag was divided between two factions, the Hats, who favored an assertive foreign policy, close relations with France, and mercantilist support for manufacturing, and the Caps, who favored peace, fiscal restraint, and Russia as a neighbor to be placated. The Hats' bid to recover territory led Sweden into a war with Russia in 1741 to 1743 that ended in defeat and the Treaty of Åbo, and Sweden entered the Pomeranian War against Prussia in 1757 as part of the Seven Years' War, with poor results. The period was also one of intellectual and scientific achievement, with figures such as Carl Linnaeus, Emanuel Swedenborg, and Anders Celsius, and of administrative development. The Freedom of the Press Act of 1766, shaped by thinkers including Anders Chydenius, abolished most censorship and provided for public access to official documents, an early statement of principles that later became important in Swedish political culture.

Foreign subsidies from France, Russia, and Britain, however, flowed to factions in the Riksdag, and political competition was susceptible to corruption and external interference. The structure also remained restricted. Voting and representation rested on the four estates, with the nobility, clergy, and burghers, and to some degree the peasants, taking part, but a large portion of the population had no voice, including most women, the landless, and the poor. In August 1772, Gustav III, supported by officers and by popular frustration with factional politics, staged a coup that restored much royal authority, ending the Age of Liberty. Whether that era should be understood as a genuine step toward constitutional government or as a shift of power among privileged groups is the subject of the debate below.

Historical Debate 1

Did Sweden's Age of Liberty Establish Meaningful Constitutional Government?

The question: Was the constitutional order of 1719 to 1772 a real step toward limited, representative government, or did it simply move power from a king to a group of privileged estates and elites?

Interpretation A — A meaningful constitutional experiment: Some historians stress that the Riksdag held real authority over taxation and legislation, that the Council was responsible to the estates, that competing parties debated policy publicly, that the Freedom of the Press Act of 1766 was an early guarantee of openness, and that the period saw intellectual and administrative vitality. In this view the Age of Liberty belongs to the longer history of constitutional and parliamentary government in Europe, and it was a serious alternative to the absolutism of its neighbors.
Interpretation B — A redistribution among privileged groups: Others emphasize that the franchise was limited to estates, that the nobility and the bureaucracy dominated the committees that mattered, that factions depended heavily on foreign subsidies, that the system did not produce stable policy and failed to protect Sweden from further defeat, and that the language of liberty described the freedom of corporate bodies and not equal political rights. On this reading, the label of liberty should be understood within the political vocabulary of the period and not as a precursor of modern democracy.

Evidence: Riksdag minutes and committee records, the instruments themselves, diplomatic correspondence recording foreign payments, the 1766 press law, and the political writings and satires of the period.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians compare Sweden with Britain and the Dutch Republic (Chapter 77), and whether they treat constitutional change as linear development or as an episode shaped by military defeat and elite interests.

Section 79.5

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth: Elective Monarchy, Noble Liberty, and Political Institutions

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth (in Polish, Rzeczpospolita, a word that translates the Latin res publica) was neither a modern nation state nor merely a Polish kingdom with a Lithuanian appendage. It was a union of two distinct political bodies, the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland, whose territories included Greater and Lesser Poland, Royal Prussia, and, since the Union of Lublin of 1569, the Ukrainian lands of Podolia, Volhynia, Bratslav, and Kyiv, and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, a large realm whose population was largely Ruthenian (the ancestors of modern Belarusians and Ukrainians) and Lithuanian. The two states shared a king, a Sejm, and a foreign policy, but they kept separate treasuries, armies, officials, and legal systems. Lithuania's Statutes, a major legal code, remained in force. The union was also a union of confessions and languages. Polish became increasingly dominant among the elite, and the Ruthenian written language that had served the Grand Duchy's chancery yielded to Polish, a change completed in the late seventeenth century.

The Commonwealth's king was elected. After the extinction of the Jagiellonian dynasty in 1572, the nobility established the practice of free election (wolna elekcja), in which the whole noble estate was entitled to gather in the field near Warsaw to choose a king. Each ruler had to accept the Henrician Articles of 1573, a set of fundamental principles that required the king to call the Sejm regularly, to respect religious toleration, to consult a council of senators, and to accept that subjects might be released from obedience if he violated the law, and each king further negotiated particular promises, the pacta conventa. The monarch commanded few resources of his own beyond royal estates. He held the right to appoint senators and officials, to distribute land and offices, and to conduct diplomacy, but taxation and legislation required the Sejm. The Commonwealth was, in short, a mixed constitution, in which monarchy, the Senate, and the chamber of deputies were meant to balance each other, and contemporaries often called it a republic with a king.

The Sejm consisted of the king, the Senate (made up of bishops, regional governors known as voivodes, castellans, and the great officers of state), and the chamber of deputies, whose members were elected by local noble assemblies called sejmiks. These sejmiks were central to political life. They chose deputies, instructed them on how to vote, and administered local government and taxation, and they gave provincial nobles a direct political role that many other European estates lacked. Lithuania held its own sejmiks and a Lithuanian tribunal, and the Crown had its own tribunal. The system aimed to protect noble rights against royal absolutism, a goal that Polish-Lithuanian nobles associated with the kingdom of the Danish crown and the France of Louis XIV and sought to avoid.

The szlachta, the legally privileged nobility, comprised perhaps six to eight percent of the population, a much larger proportion than in western Europe, though estimates vary and the share was higher in regions such as Mazovia. It ranged from the great magnate families, who held vast estates, held the highest offices, and kept clients, retainers, and private armies, to impoverished nobles who worked as stewards or farmed small plots and whose political voice was often directed by a patron. Legally, all nobles were equal, and they held a series of liberties: personal inviolability (neminem captivabimus, the principle that no noble could be imprisoned without a court verdict), exemption from most taxes, the right to political participation, and the right to own land with peasants. In practice, however, formal equality coexisted with deep economic and social inequality, and noble liberty was not political equality for the population as a whole.

The liberum veto, a principle that any single deputy could block legislation and force the dissolution of the Sejm, was first used in 1652, when the deputy Władysław Siciński broke up the session. The rule arose from an understanding that the Sejm should act by consensus as a gathering of equals, and for decades it was used sparingly. Over time, however, foreign powers and magnates discovered that a deputy could be bribed or pressured to dissolve a session, and the practice paralyzed decision making in many Sejms of the later seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. Alternatives, including confederations, which were armed alliances of nobles acting outside the Sejm for a defined purpose and which could pass binding decisions by majority, developed as a means of acting when the Sejm failed. Historians disagree about how serious the paralysis was, and about whether it owed more to the veto itself or to the behavior of those who exploited it, a question taken up in the debate in Section 79.8.

The Commonwealth's religious and social composition complicated the picture of noble liberty. The Warsaw Confederation of 1573 had guaranteed peace among confessions, and the Commonwealth sheltered Catholics, Lutherans, Calvinists, Orthodox Christians, Greek Catholics (who accepted papal authority but retained Eastern rites after the Union of Brest of 1596), Jews, and Muslim Tatars. Toleration, however, narrowed over the seventeenth century as Catholic confessionalization advanced. The Polish Brethren, or Arians, a radical Protestant group, were expelled in 1658 to 1660, and legislation of the 1650s to 1690s restricted the rights of Protestants and Orthodox. Jews lived under royal and noble protection and, from the sixteenth century, developed their own institutions of self government, including local communal councils (kahals) and a representative body, the Council of the Four Lands, which allocated taxes and negotiated with authorities. Towns were generally subordinate to nobles in political life, with the exception of the great Prussian cities such as Gdańsk (Danzig), which enjoyed autonomy and commercial weight, as the main outlet for the grain exports on which noble estates depended.

Peasants, who made up the large majority of the population, were subject to a heavy labor system. On the estates of the Polish and Lithuanian nobility, the demesne farm (folwark) produced grain for export, and peasants owed labor dues (pańszczyzna) that grew over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries from a few days per week to higher burdens in many districts, while they had limited legal recourse against lords. This system, sometimes called the second serfdom, is discussed further in Section 79.19. The Commonwealth's political institutions thus protected the freedoms of one social group while its economic structure depended on the dependence of another.

Historical Vocabulary
Szlachta, Sejm, and Liberum Veto

The szlachta was the noble estate of the Commonwealth, with a legally recognized set of liberties and a collective political identity as the political nation. The word is often translated as gentry, but this can mislead, since many members were wealthy magnates and many were poor. The Sejm was the national parliament, with the king, the Senate, and an elected chamber of deputies, and it depended on local sejmiks for its members. The liberum veto was the principle, first applied in 1652, that a single deputy's objection could end a session and void its decisions. None of these terms implies democracy in the modern sense: the political nation was a privileged minority, and the Commonwealth's constitutional liberties coexisted with serfdom, restricted town rights, and religious inequalities.

Section 79.6

The Khmelnytsky Uprising, the Cossack Hetmanate, and the Eastern Borderlands, 1648–1667

The southeastern part of the Commonwealth, the lands of Kyiv, Bratslav, and Podolia, were in the seventeenth century a frontier zone. Settlement had expanded rapidly after the Union of Lublin, brought by magnates such as the Wiśniowiecki and Potocki families who colonized the fertile steppe edge with peasant labor, and the region lay adjacent to the Crimean Khanate, whose raiders attacked for slaves and plunder. Its population was mostly Ruthenian and Orthodox, and its elite had increasingly adopted Polish language and Catholic or Uniate religion, producing tensions of language, class, and faith. The Cossacks, a community of free warriors of mixed origin who lived along the Dnieper, hunted, fished, raided, and sometimes served the crown, were a particular force. The Zaporozhian Host, organized around a fortified camp (the Sich) below the Dnieper rapids, elected its own leaders, while a smaller group of Cossacks listed on the crown's register received pay for military service. The Commonwealth wished to limit the register and to treat other Cossacks as peasants subject to lords, while the Cossacks defended their status as free warriors.

Bohdan Khmelnytsky, a Cossack officer of minor noble background who had personal grievances against a Polish noble and who had served in the register, escaped to the Sich in early 1648 and was elected hetman. The Cossack rising of that spring was strengthened by an alliance that Khmelnytsky forged with the Crimean Khan, Islam III Giray, whose cavalry under Tugay Bey fought beside the Cossacks. In May 1648 Khmelnytsky defeated Commonwealth forces at Zhovti Vody and Korsun, and in September he won again at Pyliavtsi. These victories coincided with the death of King Władysław IV in May 1648, which left the Commonwealth without a king at the critical moment. Peasants, townspeople, and some lower clergy joined the rising, and it became a social and religious conflict as much as a Cossack revolt, targeting Polish nobles, Catholic clergy, and Jewish leaseholders and townspeople, who in many areas were associated with noble estate management.

The violence against Jews and Poles was severe. Jewish communities in towns such as Nemyriv and Tulchyn, as well as many smaller settlements, were attacked and many members were killed, and Cossack and peasant forces also massacred Catholic clergy and Polish noble families, while Commonwealth forces and noble armies carried out reprisals against Cossack and peasant communities. Estimates of Jewish deaths vary widely. Older estimates running above one hundred thousand are now generally regarded as too high, and many modern historians prefer figures in the tens of thousands, while acknowledging that the sources, which include Jewish chronicles such as the memoir of Nathan Hannover, were composed for commemorative purposes and often rely on rumor. The events left a deep imprint on Jewish historical memory, and they also remain a subject of contested interpretation in Ukrainian, Polish, and Jewish historiography.

Khmelnytsky's aims evolved. At first he demanded an expanded Cossack register, the restoration of Cossack privileges, and the protection of Orthodox religion. As the rising succeeded, and especially after the Treaty of Zboriv of August 1649 with the new king, John II Casimir, recognized a Cossack territory under the hetman's authority in the palatinates of Kyiv, Bratslav, and Chernihiv, he began to build a political structure, the Cossack Hetmanate, with its own administration, army, regiments, and court. Renewed war in 1651 led to the Commonwealth's victory at Berestechko, where Khmelnytsky's Crimean allies withdrew, and to the more restrictive Treaty of Bila Tserkva. The hetman sought new protectors. Appeals to the Ottoman sultan and to Moscow, whose tsar Alexis was Orthodox, were part of this search, along with negotiations with Transylvania and Sweden.

In January 1654, at Pereiaslav, Cossack leaders and representatives of Tsar Alexis swore agreements that brought the Hetmanate under the tsar's protection. The meaning of this event remains contested. Muscovite officials understood the Cossacks as taking an oath of loyalty to the tsar, as subjects of a sovereign who granted them privileges, and in later Russian and Soviet historiography the event was presented as a reunification of Ukraine with Russia, an interpretation that was officially celebrated in 1954 on its three hundredth anniversary. Many Ukrainian historians, in contrast, have emphasized that the Cossack leaders regarded it as a military alliance or a protectorate that preserved the Hetmanate's rights and autonomy, in line with Khmelnytsky's earlier arrangements with other powers, and that the tsar's officials soon began to disregard those terms. The original documents are incomplete and the parties' understandings differed, so the most careful approach is to describe the agreement as an unequal arrangement that each side interpreted according to its own political culture and interests.

The agreement drew Muscovy into war with the Commonwealth (1654 to 1667). The Muscovite army took Smolensk in 1654 and advanced into Lithuania, capturing Vilnius in 1655, and the Swedish invasion of the same year placed the Commonwealth in a desperate position. Khmelnytsky died in August 1657, and the Hetmanate fell into a period of factional struggle later remembered as the Ruin. His successor Ivan Vyhovsky, unwilling to remain under Muscovite pressure, negotiated the Union of Hadiach of 1658 with the Commonwealth, which proposed a Commonwealth of three equal partners, Poland, Lithuania, and the Grand Duchy of Ruthenia, with a Cossack hetman. The Polish Sejm adopted a reduced version in 1659, and the arrangement, opposed by many Cossacks and nobles and by Muscovy, was never implemented. Vyhovsky was overthrown, and Cossack elites divided between a Muscovite orientation on the left bank of the Dnieper and a Polish or other orientation on the right bank.

The Truce of Andrusovo, concluded early in 1667, ended the war without Cossack participation. It divided Ukraine along the Dnieper. The Commonwealth retained the right bank, with the exception of Kyiv, which Moscow was to hold for two years, and Muscovy received the left bank, with Smolensk and the Seversk lands, while the Zaporozhian Sich was placed under joint authority. The agreement was confirmed and made permanent in the Eternal Peace of 1686, when Russia held on to Kyiv and joined the anti Ottoman Holy League. The Cossack Hetmanate on the left bank continued to exist under the tsar's authority, with its own officers, law, and regiments, under hetmen such as Ivan Samoilovych and Ivan Mazepa (hetman 1687 to 1709), until it was abolished in stages in the eighteenth century, with the office itself ending under Catherine II in 1764. The settlement thus left a Ukrainian political entity that was divided, subordinated, and still politically active, not simply a territory transferred among states.

Section 79.7

The Deluge, the Wars of the Commonwealth, and John III Sobieski

The Swedish invasion of July 1655 added a northern front to a Commonwealth already at war with Cossacks, Tatars, and Muscovy. Swedish forces entered through Pomerania and Greater Poland, and the Polish nobility, divided and unready, offered little resistance. Within weeks, regional leaders in Greater Poland surrendered, Warsaw fell in August, and Kraków capitulated in October. John II Casimir fled into Silesia. Magnates in Lithuania, led by Janusz Radziwiłł, signed the Union of Kėdainiai with Sweden, seeking protection from Muscovy. Observers believed that the Commonwealth was finished, and Polish historical memory later called these years the Deluge (potop), a name popularized by the novelist Henryk Sienkiewicz in the nineteenth century, whose national romance shaped much later perception.

The Swedish occupation provoked a reaction. Heavy requisitions, plundering by soldiers, and Swedish disrespect for the Catholic church, including the siege of the Jasna Góra monastery at Częstochowa in November and December 1655, produced armed resistance. The monastery's successful defense became a symbol of national and religious revival. In December 1655 a confederation formed at Tyszowce, announced loyalty to John Casimir, and undertook a general rising, while the king returned from exile. Peasant and noble partisans harassed Swedish garrisons, and Charles X Gustav, who was occupied elsewhere, could not hold the country. Brandenburg-Prussia, which had allied with Sweden, switched sides and, in return, gained recognition of full sovereignty in Ducal Prussia in the treaties of Wehlau and Bromberg in 1657, a concession that strengthened a rising neighbor, as Chapter 78 has shown. Muscovy, meanwhile, made a truce with the Commonwealth in 1656 and turned against Sweden, and the Transylvanian prince George II Rákóczi intervened and was forced to withdraw.

The material consequences of the wars of 1648 to 1660 were grave. Towns were burned, manorial economies collapsed in some regions, crop production fell, epidemic disease followed the armies, and the population of the Commonwealth fell sharply. Estimates are uncertain, but many historians suggest that the population declined by something like a quarter to a third in the worst affected areas and by a significant share overall, and that recovery took decades. The state treasury was exhausted. The Treaty of Oliva of May 1660 ended the war with Sweden, confirming Swedish possession of Livonia north of the Daugava and John Casimir's renunciation of the Swedish throne, and the treaties of Oliva and Copenhagen, together with the later Andrusovo truce, mark the end of this phase of conflict. The Commonwealth had survived, but it had lost Smolensk and the left bank of Ukraine, and its prestige and finances were badly damaged.

Political weakness followed. John II Casimir tried to strengthen the monarchy by proposing reforms of the electoral system and the Sejm, including the election of a successor during his lifetime, an idea (vivente rege) that alarmed the nobility who feared the revival of hereditary royal rule. When the king's supporters pursued these proposals and moved against the magnate Jerzy Lubomirski, Lubomirski led an armed rebellion (rokosz) in 1665 to 1666, defeated royal forces at Mątwy in 1666, and the king abandoned his plans. He abdicated in 1668 and left for France. The episode demonstrated that powerful magnates could resist reform by force, and that the noble nation viewed royal attempts to improve state capacity as threats to liberty.

The following decade brought conflict with the Ottoman Empire. The Cossack Hetman on the right bank, Petro Doroshenko, accepted Ottoman protection in 1669, and in 1672 an Ottoman army under Sultan Mehmed IV invaded Podolia, taking the fortress of Kamianets-Podilskyi, and forced the Commonwealth to accept the Treaty of Buczacz, which ceded Podolia and recognized Ottoman suzerainty over Doroshenko's Cossacks and a tribute payment. The Sejm repudiated the treaty, and the Crown Hetman Jan Sobieski, who had served throughout these wars, won a victory over the Ottomans at Khotyn (Chocim) in November 1673. When King Michał Korybut Wiśniowiecki died soon afterwards, the nobility elected Sobieski as John III in 1674. He fought the Ottomans again at Lviv and Żurawno, ending in 1676 with a settlement that restored part of the lost territory but left Kamianets under Ottoman control.

Sobieski's foreign policy sought alliances against the Ottomans, though his early orientation was toward France, which had supported his election and which he hoped would assist the recovery of Prussian territory. By the early 1680s, however, the Habsburg emperor Leopold I was the Commonwealth's essential partner against the Ottoman advance, and in 1683 the two signed a treaty of mutual assistance. In the summer Sobieski led a Polish-Lithuanian army to Vienna, where, on September 12, 1683, his cavalry, with the imperial forces of Charles of Lorraine and German contingents, took part in the decisive assault on the Ottoman positions. The victory brought Sobieski prestige throughout Europe, and Pope Innocent XI celebrated it. Sobieski's later years were marred by disappointment, since his hope to secure the throne for his son and to regain the Ukrainian lands failed, and the Holy League campaigns of 1684 to 1699, in which Poland-Lithuania took part, brought only modest returns. By the Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699, the Commonwealth regained Podolia and Kamianets, but the cost in resources was high.

The Eternal Peace of 1686, in which Poland-Lithuania accepted the Russian hold on Kyiv and left the left bank in Muscovite hands in return for Russian participation in the Holy League, illustrated the Commonwealth's changing position. It was an acceptance of the situation after Andrusovo, and it also reflected a judgment by Sobieski that the Ottoman threat required Russian support. Whether it was wise remains debated, but the effect was to establish Russian power on the Dnieper and to commit the Commonwealth to a role in a central European war that served Habsburg priorities as much as its own.

Section 79.8

Saxon Kings, Foreign Intervention, Reform, and the First Partition of 1772

After Sobieski's death in 1696, the election of 1697 was contested between the Prince of Conti, supported by France, and Frederick Augustus, Elector of Saxony, who converted to Catholicism and was crowned as Augustus II with the help of money and an army. His reign (1697 to 1733) inaugurated the period of Saxon rule, in which the Wettin dynasty held the Commonwealth's throne alongside its hereditary electorate. Augustus hoped to use his position, and Saxon troops, to establish a stronger monarchy, and he joined the anti Swedish coalition of 1699 to 1700 in hopes of gaining Livonia. The result was disastrous for the Commonwealth. Charles XII's invasion, the installation of Stanisław Leszczyński as a rival king in 1704, the occupation by Swedish, Saxon, and Russian armies, and the plague of 1708 to 1711 devastated the country again. Augustus returned to the throne in 1709 after Poltava, but his authority rested on Russian support.

The nobility, resentful of Saxon troops and of the king's apparent absolutist intentions, formed the Confederation of Tarnogród in 1715, and a civil conflict was resolved by Russian mediation. The result was the so-called Silent Sejm of 1717, which in a single day approved a settlement that preserved noble constitutional privileges, provided a permanent fiscal basis for military expenditure, and set an establishment of roughly 24,000 payroll portions across the Crown and Lithuania. Because those portions also covered officers’ remuneration, actual soldiers numbered substantially fewer than the nominal establishment, leaving the Commonwealth much weaker militarily than its neighbors. The Russian envoy’s role as mediator established a pattern, although the Sejm did not formally appoint Russia as guarantor of its settlement: Russian diplomacy increasingly presented itself as defender of the Commonwealth's constitutional order, which in practice served Russian interests in keeping the neighbor weak.

The next royal election, in 1733, showed how deeply external powers could shape the Commonwealth's politics. After Augustus II's death, the majority of nobles elected Stanisław Leszczyński, whom France supported, but Russian troops entered, and a minority of nobles, supported by Russia and Austria, elected Augustus III, son of the late king. The resulting War of the Polish Succession (1733 to 1735), in which French and Spanish interests were engaged in western Europe and Italy, ended in Augustus III's recognition as king, Leszczyński's compensation with the duchy of Lorraine, and a deepening of Russian influence. Augustus III reigned until 1763 but spent most of his time in Saxony and left government to his ministers and to magnate factions. The Sejm rarely completed a session, and during the reign of Augustus III most sessions were dissolved without passing legislation, which shows how effectively noble faction, foreign influence, and the veto could block action.

Yet the first half of the century also produced a movement of intellectual and political reform. Stanisław Konarski, a priest of the Piarist order, founded the Collegium Nobilium in Warsaw in 1740 to educate young nobles in civic virtue and modern subjects, and his book on the effective way of holding sejms criticized the veto. Magnate families that wanted reform, notably the Czartoryski clan (known as the Familia), allied with Russian protectors in the hope of using their influence to restructure the state, while rival magnate groups, such as the Potocki, resisted. The economy began to recover, grain exports through Gdańsk increased, and cultural life flourished, especially in Warsaw. This was not a country in uniform decay. It was a polity whose institutions were hamstrung by factions and by external intervention, but whose political culture was producing proposals for reform.

The death of Augustus III in 1763 offered the reformers a chance. Catherine II, who had come to power in Russia in 1762, wished to place a pliable candidate on the throne, and she supported Stanisław August Poniatowski, a nobleman from the Czartoryski circle who had formerly been close to her. Russian troops surrounded the electoral field, and in September 1764 Poniatowski was elected. The new king was an educated man who believed in reform. He and his supporters promoted modernization of the treasury, the army, the commission system, and education, with the National Education Commission of 1773 as a later achievement. However, Catherine and Frederick II of Prussia, who had agreed in 1764 to work together to keep the Commonwealth's constitution unchanged, resisted any strengthening of the state, and Russian officials intervened to protect the veto and noble liberties.

In 1766 to 1768, the issue of the dissidents, the non Catholic nobles, became a means of Russian intervention. Russia and Prussia demanded that Orthodox and Protestant nobles be granted equal political rights, and the Russian ambassador Nikolai Repnin used pressure and arrests to force the Sejm of 1767 to 1768 to accept a settlement that guaranteed the rights of dissidents and also the cardinal laws, including the elective monarchy and the liberum veto, under Russian guarantee. Many Polish and Lithuanian nobles, including Catholic clergy, saw this as an assault on their religion and sovereignty. In February 1768, a group of nobles formed the Confederation of Bar, in the town of Bar in Podolia, to resist Russian influence and defend Catholic privilege. Its fighters, often called Bar confederates, fought a guerrilla war against Russian troops and the royal forces, with some assistance from France and with refuge on Ottoman territory. The war also coincided with the Haidamak uprising, the Koliivshchyna of 1768, in which Ukrainian peasants and Cossacks rebelled against Polish landlords and Jewish leaseholders in right bank Ukraine, and which Russian forces suppressed.

The pursuit of confederates into Ottoman territory at Balta triggered the Ottoman declaration of war on Russia in October 1768, as Section 79.17 explains. Prussia and Austria watched the Russian successes against the Ottomans with concern. Austria had already occupied the Spiš (Zips) towns, claiming a medieval pledge, and in 1769 it extended its occupation. Frederick II of Prussia, who wished to connect Brandenburg with Ducal Prussia by acquiring Polish Prussia, proposed that the three powers divide parts of the Commonwealth as compensation for each other's gains, which would avert a wider war over Russian conquests in the south. After negotiations in 1771 and 1772, the three powers agreed to the First Partition.

By the agreements of 1772, Russia received territory in the east, including Polish Livonia, and the lands east of the Daugava, Dnieper, and Drut rivers, with the cities of Polotsk, Vitebsk, and Mogilev, in an area of roughly 90,000 square kilometers. Prussia took Royal Prussia (excluding Gdańsk and Toruń) and Warmia, as well as areas of Greater Poland along the Noteć (Netze) river, an area of some 36,000 square kilometers that linked Brandenburg to East Prussia and gave Prussia control of the lower Vistula. Austria took a large area in the south, including parts of Lesser Poland, Red Ruthenia, and the Spiš region, which became the Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria, of some 83,000 square kilometers. In total, the Commonwealth lost approximately 211,000 square kilometers, close to thirty percent of its territory, and roughly a third of its population, a figure estimated at about four million people, though totals vary with the base year and method. The partitioning powers justified the seizure by citing old territorial claims, the need to restore order amid anarchy, and the stability of the region. Under the threat of force, the Sejm of 1773 to 1775 was compelled to ratify the cession and to accept a new constitutional arrangement under Russian guarantee that included a Permanent Council as an executive body.

The partition was both a result of domestic institutional weakness and a deliberate act of coercion. Without the veto's paralysis, the Commonwealth might have maintained a larger army and a more effective treasury, but its neighbors' interventions, including the maintenance of the veto by Russia and Prussia, were not accidental, since the weakness suited them. The reform efforts of the 1760s and the Commission of National Education, the Permanent Council, and the later constitutional ideas show that internal change was underway. Partition in 1772 was not inevitable in the sense of being determined by earlier institutional flaws. It emerged from the interaction of an internal political order that resisted centralization with three expanding neighbors, a war that had consumed Russian attention in the south, and the strategic decisions of leaders who chose compensation over confrontation.

Historical Debate 2

Did the Liberum Veto and Magnate Politics, or Foreign Intervention, Cause the Commonwealth's Difficulties?

The question: To what extent did the Commonwealth's political troubles result from its own institutions, especially the veto and magnate faction, and to what extent from the military and diplomatic coercion of its neighbors?

Interpretation A — Internal institutional weakness: Some historians, emphasizing the sources of the era's reformers, argue that the veto, the dominance of magnate patronage, the low taxation of nobles, and the small army prevented the state from responding to its neighbors' growth, and that these features made the Commonwealth unusually vulnerable.
Interpretation B — Foreign intervention as decisive: Other historians stress that Russia and Prussia deliberately maintained the veto and the cardinal laws, that foreign money and troops shaped elections and sejms, that comparable fiscal and military problems existed in other composite states, and that the Commonwealth's reform efforts were suppressed by external power. They also note that many of the criticisms of the political system come from later reformers or from partitioning powers with an interest in portraying the Commonwealth as ungovernable.

Evidence: Sejm records, diplomatic correspondence of Russian, Prussian, and Austrian envoys, fiscal and military data, reform writings, and magnate archives.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the Commonwealth is seen as a failed state that deserved its fate or as a constitutional experiment overwhelmed by stronger neighbors, and it bears on how later Polish, Lithuanian, Ukrainian, Russian, and German national narratives have been constructed.

Student task: Weigh the evidence for each interpretation and describe what additional evidence would help determine how much weight each factor deserves.

Section 79.9

The Muscovite State after 1648: Autocracy, Serfdom, Religion, and Frontier Expansion

Russian power did not begin with Peter the Great. By 1648 the Romanov tsars, who had come to the throne in 1613 after the Time of Troubles, had rebuilt a state that was larger, more centralized, and administratively more capable than many later accounts allow. Muscovy possessed a developed central bureaucracy of chancery offices (prikazy) staffed by clerks and state secretaries, a service class of cavalrymen supported by land grants, a system of fortified frontier lines against the steppe, an Orthodox Church with a patriarchate (created in 1589), and a growing military establishment that, from the 1630s, included regiments of the new type trained and commanded by foreign officers. The Muscovite state was therefore not a blank slate on which Peter wrote reforms. It was a functioning autocracy with considerable administrative experience, whose institutions Peter later reorganized under wartime pressure.

Tsar Alexis (reigned 1645 to 1676) came to the throne as a teenager, and his early reign was shaken in 1648 by an urban uprising in Moscow, the Salt Riot, provoked by tax pressure and official corruption. The tsar summoned an assembly of the land (zemskii sobor) in 1648 to 1649, and it approved a comprehensive legal code, the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649. The Code, which was divided into twenty five chapters and was the principal body of Russian law until the nineteenth century, defined the tsar's honor and protected the church, regulated courts and procedure, set rules for the service class, and ruled on the status of peasants and townspeople. Its chapter on peasants abolished the limitation on how long after flight a landowner could demand the return of a fugitive, giving lords an indefinite right of recovery and increasing the legal binding of peasants to their landlords. The Code also tied townspeople to their urban tax communities. These provisions are often described as the codification of serfdom, but historians stress that enserfment was a long process and that the Code consolidated, rather than created, the system, in response to the demands of provincial servicemen for labor and the state's interest in a stable tax base.

The state's revenues were increased through new taxes, a salt tax, copper coinage that led to inflation and to the Copper Riot of 1662, and a household tax, while the army grew through the expansion of regiments of the new model. The tsar's autocratic power rested on a combination of ideology, military service, and consultation with service elites. The zemskii sobor, which had met intermittently since the sixteenth century, ceased to be convened regularly after the 1650s, and the Boyar Duma, the council of great nobles, gradually lost importance. Under Alexis's son Fedor III (1676 to 1682), the system of precedence (mestnichestvo), which had governed appointments by the genealogical rank of families, was abolished in 1682, removing a significant obstacle to appointing officers by merit and service.

Religion was a defining arena of conflict. Patriarch Nikon (patriarch 1652 to 1658, deposed 1666) undertook a reform of Russian liturgy and ritual to bring Russian practice into line with contemporary Greek Orthodox practice, including correction of service books and changes to rituals such as the form of the sign of the cross. The aim was to unify the Orthodox world under a Muscovite leadership that Nikon and the tsar regarded as the center of true Christianity, and it was influenced by the acquisition of Kyivan scholars after the incorporation of the left bank of Ukraine. Many clergy and laypeople, however, regarded the older Russian rites as the authentic forms of the faith and rejected the changes as corruption. The resulting schism (raskol) divided the Russian church. Protest centered on figures such as the archpriest Avvakum, who was exiled, imprisoned, and burned at the stake in 1682, and the monastery at Solovetsky, which resisted royal troops from 1668 to 1676. The Church Council of 1666 to 1667 deposed Nikon, who had overreached in claiming priestly superiority over the tsar, but confirmed his reforms and anathematized the old rites.

The dissenters, known as Old Believers, were persecuted and fled to forests, borderlands, the Don, and later across borders into Poland-Lithuania, the Ottoman Empire, and elsewhere, where they built communities that preserved distinct ritual and social practices. Some formed priestly communities, while others, the priestless groups, rejected the established church's priesthood altogether, and some adopted self immolation in the face of persecution. The Old Believers became a significant and enduring minority within the Russian Empire, and their existence points to the limits of the state's religious authority.

The reign also saw major social unrest. In 1670 to 1671 Stenka Razin, a Don Cossack leader, led a large rising along the lower Volga that drew in fugitive serfs, Cossacks, and non Russian peoples including Tatars, Chuvash, and Mari. Razin's followers captured Astrakhan, and the rebellion exposed the grievances of peasants and frontier peoples against the Muscovite system. It was crushed by the army, and Razin was executed in Moscow in 1671. The rebellion illustrates a pattern, evident again under Peter and under Catherine II, in which the weight of the state's demands could provoke large scale resistance, especially in frontier zones where social boundaries were fluid.

The state also expanded east. Russian fur hunters, Cossacks, and officials had crossed the Urals in the late sixteenth century, and during the seventeenth century they moved with remarkable speed across Siberia, establishing fortified outposts along rivers: Yakutsk in 1632 and Okhotsk on the Pacific in 1647. Semyon Dezhnev's expedition sailed through the strait between Asia and North America in 1648, although the achievement was not widely known at the time. The state's main interest was the fur tribute (yasak), which Indigenous peoples such as the Evenks, Yakuts, Khanty, Mansi, and others were required to pay, often under duress and through the taking of hostages. The consequences for these communities were severe: epidemic disease, violent conflict, loss of land and game, and the imposition of foreign authority. The Treaty of Nerchinsk of 1689 with Qing China set the border in the Amur region, ending a period of conflict and recognizing limits to Russian expansion in the Far East. The territorial growth of the Russian state in Siberia, which did not directly involve European rivals, is part of the background of the later empire, but its human costs and its Indigenous history must not be left out.

Historical Vocabulary
Russian Autocracy, Service Ranks, and Serfdom

Autocracy (samoderzhavie) was the Russian ruler's claim to personal and sovereign authority, not dependent on the consent of a representative body, justified through Orthodox ideas of divinely sanctioned rulership and tested in practice by the need to work through nobles and officials. Service described the obligation of the nobility and other groups to serve the tsar, in military or civil roles, in exchange for land or privileges. Serfdom was a legal condition in which peasants were bound to the land and subject to a lord's authority over their labor, obligations, and in many respects their persons, a status that developed over the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries and was consolidated in the Code of 1649 and by later legislation. In Russia, as in Poland-Lithuania and the Danish estates, serfdom was a historically specific system, different from medieval western European serfdom, and its comparative study requires attention to local law.

Section 79.10

Muscovy, the Cossacks, Poland-Lithuania, and the Southern Frontier

Muscovy's western and southern frontiers were where it intersected most directly with the European state system, and the transformation of these frontiers between 1654 and 1700 prepared the ground for Peter's reign. The war that followed Pereiaslav ended inconclusively in 1667, as Section 79.6 explained, but it established Muscovite control of the left bank of Ukraine and Smolensk and acknowledged Moscow’s temporary possession of Kyiv, already under its control; the Eternal Peace of 1686 recognized Moscow’s continued possession of Kyiv as permanent. The Russian state thus acquired a large Orthodox population with a distinct political and religious tradition, an elite of Cossack officers who served as administrators, and a body of scholars trained in the Kyivan Mohyla Academy who brought Latin learning, Baroque culture, and new theological ideas to Moscow. The relationship was not simply one of absorption, since the Hetmanate retained its own law and institutions, and Cossack officers negotiated their status with Moscow throughout the later seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.

The Eternal Peace of 1686, signed by Muscovy and the Commonwealth, confirmed the division of Ukraine and committed Russia to the Holy League against the Ottoman Empire. This represented a diplomatic landmark. For the first time Muscovy was a formal partner in a European alliance, with the emperor, Poland-Lithuania, and Venice, and its participation brought it into the center of European diplomacy. It also reflected the policy of Moscow's regency government under Tsarevna Sophia (1682 to 1689) and her favorite, Prince Vasily Golitsyn, who in 1687 and 1689 led two large expeditions against the Crimean Khanate. Neither succeeded in reaching Crimea, since the armies suffered from heat, lack of water, and steppe fires and returned without a decisive victory, but they demonstrated that Muscovy could mount substantial campaigns and wished to contest the steppe.

The Crimean Khanate, the primary southern adversary, was a Muslim Tatar state, ruled by the Giray dynasty, a vassal of the Ottoman sultan who confirmed each khan's appointment and could depose him. It controlled the peninsula and the steppe between the Dnieper and the Don, and its economy depended on pastoralism, trade, and the capture and sale of slaves from Russian, Ukrainian, and Polish-Lithuanian lands, especially in raids that carried off many thousands of captives in some years. This raiding was a significant factor in the economy of the Black Sea region, and its victims shaped the fears and policies of Muscovy and the Commonwealth. The khanate's cavalry was also an important component of Ottoman and Commonwealth campaigns, as the allies of Khmelnytsky in 1648 had shown. Russian expansion in the south therefore met a political order with its own internal logic, and the long frontier of fortified lines (the zasechnaia cherta and later lines) was Muscovy's response to a real and persistent threat.

Peter I, who assumed full power in 1696, continued the southern policy. His first major military undertaking was the siege of the Ottoman fortress of Azov, at the mouth of the Don. An attempt in 1695 failed because Russia lacked a fleet to cut off the fortress from the sea, and Peter built a flotilla at Voronezh over the following winter, using foreign shipwrights and compulsory labor. In July 1696 Azov surrendered. The conquest gave Russia its first port on the Sea of Azov and marked the start of Peter's naval program. It also illustrates the state's methods: mobilization by decree, the use of foreign expertise, and the transfer of resources on a large scale, here through the conscription of laborers and the imposition of taxes on the clergy and merchants to support the fleet.

Peter then sought allies for a larger campaign against the Ottomans. In 1697 and 1698 he traveled with a large embassy (the Grand Embassy) to the Dutch Republic, England, and the Habsburg lands, studying shipbuilding and military techniques and seeking support against the Ottomans. The mission was cut short by news of a mutiny of the musketeer regiments (streltsy) in Moscow, which Peter suppressed with severe executions in 1698. The allies, however, were occupied by the coming war over the Spanish succession and made peace with the Ottomans at Karlowitz in 1699. Russia was left to conclude a separate arrangement, a truce in 1700 that confirmed its hold on Azov, and Peter turned his attention north, where the opportunity of the Great Northern War awaited. Russian relations with Poland-Lithuania also changed. After 1686, Russia treated the Commonwealth as a weaker partner whose internal politics it could influence, and the stability of the veto system and the Commonwealth's military weakness became useful features for Russian diplomacy.

The borderland communities remained important actors. The Zaporozhian Cossacks resisted tightening Russian control, the Don Cossacks provided military service but guarded their autonomy and sheltered fugitives, and the Hetmanate's leaders, notably Mazepa, balanced loyalty to the tsar against the interests of the Cossack elite. When Mazepa joined Charles XII in 1708, the choice was not simply treason or national liberation but a calculation by a leader whose autonomy was being eroded by Peter's reforms and demands and who hoped, perhaps, to secure a political future for the Hetmanate under Swedish and Polish protection. Historians continue to disagree on his motives and on the extent of support among the Cossack officers, and the figure has been interpreted very differently by Ukrainian, Russian, and Western historians. After 1709 Peter imposed tighter control, with Russian officials supervising the hetman, and in the following decades the Hetmanate's autonomy was progressively limited.

Section 79.11

Peter the Great, the Great Northern War, and Russia's Emergence on the Baltic

Peter I (reigned 1682 to 1725, as sole ruler from 1696) came to power in the middle of a crisis. As a child he had been proclaimed co tsar with his half brother Ivan V after the streltsy revolt of 1682, under the regency of Sophia. He took effective control in 1689, and after Ivan's death in 1696 he ruled alone. His early interests, in boats, drilling regiments, and the foreign quarter of Moscow, were not mere curiosities, since they provided him with a circle of foreign and Russian associates and a military base independent of the traditional court. The Grand Embassy exposed him to western European technology and statecraft, but its motives were as much diplomatic and military as cultural, and Peter understood Europe chiefly as a source of techniques for a Russian state to adopt.

The military catastrophe at Narva in November 1700, where a Swedish army defeated the larger Russian force, taught Peter that his army needed fundamental reform. Over the following years he raised new regiments through recruitment, with a levy introduced in 1705 that took men from peasant and townsman households for lifetime service, and he created a regular army that by the end of his reign numbered well over one hundred thousand men, with additional irregular troops. Estimates of the army's size vary according to date and whether garrison troops and Cossacks are counted. He built foundries and arms factories in the Urals and elsewhere, and he created a Baltic fleet. The recruitment system was a heavy burden on the peasantry and the towns, and it produced desertion and unrest, most dramatically in the Bulavin rebellion of Don Cossacks in 1707 to 1708, which Peter crushed.

The Russian conquest of the Neva region began in 1702 to 1703 with the capture of the Swedish fortresses of Nöteborg (Oreshek, renamed Shlisselburg) and Nyenschanz. On a marshy island at the Neva's mouth, in May 1703, Peter founded the fortress of Saint Petersburg, and within a decade it became the new center of government. The city's construction was extraordinary, since the site was swampy and prone to flooding, and it required the labor of tens of thousands of conscripted peasants, Swedish prisoners of war, and others, who worked in harsh conditions with heavy mortality. The precise numbers of workers who died are unknown and the often repeated claims of enormous death tolls are speculative, but the contemporary evidence leaves no doubt that the costs were severe. The city was designed to be a port, a naval base, and a symbol, and it was a deliberate break with the Muscovite capital, though Moscow remained an important economic and ceremonial center. In 1712 the court moved to Saint Petersburg, and the government followed over the next years.

The campaign of 1708 to 1709, which led to Charles XII's defeat at Poltava, transformed Russia's position, as Section 79.4 recounts. After Poltava, Russian forces overran Livonia, taking Riga in 1710, and also took Estonia, and Peter's armies and galley fleet advanced into Finland. The Pruth campaign of 1711 was an exception. The Ottoman Empire, urged by Charles XII, declared war in 1710, and Peter led an army toward the Danube, hoping that the Orthodox princes of Moldavia and Wallachia would join him. Dimitrie Cantemir, the prince of Moldavia, did, but the Wallachian prince Constantin Brâncoveanu hesitated, and the Russian army was surrounded on the Pruth river by a larger Ottoman force and faced starvation. Peter negotiated the Treaty of the Pruth, giving back Azov and agreeing to abandon some fortifications and to withdraw from Polish affairs. The Ottoman grand vizier, who accepted the settlement, was later criticized by those who thought he could have achieved more. The episode showed that Russian power, though growing, was not unstoppable, and that the Ottoman army was still a formidable opponent.

The Baltic war ended with Nystad in 1721. The treaty, concluded with Swedish plenipotentiaries after negotiations by the Russian diplomats Jacob Bruce and Andrei Osterman, gave Russia the provinces of Livonia, Estonia, Ingria, and part of Karelia with Viborg and Kexholm, and the islands of Ösel and Dagö, while Finland was returned to Sweden and Russia paid compensation of two million rixdollars. The treaty guaranteed the Lutheran religion and the corporate privileges of the Baltic German nobility and the towns of Riga and Reval, and Peter integrated this German speaking elite into Russian service, creating a group of Baltic German nobles who would provide many officers and officials to the empire. In October 1721, the Senate and the Holy Synod, in a ceremony in Saint Petersburg, asked Peter to accept the titles of Father of the Fatherland, Peter the Great, and Emperor (Imperator). The adoption of the imperial title reflected Russia's new position, although foreign courts only gradually recognized it. The Holy Roman Emperor and the Commonwealth, whose rulers had long claimed the exclusive imperial dignity in Christendom, were reluctant to recognize it, and some states accepted it only decades later.

The war also changed the relationship between Russia and Europe. Russian diplomats now maintained permanent embassies in many European capitals. Russian armies operated in Germany, Poland, and the Baltic. Peter arranged dynastic marriages with German houses, and Russian ministers participated in European congresses. Russia's participation in the European state system, however, depended on military success and on the diplomacy of an increasingly confident state, and it did not make the country a western state in culture or society. The empire contained Orthodox Russians, Ukrainians, Baltic Germans, Finns, Estonians, Latvians, Tatars, and many other peoples, whose incorporation took diverse forms. The ceded provinces retained their institutions for decades, under a policy of accommodation that illustrates how the empire adapted its governance to local elites and avoided a simple program of centralization.

Section 79.12

Peter's Administrative, Military, Religious, and Social Reforms

Peter's reforms are best understood as a series of responses to the demands of war, not as the execution of a coherent plan of Westernization. Many were improvised, some were borrowed from Swedish and other European models, and some built on Muscovite precedent. In evaluating them, historians distinguish between a reform's intent, its formal adoption, and its actual implementation. They also ask who benefited and who paid.

The central government was reorganized in stages. In 1711, before leaving for the Pruth campaign, Peter created the Governing Senate, a small body of senior officials that was to supervise the administration in his absence, and which became a permanent institution handling finance, justice, and supervision of officials. From 1717 to 1718 he established administrative colleges, modeled in part on Swedish and German examples, to replace the old chancery offices; they were organized by function, with colleges for foreign affairs, war, the admiralty, finance and revenue, justice, and commerce among others, with collective leadership and written procedures. The colleges were staffed by Russians and foreigners, and the system was designed to improve efficiency and reduce corruption, though in practice they were plagued by shortages of trained personnel and by the persistence of patronage. In the provinces, an initial reform of 1708 to 1710 divided Russia into large governorates (gubernii), and a later reform of 1719 introduced a subdivision into provinces and districts, with the aim of improving tax collection and recruitment. The system created strong regional administration but resulted in overlapping authority and chronic confusion.

Financial needs drove the tax reforms. The state collected revenue through a household tax, but it was easy to evade by merging households, and war required more. In 1718 Peter ordered a census of the male population and, after it was carried out in 1719, he introduced a poll tax (podushnaia podat') on male peasants and townsmen, which began to be collected in 1724 and replaced the household tax. The poll tax was a heavy burden, but it enlarged the tax base, and because landlords were responsible for collection from serfs, it increased the powers of nobles over peasants. It also tied the legal categories of the population more closely to the state's fiscal and recruitment needs, and it merged various categories of unfree peasants into a single category subject to the tax, which had the effect of consolidating serfdom. Historians estimate that state revenue rose substantially during Peter's reign, though the figures are uncertain and the reliability of the contemporary accounts is debated.

The nobility was subjected to a stricter service regime. Peter required nobles to serve in the army, navy, or civil administration, often for life, and he demanded education, sending young men abroad and establishing schools for mathematics, navigation, and engineering. In 1714 he promulgated a law of single inheritance, designed to prevent the division of estates among sons and to force younger sons into service, but it was widely resisted and was repealed in 1730. In January 1722 he issued the Table of Ranks, which organized military, naval, civil, and court service into fourteen parallel ranks. Advancement was supposed to depend on service and merit, and men of non noble origin who reached specified ranks, the lowest officer rank in the military and, in the civil service, a middle rank, received hereditary nobility, while lower ranks conferred personal nobility only. The Table gave the state a unified system of rank and promotion, and it linked social status to service, but it did not abolish hereditary privilege. Nobles from established families continued to dominate the higher ranks, and the Table's effect was to supplement rather than replace birth as the foundation of status.

The church was brought under tighter state control. After the death of Patriarch Adrian in 1700, Peter left the patriarchal throne vacant, and in 1721 he established the Holy Synod, a collegial body of clergy, headed by a lay official (the chief procurator after 1722), to govern the church. The Spiritual Regulation, drafted by the Ukrainian trained prelate Feofan Prokopovich, set out the principles of the new arrangement, which put the church administration on the same footing as other state colleges. The change was a significant development, since it ended the independent patriarchate and made the church an arm of state administration, and it was intended to reinforce the idea of the tsar as the guarantor of church order. Peter also taxed monasteries, limited monastic recruitment, and used church resources for state purposes, and his government tolerated and even protected foreign Protestants, while continuing to repress Old Believers, who were subject to double taxation.

Economic development was pursued by state initiative. Peter promoted ironworks, textile mills, shipyards, and other enterprises, and many were created by the state or by merchants with state support, notably in the Urals, where the Demidov family built an iron industry that made Russia a major iron producer by the mid eighteenth century. Industrial enterprises depended on coerced labor. A decree of 1721 allowed manufacturers to purchase villages whose peasants were assigned to the works, creating a category of so called possessional workers who were bound to the factory. The state also required peasants to work on construction projects, canals, and fortifications, and to cart goods, and it imposed mercantilist regulations on trade and customs. The result was a considerable increase in military supplies and some manufactured goods, but the enterprises often relied on subsidies, and many did not survive his death.

The cultural program included a new alphabet simplified from the Church Slavonic script, a first newspaper, the introduction of compulsory Western style dress and the shaving of beards for most nobles and officials, new calendars (the year began on January 1 from 1700), assemblies at which men and women mixed socially, and the founding of an Academy of Sciences, planned in his lifetime and opened in 1725. These measures symbolized a break with Muscovite practice and were sometimes accompanied by coercion. Their effect was to create a layer of educated nobles and officials who were fluent in European languages and culture, and a visible division between this elite and the mass of the population, whose lives continued to be shaped by agrarian rhythms, the village community, and the church.

Peter's reforms met resistance, from peasants who fled, soldiers who deserted, Old Believers who regarded him as the Antichrist, nobles who resented service, and officials who ignored directives. His own son, Alexei, who had fled abroad and was thought to sympathize with opponents of the reforms, was tried and died in prison in 1718. In 1722 Peter issued a law that gave the ruler the right to name his successor, but he died in January 1725 without doing so. A balanced assessment recognizes the real institutional changes, the Senate, the colleges, the Table of Ranks, the Synod, and the standing army and navy, that enabled Russia to project power, while also acknowledging the burdens placed on the population, the uneven implementation, and the extent to which Peter's measures built on Muscovite foundations.

Section 79.13

Russia after Peter: Dynastic Instability, Imperial Consolidation, and Catherine II's Early Reign

Peter's death in 1725 without a named successor opened a period of dynastic instability that lasted until Catherine II's accession in 1762. The succession was settled by the leading officials and the guards regiments, the Preobrazhensky and Semenovsky, which supported Peter's widow, who became Catherine I (1725 to 1727), and real power rested with Prince Aleksandr Menshikov and a Supreme Privy Council. Her successor, Peter II, grandson of Peter I, was a boy who died of smallpox in 1730. The Supreme Privy Council then offered the throne to Anna, a niece of Peter I and the widow of the Duke of Courland, on conditions (the Conditions) that would have limited the autocracy, but a coalition of nobles who disliked oligarchic control and wanted to preserve autocracy supported Anna's claim, and she tore up the conditions. Her reign (1730 to 1740) was marked by the influence of German officials, including Ernst Johann Biron, and by an efficient but harsh administration, in which courtiers jockeyed for power. The system had a significant military component, and under Anna Russia fought the war of the Polish Succession and, from 1735 to 1739, an Ottoman war in alliance with Austria.

After Anna's death, a brief regency for the infant Ivan VI ended in November 1741, when Elizabeth, daughter of Peter I, seized power with the help of the Preobrazhensky guards. Elizabeth (1741 to 1762) governed through ministers and court factions, and under her Russia strengthened its position. She presided over the foundation of Moscow University in 1755, the expansion of noble privileges, the establishment of banks for nobles, the abolition of internal customs duties in 1753, and a more active foreign policy, including the war against Sweden in 1741 to 1743 that ended with the Treaty of Åbo, in which Russia gained part of southeastern Finland. Her chancellor, Aleksei Bestuzhev-Riumin, saw Prussia as a danger, and in the Seven Years' War (examined in Chapter 80), Russia joined Austria and France against Frederick II. Russian armies inflicted heavy defeats on Prussia at Gross-Jägersdorf, Zorndorf, and Kunersdorf, and in 1760 briefly occupied Berlin. The war reinforced the notion that the Russian army was a major force in Europe, though the costs of the conflict were high.

Elizabeth's death in December 1761 brought her nephew Peter III to the throne. An admirer of Frederick II, he withdrew Russia from the war and returned its conquests in Prussia, a decision that infuriated many officers and officials, while his abolition of the compulsory service of nobles in February 1762, which freed them from the obligation that had defined their relationship to the state since Peter I, was popular among nobles but did not bring him support. In June 1762 a coup led by Grigory Orlov and his brothers, with the backing of the guards regiments, deposed him in favor of his wife, who became Catherine II. Peter III was killed, apparently in the course of events involving the Orlovs, a few days later, and Catherine, a German princess who had converted to Orthodoxy, consolidated her power with the support of nobles, officials, and the church. The long chain of palace revolutions between 1725 and 1762 demonstrated the political weight of the guards and of court factions, and it shows that the autocracy was exercised through coalitions of elites and not simply by one person's will.

In foreign policy, the Russian government under Elizabeth and Catherine pursued objectives that continued those of Peter: security on the Baltic, influence in Poland-Lithuania, and expansion toward the Black Sea. Catherine, advised by Nikita Panin, initially favored a system of alliances in northern Europe, the Northern System, with Prussia, the Commonwealth, Sweden, and Britain, and she signed a treaty with Prussia in 1764. Her priorities in the Commonwealth were to secure a loyal king and a constitutional order that kept the neighbor weak, as Section 79.8 showed, and her intervention in Poland led to war with the Ottoman Empire in 1768. The war's origins lay in Russian operations against the Bar Confederates on the Ottoman border, in the Ottoman concern about Russian dominance in Poland, and in French diplomacy encouraging the Porte to resist. It was Russia's first major war under Catherine, and its outcome, discussed in Section 79.17, was a decisive success.

This chapter treats Catherine II's reign only to the extent needed for these events. Her program of legislative and administrative reform, her Instruction of 1767, her provincial reform of 1775, and her relationship to the Enlightenment are examined in Chapter 84, and the Pugachev rebellion of 1773 to 1775, which was fueled by social tensions among Cossacks, serfs, and non Russian peoples, belongs to that later narrative. It is nonetheless important to see that the empire that Catherine inherited was a consolidated military power with an expanded territory, a nobility whose position had been strengthened by the withdrawal of compulsory service, a peasantry whose serfdom had deepened, and a diverse population that included Baltic Germans, Ukrainians, Tatars, Finns, and others. Russia's emergence as a great power thus relied on a social system whose burdens were borne by the majority and whose political stability could not be taken for granted.

Section 79.14

The Ottoman European Order: Provinces, Communities, Frontier Institutions, and Political Authority

The Ottoman Empire had ruled much of southeastern Europe since the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, as Chapter 41 explained, and by 1648 its European territories, usually called Rumelia (Rum Eli, the land of the Romans), included the Balkan Peninsula from the Danube to Greece, much of Hungary until the 1680s, and the Aegean islands. The empire's capital, Istanbul, lay on the European shore of the Bosporus, and a large part of its population, tax revenue, and military manpower came from its European provinces. The sultan's lands also covered Anatolia, the Arab provinces, Egypt, and North Africa, and in thinking about Ottoman Europe it is necessary to remember that the empire was not a European state alone but a multi regional empire whose European dimension was central to its identity, finance, and politics.

Provincial government was organized through provinces (eyalets), under governors (beylerbeyis or pashas) appointed by the sultan, and subdivided into districts (sancaks). The Rumelia Eyalet had its seat in Sofia and, later, other cities, and the Danubian frontier was administered through other eyalets. Governors commanded provincial troops, collected taxes, and supervised the judges (kadis), who administered Islamic law and also notarial and administrative functions, and whose court records are among the most important sources for Ottoman social history. The kadi's court was not limited to Muslims. Christians and Jews brought cases to it, often to take advantage of its enforcement power, and the records of such courts document property transactions, marriages, disputes, and debts, with women appearing as litigants and property owners to a degree that challenges stereotypes.

The military and fiscal systems changed between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. In the classic system, sipahi cavalry received revenue assignments called timars in return for military service, and the sultan's slave soldiers, the janissaries, received pay from the treasury. By the late seventeenth century, however, warfare relied increasingly on infantry with firearms, which required cash, and the state shifted toward tax farming (iltizam), in which tax revenues were auctioned to contractors, and, from 1695, to the life term tax farm (malikâne), which gave holders a long term interest in the revenue source in return for a large advance payment to the treasury. Many timars were converted to tax farms or became hereditary in practice, and the sipahi cavalry lost military importance. The janissary corps also changed. Its members took up trades, married, and passed membership to their sons, and many on the rolls did not serve, while the corps developed ties to urban guilds and neighborhoods. The janissaries thus became both a military force and a political interest group in Istanbul and the provinces, capable of defending their privileges and of toppling ministers and, on occasion, sultans.

The weight of provincial notables (ayans) grew in these decades. Ayans were local leaders, often wealthy landholders, tax farmers, or officials, who mediated between the state and local communities, raised troops, and in some cases dominated their regions. The central government relied on them to collect taxes and provide troops, and by the early eighteenth century they had acquired formal and informal powers in many Balkan districts. Historians have debated whether the ayans' rise should be seen as a sign of imperial fragmentation or as part of a negotiated adjustment between center and provinces, a question that bears on the debate on Ottoman decline presented in Section 79.16.

Religious diversity was fundamental. Muslims, most of them Turkish speaking in Anatolia and Thrace but including Slavic speaking converts in Bosnia and Albanian speaking communities, were a dominant group in the cities and in some regions, though most inhabitants of the Balkans were Orthodox Christians. Their communities were organized under the leadership of the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Constantinople, which also had authority over many Slavic, Romanian, and Arab Orthodox believers, and in the Serbian lands the autonomous Patriarchate of Peć, restored in 1557, was abolished in 1766, and the Archbishopric of Ohrid in 1767, which brought these churches under Constantinople's control. Armenians, Jews, and Catholics were organized under their own religious leaders. The system of recognized non Muslim communities, often called the millet system in older scholarship, is now understood by many historians as a set of evolving arrangements and practices rather than a fixed legal structure, and its details varied by period and place. Non Muslims paid a poll tax (cizye) and were subject to certain legal restrictions, but they enjoyed protection of life, property, and worship, and they managed their own religious and family affairs through their communities' courts and leaders. Jewish communities, which expanded after the Iberian expulsions of the 1490s, were important in Salonica (Thessaloniki), Istanbul, Sofia, and elsewhere, and engaged in trade, crafts, medicine, and tax farming. The Ottoman Balkans also witnessed conversions to Islam, with great regional variation, in Bosnia, Albania, and the Rhodope, among other regions.

The Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia were not provinces but tributary states, governed by Christian Orthodox princes (voivodes or hospodars) who paid annual tribute and delivered goods to the sultan, and the principalities kept their own boyar nobility, law, and church. Until the early eighteenth century the princes were chosen from local boyar families, but after the Pruth episode of 1711, when the Moldavian prince Cantemir sided with Peter, the Porte began to appoint Phanariot Greeks, members of Greek families of Istanbul's Phanar quarter who served as the sultan's interpreters and diplomats, to the princely thrones, Moldavia from 1711 and Wallachia from 1716. Phanariot rule brought Greek culture and heavy fiscal demands to the principalities, and it linked the families who exercised it to the Ottoman court, while also creating a class of Orthodox Christian elites with international connections. Transylvania, which in the seventeenth century had been an Ottoman tributary principality under its own princes, passed under Habsburg control after 1699.

The Crimean Khanate had a distinctive position. The Giray khans ruled their own state, with its own nobility, army, and law, and the Ottoman sultan was their suzerain. The khan was confirmed by the sultan, who could depose him, and the khanate supplied cavalry for the sultan's campaigns and received subsidies. Its society was organized into clans led by great Tatar families, and its economy depended on pastoralism, agriculture, trade through ports such as Caffa (Kefe), and the slave trade. It was therefore a client state of the Ottoman Empire, with considerable autonomy in internal matters and active diplomacy of its own, including relations with Poland-Lithuania, Muscovy, and the Cossacks. Its position as the key link between the empire and the northern steppe made it central to the events discussed in the following sections.

The Ottoman state thus combined central authority with local negotiation. The sultan's household and the grand vizier directed policy, the provinces were managed by a mixture of appointed officials and local notables, and communities of different religions had defined places. Evidence for this system comes from imperial registers of population and revenue, from the records of kadi courts, from the correspondence of the Ottoman chancery, from chronicles written by Ottoman historians, and from the accounts of foreign travelers and diplomats. These sources must be read with attention to their origins. State registers show what the government wished to count, kadi records capture mainly those who used the courts, and foreign accounts often reflect the prejudices of their authors, while the voices of peasants, women, and non elite Christians are less directly represented.

Historical Vocabulary
Ottoman Provincial Government and the Status of Tributary Polities

An eyalet was an Ottoman province governed by a pasha, subdivided into sancaks. A timar was a revenue assignment given to a cavalryman in return for military service, a system that declined as the state turned to tax farming. An ayan was a local notable who mediated between the central government and a district. A tributary state, such as Moldavia, Wallachia, or earlier Transylvania, retained its own ruler and institutions and paid annual tribute to the sultan, while the Crimean Khanate was a vassal state under the Ottoman sultan's suzerainty, a relationship that involved confirmation of the khan, military service, and subsidy but not direct provincial administration. Suzerainty thus describes a layered relationship distinct from both direct rule and modern sovereignty, and it is essential for understanding the arguments over the status of Crimea in 1774.

Section 79.15

The Ottoman-Habsburg Wars, Vienna in 1683, and the Treaty of Karlowitz

The Ottoman Empire experienced a period of political crisis in the mid seventeenth century, with child sultans, factional struggles in the palace, rebellions, and fiscal strain, followed by a revival under the Köprülü family of grand viziers. Mehmed Köprülü became grand vizier in 1656, in the reign of the young Mehmed IV (1648 to 1687), with extensive powers, and used them to restore order, suppress rebels, and reinvigorate the military. His son Fazıl Ahmed Pasha, grand vizier from 1661 to 1676, continued the policy abroad. The Ottomans took the fortress of Uyvar (Nové Zámky) in Habsburg Hungary in 1663, were defeated at Szentgotthárd in 1664, and signed the Peace of Vasvár, which left the situation largely unchanged. In 1669 they captured the Venetian stronghold of Candia on Crete after a long siege, and in 1672 they seized Podolia from the Commonwealth, as seen above. The state had recovered substantial strength.

Kara Mustafa Pasha, who became grand vizier in 1676, hoped to continue this record. By the early 1680s the Habsburgs were distracted by the policies of Louis XIV on the Rhine, and Hungarian nobles, led by Imre Thököly and supported by the Ottomans, had risen against Habsburg rule. The Ottoman court decided on a major campaign. In 1683 a large army, with contingents from Crimea, Transylvania, Wallachia, and Moldavia, marched along the Danube and, in July, laid siege to Vienna. Estimates of its size vary widely among sources, and historians generally consider the larger contemporary figures to be exaggerated. The aims of the campaign are debated, and while some contemporaries believed Kara Mustafa intended to take Vienna and carry the war into central Europe, others note that earlier Ottoman campaigns sought limited gains. The siege lasted two months, and the city's garrison, commanded by Count Ernst Rüdiger von Starhemberg, held out under severe strain, while Emperor Leopold I had left for Passau.

The relief of Vienna was an allied effort. Leopold had negotiated an alliance with Sobieski in March, and Pope Innocent XI provided subsidies and diplomatic backing. An army of Habsburg imperial troops under Charles of Lorraine, Bavarians, Saxons, Franconians, and Swabians, and a Polish-Lithuanian force under Sobieski, who assumed overall field command, gathered on the Danube and attacked on September 12, 1683. The decisive moment was a cavalry charge by the Polish hussars and imperial cavalry from the Kahlenberg heights, which broke the Ottoman lines, and the besiegers retreated. Kara Mustafa was executed by order of the sultan in December. The victory was celebrated across Catholic Europe, and later memory, in Polish, Austrian, and Turkish traditions, has shaped its interpretation, sometimes as a clash of civilizations and sometimes as a triumph of national heroes. A more cautious historical reading stresses that the allied army included many peoples, that the Ottoman army contained many Christian vassals, and that the Ottoman and Habsburg powers had long conducted both war and diplomacy.

The allied success opened a long war. In March 1684 the emperor, the Commonwealth, and Venice formed the Holy League under papal sponsorship, joined by Russia in 1686. Habsburg forces took Buda in 1686, ending nearly a century and a half of Ottoman rule there, won at Mohács in 1687, and took Belgrade in 1688, while Venice conquered the Peloponnese (the Morea) and attacked Athens, where the Parthenon, used by the Ottomans as a powder magazine, was heavily damaged by a Venetian shell in 1687. The Ottoman government, strained by the war and by internal revolts, deposed Mehmed IV in 1687. The Habsburg armies, however, were diverted by the war with France (the Nine Years' War, 1688 to 1697), and the Ottomans recaptured Belgrade in 1690 and reconquered Serbia. In 1691 the Habsburgs won a major victory at Slankamen. The Habsburg advance in the Balkans encouraged a large migration of Serbian Orthodox families, under Patriarch Arsenije III Čarnojević in 1690, into Habsburg territory, where they received special privileges, an episode explained in the Habsburg context in Chapter 78.

The war ended after the victory of Prince Eugene of Savoy over Sultan Mustafa II at Zenta, on the Tisza river, on September 11, 1697, in which the Ottoman army, caught crossing the river, suffered heavy losses. Peace negotiations opened in late 1698 at Karlowitz (Sremski Karlovci), mediated by England and the Dutch Republic, whose representatives were interested in ending a war that distracted the Habsburgs from the Spanish succession. The Ottoman negotiators, among them Rami Mehmed Efendi, and the dragoman Alexander Mavrocordatos, a Greek Orthodox official of the Porte, drew on diplomatic skill, and the settlement was concluded in January 1699.

Under the Treaty of Karlowitz the Habsburgs received almost all of Hungary, except the Banat of Temesvár, along with Transylvania and most of Slavonia and Croatia, while the Ottomans retained Belgrade and the Banat. The Commonwealth regained Podolia and Kamianets, Venice received the Morea and some territories in Dalmatia, and Russia, which did not sign, concluded a two year armistice and later a treaty confirming its hold on Azov. The treaty marked a turning point, since it was the first time the Ottomans had accepted the loss of extensive European territory through a negotiated settlement, and it set a pattern of using defined borders, commissions to mark frontiers, and mediators. It also demonstrated Ottoman diplomatic adaptability, since the empire treated it as a truce between sovereigns, and the Porte's officials developed a more active diplomatic corps. For the Habsburgs, Karlowitz inaugurated a new era of power in southeastern Europe, with consequences discussed in Chapter 78.

Section 79.16

Ottoman Adaptation, Recovery, and Frontier Politics, 1700–1739

The loss of Hungary led the Ottoman elite to examine the sources of weakness and to experiment with solutions. In the early eighteenth century, a series of officials and sultans worked to stabilize the finances through the malikâne system, to improve the army, and to strengthen diplomacy. The reign of Ahmed III (1703 to 1730) began with the deposition of his brother Mustafa II, following a revolt in Edirne in 1703 in which janissaries, ulema, and others took part, and reflected the importance of the capital's political forces. Ahmed's government was led for much of the period by able viziers, and from 1718 by Nevşehirli Damad İbrahim Pasha, the sultan's son in law, who served as grand vizier until 1730.

Military affairs continued to demand attention. The Ottomans fought Russia in 1710 to 1711, with the victory at the Pruth as described above, and in 1714 to 1718 they went to war with Venice, taking back the Morea in 1715, and with the Habsburgs. The Habsburg commander Prince Eugene of Savoy defeated them at Petrovaradin in 1716 and took Belgrade in 1717. The Peace of Passarowitz (Požarevac), signed on July 21, 1718, confirmed Habsburg gains, including the Banat of Temesvár, Belgrade, northern Serbia, and a part of Wallachia (Little Wallachia, or Oltenia), and left the Morea under Ottoman rule while Venice retained a few Dalmatian and Albanian positions. It was the high point of Habsburg expansion in the Balkans in this period. The Ottomans did not accept this as final.

The years from 1718 to 1730 are often called the Tulip Era (Lale Devri), a label coined by the early twentieth century historian Ahmed Refik, referring to a period of relative peace, refined court culture, and a fashion for tulips. The period saw the creation of new palaces and gardens, including the Sadabad palace on the Golden Horn, and the establishment of the first Ottoman Turkish printing press, set up by Ibrahim Müteferrika, a Hungarian born convert who received an imperial license in 1727, and whose first book, a dictionary, appeared in 1729. An Ottoman ambassador, Yirmisekiz Mehmed Çelebi, visited France in 1720 to 1721, and his reports influenced taste. Historians now treat the notion of the Tulip Era with caution. It risks presenting the period as one of frivolous luxury, when it also included serious diplomatic, fiscal, and cultural projects, and the term itself reflects the views of a later generation that criticized the court.

The era ended in the revolt of 1730. The Ottoman war with Safavid Persia, which had collapsed under Afghan invasion, produced gains followed by reverses under the rising Persian leader Nadir, and public discontent with taxation and a perceived indulgence by the court led to rebellion in September 1730, led by an Albanian janissary, Patrona Halil. Rebels, joined by artisans, soldiers, and others from the capital's lower orders, demanded the execution of the grand vizier. The sultan, unwilling to resist, had Damad İbrahim strangled and abdicated in favor of his nephew, Mahmud I (1730 to 1754). The new government executed the rebel leaders within weeks, and the episode shows the power of Istanbul crowds and military groups to bring down a government, but it was followed by continuity in policy rather than reversal.

Mahmud I's government pursued military reform with foreign help. Claude Alexandre de Bonneval, a French nobleman who had served the Habsburgs and entered Ottoman service, converting to Islam and taking the name Ahmed Pasha, led an effort to reorganize the artillery and create a corps of bombardiers (humbaracı) with European techniques. The effort met opposition from the janissaries, and it was limited in scale, but it illustrates a continuing practice of adaptation, in which Ottomans selectively borrowed methods and used foreign experts. At the same time, the army remained a mixture of types, and logistics, finance, and the quality of command remained uneven.

The wars of 1736 to 1739 demonstrated the mixed results. Russia, under Anna, and the Habsburg Monarchy attacked the Ottomans in alliance. Russian armies under Field Marshal Burkhard Christoph von Münnich took Azov in 1736, Ochakov in 1737, and Khotin and Iași in 1739, but they suffered extreme losses from disease and supply shortages. The Habsburg effort collapsed in Serbia and Bosnia, where Ottoman forces defeated the imperial army at Grocka in July 1739 and recovered Belgrade by siege. With the Habsburgs seeking peace and Russia isolated, French mediation led to the Treaty of Belgrade in September 1739. Under its terms the Habsburgs returned Belgrade, northern Serbia, and Oltenia to the Ottomans, setting the border at the Sava and Danube, while Russia received Azov, with its fortifications dismantled, and the right for Russian merchants to trade on the Black Sea, but only in Ottoman ships, and was forbidden to keep a Black Sea fleet. The settlement was a significant Ottoman diplomatic and military success, and it ushered in nearly three decades of peace on the European frontier, during which the empire concentrated on internal affairs.

The long peace after 1739 was not a period of passivity. The government tried to manage finances, build roads and fortresses on the Danube, and maintain a diplomatic presence in the European capitals. Provincial notables strengthened their positions, merchants from many communities, including Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Serbs, expanded trade with the Habsburg monarchy, the Russian ports, and western Europe, and Ottoman exports of raw materials and agricultural goods grew. The period also saw Greek and other Christian merchant families gain wealth and cultural influence, with long term implications for the political life of the Balkans.

Historical Debate 3

Should Eighteenth-Century Ottoman History Be Interpreted as Decline or as Institutional Adaptation?

The question: Did the Ottoman Empire after Karlowitz experience a long term decline from a classical peak, or did it change its institutions to meet new circumstances?

Interpretation A — Decline: Older scholarship, influenced by the writings of Ottoman observers and by nineteenth century European views, argued that the empire's classical institutions decayed after the sixteenth century, that provincial notables and tax farmers weakened central authority, that military defeats showed relative weakness compared with Habsburg and Russian armies, and that these developments led to the later crises. Some stress the real territorial losses at Karlowitz, Passarowitz, and Küçük Kaynarca.
Interpretation B — Adaptation and negotiated change: Many recent historians emphasize that the Ottoman state was a flexible, long lasting political system that adjusted its fiscal and military institutions, that tax farming and ayans' roles reflected workable arrangements, that the empire recovered territory in 1715 and 1739, that it participated in diplomacy as a legitimate power, and that the idea of decline reflects a comparison with an idealized sixteenth century and with later European power. They also warn against explaining each Ottoman setback by reference to a single trajectory.

Evidence: Fiscal registers, tax farming contracts, provincial court records, chronicles, diplomatic correspondence, and evidence for military organization and supply.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how historians understand southeastern European history, the origins of later Balkan states, and the comparison between Ottoman and European state formation, as well as the narrative of the Eastern Question in later chapters.

Student task: Select two events from this division and explain how a historian favoring decline and one favoring adaptation would interpret each, then say what evidence might help decide between them.

Section 79.17

Russia, the Crimean Khanate, and the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca, 1774

The long peace between Russia and the Ottoman Empire ended in October 1768. The immediate trigger was an incident at Balta, a town on the Ottoman side of the border in Podolia, where Russian troops pursuing Bar confederates and Ukrainian rebels in the Koliivshchyna uprising crossed into Ottoman territory. The Ottoman government, which had been urged by France and by Polish confederates to resist Russian domination of Poland, issued a declaration of war on Russia under Sultan Mustafa III. The Ottoman court's calculations are debated: it was concerned about the threat posed by Russian control of Polish affairs, but it was ill prepared for war, and the army suffered from poor organization and morale. Catherine II, who had not expected the conflict, saw an opportunity to improve Russia's position on the Black Sea and to gain a reputation.

The war was fought on several fronts. A Russian army under Prince Aleksandr Golitsyn and then Pyotr Rumyantsev advanced into Moldavia and Wallachia, and in July 1770 Rumyantsev defeated much larger Ottoman forces at the Larga and Kagul rivers, victories that became legendary in Russian military history. In the Mediterranean, a Russian squadron, which had sailed from the Baltic around Europe under Aleksei Orlov, with British naval officers and ports assisting, destroyed the Ottoman fleet at the Battle of Çeşme on July 5 to 7, 1770, after a clash at the Chios strait, using fireships. The expedition provoked uprisings among Greeks in the Morea, which the Russians failed to support adequately and which Ottoman forces and Albanian irregulars suppressed brutally. In 1771, Russian forces under Prince Vasily Dolgorukov overran the Crimean peninsula, taking Perekop and Kerch, and installed a khan, Sahib II Giray, favorable to Russia. Ottoman failures were attributed to poor command, supply problems, discontent in the army, and the burden of fighting simultaneously on the Danube and in Crimea, but Russia also faced difficulties, including plague, supply problems, and, from 1773, the Pugachev rebellion.

The peace was concluded on July 21, 1774 (New Style; July 10 Old Style) at Küçük Kaynarca (Kuchuk Kainardzha), a village south of the Danube in what is now Bulgaria, following negotiations conducted in the field with Rumyantsev. The sultan who ratified it was Abdülhamid I, who had succeeded Mustafa III in January. The treaty included provisions on territory, commerce, diplomacy, and religion. Russia gained the fortresses of Kerch and Yenikale on the Crimean strait, Kinburn at the mouth of the Dnieper, Azov, and a strip of coast on the Black Sea between the Dnieper and the Bug rivers. It also received the right of free navigation for its merchant ships in the Black Sea and through the straits, a diplomatic presence in Istanbul and the right to appoint consuls, and an indemnity of 4.5 million rubles. The Crimean Tatars, together with the Budjak and Kuban Tatars, were declared independent of both the Ottoman and Russian empires, though the sultan retained a religious role as caliph, a spiritual authority for Muslims. The principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia were returned to the Ottomans, with the promise of moderate treatment and tax relief, and the right of the Russian envoy to make representations on their behalf.

The treaty's religious clauses have a substantial historiography. Article 7 contained a promise by the Porte to protect the Christian religion and its churches, and Article 14 permitted the Russians to build a church in Constantinople and gave the Russian minister the right to make representations about it. Later Russian governments claimed that these provisions gave them a right to protect Ottoman Orthodox Christians in general, and the argument was invoked in the nineteenth century as a justification for intervention, including in the lead up to the Crimean War. Many historians, however, emphasize that the text itself was narrower. It conferred a right to make representations concerning a specific church and to intercede on behalf of the principalities, and it did not establish an uncontested blanket protectorate over all Ottoman Orthodox subjects, which the Ottoman government always denied. The distinction between what the treaty said and what Russia later claimed that it said is essential for understanding the history of the Eastern Question.

The status of the Crimean Khanate illustrates a similar point. The declaration of independence was a diplomatic formula that ended Ottoman suzerainty in legal form while giving Russia, which held Kerch and Yenikale and enjoyed influence in the peninsula, opportunities to intervene. It was not annexation. In the following years Russian and Ottoman rivalry over the choice of khan continued, and Russia annexed the Crimean Khanate in 1783, a development that falls outside this chapter and that must be distinguished from the arrangements of 1774. The Tatars, who had their own political institutions and who had been important actors for centuries, thus lost their position within a decade, and the Muslim population of the northern Black Sea coast would be gradually displaced under Russian rule.

For the Ottomans, the treaty marked a significant defeat, one in which the empire gave up a Muslim vassal state's dependence and ceded coastal territory, and one that brought the empire into a new strategic situation on the Black Sea. The sultan's government interpreted it as a setback to be overcome, not as the final outcome, and it remained an active participant in diplomacy, fighting Russia again in 1787 to 1792. For Russia, it was a landmark in its policy of southern expansion and brought commercial access that fostered the growth of Black Sea ports such as Odessa later in the century. It also reinforced the pattern, visible in the First Partition two years before, in which the expansion of Russian influence was linked to the Commonwealth's and the Ottoman Empire's difficulties. Each was an independent development, but the Russo-Ottoman war and the partition were connected by the diplomatic bargaining among Russia, Prussia, and Austria.

Section 79.18

The Baltic and Black Sea Systems: Warfare, Diplomacy, Trade, and the Changing Balance of Power

The Baltic and the Black Sea were connected by rivers, by the policies of the same rulers, and by the movement of armies, but they were distinct strategic regions with different commercial and political structures. The Baltic was a northern European trading sea, linked through the Sound to the North Sea and the Atlantic, and its commerce, handled by Dutch, English, German, Swedish, Danish, and Russian merchants, carried grain, timber, tar, iron, copper, hemp, and flax westward and manufactured goods, salt, wine, and colonial products eastward. Its ports, Lübeck, Danzig, Riga, Reval, Stockholm, and later Saint Petersburg, were focal points of a regional economy. The Black Sea, in contrast, was in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries largely an Ottoman maritime space, with its trade focused on provisioning Istanbul, and its northern coasts were the domain of the Crimean Khanate and the steppe. Access to it by non Ottoman shipping was restricted, and Russia's acquisition of a presence there after 1774 changed this arrangement.

In the Baltic, the central development was the replacement of Sweden by Russia as the strongest military power. Sweden's dominance had rested on a combination of factors: a military organization that mobilized a large share of a small population, income from conquered provinces and tolls, French subsidies, the diplomatic exploitation of rivals' divisions, and the strategic centrality of the Baltic provinces. Its weaknesses were a narrow resource base, vulnerability to coalition, and a long, dispersed frontier. In the Great Northern War, Russia combined a population many times larger, a state able to extract men and money, a willingness to learn from defeat, and the geographical advantage of depth that allowed it to absorb Charles XII's invasion. After 1721, Sweden remained a significant power but could no longer dictate Baltic affairs, and in 1743 and 1790 to 1791 it fought Russia again without recovering its lost provinces. Denmark-Norway, by contrast, secured its position at the Sound, retained Schleswig after 1720, and continued to prosper through the commerce of the neutral shipping that the Dutch and later others relied upon.

The Commonwealth's position in the Baltic shifted too. Its Baltic coast included Royal Prussia and, until 1660, a claim to Livonia, and its grain exports through Gdańsk depended on the Vistula. The loss of Livonia, the rise of Prussia in Ducal Prussia and Brandenburg, and the dominance of Russia on the Baltic's eastern shore combined to reduce the Commonwealth's influence. The First Partition gave Prussia control over the lower Vistula, with obvious implications for the grain trade and for Gdańsk's independence. The role of customs duties and river tolls in this transformation was real. Prussian officials after 1772 imposed duties that damaged Gdańsk's commerce, an episode that revealed the instrument of economic pressure available to a neighbor.

In the south, the transformation of the Black Sea region proceeded more slowly but along similar lines. Ottoman control of the Black Sea coast, from the Danube mouth to the Caucasus, had rested on fortresses, naval power, the vassal status of the Crimean Khanate, and the Danubian principalities' supply of grain, sheep, and timber to Istanbul. The Muscovite and Russian effort to reach the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea was constrained by distance, steppe geography, the lack of a navy, and the capacity of the Tatar cavalry to raid the frontier. Russia's progress, from Azov in 1696, through its loss in 1711 and recovery in 1736, to the gains of 1774, depended on the construction of a chain of fortified lines, the settlement of the steppe, the creation of a navy, and the organization of a supply system for armies operating far from their bases. After Küçük Kaynarca, the Black Sea changed from an Ottoman lake into a contested space, and the Habsburgs, who also had interests in the Danube and the Balkans, saw Russian advance as a threat.

Diplomacy tied the two regions together. The Great Northern War and the Pruth campaign showed that a Baltic conflict could provoke an Ottoman war, as Charles XII hoped. The war of 1768 to 1774 and the First Partition were linked through the bargaining of the three eastern monarchies. Prussia, whose interest in Poland was greater than its interest in the Ottoman lands, assisted Russia against Austria's potential involvement on the Ottoman side, and Austria, which had signed a treaty with the Ottomans in 1771 to restrain Russia, shifted toward participation in the partition. France, which had traditionally supported Sweden, Poland, and the Ottomans against the Habsburgs and Russia (an eastern barrier policy, the barrière de l'Est), found its influence declining after the Seven Years' War, and Britain valued Russian naval stores and trade but did not intervene to prevent Russia's growth. The results were a diplomatic system in which three monarchies, Russia, Prussia, and Austria, were increasingly able to shape the fate of their neighbors, a development that will be seen in the context of general European diplomacy in Chapter 80.

Historians therefore avoid two simple explanations. The first holds that Russia's rise was inevitable because of its size and population, which overlooks the limits of its resources, the setbacks at Narva and the Pruth, the dependence on foreign officers and subsidies, and the fragility of its dynasty. The second holds that Sweden, Poland-Lithuania, and the Ottoman Empire were doomed to decline, which overlooks Swedish resilience under Charles XI, the Commonwealth's reform movement, and the Ottoman recoveries of 1715 and 1739. The balance changed because of the interaction of institutional capacity, wartime decisions, dynastic accidents, geography, and the behavior of other powers. The changes were also reversible in principle, since Sweden's loss of provinces did not eliminate it from the system, and the Ottoman Empire remained a major European power long after 1774.

Cause and Effect

War Finance, Institutions, and Territorial Change in the North and East

Long-term causes

Differences in fiscal and military capacity: Sweden's small population and allotment army, the Commonwealth's low taxation and reliance on noble consent, Russia's large population and service state, and the Ottoman reliance on tax farming and provincial notables, all within the geography of the Baltic, the East European Plain, and the Black Sea steppe.

Short-term causes

The accession of a young king in Sweden, the coalition of 1699 to 1700, the Commonwealth's disputed successions, the Bar Confederation and the Balta incident, and the diplomatic bargaining among Russia, Prussia, and Austria after 1768.

Event or transformation

The shift of Baltic dominance from Sweden to Russia, the First Partition of Poland-Lithuania, the Ottoman loss of Hungary and later of control over the northern Black Sea, and the emergence of Russia, Austria, and Prussia as the chief arbiters of the region.

Immediate effects

Sweden lost its eastern Baltic provinces and its imperial status, the Commonwealth lost roughly a third of its territory and population, Russia gained Baltic provinces and a Black Sea presence, and the Ottomans restructured frontier diplomacy and fiscal arrangements.

Long-term consequences

A new regional order in which Russia was the major northeastern power, the weakening of the Commonwealth's independence, the intensification of Eastern Question rivalries, and long term changes in the lives of peasants, Cossacks, Tatars, Jewish communities, and Baltic peoples.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the outcomes. Military performance depended on finance and supply; finance depended on institutions and elites' consent; institutions were shaped by earlier wars and by foreign pressure; and decisions by individual rulers interacted with structures they could not fully control.

Historical complexity: Sweden's efficient fiscal military state still lost in 1721, and the Commonwealth's weak central state survived many crises before 1772. Outcomes depended on both internal arrangements and the choices of neighbors.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Regional Authority in 1648, 1683, 1721, 1763, and 1774

What changed?

  • 1648: Sweden is the dominant Baltic power; the Commonwealth is large but entering a crisis; Muscovy is rebuilding; the Ottoman Empire is under strain but about to revive.
  • 1683: Sweden is an absolutist military state; the Commonwealth, recovering, sends troops to Vienna; Russia is a rising partner of the Holy League; the Ottoman Empire is at the height of its late campaigns.
  • 1721: Russia has replaced Sweden on the Baltic; Sweden enters the Age of Liberty; the Commonwealth is dependent on Russian mediation; the Ottoman Empire has recovered the Morea and Azov.
  • 1763: Russia is a major power in the Seven Years' War and in Poland; Sweden is a factional parliamentary monarchy; the Ottoman Empire has enjoyed long peace.
  • 1774: Russia has a Black Sea foothold; the Commonwealth has lost a third of its territory; the Ottomans have accepted a major defeat.

What remained continuous?

  • Peasant labor and dependence under lords or states in most of the region
  • The central role of religious institutions and communities
  • The importance of local elites in imperial administration, from Baltic Germans to ayans to Cossack officers
  • The geography of rivers, steppe, and sea that shaped campaigns

Who experienced change most directly? Soldiers and peasants subject to recruitment and taxation, populations in ceded provinces, Cossacks and Tatars whose political autonomy narrowed, and nobles whose political roles changed. Who experienced greater continuity? Many village communities, whose obligations changed in form but not in essence, and Baltic and Balkan elites who kept their privileges under new rulers.

Why the pattern matters: Changes in imperial influence, such as who ruled the Baltic or the northern Black Sea, were often more rapid than changes in internal government and local society, and students should distinguish the two.

Section 79.19

Nobility, Peasants, Religious Communities, Frontier Peoples, and Everyday Life

The political history of this chapter was experienced differently by those who stood at different points in the social order. Territorial expansion meant new titles, offices, and estates for rulers and nobles, but for farmers, soldiers, and subject peoples it often meant taxation, recruitment, requisition, and displacement. A narrative that follows only dynasties and treaties therefore omits much of what states demanded and what societies endured.

Rural society varied sharply. In Sweden and Finland, farmers who owned or rented their land were represented in the Riksdag, paid taxes to the crown, and supported soldiers under the allotment system, and their communities were bound together by parish institutions and the church's supervision of literacy and conduct. Women ran farms, managed household finances, worked in fishing, textiles, and dairying, and in the absence of men on military service took on wider responsibilities. In the north, Sámi communities were subject to Swedish and Norwegian taxation and missionary effort. The Swedish crown encouraged settlement in the northern Lappmark regions from the 1670s, which reduced Sámi access to land, and the Sámi were also affected by conscription of men for transport duties connected with mining. The Strömstad treaty of 1751 between Denmark-Norway and Sweden, which fixed the border in the north, included an addendum, the Lapp Codicil, that recognized the right of Sámi herders to move across the border with their reindeer, a recognition of the realities of nomadic life in a landscape that states divided.

In Denmark, peasants held land as tenants of nobles or the crown, and the adscription of 1733 tied men to their estates, while in Norway most farmers were free owners or tenants, and the timber trade, fishing, and shipping offered some prosperity. Women in Norwegian coastal communities often managed farms and trade while men were away at sea. In Poland-Lithuania, peasants performed labor dues on noble estates, and these increased over the period. A lord had extensive authority over his peasants, including jurisdiction in many matters, and peasants had limited access to royal courts. Noble households ranged from magnate courts with hundreds of servants to modest manors, and noblewomen managed estates, negotiated marriages, and, in some cases, played a role in politics through family networks. The Jewish population, which grew considerably in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, lived in towns and private towns owned by nobles, worked as merchants, craftsmen, innkeepers, and estate leaseholders, and governed itself through communal institutions. Jewish leaseholding on noble estates placed many Jews between lords and peasants, creating tensions that surfaced in 1648 and 1768, and it is a mistake to see Jews only as victims or only as intermediaries, since the community was socially diverse, with rabbis, scholars, artisans, wealthy contractors, and the poor, and it engaged in vibrant religious and intellectual life, including the rise of Hasidism in Podolia in the middle of the eighteenth century.

In Russia, the social order was defined by obligations to the state and to landlords. Nobles served in the army and administration, serfs paid dues in labor or cash to landlords and the poll tax to the state, state peasants paid taxes to the crown, and townspeople were bound to their urban communities. The recruitment levy took young men from villages for lifelong service, a sentence so severe that it was treated with ritual mourning in some communities. Peasant women managed households and fields in the absence of recruited men, wove and spun, and participated in village assemblies in some contexts. The resettlement of peasants and the forced labor on construction projects and in factories increased the burdens. Old Believers and sectarian groups lived under pressure, and Indigenous peoples of Siberia and the Volga region, such as Tatars, Bashkirs, Chuvash, Mari, and Udmurts, were subject to tribute, missionary pressure, and settlement of Russians in their lands, which fed uprisings, notably the Bashkir revolts of the early eighteenth century.

In the Ottoman Balkans, the rural population, mostly peasants called reaya, paid taxes in cash and kind to the state or to tax farmers, and in the eighteenth century many peasants on large estates (çiftliks) in some regions lived under conditions of heavy dependence on local lords, while in mountain districts such as Montenegro and parts of Albania and Bosnia, communities enjoyed substantial autonomy under customary leaders. Cities such as Sarajevo, Sofia, Salonica, and Belgrade were centers of artisan production, regulated by guilds that often included members of several religions, and of trade networks. Women appear in kadi court records as owners of property and shops, as litigants in inheritance and marriage disputes, and as donors to religious foundations, although legal and social constraints limited their roles. Orthodox Christian communities were organized around parish and monastic institutions, with church leaders and local notables mediating with the Ottoman authorities. The Jewish and Armenian merchants of Salonica and Istanbul, and the Greek, Serbian, and Vlach merchants who traded between the Ottoman lands and the Habsburg monarchy, contributed to a commercial life that crossed imperial borders, while Muslim and Christian communities lived in close proximity in many towns and villages.

Frontier peoples had a distinctive position. The Zaporozhian and Don Cossacks combined military organization with their own assemblies and elected leaders, and they negotiated with the Commonwealth, Muscovy, and the Ottomans. Crimean Tatars lived under a system of clan chiefs and khans, with a way of life that combined herding, farming, and raiding, and their experience of Russian expansion would be one of progressive loss. Baltic peasants, Estonian and Latvian, lived under German speaking nobles in conditions of serfdom that persisted under Swedish and then Russian rule, and the devastation of the Great Northern War, including the plague of 1710, killed a large share of the population in parts of Estonia and Livonia. Finland suffered the occupation known as the Great Wrath (Isoviha) from 1713 to 1721, when Russian forces ruled, with documented violence, deportations, and economic disruption.

War and its aftermath affected all these groups. Disease, as important as combat, followed armies: plague in the Baltic in 1710 and in the Commonwealth in 1708 to 1711, typhus and dysentery in the Russian and Ottoman armies. Armies lived off the land, and billeting and requisitions fell on peasants. Soldiers' families were left to cope, and prisoners of war, such as the Swedish prisoners sent to Siberia after Poltava, built new lives far from home, some becoming teachers and craftsmen. The social costs of state expansion were therefore heavy and unequal. Elites who gained from expansion, and the ports and towns that profited from the new trade, were a minority, while many others experienced the same events as burden and loss.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Frontiers, and the Costs of War

Across the north and east, the household was the basic unit of production, whether it was a Swedish farm supporting a soldier, a Polish manorial village owing labor, a Russian peasant family owing dues and recruits, or an Ottoman household paying tax to a state or tax farmer. Food depended on grain, with rye bread and porridge across the north and east, wheat and maize in southern districts, fish on the northern coasts, and sheep and cattle in the steppe borderlands. Housing was typically of timber in the forested north and of mud brick, stone, or timber elsewhere, and heating and cooking relied on wood, so that the timber trade was part of daily life.

Religion structured the year. Lutheran parishes in Sweden examined the population's knowledge of the catechism, Catholic and Orthodox feast days organized work and leisure in Poland-Lithuania and Russia, Old Believers kept their own rites, Jewish communities observed the Sabbath and festivals, and Muslim and Christian communities in the Ottoman Balkans lived by their own calendars and holidays. War intruded through recruitment, billeting, epidemic disease, and destruction, and the famine of the 1690s, the plague of the 1700s, and the devastation of the Deluge remind students that demographic catastrophe was a repeated experience. Education and literacy varied: Swedish parishes promoted reading, Orthodox monasteries and Jewish schools taught their own traditions, and Polish noble youth attended Jesuit or, increasingly, Piarist colleges.

Section 79.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The central lesson of this chapter is that the political transformation of northern and eastern Europe between 1648 and 1774 was not a single process of modernization along a common path. Sweden built a fiscal military state that rose to Baltic dominance and then lost it, passing from royal absolutism under Charles XI to a parliamentary regime of estates after 1719. Denmark-Norway consolidated a hereditary absolutism written into law, which strengthened a service elite while leaving social hierarchies largely in place. The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth sustained a republic of nobles with an elected king, and despite devastating wars and growing foreign pressure it retained institutions and a reform movement that the First Partition interrupted. Russia combined autocracy, serfdom, and a service nobility with an unprecedented mobilization of resources that made it the dominant northeastern power. The Ottoman Empire managed a complex European domain through provincial negotiation, tributary arrangements, and diplomatic adjustment, suffering losses and also achieving recoveries.

These systems were connected by war and diplomacy. The Great Northern War reached the Ottoman frontier, the Commonwealth's weakness invited intervention that provoked the Russo-Ottoman war of 1768, and the partition of 1772 was partly a diplomatic answer to Russian successes in the south. At the same time, the lives of peasants, nobles, Jews, Cossacks, Tatars, Sámi, and Balkan Christians were shaped by wars in ways that cannot be read from treaties alone. Students should therefore read the following review materials as a way of testing whether they can explain changes in power, constitutions, and society together.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how Sweden, Denmark-Norway, Poland-Lithuania, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire competed, governed, and adapted within the northern and eastern European political order between 1648 and 1774, a period bounded by the settlement of Westphalia and by the First Partition of Poland-Lithuania in 1772 and the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774. Its central argument is that these powers represented different systems of government and different combinations of military, fiscal, and social arrangements, and that changes in the balance among them resulted from interaction, not from a uniform European modernization.

In the Baltic, Sweden's position after Westphalia rested on conquered territories, military reputation, and the crown's use of land grants and subsidies. The Northern Wars of 1655 to 1660 extended Swedish power to Scania but exposed the limits of its resources. Charles XI, using the war of 1675 to 1679 and the resentment of non noble estates, established royal absolutism through the Riksdag of 1680 and the reduction of noble land, supporting the allotment army and a reorganized administration. Denmark-Norway, in contrast, turned to hereditary absolutism in 1660 after the crisis of 1658 to 1660, and the King's Law of 1665 gave it a written constitution that remained in force for nearly two centuries.

The Great Northern War, which began in 1700 as a coalition against a young king, ended with Sweden's loss of Livonia, Estonia, Ingria, and part of Karelia to Russia in the Treaty of Nystad of 1721 and its earlier loss of German territories to Hanover and Prussia. Finland remained Swedish until 1809. After Charles XII's death, the Riksdag's constitutions of 1719 and 1720 initiated the Age of Liberty, in which parties of Hats and Caps competed under the influence of foreign subsidies, and the Freedom of the Press Act of 1766 represented a notable innovation. The question of whether the era was a real constitutional experiment or a redistribution of power among privileged groups remains contested.

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth was a union of two states with an elected king, noble assemblies, and strong provincial institutions, combining noble liberty with serfdom and unequal religious status. The Khmelnytsky Uprising of 1648 created a Cossack Hetmanate whose leaders pursued their own political aims, and the Pereiaslav agreement of 1654, interpreted differently by the parties, led to a war that ended with Andrusovo in 1667 and a division of Ukraine along the Dnieper. The Swedish invasion of 1655 and the other wars devastated the country, yet the Commonwealth survived and, under John III Sobieski, contributed to the relief of Vienna in 1683.

In the eighteenth century, Saxon kings, magnate factions, the liberum veto, and Russian guarantees limited the Commonwealth's capacity to act, but a movement of educational, fiscal, and constitutional reform grew, and Stanisław August Poniatowski's election in 1764 coincided with attempts to modernize the state. Russian and Prussian insistence on the cardinal laws, the dissident question, the Confederation of Bar, and the Russo-Ottoman war created the conditions for the First Partition of 1772, in which Russia, Prussia, and Austria annexed about thirty percent of the territory and roughly a third of the population. The partition resulted from foreign coercion combined with domestic institutional weakness and was not inevitable.

Muscovy had been rebuilt after the Time of Troubles into an administratively capable autocracy by 1648. The Law Code of 1649 consolidated serfdom and the status of townspeople, the church schism produced the Old Believers, and expansion into Siberia brought tribute and hardship to Indigenous peoples. Russia's involvement with Ukraine, the Commonwealth, and the Crimean Khanate drew it into European diplomacy, culminating in the Eternal Peace of 1686. Peter I, after the defeat at Narva, reorganized the army, founded Saint Petersburg, won at Poltava, and secured the Baltic provinces at Nystad, taking the imperial title in 1721. His reforms, the Senate (1711), the colleges (1717 to 1718), the poll tax, the Holy Synod (1721), and the Table of Ranks (1722), strengthened the state and increased its demands on society, and they built on Muscovite foundations rather than creating a new state from nothing.

After Peter, a series of palace revolutions showed the influence of guards regiments and court factions, but Russian military and diplomatic power continued to grow, as shown in the Seven Years' War and in the intervention in Poland under Catherine II. The Russo-Ottoman war of 1768 to 1774 ended with Küçük Kaynarca, which gave Russia coastal territory and Black Sea navigation, declared the Crimean Tatars independent of both empires, and included religious articles whose scope has been disputed. The treaty did not annex Crimea, which Russia did in 1783, and it did not grant an uncontested protectorate over all Ottoman Orthodox Christians.

The Ottoman European order combined provincial governors, kadis, timar holders and later tax farmers, janissaries, local notables, tributary principalities, a vassal khanate, and recognized religious communities. Its wars with the Habsburgs and the Holy League after 1683 cost it Hungary at Karlowitz in 1699, but the empire recovered the Morea in 1715 and Belgrade in 1739 and adapted its fiscal, diplomatic, and military institutions. Historians are divided between interpretations of decline and adaptation, and the evidence supports a more nuanced picture of both loss and resilience.

The comparison of the Baltic and Black Sea systems shows how maritime access, river routes, customs, alliances, and state finance shaped the changing balance of power. Russia's rise, Sweden's loss of dominance, the Commonwealth's partition, and the Ottoman redefinition of its frontiers were produced by competing institutions, resources, military capabilities, and diplomatic decisions. The experiences of peasants, nobles, religious minorities, frontier peoples, women, and others show that territorial change had different meanings for different groups and that its costs fell unevenly.

Taken together, the chapter demonstrates that European political development in this period was not confined to the western monarchies and the Dutch Republic. The northern and eastern powers provided alternative constitutional models and imperial patterns, and their interactions prepared the political geography of the later eighteenth century that Chapters 80 and 84 will examine. It also cautions against reading later national borders and identities back into the period and against assuming that the outcomes of 1721, 1772, or 1774 were predetermined.

A system in which a monarch claims sovereign authority without the consent of representative bodies, as in Denmark-Norway after 1665 and Sweden after 1680; in practice dependent on officials, the church, and local cooperation.

The Swedish assembly of nobility, clergy, burghers, and peasants, whose authority grew after 1719.

The recovery by the Swedish crown of lands and revenues earlier alienated to nobles, carried out from 1680 under Charles XI.

The Swedish arrangement by which districts and groups of farms maintained soldiers in return for land and supplies.

The Danish-Norwegian fundamental law of 1665 establishing hereditary absolutism and requiring adherence to the Lutheran confession.
Age of Liberty
The Swedish period from 1719 to 1772 in which the Riksdag and Council held greater authority than the crown.
Hats and Caps
The two factions of the Swedish Riksdag from the 1730s, generally associated with an assertive foreign policy and with peace and fiscal restraint respectively.
Szlachta
The legally privileged noble estate of the Commonwealth, ranging from magnates to poor nobles.
Sejm and sejmik
The national parliament of the Commonwealth and the local noble assemblies that elected its deputies.

The principle, first used in 1652, that one deputy could end a Sejm session and void its measures.
Confederation
In the Commonwealth, an armed association of nobles formed for a defined political purpose and able to act outside the Sejm.
Magnate
A member of the great noble families who held vast estates, high offices, and networks of clients.
Folwark and pańszczyzna
The manorial grain farm of the Commonwealth and the labor dues owed by peasants to the lord.

The elected Cossack leader and the Cossack political entity created by the uprising of 1648.

The 1654 arrangement between Cossack leaders and Tsar Alexis, interpreted differently by the parties and by later historians.

The 1596 agreement creating the Greek Catholic (Uniate) church within the Commonwealth, which retained Eastern rites and accepted papal authority.
Sobornoe Ulozhenie
The Russian law code of 1649, which codified autocratic authority, courts, and the status of peasants and townspeople.

A legal condition of dependence on a lord, varied in form among Russia, Poland-Lithuania, Denmark, and the Baltic provinces.

Russian Orthodox Christians who rejected the church reforms of Patriarch Nikon and the state's enforcement of them.

Peter I's 1722 system of fourteen parallel ranks linking service to status.

The collegial body established in 1721 to govern the Russian church in place of the patriarchate.

The tax on male peasants and townsmen introduced under Peter I and collected from 1724.

An Ottoman revenue assignment in return for cavalry service, largely replaced by tax farming.

The Ottoman life term tax farm introduced in 1695.

A member of the Ottoman standing infantry corps, which evolved into a political and economic interest group.

An Ottoman provincial notable who mediated between the government and local society.

A member of the Greek elite of Istanbul's Phanar quarter, who served as interpreters and as princes of Moldavia and Wallachia from 1711 and 1716.

A layered relationship in which a ruler retains internal authority while paying tribute and accepting an overlord's confirmation, as with Wallachia and the Crimean Khanate.

The division of the Commonwealth's territory among neighboring powers, first in 1772.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the history of European states cannot be told from the western monarchies alone. Between 1648 and 1774, the political order of the north and east was shaped by distinct systems, a Baltic empire and its constitutional reversal, an absolutist composite monarchy, a noble republic, an autocracy that became an empire, and a multi religious Ottoman domain, each of which responded to the pressures of war and finance in its own way. Together they demonstrate that there was no single route to the early modern state.

The chapter also reveals how the changing balance in this region would shape later developments. Russia's presence on the Baltic and the Black Sea, the partition of the Commonwealth, the Habsburg and Prussian expansion, and the redefined Ottoman frontier set the stage for the diplomacy of the later eighteenth century treated in Chapter 80 and for the reform programs examined in Chapter 84. The arrangements of 1772 and 1774 were not final, and the later partitions, the annexation of Crimea in 1783, and the nineteenth century Eastern Question lie beyond this chapter's scope.

Many features of the period survived its political changes. Peasant dependence, religious diversity, the role of local elites, and the geography of rivers and seas shaped life under each government. Cossacks, Tatars, Jewish communities, Balkan Christians, Sámi, and Baltic peoples remained historical actors whose choices and experiences complicate narratives centered on rulers. The chapter connects regions that are often studied separately, from Stockholm to Istanbul and from Copenhagen to Kyiv, and it contributes to Volume III's argument that European state formation took several forms in which religious, dynastic, fiscal, and military factors interacted.

Finally, the chapter invites students to be careful with hindsight. Sweden's greatness, Poland-Lithuania's partition, Russia's ascent, and Ottoman decline were not scripts written in advance. They were outcomes of decisions made by people who often did not foresee the results, in conditions shaped by institutions and resources they inherited. Reading the period in this way is a necessary preparation for the larger comparative questions of Part XII and of the volumes that follow.
