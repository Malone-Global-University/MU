Peasant Revolts, Urban Unrest, and Social Change

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

After the Black Death, Europe had fewer people but not fewer landlords, employers, tax collectors, or expectations. The decades that followed were a long negotiation over who would work, for whom, at what price, and under what obligations, and when negotiation failed, villagers, weavers, and wool workers sometimes took up arms.

On 30 May 1381, a royal commissioner named Thomas Bampton arrived at Brentwood in Essex to investigate why so many people had escaped payment of the latest poll tax. The villagers of nearby Fobbing, Corringham, and Stanford refused to cooperate, and within days the dispute had spread across Essex and into Kent. Within two weeks, thousands of men and women from the villages and small towns of southeastern England were in London, where they burned the palace of the realm's most powerful magnate, destroyed legal records, executed the Chancellor and the Treasurer, and met the fourteen year old King Richard II at Mile End. Few events in medieval English history are better documented. Yet the documents, chronicles written by hostile monks, royal indictments drawn up by the victors, and a handful of rebel letters preserved by their enemies, tell us about the rebels mainly through the eyes of those who feared them.

The rising of 1381 was the largest and most dramatic of a cluster of popular movements that shook late medieval Europe, but it was not an isolated explosion. In France, villagers of the Beauvaisis and the Île de France had risen in the Jacquerie of 1358. In Florence, in the summer of 1378, wool workers who had no guild of their own briefly helped govern the city. In Flanders, the great textile city of Ghent fought its count and eventually the king of France between 1379 and 1385. In Languedoc, Rouen, and Paris, tax revolts and urban disturbances followed in the early 1380s, and in Catalonia the dependent peasants known as remences pressed for generations against the "bad customs" of their lords. Chapter 50 traced the demographic catastrophe that began in 1347 to 1351, and Chapter 51 examined the wars and fiscal demands of the Hundred Years' War. This chapter asks what happened to ordinary people, and to their relations with elites, in the world those two crises created.

The central problem is one of bargaining power. When a third or more of a region's population died within a few years, the survivors found themselves scarce relative to the land, workshops, and employers that remained. Wages rose in many places, rents and services became harder to collect, and tenants and servants discovered that they could move, refuse, or negotiate. Landlords, employers, town councils, and governments responded with regulation, coercion, and adaptation. Neither side acted uniformly, and the results varied enormously from England to Catalonia, from Tuscany to the lands east of the Elbe. A chapter on revolt that ignored this larger story would reduce a long social transformation to a handful of famous dates, so this chapter treats the revolts as the most visible moments in a much longer struggle over work, rent, status, and political voice.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, the common people of late medieval Europe were not a single class. Peasants held very different amounts of land, under free and unfree tenures, with different rights and burdens; wage laborers, servants, artisans, journeymen, and the urban poor each faced different circumstances, and rich peasants and urban masters sometimes sided with elites against poorer neighbors. Second, the revolts were not modern class revolutions, and rebels spoke the languages of their own time: loyalty to the king, hostility to corrupt officials, appeals to ancient custom, scripture, and the common good. Third, a revolt's military defeat did not mean that nothing changed. Many of the most important transformations of the period occurred through quiet individual and collective choices rather than through rebellion.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich yet unusually biased. Manorial account rolls and court rolls record wages, rents, fines, and flight, but they were written by lordly administrators. Royal statutes and ordinances record what governments wished to happen, not what did. Chronicles of rebellion were composed by clergy and urban elites who were frequently victims or witnesses of the violence. Letters of remission, pardons recording the circumstances of crimes, preserve fragments of the speech of French rebels, but within the narrative conventions of a petition for mercy. Students must therefore learn to read these sources against their grain, asking at every point whose voice is present and whose has been lost.

Within Part VIII, this chapter occupies a pivotal place. Chapter 50 established the demographic shock, Chapter 51 the political and fiscal pressures of war, and Chapter 53 will turn to the institutional and religious crisis of the western church, while Chapter 54 will broaden the view to Burgundy, the Hanseatic world, Poland-Lithuania, and other peripheries. Here the emphasis falls on society and the economy: how work and lordship were reorganized, why resistance took the forms it did, and why different regions diverged. Within Volume II, the chapter marks the end of the high medieval agrarian order described in Chapters 34, 42, and 49, and anticipates the more commercial, more mobile, and more unequal societies of the fifteenth century.

Learning Objectives

Historical Context

The social order shaken in the later fourteenth century had been built during the high medieval expansion examined in Chapters 42 and 43. Between roughly the eleventh and early fourteenth centuries, population grew, forests and wetlands were cleared, markets and towns multiplied, and lordship over land and people was consolidated, as described in Chapter 34. Peasants, who made up the large majority of the population everywhere, held land from lords in return for rents, labor services, and dues, but the conditions of tenure varied sharply. In much of England and northern France, many tenants were unfree, bound to a lord's manor and subject to restrictions on movement and marriage, while in other regions, including parts of Italy, Flanders, and southern France, personal servitude was weaker or absent. By around 1300, many regions seem to have been densely populated relative to prevailing agricultural technology, and the famines of 1315 to 1317, examined in Chapter 50, suggest that the system was already under strain. Historians disagree about whether Europe was truly overpopulated, an issue that returns in the Historical Debate in this chapter.

The Black Death reached Mediterranean ports in late 1347 and spread across the continent over the following four years, reaching England in the summer of 1348. Mortality varied widely by locality and cannot be measured precisely, but a range of roughly one third to one half of the population in many regions is commonly cited, with some communities devastated and others less so. Crucially, plague did not strike once. Later outbreaks, notably in 1361, 1369, and the mid 1370s, returned repeatedly, and kept populations from recovering. Population levels in many regions did not begin to rise substantially until the later fifteenth century or after. The labor market of the later fourteenth century was therefore shaped not by a single shock but by a prolonged condition of low and sometimes still falling population.

At the same time, war, taxation, and political conflict continued. The Hundred Years' War, traced in Chapter 51, imposed heavy fiscal burdens on England and France, devastated parts of the French countryside, and encouraged roving armed companies. Royal governments, hungry for revenue, introduced taxes that reached further down the social scale than before, including the poll taxes of the late 1370s in England. Italian city states fought their own wars with heavy debts and forced loans. The social conflicts of this chapter thus arose where demographic scarcity, rising expectations among workers and tenants, and expanding fiscal and legal demands met one another.

This chapter also respects its boundaries. The causes and course of the Hundred Years' War belong to Chapter 51, and the Battle of Poitiers appears here only as background to French social conflict. Wyclif, the Great Schism, and church reform belong to Chapter 53, so the religious language of the 1381 rebels is examined here only as part of rebel ideology. Burgundian state formation, the Hanseatic world, and the European peripheries belong to Chapter 54, and Ghent appears here as an urban social revolt. The cultural and political world of Florence belongs to Chapter 56, so the Ciompi are treated here as a labor and political crisis.

Section 52.1

Europe after the Black Death: Labor Scarcity and Social Negotiation

The simplest economic consequence of the Black Death is also the most important: after 1348, there were fewer people to work the same land, staff the same workshops, and serve in the same households. A landlord who had once chosen among several applicants for a vacant holding now found that none appeared. A farmer who needed hands at harvest found that those hands cost more. A town master seeking an apprentice found fewer families able to supply one. Throughout late medieval Europe, scarcity shifted the balance of advantage, at least potentially, toward those who sold labor or rented land and away from those who bought labor or collected rent.

The word "potentially" matters. Scarcity did not translate into prosperity automatically or equally. Plague killed the poor and the prosperous alike, and many survivors lost parents, spouses, and children, along with the labor of their households. Inheritance sometimes consolidated holdings into the hands of fortunate survivors, but it also left some with land they could not work. Prices did not move in one direction, and recurring epidemics and wars kept demand and supply unstable. Whether workers gained depended on local conditions, on the strength of lordly control, on the availability of alternative employment in towns, and on how successfully elites could use law and force to preserve older arrangements.

The social history of the period is therefore a history of negotiation under pressure. Individual tenants bargained with stewards over rents and services, laborers moved to better paying employers, village communities acted collectively to resist new demands, and town councils and royal governments attempted to set rates and limit movement. When negotiation broke down, usually when new financial demands or a determined assertion of lordly rights seemed to violate custom, the result could be rebellion. The structure of the chapter follows this progression, from the economic bargaining of ordinary life, through the elite response, to the major revolts, and finally to the longer term transformation of households, status, and regional social systems.

Section 52.2

Reconstructing Wages, Rents, Labor, and Revolt

Historians reconstruct this world from records that were created for administrative, legal, or narrative purposes, not to document the lives of the poor. Manorial account rolls, kept by estate officials, list receipts and expenditure, including wages paid to laborers, rents received, and holdings left vacant. Manorial court rolls record disputes, fines for leaving the manor or marrying without license, and the transfer of land. Estate surveys, rentals, and extents describe tenants and their obligations. Royal and municipal tax records, such as the English poll tax returns and the Florentine tax declarations of the following century, offer glimpses of population and wealth. Wage ordinances and statutes record the intentions of governments, while records of their enforcement, such as indictments and fines, show how often they were applied. Guild statutes, apprenticeship contracts, and notarial records illuminate urban work, and wills and household accounts add information about consumption and family life.

Narrative sources for revolt include chronicles, such as those of Jean Froissart on the Jacquerie, the anonymous Anonimalle Chronicle and the chronicle of Thomas Walsingham on 1381, and the diaries and chronicles of Florentine observers on the Ciompi. They are valuable but partisan. Froissart wrote for a noble audience and described the French rebels with contempt, and Walsingham, a monk of St Albans, was bitterly hostile to the rebels who had attacked his abbey. Royal indictments from 1381 list those accused, naming places and sometimes rebel organization, but they reflect the charges brought by the crown. Letters of remission from France preserve the circumstances of acts for which pardon was sought, in narratives shaped by the need to appear innocent or provoked. Rarely do rebels speak in their own words, and the few surviving rebel texts, such as letters attributed to John Ball preserved by chroniclers, come to us through their enemies.

Two general problems follow. The first is survivorship bias. Estates with continuous account rolls tend to be large ecclesiastical estates with professional administrations, which may not represent the experience of small lay manors or free peasants who rarely appear in lordly records. Landless laborers, servants, and women appear only occasionally, often in connection with offenses or wage regulation. The second problem is the difficulty of measuring change over a period when population, prices, and wages were all moving. A record of high wages in one year, or of a lord's reduced income, may reflect local conditions rather than a general trend. Historians therefore combine documentary evidence with archaeology, including the study of deserted villages and changing housing, and with scientific evidence from bones and cemeteries, as discussed in Chapter 50. The best modern work is cautious about generalization, and students should treat general statements about wages or serfdom as claims about patterns in particular regions and periods.

Section 52.3

Wages, Prices, and the Post Plague Labor Market

In many regions, money wages rose sharply in the years after the first plague, in some English series by a third or more within a generation, though the pace and extent varied by region and occupation. Employers who had to compete for scarce workers paid more, offered better meals, or provided additional benefits. Harvest laborers, who were needed at precisely the moment when delay meant loss, had particular leverage, as did skilled workers in the building trades. In some regions, the gap between the wages of skilled craftsmen and unskilled laborers narrowed, because unskilled work became comparatively scarce and valuable.

Historians distinguish nominal wages, the amount of money paid, from real wages, the purchasing power of that money. Real wages depend on prices, and prices behaved unevenly. In the first years after the plague, many goods became more expensive, in part because labor costs rose and disruption reduced supply. Over the longer run, as population stayed low, demand for grain weakened and cereal prices stagnated or fell in many regions, which meant that workers who bought food benefited, while lords and farmers who sold grain saw their incomes squeezed. In England, long series of building wages and prices have suggested that real wages for many workers rose over the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, reaching a high point in the mid fifteenth century. Earlier writers sometimes spoke of a "golden age" of the laborer, but recent scholarship is more cautious, stressing that the gains were uneven, that workers lost income when plague or war interrupted work, and that many people had no regular access to wage employment.

The market itself was changing. Annual servants, young people who lived in a household for a fixed term, remained common, but more laborers worked by the day or by the task, moving among employers as demand dictated. Seasonal migration to harvest work became more important in some regions. Labor mobility was valuable because employers in one place offered better terms than those in another, and workers learned to exploit those differences. Towns, whose own populations had been depleted, sought immigrants and often offered favorable conditions to attract them, drawing laborers from the countryside and reinforcing the pressure on rural lords. Employers, including lords and farmers, frequently responded by paying above the legal rates and by competing for workers in violation of the statutes intended to prevent exactly that, which meant that the labor laws were opposed not only by workers but also, in practice, by many employers.

The uneven relationship between wages and prices also explains why scarcity alone did not produce harmony. A laborer who saw wages rise could still be undermined by an epidemic, a bad harvest, or a sudden tax. A tenant who paid lower rents in a good decade could face renewed demands in the next. Expectations rose with improved conditions, and the sense that gains had been won, or that customary protections were being taken away, helps to explain why disputes could become so bitter.

Section 52.4

Governments and Elites Try to Control Labor

The most famous response to labor scarcity was legislative. In June 1349, while plague was still active in England, Edward III and his council issued the Ordinance of Labourers, which required able bodied men and women without independent means to accept work at the wages customary before the plague, forbade employers to offer more, and barred workers from leaving employment before the end of their agreed term. In 1351 Parliament enacted the Statute of Labourers, which confirmed and elaborated the ordinance by specifying maximum wages for particular trades and by creating justices to enforce them. Parliament, where landholding gentry and lords were strongly represented through the Commons and Lords, continued to petition for stricter enforcement over the following decades, and later legislation, including the Statute of Cambridge of 1388, sought to control the movement of laborers and the treatment of beggars.

England was not alone. In 1351, the French crown issued an ordinance regulating wages and prices, and the Cortes of Valladolid in Castile approved measures to fix wages and limit the claims of craftsmen and laborers. Portuguese legislation of 1375, the Lei das Sesmarias, sought to compel the cultivation of abandoned land and compel idle workers into agricultural service. In Italy and the German lands, city councils regulated wages, hours, and the movement of workers in their own territories. The common thread was a desire to preserve the pre plague balance between employers and workers by legal compulsion, and to treat wage demands as a moral and social offense rather than a market response.

Enforcement was uneven. In England, thousands of prosecutions were brought in the years after 1351, and the fines levied produced significant revenue for the crown, so the machinery operated in practice. Yet prosecutions were concentrated in certain periods, depended on local jurors who were often themselves employers, and were more likely to catch the visible and poor than those with influence. Employers continued to outbid one another, and workers continued to move. Historians have debated whether the labor legislation succeeded in holding wages below what the market would have set, with some arguing that it had a modest dampening effect and others that it was largely ineffectual. What is clear is that the laws expressed the anxieties of the propertied and created a legal framework of resentment. The statute, enforced by local landowners against their neighbors, made labor law a daily grievance, and, as Section 52.8 shows, it remained a significant background to the rising of 1381.

Primary Source Window

The Ordinance of Labourers, England, 1349

Creator: Edward III and his royal council · Date: June 1349 · Type: A royal ordinance, a legal text issued by the crown in a moment of emergency and later confirmed by statute

Context: The ordinance was issued when plague was still active in England, before Parliament had met to consider its consequences. It is preserved in royal records and in later collections of English statutes, and it was followed by the Statute of Labourers of 1351, which added specific wage rates and enforcement machinery.

Paraphrase of the Ordinance of Labourers (1349) The preamble states that, because a large part of the population, especially workers and servants, had died in the plague, survivors saw the shortage of workers and refused to serve unless they received excessive wages, and that some preferred to beg in idleness rather than work. The ordinance therefore requires that every able bodied person under sixty, free or unfree, without land or independent means, accept work when offered at the wages customary in the region in the years before the plague. It forbids workers to leave service before the agreed term, forbids employers to pay or accept more than the customary wage, and forbids charity to able bodied beggars. This is a paraphrase of the sense of the document, not a translation of its wording.

Analysis: The ordinance is valuable because it shows how royal government and the landholding elite understood the new situation: as a moral failure of workers who were refusing to serve at fair prices, not as a market adjustment. It also shows by implication what workers were doing, namely refusing work, demanding higher pay, and leaving employers. Its language of idleness and begging reflects the preoccupations of those who wrote it. The document cannot tell us how many workers actually received higher wages, how often the law was applied, how workers themselves understood their choices, or how employers who violated its terms were treated. It must be read together with enforcement records and wage series.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the ordinance say workers and employers were doing, and what does it require instead?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purposes beyond simple economic regulation?
  3. What can historians learn from it about elite attitudes toward labor and poverty?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about wages, enforcement, or worker experience?
Section 52.5

Lords, Tenants, and the Transformation of the Manor

On many estates in England, northern France, and parts of Germany, lords had long drawn income from a demesne, the portion of the manor held directly and worked partly by the labor services of tenants. Tenants might owe a certain number of days of work each week, extra work at harvest, rents in cash or kind, and various dues, among them a fine to marry a daughter outside the manor, known in England as merchet, and a payment on death, known as heriot. Some tenants were free, owing only rents, while others, called villeins or serfs, were unfree, subject to restrictions on movement and to such dues. Chapter 34 described the origins of this system; its late medieval transformation was gradual and varied.

Historical Vocabulary
Serfdom and Villeinage

Serfdom is a modern, general term for a range of dependent statuses in which tenants were legally tied to a lord, owed servile dues, and lacked full freedom of movement and marriage. In England the equivalent status was villeinage, in which a villein was unfree in relation to his lord but, in the common law of the period, generally free in relation to others. Serfdom was not slavery, since serfs held land, had families, and had enforceable customary rights, yet it was a status of real legal inferiority. Its forms differed widely: in Catalonia, in northern France, in England, and in later eastern Europe, the burdens and the legal definitions were not the same, and some regions had little personal servitude at all. Students should avoid treating serfdom as a single institution, and should note that rebels and lords often used the vocabulary of freedom and bondage strategically.

After 1348, many lords found it difficult to keep tenants at the old terms. Deaths left holdings vacant, and when lords tried to fill them, prospective tenants could demand lower rents, fewer services, or better tenure, and could go elsewhere if refused. Where the demesne depended on labor services, the declining numbers of tenants and their growing reluctance made services harder to collect, and lords found it cheaper to accept money in place of work, or to hire wage labor at rising cost. Lords' incomes fell on many estates, particularly where rents had been fixed in money or where grain prices were weak. By the early fifteenth century, many English lords had abandoned direct cultivation of their demesnes and leased them to tenants, a pattern repeated in parts of northern France.

Historical Vocabulary
Commutation and the Leasing of Demesnes

Commutation is the replacement of labor services with money payments. It was not new in 1348, having occurred in many regions during the thirteenth century, but it accelerated after the plague. Leasing of demesnes refers to a lord's decision to rent out the land he had previously farmed himself, converting himself from an agricultural manager into a rentier. Both changes reduced lordly control over daily labor while preserving lordly claims to rent and jurisdiction. In England, many formerly servile holdings came to be held by copyhold, a tenure recorded in the manor court roll, with terms protected by custom. Students should not read these developments as the "end of feudalism," a phrase that oversimplifies a long and varied process.

These changes did not mean the end of serfdom, nor were they uniform. Lords, particularly large ecclesiastical landlords such as great abbeys, often resisted, insisting on the full enforcement of servile dues and claiming the right to retrieve fugitive tenants. Tenants in turn resisted, sometimes by organizing. In 1377, in what contemporaries called the Great Rumour, villagers on many manors in southern England sent representatives to London to claim exemption from servile obligations on the basis of entries in Domesday Book, a claim that suggests both legal awareness and collective organization. The relationships among lords, tenants, and officials were constantly negotiated, and each settlement depended on local custom, personalities, and strength.

The central point is that demographic decline weakened some forms of serfdom, particularly those that depended on dense, captive populations and personal labor services, but did not simply terminate lordship. Lords retained property, jurisdiction, and the capacity to extract rent. Servile status survived on some estates into the sixteenth century, and Elizabeth I's government was still arranging the manumission of bondmen on royal estates in the 1570s. The late medieval transformation therefore changed the form of lordly power more than its existence, a theme developed in Section 52.15.

Section 52.6

Flight, Migration, Bargaining, and Everyday Rural Resistance

Most resistance in the late medieval countryside was neither violent nor dramatic. Historians sometimes describe it as everyday resistance, a term for the many small, often individual or local acts by which dependent people limited the claims made upon them. The most common was departure. Unfree tenants who left a manor without license could be pursued by lords, but a fugitive who reached a town, a different estate, or a region of labor shortage often found employers willing to hire without asking questions. Many towns recognized, in varying forms, that a migrant who lived in the town for a year and a day might claim freedom from his former lord, a principle summarized in the German saying that town air makes free, though its application differed by place and was not always secure. Manorial courts in England record numerous tenants who had "withdrawn" from the manor, and lords paid officials to track them down or collected fines in return for permission to reside elsewhere.

Other forms of resistance operated within the village. Tenants refused or performed poorly the labor services they owed, concealed livestock and grain from assessors, grazed animals on lord's land, hunted and gathered in lords' woods, and fell into arrears on rent. They litigated, bringing cases in manorial and royal courts to defend customary rights, and sometimes pooled funds to support collective suits. They negotiated, accepting new leases that reduced burdens in return for formal agreement. And they abandoned marginal holdings, shifting to better land or to wage work and leaving lords with unproductive plots. Where whole communities acted together, as in the Great Rumour of 1377, resistance shaded into organized political action.

These forms of resistance mattered because they were cumulative. A lord who faced a few fugitives could pursue them, but a lord whose entire tenantry had begun to drift away, or who found that every vacancy took longer and cost more to fill, was under steady pressure to concede. The contrast with spectacular revolt is instructive. Revolts were usually crushed, but quiet resistance won gradual concessions, which is one reason why historians who study long term change often emphasize the everyday over the dramatic. At the same time, everyday resistance depended on the possibility of escape and bargaining, so it was less available where lords controlled the courts, where alternatives were scarce, and where governments were willing to enforce restrictions, as the later history of eastern Europe discussed in Section 52.16 shows.

Section 52.7

France after Poitiers: Taxation, Lordship, and the Jacquerie of 1358

Chapter 51 explains how the capture of King John II of France at Poitiers in 1356 plunged the kingdom into political crisis, with the Dauphin Charles acting as regent while the crown struggled to raise money for the war and the king's ransom. For the rural population of northern France, the crisis meant heavier taxation, the devastation of fields by passing armies and unemployed soldiers, and demands for labor to repair noble fortresses, while the nobility, whose traditional justification was the protection of the realm, had just suffered a catastrophic defeat. Contemporary writers openly blamed nobles for the disaster, and rural communities in the Oise valley, the Beauvaisis, Picardy, and the Île de France had endured the plundering of armed companies for two years.

The uprising known as the Jacquerie began in late May 1358, according to the chronicler Jean Froissart, at Saint Leu d'Esserent in the Beauvaisis, and spread rapidly across northern France. The name derives from "Jacques Bonhomme," a derisive nickname for peasants, and the rebels were called Jacques. Contemporary accounts, written mainly by nobles and clergy, describe the burning of castles and manor houses and the killing of lords, their families, and servants, and they portray the rebels as savage. Modern historians treat the most lurid atrocity stories with caution, because they served the interests of those who wished to justify merciless reprisals, though there is no doubt that violence occurred and that nobles feared it intensely. The rebels organized under a captain, Guillaume Karle, described by Froissart as a capable leader, and in some districts they cooperated with the urban forces of Étienne Marcel, the Parisian provost of merchants who was leading a separate political challenge to royal authority.

The rebellion collapsed within weeks. On 10 June 1358, Charles II of Navarre, a prince who had been negotiating with both sides, invited Guillaume Karle to negotiations near Mello, seized him despite the expectation of safe conduct, and then attacked the rebel army after depriving it of its principal leader. Karle was subsequently executed. The nobility then carried out brutal reprisals across the countryside, and chroniclers claimed enormous numbers of rebel deaths, figures that cannot be taken at face value though the slaughter was severe. Marcel was murdered at the end of July, and the Dauphin recovered Paris. In the following years, royal letters of remission pardoned many communities, and local rebels and nobles returned to an uneasy coexistence.

The Jacquerie cannot be called a class revolution. The rebels included not only poor peasants but also better off villagers, artisans, and even some minor nobles and clerics, as evidence from letters of remission and chronicles suggests. Their targets were not lordship in general but nobles who had failed in their duties, and their aims, so far as can be reconstructed, concerned protection, justice, and the restoration of a proper order, rather than the abolition of the social hierarchy. Violence by peasants was matched by violence of nobles, and the nobility feared that the peasants had grasped for themselves the right to judge their lords. The uprising demonstrates how war, taxation, and the collapse of lordly legitimacy could combine to produce rural rebellion, and also how rapidly an overwhelmingly armed and organized elite could suppress it. Historians continue to debate how far the Jacquerie was connected to the post plague labor market, since its immediate causes lay in war and taxation more than in wage conflicts.

Section 52.8

England before 1381: Labor Laws, Taxation, and Local Grievances

By the 1370s, English society had lived for a generation with the consequences of plague and labor legislation. Courts of justices of labourers, and later of the peace, enforced wage ceilings against laborers and enforced servile dues against tenants, drawing on local jurors and officials who were often the neighbors of the accused. Resentment accumulated at the village level, where the new class of local officials, constables, bailiffs, stewards, and jurors, applied statutes that the poor regarded as unjust. At the same time, an expanding class of lawyers and clerks, knowledgeable in the new written procedures of the royal courts, administered the records on which lords relied to claim dues and which rebels would later seek to burn.

The legal awareness of ordinary people should not be underestimated. The Great Rumour of 1377, in which villagers on many estates, including those of the great monasteries, claimed freedom from servile dues on the basis of Domesday Book, shows that tenants understood royal records and courts and could organize across manors. Kent, where tenure was often less servile than in neighboring regions, and Essex, which was densely populated and heavily involved in the commerce of London, contained many of the people who would later rise. These counties were not the poorest in England but among the most commercialized, with large numbers of prosperous villagers, artisans, and wage workers, and strong links to London markets and to one another.

Royal taxation brought a new grievance. Richard II had come to the throne in 1377 at the age of ten, and the government, dominated by his uncle John of Gaunt and a council of magnates, was struggling to pay for a war with France that was going badly. In 1377 Parliament granted a poll tax of a groat, four pence, on every adult. A second poll tax in 1379 was graduated according to rank, and a third, granted in late 1380, demanded three groats, or one shilling, from every person over the age of fifteen, with the expectation that wealthier members of each community would help to cover the poor. The burden on poorer households was considerable and unusually visible, because it required a count of individuals rather than of property. Returns for 1381 recorded markedly fewer taxpayers than in 1377, which most historians read as evidence of widespread evasion, including the concealment of young people and the dishonest reporting of households. In March 1381 the government appointed commissions to investigate the shortfall, and the arrival of those commissioners in Essex lit the fuse.

None of these grievances was alone sufficient. The labor laws, servile dues, the poll tax, discontent with the conduct of the war and the corruption of royal officials, resentment at ecclesiastical landlords, and the political uncertainty of a boy king all contributed. Historians generally reject any single cause explanation, emphasizing instead how existing local conflicts, a new and visible fiscal demand, and the heightened confidence of workers and tenants combined in a moment of political opportunity.

Section 52.9

The English Rising of 1381

The rising began in Essex at the end of May 1381, when villagers resisted the commissioners at Brentwood and Fobbing, and spread into Kent, where rebels attacked Rochester Castle, entered Canterbury, and released prisoners from the archbishop's prison at Maidstone, including the radical priest John Ball. At Maidstone the rebels chose as their leader Wat Tyler, whose background is poorly documented; chroniclers described him as a Kentishman with military experience, though this is uncertain. Rebel bands, claiming to act in the name of King Richard and the "true commons," marched toward London, and, according to chroniclers, Ball preached to them at Blackheath on the theme of natural equality, using a rhyme often paraphrased as asking who was the gentleman when Adam delved and Eve span. The text of the sermon is known only through hostile sources and should be treated as an approximation.

On 13 June the rebels entered London, helped by sympathizers within the city who opened the gates. Over the next two days they burned John of Gaunt's Savoy palace, one of the grandest houses in England, broke open the Fleet and Marshalsea prisons, and attacked the properties of lawyers and officials, destroying legal records. They also murdered a number of Flemish immigrants, whose presence in the English cloth trade made them targets of resentment. On 14 June the young king met rebels at Mile End and agreed to grant charters of freedom and pardon, and while he did so another group entered the Tower of London and seized and beheaded Archbishop Simon Sudbury, the Chancellor, and Robert Hales, the Treasurer, whom the rebels regarded as the principal architects of the poll tax. On 15 June, at Smithfield, the king met Tyler for a second conference, during which a quarrel broke out, Tyler was wounded by the mayor of London, William Walworth, and killed. The king, riding forward to the leaderless rebels with an offer to be their captain, restored order long enough for the rebels to disperse.

The Anonimalle Chronicle preserves what are probably the most reliable reports of the demands presented at Smithfield, which included the abolition of serfdom, an end to all lordship except that of the king, the limitation of rent to a modest fixed sum per acre, freedom to buy and sell in markets, and, according to some accounts, a redistribution of the property of the church. The documents show a movement with coherent political ideas: loyalty to the king, hostility to his "traitorous" counselors, claims to freedom as a birthright, and use of the language of scripture and the common good. Historians since the work of Rodney Hilton have emphasized the social breadth and political seriousness of the rebellion, and in more recent decades historians such as Steven Justice have analyzed the rebels' use of written communication and their knowledge of law, arguing that rebel ideology was intellectually self conscious rather than the result of passion alone.

The rising also spread beyond London. In Essex, Hertfordshire, Suffolk, Norfolk, and Cambridgeshire, villagers and townsmen attacked religious houses, burned manorial records, and forced lords to grant concessions, and the rebellion reached as far as Yorkshire in smaller outbreaks. At St Albans, townspeople pressed the abbey for the liberties of the town, and tenants claimed the right to grind their own grain, an old dispute over lordly control of milling. Bury St Edmunds saw the murder of the prior and a chief justice. In late June, forces led by Henry Despenser, the bishop of Norwich, defeated rebels at North Walsham, and on 28 June royal troops defeated the Essex rebels at Billericay. Ball was captured and executed at St Albans in mid July, and a wave of trials followed, in which many rebel leaders were executed and many others fined or pardoned.

The king revoked the charters of freedom granted in June, and Parliament in November declared the manumissions void, a decision that affirmed the status of bondmen. A chronicler reported that Richard told the rebels that they were villeins and would remain so, though the report comes from a hostile source and the words may be invented. The rising did not abolish serfdom, nor did it immediately change the system of labor regulation. The poll tax was never repeated in that form, and Parliament was cautious about novel taxation for years. The deeper consequences were less direct. Many lords, shaken by the rising, adjusted their dealings with tenants, and servile dues continued to decline over the following century, though the revolt itself was not the immediate cause. Historians have therefore stressed that 1381 should be understood as part of a longer process in which the status and obligations of tenants were gradually eroded.

Cause and Effect

The English Rising of 1381

Long-term causes

Demographic decline after 1348, rising expectations among wage workers and tenants, the survival of servile dues, labor laws enforced by local elites, and the spread of literacy and legal knowledge among ordinary villagers.

Short-term causes

The poll taxes of 1377, 1379, and 1380, the investigations into evasion in spring 1381, resentment at a faltering war, the unpopularity of royal ministers, and the political weakness of a boy king.

Event

A rising centered in Essex and Kent and extending to London and the eastern counties, with attacks on officials, lawyers, records, and lordly property, and demands to end serfdom and limit lordship.

Immediate effects

The deaths of Archbishop Sudbury, Treasurer Hales, and Wat Tyler; temporary royal concessions; suppression and executions; the revocation of manumissions; and a long reluctance to levy novel taxes.

Long-term consequences

No abolition of serfdom by the rising itself, but continued erosion of servile status through lordly adaptation and tenant pressure; heightened elite fear of popular politics and a lasting memory of 1381 in English political culture.

Historical complexity

No single grievance explains the rising. Participants included wage workers, village elites, artisans, and clergy, and their aims were not identical. The revolt reflected both a changed economy and a particular political moment.
Section 52.10

Flanders and the Revolt of Ghent, 1379–1385

Flanders was among the most urbanized and commercialized regions of Europe, with a dense network of towns, among them Ghent, Bruges, and Ypres, whose prosperity rested on the manufacture of woolen cloth from imported English wool. Ghent, one of the largest cities in northwestern Europe, was dominated by textile production, and its politics were shaped by the organized crafts: weavers, fullers, and other artisans whose guilds held political rights and often rivaled one another and the patrician families that had once governed. The counts of Flanders, nominally vassals of the French king, tried to control the cities, whose privileges, fiscal obligations, and commercial interests frequently clashed with princely ambitions. The region's dependence on English wool also entangled it in the Hundred Years' War, since French policy and English wool supplies pulled Flemish towns in opposite directions.

Conflict broke out in 1379 when Louis of Male, the count, and the city of Ghent quarrelled over Ghent's rights, including its attempts to control water routes in the region that competed with Bruges. Ghent's crafts, and in particular the militant faction known for its white hoods, rose against the count's authority, and the struggle became entangled with rivalries between Ghent and the other Flemish towns and among the guilds within Ghent itself. In 1382, under the leadership of Philip van Artevelde, son of a famous earlier leader of the city, Ghent defeated the count's forces and captured Bruges. The count appealed to his overlord, the king of France, and a royal army, together with the count's loyalists, defeated the Flemish militias at Roosebeke in November 1382, where Artevelde was killed. Ghent nonetheless held out, and the conflict was ended only in 1385 by the Peace of Tournai, after the count's death had brought his inheritance to Philip the Bold, duke of Burgundy.

The Ghent revolt was about the autonomy of a major commercial city, but it was also about the status of artisans, taxation, and political participation. Textile workers lived with the constant insecurity of a trade exposed to wars, embargoes, and the cost of wool, and their guilds' hold on civic power was a form of protection. The revolt thus differed from the rural rebellions of France and England: its participants were organized in institutions with political rights, and their conflict was with a prince rather than with local lords. It nonetheless belongs in a chapter on social change because it shows the capacity of skilled and semi skilled urban workers to act politically, and because its outcome shows the limits of princely victory: Ghent's eventual submission did not erase its communal privileges, since the Peace of Tournai confirmed them and granted an amnesty, while requiring the city to recognize its ruler and abandon its English alliance. The broader effects of the conflict on the Burgundian state are examined in Chapter 54.

Ghent was part of a wider cluster of urban unrest in the early 1380s. In France, Rouen rose in the Harelle in February 1382, and Paris in the Maillotin revolt in March, both against taxation in the unsettled period after the death of Charles V in 1380. In Languedoc, bands called Tuchins attacked tax collectors and the wealthy. Historians have debated whether these movements formed a connected phenomenon, an issue raised by Michel Mollat and Philippe Wolff in a classic study of popular revolutions of the period, or whether similar grievances produced separate, local reactions. The evidence for direct coordination is thin, but the shared context of taxation, war, and economic dislocation is clear.

Section 52.11

Florence and the Ciompi Revolt of 1378

Florence in the later fourteenth century was one of the largest and richest cities of Europe, with its wealth built on banking and the manufacture of woolen cloth. The cloth industry employed thousands of workers, from combers and carders who prepared the wool to weavers, dyers, and finishers, under the control of wealthy merchants organized in the Wool Guild, the Arte della Lana. The Guild also exercised jurisdiction over the workers, which gave employers legal power over their employees. The city's political life was governed by guilds, but many of the lowest paid workers had no guild of their own and thus no formal political voice. Contemporaries and later historians used the term popolo minuto, the little people, for these workers and other poor residents.

Historical Vocabulary
Popolo Minuto and Ciompi

Popolo minuto ("little people") was a Florentine term for the lower strata of the city, especially wage workers in the textile trades who were excluded from guild membership and political office. Ciompi was the name given to the wool workers who took part in the revolt of 1378; its origin is uncertain, and it may derive from a French word for companions or from a regional term. The term should not be confused with a modern proletariat. The Ciompi were workers in a craft industry organized through merchant employers and guild jurisdiction, and their demands concerned guild membership, wage conditions, and political inclusion as much as ownership or class conflict in a modern sense.

The revolt grew out of a political and fiscal crisis. Florence had fought the War of the Eight Saints against the papacy between 1375 and 1378, a conflict that was expensive and left the city deeply in debt, with forced loans falling heavily on citizens. Factional conflict among the city's elite families sharpened, and in the summer of 1378 political disturbances among the guild community opened an opportunity for workers. In July, wool workers and others rose in the streets, seized the Palazzo della Signoria, and forced a change in government. A wool carder named Michele di Lando became the head of the city's executive body, and the new regime created new guilds to represent the previously excluded workers, ensured that members of the new bodies obtained a share in political offices, and enacted relief measures concerning debts and taxes.

The regime lasted only weeks. Workers pressed for further concessions, including wage increases and restrictions on the Wool Guild, but divisions emerged between more moderate leaders and radical groups, and the established guilds, supported by other economic interests, regained control. By the end of August the guild created for the Ciompi had been abolished, and over the following years the city's government moved toward a narrower oligarchy under which the influential families, notably the Albizzi, dominated. Many participants were exiled or executed. Florence's later political history, including the rise of the Medici, examined in Chapter 56, was shaped by this experience, since the city's elites had learned how quickly the excluded could threaten their position.

The Ciompi revolt does not fit modern categories. It was neither a simple wage dispute nor a revolution of an industrial proletariat, and it took place in a city where wealth was concentrated in the hands of employers who also controlled political institutions. It nevertheless shows how exclusion from guilds could produce a political demand, and how workers could briefly enter government in a Mediterranean city, though the evidence for their motives comes largely from chroniclers who were hostile or at least not sympathetic.

Section 52.12

Guilds, Masters, Journeymen, Apprentices, and the Urban Poor

The great revolts were only the most visible expression of urban tension. Late medieval towns were hierarchical societies in which craft guilds regulated production, training, quality, and entry into trades, and in many towns guild masters also held political power. At the top of a craft stood masters, who owned workshops and tools and employed others. Below them came journeymen, who had completed apprenticeship but did not own a shop, and apprentices, who lived in the master's household and were trained under contract. Beyond the guilds lay a wide population of unskilled workers, casual laborers, servants, and the poor, who lacked organization and political representation.

Labor scarcity improved the position of skilled workers, but also provoked conflict within the crafts. Masters, whose interests were threatened by rising wages and by journeymen who could move to other towns, sought to restrict entry into mastership, extend the length of apprenticeship, and limit the number of journeymen who could set up independently. Journeymen responded by forming their own associations, especially in German towns, which provided mutual support and sometimes organized work stoppages and boycotts. Documented disputes of this kind, including episodes involving weavers and other trades in the Rhineland, appear in the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, though the sources are fragmentary and often record such action as sedition. The Cologne weavers' revolt of 1369 to 1371, which brought a temporary expansion of craft political power before the weavers' defeat in November 1371, shows that textile workers could challenge ruling elites in towns outside Flanders and Italy.

The urban poor appear in the sources mainly in the context of provisioning and public order. Town councils regulated the price and quality of bread, meat, and ale, and disturbances over food shortages and prices recur in the records. Taxation also touched the poor through excise duties on basic goods, which were a common cause of urban revolts such as the Harelle and the Maillotin rising in 1382. Many towns, anxious about vagrants and beggars, distinguished between those deserving of charity and able bodied people who were expected to work, a distinction echoed in the Ordinance of Labourers. Towns were also destinations for rural migrants, whose arrival could depress wages in some trades and intensify competition, and who were sometimes subjected to restrictions that mirrored those imposed on rural workers.

Urban political conflict therefore involved more than a division between rich and poor. Patricians, guild masters, journeymen, and unskilled workers each had distinct interests, and alliances shifted. Guild masters could join with workers against merchant elites, as in Ghent, and then act against workers who threatened their own position, as in Florence. Understanding this complexity prevents the oversimplification of urban revolts as moments of unity among "the people."

Section 52.13

Women, Work, Household Economy, and Demographic Change

Women worked in every part of the late medieval economy: in agriculture, in household production, in textile manufacture, in brewing and retail trade, and as servants. After the Black Death, labor scarcity increased demand for women's labor in some settings, and wage rates for women rose, as they did for men. Women took on agricultural tasks such as weeding, reaping, and tending livestock, and the demand for textile workers, especially spinners, was high in regions with a developed cloth industry. Women in towns could support themselves as servants, as brewers and sellers of ale, or as workers in the cloth trades.

Historians have long debated whether the plague "liberated" women. The evidence suggests a more qualified conclusion. Women's wages remained lower than men's for comparable work, and legislation such as the Statute of Labourers set separate and lower rates for them in some tasks. Guilds often limited women's participation, and in some trades women were pushed out as competition and commercialization increased. Brewing in England, for example, was heavily female in the generations after the plague, but over the following centuries commercial brewing became increasingly male dominated. Widows had notable opportunities, since they could inherit property, continue a husband's trade, or hold land as tenants, though widowhood also carried economic risk and, in some places, pressure to remarry.

Regional patterns of marriage and household formation differed. In northwestern Europe, evidence from later periods suggests a pattern of relatively late marriage and extensive life cycle service, in which young people worked in other households before marrying, which is sometimes called the European marriage pattern, though its origins and chronology are debated. In Tuscany, the Florentine tax survey, or catasto, of 1427 shows a different pattern, with young brides and a large age gap between spouses. Such differences meant that labor scarcity affected women's lives differently in different regions. Inheritance practices, dowry systems, and the strength of lordly and guild controls shaped the options available. The simple claim that the plague created a golden age for women is therefore not supported by the evidence, though real and sometimes lasting opportunities appeared in particular settings.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Post Plague Household

A village household in the later fourteenth century was both a family and an economic unit, with husband, wife, children, and sometimes servants or lodgers contributing to production. Following the plague, many households consisted of survivors who had inherited land from kin, and the vacant holdings in the village offered opportunities to enlarge. The household economy combined work on the holding, wage labor on larger farms or the lord's demesne, production of cloth, cheese, or ale for sale, and the use of common land and woods. Evidence from documents and excavation suggests that, in parts of England, some peasant housing was rebuilt more durably in the fifteenth century, that diets in many regions included more meat, ale, and wheaten bread than before, and that possessions such as metal pots and furnishings became more common, though these improvements varied and were not shared by the poorest.

Recurrent epidemics meant that families remained vulnerable. Children were often among the victims of the outbreak of 1361, and the loss of an adult worker could push a household into poverty. Charity from churches, guilds, and neighbors helped some households, and the attitude of elites toward the poor, as the labor laws show, distinguished between those able to work and those thought unable. The experience of ordinary people thus combined greater opportunities with persistent insecurity.

Section 52.14

Social Mobility, Consumption, and New Aspirations

The deaths of so many people created vacancies in tenures, workshops, and offices, and the survivors who filled them sometimes rose in status. In the countryside, a peasant who acquired several vacated holdings could become a substantial farmer, and a class of prosperous tenants, called in England by the fifteenth century yeomen, took shape, holding larger farms, leasing demesnes, and serving as village officials and jurors. Wage workers who saved could acquire a holding, and in towns, young migrants with skills could establish themselves. Mobility was real but limited, and it depended on the availability of land and capital as well as on the individual's circumstances. For many laborers and servants, the best outcome was a more secure position, not a rise into a higher station.

Higher incomes enabled some households to spend more on food, drink, clothing, and household goods, and contemporary observers noticed. English poetry of the later fourteenth century, including William Langland's Piers Plowman in its different versions, complains that laborers were no longer content with plain food and expected better, and later writers such as John Gower, writing after 1381, expressed alarm at the presumption of lower orders. These works are literary and satirical, and reflect elite attitudes more than the facts of consumption, but they illustrate a widespread anxiety that the old marks of rank were becoming blurred.

Governments responded with sumptuary legislation, laws restricting dress and sometimes food according to rank. English statutes of 1363 prescribed which cloths and furs persons of different social levels might wear, and Italian cities regulated clothing, including women's dress, with detailed rules. Such laws expressed an ideal of a hierarchical society in which appearance reflected status and, like the labor laws, they were enforced unevenly. They also reflected moral concerns about luxury and display, and fiscal motives, since fines produced revenue. Sumptuary laws are valuable evidence that people were spending more on dress and that those with power regarded this as a problem.

The history of mobility and consumption must not be overstated. Many people remained poor, landless, and vulnerable, and inequality persisted and in some places grew, as wealthier villagers accumulated land. Mobility benefited those who could take advantage of new opportunities and was lower where lords tightly controlled land and movement. It nonetheless contributed to a more commercial, more differentiated rural society in western Europe by the fifteenth century.

Section 52.15

How Elites Adapted

It would be a serious misreading to describe the period as one in which commoners rose and nobles fell. Elites were not passive, and many adapted successfully. The leasing of demesnes, described in Section 52.5, turned lords into rentiers, and although rents declined in some regions, a lord with sound administration and large estates could maintain a comfortable income. Lords negotiated with tenants, offered concessions to retain them, and sometimes granted long leases or secure tenure in return for stable payments. Many shifted toward pastoral farming, especially sheep raising for the wool trade, which required less labor than arable farming, and in parts of England and Castile this transformed the use of land. In Tuscany, landowners increasingly used sharecropping, known as mezzadria, in which a tenant family worked a farm in return for a share of the produce, a system that spread over the following century and that bound tenants to the land through contract rather than servile status.

Elites also turned to other sources of income and power. Nobles served kings and princes as soldiers, officials, and administrators, and war, despite its costs, offered opportunities for ransom and office. In England, lords built networks of retainers who wore their livery and supported them in local politics and in the courts, and historians continue to debate how far this "bastard feudalism" reflected a weakening of older ties or merely their adaptation. Lords also allied with prosperous peasants and urban elites, who often shared an interest in maintaining order and who served as jurors, officials, and local leaders. The relationship was complex: the same wealthy villagers who led resistance in 1381 in some places served the lord's interests in others.

Governments, in turn, intervened to protect the interests of the propertied through labor laws, sumptuary legislation, and, after 1381, greater caution in taxation. Elites also used violence and law selectively, and the brutal suppression of the Jacquerie and of the English rising shows the readiness of the propertied to defend their position. The result was not the collapse of the old elite but its reconfiguration, with a new emphasis on cash relations, contract, and political management. This adaptability helps explain why the social hierarchies of western Europe, though modified, endured into the early modern period.

  • Labor services declined and money rents grew, especially in western Europe, and many lords leased out demesnes.
  • Wage labor became more mobile and better paid in many regions, and some tenants gained secure tenures such as copyhold.
  • Governments tried new forms of labor and sumptuary regulation, and ordinary people showed new political assertiveness.

What Remained Continuous?

  • Lords retained property, jurisdiction, and political influence, and servile status survived on some estates for generations.
  • Gender inequality, wealth inequality, and the dependence of most people on agriculture and household production persisted.
  • Governments continued to rely on coercion, taxation, and the cooperation of local elites.

Who experienced change most directly? Tenants and wage workers in commercialized regions of western Europe, and skilled urban workers, saw the most change. Who experienced greater continuity? The poorest laborers, many women, and the dependent peasantry of regions where lordship intensified. Why the pattern matters: It shows that social change occurred through gradual shifts in bargaining power, not through a single break, and that the same demographic shock produced different results in different institutional settings.

Section 52.16

Regional Divergence across Late Medieval Europe

The central lesson of the comparative evidence is that the Black Death produced no single social outcome. In England, northern France, and the Low Countries, the combination of dense commercial networks, active courts, accessible towns, and weakened labor services led to commutation, leasing, and the gradual decline of personal servitude, with a more differentiated and commercial rural society. In parts of Italy, the growth of sharecropping and urban investment in land reshaped the countryside along different lines, with tenants bound by contract and with cities exercising political control over their hinterlands. In Flanders and northern Italy, the largest movements arose in towns dominated by textile production.

In other regions, lords were able to tighten rather than loosen control. In Catalonia, the remences, peasants bound to the land who could leave only by paying a redemption fee, and who suffered the so called bad customs, including payments for marriage, inheritance, and for the lord's claim on property, saw their position worsen in the late fourteenth century as lords, facing declining revenue, enforced servile obligations more strictly. Their grievances produced organized agitation by the 1380s and eventually armed conflict in the fifteenth century, including the remence wars of 1462 to 1472 and 1484 to 1485. Their resistance, and the intervention of the crown, which sought to weaken the nobility, led in 1486 to the Sentence of Guadalupe, issued by Ferdinand II, which abolished personal remença and the principal bad customs in return for redemption payments, while leaving ordinary rents and other feudal or emphyteutic obligations intact. In Valencia and parts of Aragon, the subject population included Muslim mudéjar peasants under lordship, whose experience combined religious and social subordination. In 1391, amid religious hostility, political instability, and social tension, anti-Jewish violence spread through parts of Castile and the Crown of Aragon, a reminder that unrest could also fall on minorities, as examined further in Chapter 49.

East of the Elbe, in Brandenburg, Mecklenburg, Pomerania, Poland, and neighboring regions, the situation is more complex, and this chapter treats it comparatively, since Chapter 54 owns the institutional history of those regions. Earlier settlement by migrants had often offered tenants relatively favorable terms, but in the later Middle Ages and early modern period, nobles in many areas strengthened their jurisdiction over peasants, restricted movement, and increased labor services. Historians describe the later outcome as a "second serfdom," though the chronology, extent, and causes remain disputed, and most of its consolidation occurred after 1500. The contrast with the west suggests that labor scarcity could lead to greater coercion when lords controlled courts, markets, and political institutions, and when peasants lacked alternatives or collective means of resistance. Comparison beyond Europe is suggestive: historians of Mamluk Egypt have also documented agrarian dislocation and revenue decline after the plague, though under very different institutions.

Historical Debate

Demography or Class Power: What Explains Late Medieval Agrarian Change?

The question: Why did labor scarcity after the Black Death weaken serfdom in western Europe and strengthen it in parts of eastern Europe? Did demographic change drive the outcome, or did the balance of power among classes and the institutions that expressed it?

Interpretation A: Demography as the principal driver. Associated with historians such as M. M. Postan, this view stresses that population movements, the pressure of numbers on land before 1348 and the scarcity of labor afterward, determined rents, wages, and tenure. Lords lost power when tenants became scarce, so commutation and the decline of servitude followed in regions where lords competed for workers.
Interpretation B: Class power and institutions. Associated with Robert Brenner in a famous 1976 article that provoked wide debate, and with other historians influenced by Marxist traditions, this view argues that demographic change alone cannot explain divergent outcomes, because similar population movements led to different results in England, France, and eastern Europe. The decisive factors were the relative strength of lords and peasant communities, the nature of the state, and the property relations that structured conflict.

Evidence: Both views draw on estate records, population evidence, and comparative regional history. The demographic approach finds support in the broad correlation between population decline and falling rents, while the class power approach highlights divergences between regions, such as Catalonia and England, and the role of courts, local institutions, and the state in determining outcomes. Many historians now combine elements of both, as well as attention to markets, urban demand, and political structures.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians interpret not only the late Middle Ages but also the origins of agrarian capitalism and the divergent paths of European regions. It also shapes how we understand peasant agency: as a response to impersonal forces or as an active contest over power.

Student task: Identify evidence in this chapter that supports each interpretation, then explain what additional evidence would help to decide between them.

Section 52.17

Revolt, Social Change, and the Limits of Transformation

By the early fifteenth century, the societies of western Europe had changed in important ways. Wages in many places were higher in real terms than before the plague, labor services had declined sharply, many tenants held secure and cheaper tenures, workers were more mobile, and more people could acquire land or enter skilled trades. Ordinary people had learned to use courts, petitions, and collective action, and the language of freedom and the common good had acquired a political resonance that officials could not ignore. The experience of 1358, 1378, and 1381 left memories among elites and common people alike, and when new revolts occurred in later centuries, including the German Peasants' War of 1524 to 1525 examined in Chapter 62, rebels and rulers drew on a longer tradition of conflict.

Yet the limits of change were equally important. Hierarchy persisted. Lords kept property and jurisdiction, and in some regions strengthened them. Gender inequality in wages and legal status endured, wealth inequality continued, and governments retained and often expanded their coercive powers. The revolts themselves were suppressed, their leaders killed, and their immediate demands, from the abolition of serfdom in England to the political inclusion of the Ciompi in Florence, were not achieved. Yet it does not follow that nothing changed. The most significant transformations resulted not from the revolts but from the cumulative effects of demographic change, market adjustment, and everyday resistance, with the revolts serving as evidence of the tensions behind those processes and, on occasion, as influences on elite behavior. Nor did rebellion cause every later social transformation.

The social history of the post plague decades therefore shows both the capacity of ordinary people to shape their circumstances and the resilience of established power. It prepares the ground for the following chapters. Chapter 53 turns to the crisis of the western church, where dissent and reform would challenge authority in a different sphere, and Chapter 54 broadens the view to the political and commercial powers of the European peripheries. The social changes traced here would continue to shape the commercial, religious, and political history of Europe into the Renaissance and Reformation, and the economic questions raised here recur in the history of enclosure, wage labor, and agrarian capitalism examined in later volumes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the demographic catastrophe of the Black Death and its recurrences changed the relationships among workers, tenants, lords, townspeople, and governments in late medieval Europe. Its central argument is that labor scarcity altered bargaining power, but that the outcomes depended on institutions, local conditions, and political choices, so that the consequences differed sharply from region to region.

After 1348, wages rose in many regions, and workers and tenants gained greater ability to move, refuse, and negotiate. Real wages depended on prices, which behaved unevenly, and the benefits were not shared equally across occupations, genders, or regions. Evidence from account rolls, court rolls, statutes, and chronicles allows historians to reconstruct these changes, but it is shaped by the interests of those who produced it, and survivorship bias limits what can be known about the poorest.

Governments and elites responded with legislation. The English Ordinance of 1349 and Statute of 1351, with comparable measures in France, Castile, Portugal, and elsewhere, attempted to freeze wages and restrict mobility. Enforcement was uneven, employers themselves competed for workers, and the laws, applied by local landowners, became a persistent grievance.

On estates, labor services declined, commutation and leasing spread, and tenants gained more secure tenures such as copyhold, yet lords retained property and jurisdiction, and servile status survived on some manors into the sixteenth century. Flight, litigation, concealment, refusal of services, and collective negotiation were the most widespread forms of resistance, and their cumulative effect was often greater than that of open rebellion.

The revolts of the later fourteenth century were responses to particular combinations of fiscal pressure, wartime disruption, and social tension. The Jacquerie of 1358 grew out of the crisis after Poitiers; the English rising of 1381 combined resentment of servile dues and labor laws with the anger provoked by the poll tax; the Ciompi revolt of 1378 and the revolt of Ghent expressed the political demands of textile workers and artisans. None was a modern class revolution. Participants were socially varied, their aims were expressed in the language of loyalty, custom, and the common good, and all were suppressed.

Within towns, tensions among masters, journeymen, apprentices, and the unskilled shaped guild politics, and urban unrest over taxation and food prices was frequent. The effects of demographic change on women were mixed: wages and employment opportunities improved in some settings, but women continued to earn less, faced restrictions from guilds and law, and experienced regionally varied patterns of marriage and household formation.

Social mobility increased for some, giving rise to prosperous tenants and more commercial villages, and higher consumption provoked anxieties expressed in literature and sumptuary legislation. Elites, however, adapted through leasing, sharecropping, pastoral farming, service to princes, and alliances with local notables, so that the result was a reconfiguration of power, not a collapse.

Regional divergence is the chapter's most important comparative lesson. England, northern France, and the Low Countries saw weakened serfdom and increased commercialization; Tuscany saw the growth of sharecropping; Catalonia saw intensified servile obligations and a long struggle culminating in the Sentence of Guadalupe of 1486; and lands east of the Elbe saw, over a longer period, a strengthening of lordship. Historians debate whether demographic or class power explanations best account for these differences, and many combine the two.

The limits of transformation were as significant as its extent. Hierarchy, wealth inequality, gender inequality, and coercive government endured. Revolts failed militarily, but their failure did not mean that nothing changed, and many changes occurred through slower processes. These developments set the social background for the religious, political, and commercial transformations examined in the following chapters.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Labor scarcity
A shortage of workers relative to available land and employment, produced after 1348 by population loss, and increasing the bargaining power of those who sold labor.
Nominal wage
The amount of money paid for work, without adjustment for the cost of living.
Real wage
The purchasing power of wages, calculated by comparing money wages with the prices of necessities.
Serfdom
A range of dependent tenures in which tenants were bound to a lord, owed dues, and lacked full freedom of movement and marriage.
Villeinage
The English form of servile tenure, in which a villein was unfree in relation to his lord.
Demesne
The part of a manor held directly by the lord and worked by labor services or hired workers.
Labor services
Work owed by tenants to the lord, such as weekly labor and extra work at harvest.
Commutation
The replacement of labor services by money payments.
Merchet
An English servile fine, paid for permission to marry or marry a daughter outside the manor.
Heriot
A death duty, usually the best beast or goods of a deceased tenant, owed to the lord.
Copyhold
A tenure held by copy of the manor court roll, with terms protected by custom.
Leasing of demesnes
The renting out of a lord's home farm to tenants, replacing direct management.
Mezzadria
Tuscan sharecropping, in which a farm family worked land in return for a share of the produce under contract.
Ordinance of Labourers
The 1349 English royal ordinance requiring able bodied persons to work at pre plague wages.
Statute of Labourers
The 1351 parliamentary law confirming and extending the ordinance, with wage rates and enforcement.
Great Rumour
The 1377 movement in which villagers in southern England sought exemption from servile dues on the basis of Domesday Book.
Poll tax
A tax levied per head, not on property; the English poll taxes of 1377 to 1380 were a trigger of the 1381 rising.
Jacquerie
The rural uprising of 1358 in northern France, named from the nickname Jacques Bonhomme.
Letters of remission
French royal pardons that recount the circumstances of crimes, a valuable source for rebel behavior and speech.
Popolo minuto
The "little people" of Florence, including workers excluded from guilds and political office.
Ciompi
The wool workers of Florence who took part in the revolt of 1378.
Wool Guild (Arte della Lana)
The powerful Florentine guild of wool manufacturers, which also exercised jurisdiction over workers.
Journeyman
A trained craftsman who worked for a master and did not own a workshop.
Sumptuary law
Legislation regulating dress, food, and display according to social rank.
Yeoman
A prosperous English farmer below the gentry, typical of the more differentiated rural society after the plague.
Remence
A Catalan peasant bound to the land who could leave only by paying a redemption fee.
Bad customs (mals usos)
The servile payments and claims to which Catalan remences were subject.
Everyday resistance
Small, often individual or local acts, such as flight and refusal, by which dependent people limited the claims made on them.
Survivorship bias
The distortion produced when surviving records overrepresent certain institutions or groups, such as large ecclesiastical estates.
Second serfdom
A historians' term for the intensification of peasant dependence in parts of eastern Europe in the later medieval and early modern periods, disputed in chronology and extent.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the crisis of the fourteenth century was not only a story of death and disorder but also of negotiation. Ordinary people, by moving, bargaining, litigating, and sometimes rising in arms, helped to determine how a shattered society would be rebuilt. The chapter reveals how labor, land, and political power were intertwined, and how a demographic event acquired social meaning through the institutions and conflicts that surrounded it.

It also shows how different Europe's regions could be. The same shock that weakened servitude in England and the Low Countries intensified it in Catalonia and, over a longer period, in lands east of the Elbe. This lesson reinforces a theme of the textbook: that no single region, whether England, France, or Italy, defines a European norm, and that comparison among regions is essential to understanding historical change.

The chapter contributes to the argument of Volume II by marking the end of the high medieval agrarian order and the emergence of more commercial, mobile, and differentiated societies. Its themes of wage labor, market relations, state regulation of work, and popular political claims recur in later volumes, in the Reformation's peasant movements, in early modern debates about poverty and labor, and in the industrial era. Yet these later developments were not predetermined, and the chapter's cautions against teleology apply: the revolts did not cause the later transformations, and the transformations were not inevitable.

Chapter 53 now turns from social conflict to the institutional and religious crisis of the western church, where the authority of the papacy and clergy would be contested in a different arena. Understanding the social tensions traced here helps explain why calls for reform found audiences among many groups, and why religious and social questions often became entangled in the centuries that followed.
