Church, Doctrine, and Society in Late Antiquity

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Constantine's patronage did not settle Christianity. It changed the conditions under which Christians disagreed with one another, and it turned questions that had once belonged to Christian communities into questions that emperors, city councils, soldiers, and crowds all felt entitled to answer.

In the spring of 386 CE, the bishop of Milan found himself in a standoff with the imperial court in whose city he lived. The emperor Valentinian II was a boy, and his mother, Justina, adhered to a form of Christianity that Ambrose, the city's bishop, regarded as false. The court asked for a basilica in Milan for the use of its own Christian congregation. Ambrose refused. According to Ambrose's own letters, and to the later recollection of Augustine, who was then living in the city, crowds of Milanese Christians gathered in and around the church while soldiers were posted nearby, and the congregation sustained itself with psalms and hymns. The court eventually withdrew its demand. Nothing in this episode was possible in the year 300. A bishop had no such standing in the eyes of the state, an emperor's household had no reason to seek a great church for its own worship, and a dispute over the wording of a creed would not have reached the palace.

The Milan confrontation captures the paradox at the center of this chapter. In the decades after Constantine's death in 337, the Christian church became far more powerful, wealthier, more visible, and more closely tied to the Roman state than it had ever been. At the same time it was more divided than ever, and its divisions were more consequential than ever, precisely because emperors now cared how they were resolved. The church did not emerge from Constantine's reign as a unified institution awaiting further growth. It emerged as a partially organized network of communities, spread across a very large, linguistically diverse empire, whose members disagreed about the nature of Christ, the authority of bishops, the validity of sacraments, the proper Christian way of life, and the proper relationship between the church and the emperor.

This chapter examines what happened inside Christian institutions, and across Roman society, once Christianity possessed imperial attention and support. Chapter 25 examined how Diocletian and Constantine reconstructed the Roman state and transformed Christianity's legal and political position. Chapter 26 shifts the focus away from the emperors as builders of a new order and toward the church itself: its councils and theological controversies, its bishops, its ascetics and monks, its households and charitable practices, its conflicts with dissenting Christians and with adherents of other traditions, and its uneven penetration of city and countryside. The chapter's central argument is that Christianization was a long, regionally variable, and contested social process, not a single event, and that neither Constantine's conversion nor Theodosius I's legislation produced a fully Christian society.

The chapter also insists on the persistence of religious diversity. Traditional cults, philosophical schools, and household rituals, which Christians came to lump together under the label "pagan," continued to be practiced in many regions through and beyond the fourth century. Jewish communities remained substantial and active in Palestine, Syria, Asia Minor, Italy, North Africa, and elsewhere. Christian communities themselves were split among rival groups, each of which considered itself the true church. To write about these developments as a story of Christian triumph would misrepresent the evidence, which shows a society negotiating religious change under conditions of uneven power, and sometimes of violence.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich, but it is also unusually partisan. Much of what survives was written by bishops and their supporters: conciliar creeds and canons, sermons, letters, treatises, and hagiographical biographies. Such sources tell us a great deal about what churchmen wanted, feared, and argued, and less about what ordinary worshippers, non-Christians, and defeated Christian factions thought. Imperial laws, preserved chiefly in the Theodosian Code compiled in the 430s, show what emperors wished to prescribe, not necessarily what happened. Archaeology, inscriptions, papyri from Egypt, and the writings of non-Christian authors such as Ammianus Marcellinus, Libanius, and Symmachus help correct the picture. Throughout the chapter, students should ask whose voice a source represents and whose voice is missing.

Within Part IV, this chapter follows the imperial reconstruction of Chapter 25 and prepares the ground for Chapter 27, which examines migration, warfare, and the formation of successor states in the western empire. The two chapters overlap chronologically, since the church's development and the empire's military and political fragmentation unfolded together, but they treat different subjects. Within Volume I, Chapter 26 shows how one of the ancient world's most consequential transformations occurred: the movement of Christianity from a minority religion into a major institution of Roman public life, a movement that later shaped the medieval Christendoms examined in Volume II without yet having produced them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why imperial patronage after Constantine intensified, rather than resolved, disputes among Christians.
  2. Analyze the Arian controversy and the Council of Nicaea, including the meaning of homoousios, and explain what Nicaea did and did not settle.
  3. Trace the shifting doctrinal coalitions of the fourth century, and explain why "Arian versus Catholic" is a misleading description of them.
  4. Describe the growth of episcopal authority, distinguishing the fourth-century bishop from later medieval bishops and popes.
  5. Compare solitary and communal forms of monasticism and assess the roles of Antony, Pachomius, Basil of Caesarea, and women ascetics.
  6. Interpret evidence for Christian household life, gender, charity, and popular religious practice, recognizing the limits of clerical sources.
  7. Assess the reign of Julian as evidence that Christianization was neither complete nor irreversible in the 360s.
  8. Distinguish the multiple processes that together made up Christianization, and explain why the image of a sudden conversion of the empire is inaccurate.
  9. Evaluate the relationship between imperial legislation and social reality, using the Edict of Thessalonica of 380 as a case study.
  10. Contextualize the confrontation between Ambrose and Theodosius I without exaggerating the power of either bishop or emperor.
  11. Identify the continued presence of Jewish communities and traditional religious practice in the late fourth-century Roman world.
  12. Connect the developments of this chapter to the political and military transformations of the western empire examined in Chapter 27.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did imperial support for Christianity make internal Christian disagreements more, rather than less, politically dangerous?
  2. How did fourth-century Christians decide what counted as correct belief, and what role did councils, bishops, and emperors each play in that process?
  3. In what ways did the bishop become a new kind of public authority in Roman cities, and in what ways did bishops continue to operate within older Roman systems of patronage and civic life?
  4. Why did ascetic and monastic movements arise when they did, and how did they challenge or reinforce the authority of bishops?
  5. What evidence allows historians to study everyday Christian practice, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  6. What did the reign of Julian reveal about the strength and vulnerability of imperial Christianity?
  7. How should historians distinguish between what late Roman laws prescribed and what actually changed in the lives of Romans?
  8. Why is it inaccurate to describe the late fourth-century empire as a "Christian empire" without qualification, and what is a more careful description?

Historical Context

Chapter 23 traced how Christian communities emerged within the religious world of the early Roman Empire and grew despite intermittent persecution, and Chapter 25 examined Diocletian's reforms, the Great Persecution that began in 303, and Constantine's rise, his patronage of Christianity, and his foundation of Constantinople. Students should carry forward several conclusions from those chapters. By Constantine's death in 337, Christianity was legally protected, lavishly favored by the emperor, and increasingly supported through imperial subsidy, tax and service exemptions for clergy, and church building. Bishops had become public figures. Emperors had intervened in ecclesiastical disputes, including the schism in North Africa that would become the Donatist controversy and the dispute in Alexandria over the teaching of the presbyter Arius. Christians were probably not yet an empire-wide majority in 337, but no reliable census exists, and modern estimates of the Christian share of the population differ substantially, so any figure should be treated as an informed guess.

The Christian communities that received imperial favor were not uniform. They differed by language, since Greek dominated theological discussion in the east, Latin in most of the west and in Roman Africa, and Syriac and Coptic were the everyday languages of substantial Christian populations in Syria, Mesopotamia, and Egypt. They differed by regional tradition, since the churches of Alexandria, Antioch, Rome, Carthage, and Asia Minor had developed distinct habits of worship, exegesis, and organization. They differed in their memory of persecution, which had been intense in some regions and light in others, and which left a bitter question in its wake: how should the church treat those who had handed over scriptures or otherwise compromised under pressure? Some communities rested on relatively well-off urban elites, others on artisans, small farmers, and the poor. All of them regarded the truth of their teaching as universal, which is precisely why disagreement about that truth could not be shrugged off.

The religious landscape surrounding these communities was crowded. Roman religion consisted of civic cults tied to particular cities, state cults of the emperor and the Roman gods, household rituals, mystery religions such as those of Isis, Mithras, and Demeter at Eleusis, local sanctuaries of a vast range of deities, and philosophical traditions, especially Platonism in its late antique forms, that offered their own accounts of the divine. Judaism was an ancient religion whose communities held specific legal protections, exemptions, and recognized institutions within the empire, and it had vibrant communities in nearly every region. Other movements, such as Manichaeism, which had spread from Sasanian Persia, added further diversity, and Christian communities of very different types coexisted with all of them. Constantine's conversion changed the relative standing of these traditions but did not remove any of them from the scene.

Two boundaries frame this chapter's scope. It concentrates on the years from Constantine's death in 337 to the death of Theodosius I in 395, reaching back to the Council of Nicaea in 325 because Chapter 25 deliberately deferred the theological meaning of that council to the present chapter, and glancing forward to about 400 only where needed. It also concentrates on church institutions, theology, and religious society. The military and political consequences of the movements of Goths, Huns, and other peoples, including the disaster at Adrianople in 378, belong to Chapter 27, and the chapter refers to them only where religious history requires. Nor does the chapter retell the reign of Constantine, which Chapter 25 has already examined.

A final piece of context concerns geography. Christianity's institutional centers in this period lay predominantly in the eastern Mediterranean and in Roman North Africa, in cities such as Alexandria, Antioch, Caesarea in Cappadocia, Carthage, and, from its foundation, Constantinople, with important western centers at Rome and Milan. The empire was administratively divided, with the eastern and western halves often ruled by different emperors, and religious conflicts frequently followed this political geography. Different imperial jurisdictions and regional episcopal networks shaped the alliances of particular disputes, but these divisions did not yet constitute a fixed religious split between a Latin West and a Greek East. The much later separation between Latin and Orthodox Christendom, and the history of the eastern Roman Empire (called Byzantine by modern historians though its inhabitants understood it as Roman), are traced in Chapters 36 through 41.

Section 26.1

The Church after Constantine

The starting point for understanding the church in the decades after 337 is a sober assessment of what Constantine had and had not accomplished. He had changed Christianity's political position. Christian clergy received exemptions from certain municipal burdens, churches could receive legacies, the emperor funded major buildings in Rome, Jerusalem, Constantinople, and elsewhere, and imperial officials regularly dealt with bishops as representatives of their communities. What Constantine had not done was create doctrinal agreement, a single administrative church, or a demonstrably Christian majority across the empire. His own interventions, as Chapter 25 noted, were driven by his desire for religious concord as a support for imperial order. That desire proved an unreliable guide, because concord could be defined only by choosing sides.

The structure of Christian communities at this moment is worth describing carefully. The basic unit was the local church of a city, headed by a bishop (Greek episkopos, "overseer"), assisted by presbyters (elders, the ancestors of the later priest), deacons, and other ministers. By the fourth century, the model of a single bishop in each city, who alone ordained clergy and presided over the central rites, was widely accepted, though variations survived, especially in Egypt and in rural areas. Churches in a civil province were gradually grouped together under the leadership of the bishop of the provincial capital, an arrangement given recognition by the Council of Nicaea, and the terms "metropolitan" and "province" entered ecclesiastical language. These structures paralleled the administrative geography of the empire, a resemblance that reflected both convenience and imitation, but they never merged with it. Ecclesiastical authority ran through persuasion, prestige, and shared practice, as well as through the ability of bishops to depose one another and, increasingly, to call upon the state.

The church was also very unevenly distributed. In many eastern cities, especially in Asia Minor, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, Christians formed a substantial share of urban populations by the mid-fourth century. Christian presence was highly uneven. It was especially strong in many eastern Mediterranean cities and parts of Roman Africa, while large areas of the western provinces and many rural districts were less densely Christianized. Rural Christian communities nevertheless existed in many regions, so the contrast was one of degree and pace, not of urban presence versus rural absence. Christian churches stood as new landmarks among older temples, baths, theaters, and forums. The visual presence of Christianity in a city's landscape grew steadily across the century, but its social presence in the surrounding villages grew more slowly. Chapters 22 and 23 describe the cities and provinces of the empire at its height, and this chapter asks how Christianity moved across that landscape.

Finally, the diversity of Christian belief and practice must be kept in mind. Disagreements about the following questions, some already old and some newly urgent, ran through the communities of the 330s: the relationship between the Father and the Son, and later the Holy Spirit; the relationship between the human and divine in Christ, a topic that would dominate the fifth century; the authority of a bishop, particularly one whose predecessors or consecrators were suspected of having yielded to persecutors; the boundaries of the church, especially the status of baptism and ordination performed by dissenting Christians; the appropriate form of Christian life, from the married household to the ascetic community; and the proper role of the emperor in religious affairs. None of these questions was purely theoretical. Each affected who would hold a bishop's chair, control a church's wealth, or be admitted to communion.

The result was the paradox noted in the chapter opening. The church grew stronger at exactly the moment its internal divisions became more politically consequential. Before Constantine, a dispute among Christians had few external consequences. After Constantine, it could shape imperial policy, cause the exile of prominent men, and provoke riots in major cities. This is the first great theme of the chapter, and it begins with the most famous controversy of the age.

Section 26.2

Nicaea and the Problem of Christian Doctrine

Around 318 CE, a dispute erupted in Alexandria between the city's bishop, Alexander, and one of his presbyters, Arius, a well-regarded and popular teacher. The dispute concerned extremely difficult questions about God's unity, the divinity of Christ, and the relationship between the Father and the Son. Arius, as his teaching is known from the fragments and hostile reports that survive, sought to protect the uniqueness and unoriginated character of God the Father. He held that the Son was begotten of the Father and was therefore not eternal in the way the Father was, and not of the same being as the Father, though he was a perfect creature and a unique mediator of creation and salvation. Alexander and his supporters saw in this teaching a threat to Christian belief in the full divinity of Christ, in whom, they held, God himself had saved humanity. Students should resist the caricature of Arius as a man who simply "denied that Jesus was God." He was working within a tradition of Christian thought that emphasized the Father's primacy, and his opponents were working within another tradition that emphasized the Son's full share in divinity. Both sides appealed to scripture and to earlier teachers.

The dispute spread rapidly across the Greek east, partly through letters and partly through the songs and slogans that supporters of each side circulated. Bishops of major cities took sides, among them Eusebius of Nicomedia, a bishop with strong court connections who supported Arius, and Eusebius of Caesarea in Palestine, the church historian, who sought a moderate position. By 324, when Constantine defeated Licinius and became sole ruler of the empire, he inherited a religious quarrel in the east that threatened the unity he valued. He first tried to persuade the parties to reconcile by letter, and when that failed, he summoned bishops to a council at Nicaea (modern Iznik in northwestern Turkey) in 325, paying for their travel and hosting the gathering. The tradition that 318 bishops attended is symbolic and later, and modern estimates are lower, typically somewhere near 250 to 300, most from the eastern provinces, with only a handful from the Latin west.

The council addressed several issues. It considered the question of the Melitians, a schismatic group in Egypt; it took measures regarding the calculation of the date of Easter, seeking uniformity among Christian communities, though uniformity would take generations to achieve; and it adopted twenty canons, or disciplinary rulings, including provisions about ordinations, the authority of bishops in a province, the honor due to major sees, and the readmission of those who had lapsed. Its most famous work, however, was the creed. The council condemned Arius's teaching and adopted a statement of faith that affirmed that the Son was begotten, not made, and was homoousios with the Father, a Greek term usually rendered "of one substance" or "of one being." The creed ended with anathemas, or condemnations of propositions such as the claim that there was a time when the Son did not exist. Arius and two bishops who refused to sign were exiled.

Historical Vocabulary
Homoousios and "Nicene"

Homoousios is a Greek adjective, built from homo ("same") and ousia ("being" or "substance"), used by the Council of Nicaea to say that the Son shares the same divine being as the Father. The word did not appear in scripture, and it was ambiguous in earlier theological use, so that some bishops worried that it implied that Father and Son were not distinct persons at all. Constantine reportedly urged its acceptance, but many eastern bishops signed the creed with reservations. "Nicene" is a modern and later ancient label for those who upheld the Council of Nicaea's language, and it became clearer as a party designation only across the middle decades of the fourth century. It is also important not to confuse the creed adopted at Nicaea in 325 with the longer creed used in worship today, often called the Nicene Creed, which is closely associated with the Council of Constantinople of 381, though scholars debate exactly how it originated and it is first securely attested at the Council of Chalcedon in 451.

What Nicaea settled, therefore, was a set of formal statements and a condemnation, backed by the emperor. What it did not settle was the actual consensus of Christian communities. The word homoousios continued to trouble many bishops, and Constantine himself soon changed course, recalling some of Arius's supporters from exile within a few years. Athanasius, who had attended Nicaea as a deacon in Alexander's entourage and became bishop of Alexandria in 328, would spend much of his life defending the council's language, but the "Nicene" position was not universally accepted even in Alexandria's neighborhood for decades. Nicaea was also not immediately regarded as the paradigmatic "ecumenical council," a term meaning a gathering representing the whole inhabited world, that later tradition made it. Its authority grew over time as a result of the struggles that followed, not simply from the event itself. This is one of the chapter's major lessons: a council's decree and actual ecclesiastical consensus are not the same thing.

Historians therefore interpret Nicaea as an opening, not a conclusion. It established the pattern of an emperor summoning bishops, providing resources, and enforcing decisions with exile, a pattern that would recur many times. It established the creed as a tool of definition. And it launched a debate about the meaning of central terms that would occupy the church for two generations, and that in a sense continued in different forms for centuries.

Section 26.3

After Nicaea: Emperors, Bishops, and Competing Christianities

The three decades after Nicaea show how unstable doctrinal outcomes were when they depended on imperial favor. In the last years of Constantine's reign, the emperor allowed a reconsideration of the situation. Eusebius of Nicomedia returned from exile, and, in 335, a council at Tyre condemned Athanasius on charges relating to his conduct as bishop, after which Constantine exiled him to Trier. Arius himself was apparently about to be readmitted to communion at Constantinople when he died suddenly in 336. Constantine died the following year, reportedly baptized by Eusebius of Nicomedia on his deathbed, a detail that reminds us how close the Nicomedian bishop was to the court and how unhelpful the label "Arian" is for describing him.

Constantine's sons divided the empire, and after some years of civil conflict Constantius II, who ruled the east from 337 and the whole empire from 353 until his death in 361, became the emperor who most shaped the fourth-century church. Constantius was a devoted Christian and an energetic convener of councils. He wanted an empire united in a single confession, and he believed that the Nicene formula, with its controversial word, was an obstacle to unity. Over the years he sought various compromise formulas. Athanasius was exiled repeatedly, several times from Alexandria, and spent long periods in the west and in the Egyptian desert, where he found refuge among monks. Western bishops who refused to condemn him were themselves exiled, including Hilary of Poitiers and, for a time, Liberius, bishop of Rome. The Council of Serdica, held about 343 in the borderlands between east and west, ended with two mutually hostile groups of bishops issuing separate rulings, a sign of how regional episcopal blocs and imperial geography could shape a dispute, though not yet of a permanent division between Greek and Latin churches.

In the late 350s, Constantius supported a series of formulas at councils held in Sirmium, at Rimini in the west, at Seleucia in the east, and finally at Constantinople in 360. These statements rejected homoousios and related terms as unscriptural and affirmed that the Son was "like" the Father, the position designated Homoian (from Greek homoios, "like"). Other groups held different positions. Some, including Basil of Ancyra, preferred to say that the Son was of "similar substance," homoiousios, a stance that stood between the Nicene party and the Homoians. Others, known as Anomoeans, such as the theologians Aetius and Eunomius, went further and insisted that the Son was "unlike" the Father in essence. Still others held to Nicaea's wording. The coalitions among these groups shifted repeatedly. Homoiousians and Nicenes gradually found common ground, while Homoians and Anomoeans came into conflict with each other.

The term "Arian" is misleading when applied to these groups without qualification. It was largely a polemical label attached by opponents, especially by Athanasius and later Nicene writers, to a variety of Christians who mostly did not follow Arius himself and did not accept the name. Later church tradition grouped them together as "Arianism," a habit that has shaped the historical record and, in turn, older textbooks. Modern historians generally prefer to speak of the specific positions and the changing coalitions that held them. The distinction matters because it shows that fourth-century theological politics were fluid, and that Christians on the "wrong" side of later orthodoxy did not view themselves as heretics but as defenders of scripture, tradition, and often of imperial unity.

Historical Vocabulary
Orthodoxy, Heresy, and "Arian"

Orthodoxy (from Greek roughly "right belief" or "right glory") and heresy (from Greek hairesis, "choice" or "school") are terms that come out of the same disputes they describe. A position became "orthodox" when it prevailed and "heretical" when it lost, and the winners typically wrote the history. In this textbook, "orthodox" with a lowercase initial means a position later accepted as correct by a given church, while "Orthodox" with a capital refers to the Eastern Orthodox tradition discussed in Chapters 38 and 40. "Arian" is such a label: it names Christians whom their opponents linked to Arius, not a self-description used by most of them. Historians use the term with care, prefer specific designations such as Homoian, and remain aware that most theological positions of the period were formed in the course of debate, not in advance of it.

The emperors after Constantius illustrate the same instability. Julian, discussed in Section 26.7, withdrew imperial favor from Christianity in general. Valentinian I, who ruled the west from 364 to 375, was a Nicene Christian who mostly avoided interference in doctrinal disputes, while his brother Valens, who ruled the east until 378, supported Homoian Christians and sometimes coerced Nicene ones. Only under Theodosius I did imperial policy settle firmly, and for the long term, on the Nicene side. The doctrinal outcome was therefore contingent on imperial politics and on the decisions of individual rulers. Nicene Christianity did not triumph simply because its arguments were stronger, though many Christians believed they were. It triumphed because a series of developments, including the persuasive work of theologians and the eventual alliance with a ruler, aligned in its favor.

The intellectual side of this process should not be neglected. In the 360s and 370s, a group of theologians in Asia Minor, the Cappadocians Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, and Gregory of Nyssa, developed a vocabulary that helped bridge the parties. They distinguished between one divine ousia, "being," and three hypostaseis, a Greek term here understood as the distinct persons of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. That formulation allowed those who feared that homoousios erased the distinction between Father and Son to accept Nicaea's language, and it prepared the way for the settlement of 381. Meanwhile, in the Latin west, thinkers such as Hilary of Poitiers, who had learned Greek theology during his exile in Asia Minor, and Ambrose worked to bring Latin Christianity into line with the developing Nicene consensus. The doctrine of the Trinity, in other words, emerged in the form that later Christians would recognize as a product of these fourth-century controversies, not as a formula fully handed down at the beginning.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Christian Theological Disputes Become Imperial Political Problems?

Long-term causes

The growth of networks of bishops in communication with one another across the empire, and the shared conviction of Christians that their teaching was universally true, so that disagreement in one city concerned the whole church.

Short-term causes

Constantine's patronage, his desire for religious concord as a support for imperial unity, and his willingness to summon councils, pay for them, and enforce their outcomes with exile.

Event or transformation

Emperors began to convene councils, adjudicate appeals, and depose or restore bishops, so that doctrinal statements acquired political consequences and political outcomes shaped doctrine.

Immediate effects

Exile and reinstatement of bishops, competing councils issuing contradictory rulings, court factions aligned with theological parties, and popular mobilization in cities on behalf of favored bishops.

Long-term consequences

Closer entanglement of church and state, a lasting association between imperial authority and the definition of orthodoxy, and the growing importance of doctrinal labels as markers of communal identity.

Historical complexity

Imperial involvement could enforce a decision, but it could also intensify disputes, create new factions, and make a defeated party into martyrs. Emperors were not neutral arbiters, and their preferences changed.

Why it matters: Reading the fourth century as a simple contest between correct belief and error obscures the fact that the boundaries of orthodoxy were being drawn during the controversies themselves, under conditions that included imperial preference, personal rivalry, regional loyalty, and genuine intellectual difficulty.

Bishops and the Growth of Ecclesiastical Authority

One of the most important developments of the fourth century was the emergence of the bishop as a new kind of public authority. The outline of this change can be summarized in a sentence, but its texture varied enormously from city to city. Bishops had always been leaders of their communities. By the later fourth century, they had become teachers who preached regularly to large urban audiences, judges and arbitrators whose rulings people sought voluntarily, patrons who distributed charity and directed the resources of church endowments, representatives of their cities in dealings with governors and emperors, builders of churches, hospices, and cemeteries, and, in moments of crisis, political actors whose words and choices mattered to the whole civic community.

Several factors produced this expansion. Imperial legislation gave clergy exemption from certain municipal services, allowed churches to receive bequests, and, from Constantine onward, recognized bishops' arbitration of disputes for parties who chose it, a form of jurisdiction sometimes called episcopalis audientia. Historians continue to debate how widely and how often this arbitration was used and how formally it was recognized, and it is safest to say that it gave bishops an additional avenue of influence, not that it created a parallel court system. Church wealth, drawn from gifts of land and money and from imperial grants, allowed bishops to sponsor poor relief and building on a scale previously undertaken only by rich private patrons and city councils. The bishop's role in the liturgy, in preaching, and in deciding who might be admitted to communion gave him spiritual authority that reinforced the social kind.

It would nevertheless be a mistake to write that bishops "replaced" Roman government. They operated within Roman civic society and largely by its rules. Municipal councils, the local landowning elite, and imperial officials continued to exist and continued to govern. Many of the most prominent fourth-century bishops were themselves products of the elite: Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus came from wealthy provincial families and had received a classical rhetorical education, and Ambrose was the son of a high imperial official and was serving as governor of a province when the people of Milan acclaimed him bishop in 374, apparently while he was still a catechumen, that is, unbaptized. Such men brought to the episcopate the habits of patronage, diplomacy, and public speech that had long defined civic leadership, and they used them on behalf of their churches. The rise of the bishop is thus a story of continuity as much as of change. A new office was filled by men who exercised authority in largely familiar ways.

Episcopal elections show how public and contested the role could be. By custom, bishops were chosen with the consent of the clergy and people of a city and consecrated by neighboring bishops, and the Council of Nicaea required the participation of the bishops of the province. In practice, elections could involve the acclamation of crowds, the influence of powerful patrons, and violence. The election of Damasus as bishop of Rome in 366 was followed by street fighting between his supporters and those of his rival Ursinus that left many dead in a Roman basilica, according to the non-Christian historian Ammianus Marcellinus, who was unsympathetic to the wealth of Roman bishops and whose account must be read with that in mind. Martin, a former soldier and monastic founder, was reportedly acclaimed bishop of Tours in 371 by the townspeople against the reluctance of some bishops, if the admiring biography by Sulpicius Severus can be trusted. These examples show a public office with public stakes.

Episcopal geography also mattered. Certain cities carried particular prestige. Rome was the imperial city of ancient memory and the church associated with the apostles Peter and Paul, and its bishop enjoyed exceptional respect, particularly in the Latin west. Damasus, who held office from 366 to 384, promoted the veneration of martyrs at Rome's cemeteries and asserted the "apostolic" character of his see, and later bishops such as Siricius began to issue rulings in the form of letters to other bishops. Yet the bishop of Rome did not exercise the centralized jurisdiction associated with the medieval papacy, whose growth is examined in Chapter 44. Eastern churches generally regarded Rome as an honored church whose opinion mattered, not as a court of universal appeal, and even in the west, bishops such as Ambrose and, later, Augustine addressed Roman authority with respect but not submission.

Alexandria, the great Egyptian port and intellectual center, had a bishop whose authority over Egypt and neighboring provinces was recognized by the Council of Nicaea. Under Athanasius and his successors, the see acquired great influence, in part through its links with Egyptian monks. Antioch, the capital of Syria and a major Roman eastern city, was riven in these decades by a schism over the succession, with rival Nicene bishops and Homoian rivals each claiming legitimacy. Jerusalem's bishop received special honor at Nicaea because of the city's sacred associations, and Jerusalem became a pilgrimage center. Constantinople, the new imperial city, initially had no ancient Christian tradition and no apostolic foundation, but the presence of the court gave its bishop unusual visibility. In the west, Milan, an imperial residence in the later fourth century, and Carthage, the metropolis of Roman Africa, were leading sees. Students should avoid reading the later formal ranking of five patriarchates back into this period. The relationships among these churches were still being negotiated, and rank often followed the political fortunes of cities and emperors.

Section 26.5

Monasticism, Asceticism, and New Forms of Christian Authority

Asceticism, meaning the disciplined renunciation of ordinary comforts and social obligations for a religious purpose, existed in Christian communities long before the fourth century. Men and women had adopted lives of celibacy, fasting, and prayer while remaining within their households and cities. What changed in the fourth century was the scale, the visibility, and the institutional form of ascetic life. From the deserts of Egypt to the highlands of Asia Minor and the cities of Syria, communities of men and women arose who withdrew, to varying degrees, from ordinary social life. These movements developed alongside, and partly in response to, the transformation of the church into a wealthy and public institution. For many Christians, the ascetic offered a new model of holiness alongside the bishop and the martyr tradition, at a time when state-sponsored persecution of Christians within the Roman Empire had largely receded.

Two broad forms are conventionally distinguished. Eremitic monasticism (from the Greek eremos, "desert") consisted of solitary or loosely grouped ascetics living apart from the settled world. Cenobitic monasticism (from Greek roughly "common life") involved communities living together under a shared discipline. Both forms flourished in the fourth century, and the boundary between them was often fluid.

The best-known solitary ascetic is Antony of Egypt, traditionally dated to about 251 to 356. Almost everything we know about him comes from the Life of Antony, written soon after his death by Athanasius of Alexandria and translated into Latin within a couple of decades. The Life depicts Antony as a young man from a Christian family in the Egyptian countryside who gave away his inheritance, withdrew from his village, and progressively sought greater solitude in the desert, where he battled demons, practiced severe discipline, and became a spiritual counselor to visitors. It presents him as unlettered but wise, loyal to the bishops, and opposed to what Athanasius called Arian error. Students should read this text critically. It is a hagiography, or saint's life, written by a bishop who was engaged in intense ecclesiastical struggles and who had good reason to present the most admired holy man of Egypt as a supporter of his own cause. The Life tells us a great deal about ascetic practice and expectations, and about what Athanasius hoped Christian authority would look like, and less that can be securely verified about the historical Antony. It nevertheless proved enormously influential. Augustine, in his Confessions, recalled the impact that stories of Antony had on him and his friends, and the text helped make the desert holy man a model across the Christian world.

Communal monasticism owes much to Pachomius, born around 292 in Upper Egypt, who is said to have served in the army before converting to Christianity. Around 320 to 330 he organized monks into a disciplined community at Tabennisi, on the Nile, with shared property, regular work, communal meals and prayer, and obedience to a superior. According to the monastic tradition, the Pachomian koinonia grew into a network of nine men's communities and two women's communities by the time of Pachomius's death. Later sources attribute very large numbers of monks to the movement, but exact totals cannot be verified. The Pachomian rules survive in later translations, including a Latin one by Jerome, and Coptic-language texts from Egypt reveal a monastic world that operated in the language of the Egyptian countryside, not of the Greek-speaking city elite. The evidence has been enriched by papyri and by the fourth-century Coptic manuscripts discovered at Nag Hammadi in 1945, whose relationship to monastic communities is debated among scholars.

Elsewhere, ascetic practice took other forms. In Syria, ascetics associated with the "sons and daughters of the covenant" and others practiced extreme disciplines, and Syriac authors such as Ephrem of Nisibis and Edessa, who died in 373, combined ascetic ideals with poetry and teaching. In Asia Minor, Basil of Caesarea, after visiting the monastic centers of Egypt and Syria, established a program for communal ascetic life that emphasized shared living, work, and service to the poor and sick, and criticized the excesses of purely solitary life. Basil's rules, elaborated in his writings and revised over time, became among the most influential models for eastern monastic practice. His sister Macrina, a decisive influence on the family, converted the household estate at Annisa in Pontus into an ascetic community of women and men, and her brother Gregory of Nyssa later wrote her biography, one of the most valuable sources for female asceticism in the century. Her example is a reminder that ascetic history is not solely a male story.

Why did men and women choose ascetic life? The sources point to a mixture of motives. Renunciation of property and status was a scriptural ideal, particularly influential in the Gospels. Sexual abstinence was understood as a way of imitating angelic life and of freeing the person for prayer. Discipline of the body was a means of conquering passions. Some ascetics sought spiritual authority, since their reputation for holiness could give them more influence over communities than a bishop could claim. Some may have sought escape from taxes, curial obligations, or family pressures, and later laws tried to prevent men from becoming monks to avoid municipal duties. It would be wrong to characterize the movement as a simple flight from a corrupt civilization. Ascetics remained closely connected to villages, pilgrims, markets, bishops, patrons, and political networks. Monks worked, sold products, gave advice, and interceded with officials. Desert monasteries were pilgrimage destinations and sources of charitable relief.

Monasticism spread westward with remarkable speed. Athanasius, during his exile in the west, brought knowledge of Egyptian ascetics, and the Latin translation of the Life of Antony circulated widely. Jerome, who lived in Rome from 382 to 385, guided a circle of aristocratic women, including Marcella and Paula, in ascetic study of scripture, and later settled in Bethlehem. Melania the Elder, a wealthy Roman widow, founded a monastic community on the Mount of Olives in Jerusalem. In Gaul, Martin of Tours established a monastic community at Marmoutier. These western developments were different in scale and character from the Egyptian ones, and later chapters on the formation of Latin Christendom, particularly Chapter 30 and Chapter 35, trace how western monasticism developed under the influence of these models.

Monasticism generated tensions. Bishops sometimes worried that charismatic ascetics could undermine episcopal authority, and some ascetics were critical of clerical wealth. Extreme practices provoked criticism from within the church: a council held at Gangra in Asia Minor, probably in the mid-fourth century, condemned ascetics who rejected marriage or disrupted households. In the west, Jovinian challenged the superiority of virginity over marriage in the 390s and was condemned. Monks could also be instruments of violence. The pagan orator Libanius, in a speech to Theodosius I, complained about black-robed men destroying rural temples, though his portrait is hostile and rhetorical. Egyptian monks came into Alexandria in support of their bishops in more than one confrontation. At the same time, monks served as intermediaries, healers, and critics of the powerful, and some wielded their moral prestige in ways that bishops and emperors could not ignore. Monasticism therefore created new forms of authority within the church, partly complementary to and partly in competition with that of the bishop.

Section 26.6

Christian Households, Gender, Charity, and Everyday Religious Life

Councils and bishops occupy the center of most accounts of fourth-century Christianity, but the history of the church is also the history of households, neighborhoods, and ordinary religious habits. What did Christianization actually change in people's lives? The evidence permits only a partial answer. Sermons, pastoral letters, canons, and church regulations tell us what clergy wanted and, indirectly, what they found people doing. Tombstones, house remains, papyrus letters, mosaics, and material objects add a different perspective. Historians therefore reconstruct everyday religion by combining these sources and reading the complaints of preachers as evidence of practices they hoped to change.

The rhythms of Christian life were visible in the calendar and the body. Christians gathered for worship on Sunday, a day the emperor Constantine had made a day of rest for city courts and workshops. They observed Easter, the central feast, and its preceding fast, and by the 330s a Christmas feast on 25 December is attested at Rome, while eastern churches celebrated the Epiphany. They fasted on particular days, prayed at home, and gave alms. Baptism was a major threshold. Many Christians postponed it until late in life, or until a crisis, partly because of the seriousness of the commitment and partly because of a fear of sinning after baptism. Constantine himself was baptized only shortly before his death, and Ambrose was unbaptized when chosen bishop. The catechumenate, a period of instruction, was a long-standing feature, and lectures for candidates survive from Jerusalem and elsewhere. Churches were also becoming places of commemoration: the cult of martyrs, in which the tombs of those killed in persecution became sites of prayer and feasting, expanded, and the discovery and movement of relics became a feature of fourth-century Christian life. Emperors and bishops promoted such cults, and communities embraced them.

The Christian household was a major site of religious formation. Marriage and family life remained the norm for most Christians, and preachers such as John Chrysostom at Antioch and Constantinople offered guidance to married people, addressed slave-owners and slaves, and criticized behavior they found unseemly. At the same time, the church promoted ideals of sexual restraint, and celibacy came to be regarded as a superior calling. This produced tensions. Christian teaching could constrain women, in that it emphasized obedience within marriage and imposed strict sexual rules, while also providing some women with alternatives to marriage and household roles. Widows, who had a recognized place in church life from the earliest period, could remain unmarried, control property, and act as patrons. Consecrated virgins and female ascetics were admired. Wealthy women, such as Melania the Elder, Paula, and later Olympias of Constantinople, used their fortunes to fund churches, hospices, and monasteries and to support scholars and bishops. Their independence had limits, since they depended on the approval of male clergy and family, and it would be wrong to see ascetic Christianity as a simple story of liberation. Households also included enslaved people, whose experience of Christianity has left far less evidence. Late antique Christian churches did not mount a general campaign to abolish slavery, and Christian households continued to own enslaved people. Preachers usually urged humane treatment and praised manumission, although individual voices, notably Gregory of Nyssa, could condemn slaveholding itself in unusually sweeping terms.

Charity was a further area of change. Christian communities had cared for the poor, widows, orphans, and the sick from early days, and church resources now allowed this work to expand. Bishops supervised lists of people dependent on church support, distributed food, and organized hostels and hospitals. Basil of Caesarea's large complex on the edge of Caesarea, which included a hospital for the sick and lodging for travelers, became famous. Chrysostom claimed that the church of Antioch supported thousands of widows, virgins, and other poor, though such figures are the preacher's own and cannot be verified. Ambrose, in a work on the duties of clergy, defended melting down church vessels to ransom captives. Christian charity differed from classical civic benefaction especially in its emphasis on direct support for the poor, sick, strangers, widows, and other needy people, while also adapting older Roman habits of patronage and elite public giving. It gave Christian leaders a new claim on the loyalty of the urban poor. Even Julian, as Section 26.7 shows, recognized the effectiveness of Christian charity and wished to imitate it.

Christians practiced their faith within families and neighborhoods that also contained non-Christians, and they combined inherited habits with Christian ones in ways that clergy tried to shape, and often could not control. Preachers also exaggerated for effect, so their complaints must be handled as evidence about their aims as much as about their audiences.

Section 26.7

Julian and the Persistence of Religious Pluralism

The reign of Julian, from 361 to 363, is indispensable for understanding the period because it proves that Christianization was neither complete nor irreversible. Julian was Constantine's nephew, a survivor of the dynastic bloodbath that followed Constantine's death, and was educated as a Christian. As a young man, however, he studied philosophy, was drawn to Neoplatonism, and came to embrace the traditional gods. As Caesar in Gaul in the 350s he was a capable administrator and general, and in 360 his troops proclaimed him Augustus at Paris. When Constantius II died in 361 on his way to confront him, Julian became sole emperor, and he began openly to promote traditional cult.

His program was multifaceted. He restored temples and sacrifices, appointed priests, and sought to organize traditional religion into a structured institution modeled in part on what he admired in the church. In a surviving letter, he urged a priestly official to imitate Christian charity toward the poor and to emphasize moral discipline, since he thought Christian care for strangers and the needy was a reason for the success of what he called the Galilean faith. He reduced the privileges Christians had acquired: he withdrew certain clerical exemptions and subsidies and, in 362, issued a law on teachers that required approval of municipal councils and the emperor. Christians saw in it a way of barring them from teaching the classical literature that was the basis of education, since, in Julian's view, teachers who did not honor the gods of Homer and Hesiod should not teach them. He also recalled bishops exiled by Constantius, including Athanasius, a decision that in practice renewed conflict among Christians, and is commonly interpreted as a means of weakening the church by allowing its divisions to reopen. He seems to have encouraged the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem in 363, an attempt to conciliate Jews and embarrass Christians, but the project was abandoned, and Ammianus Marcellinus, a sympathetic non-Christian observer, reports that fire brought the work to a halt.

Julian died in June 363 during a campaign against Sasanian Persia, and his brief reign left a mixed legacy. Christian writers labeled him "the Apostate," a polemical term that expressed the view that he had abandoned baptism and that he was an enemy of the church. Historians use the name with care and often prefer to call him simply Julian. His effort to revive traditional cult failed for reasons that include the shortness of his rule, the lack of a shared institutional structure among traditional cults, and the fact that many Romans of every class were unenthusiastic about the philosophical form of religion he favored. Yet the episode revealed a great deal. In the 360s, traditional cult was still viable, and many cities had functioning temples and priests. Educated non-Christians retained a powerful intellectual and literary tradition, and Christian dominance was not yet inevitable. Julian's successor Jovian restored Christian privileges. Valentinian I generally pursued a comparatively restrained religious policy in the west, while Valens supported Homoian Christianity in the east and at several points exiled or coerced Nicene and Homoiousian opponents. The episode remained a warning that imperial favor could be withdrawn.

Historical Vocabulary
"Pagan" and Traditional Religion

Pagan (Latin paganus) became, in Christian Latin usage of the fourth century, a broad label for everyone who was neither Christian, Jewish, nor a member of another recognized group. Its origin is debated: the word originally meant a villager or civilian, and some scholars link its religious sense to the idea of someone not enlisted in Christ's service. It was an outsider's term, a Christian umbrella category covering an enormous range of inherited practices that were never a single, organized "religion" and whose adherents did not think of themselves as members of one movement opposed to Christianity. Traditional religion, the term this textbook usually prefers, included civic cults, the worship of the emperor and state gods, household rituals, mystery cults, local sanctuaries, philosophical traditions, sacrifice, festivals, and the institutions of temples and priesthoods. Some adherents were deeply religious and intellectually sophisticated, others were simply following custom, and many took part in civic festivals without exclusive commitment. Understanding this variety is essential to understanding why Christianization was slow, uneven, and often a process of adaptation.

Traditional religion did not end with Julian, nor with Constantine. Under Constantius II, laws against sacrifice and the closure of temples are attested, but historians debate how consistently they were enforced, and Ammianus reports that Constantius, on visiting Rome in 357, admired the city's monuments and left its traditional priesthoods intact. In the 360s and 370s, members of the senatorial aristocracy at Rome, such as Vettius Agorius Praetextatus and Quintus Aurelius Symmachus, held traditional priesthoods alongside high public office, while Christians and non-Christians of the elite mingled socially and shared education, friendship, and public life. Intellectuals such as Themistius, a philosopher-orator who served Christian emperors, spoke in favor of religious tolerance. The Altar of Victory in the Roman Senate house, a symbol of traditional state religion, was removed by Constantius, restored, and removed again by Gratian in 382, prompting Symmachus in 384 to plead for its return in a famous petition to Valentinian II, which Ambrose opposed. The debate over the altar shows a public argument, conducted in polite terms, in which non-Christian aristocrats still had a voice.

Jewish communities were a third major element of the religious landscape. Jews lived in cities across the empire, in Palestine, Syria, Asia Minor, Italy, North Africa, Egypt, and beyond, and in the countryside of Galilee, where communities were especially dense. They had synagogues, communal leaders, and scholars, and in the later fourth century the rabbinic movement was producing the texts that would culminate in the Palestinian Talmud. The great synagogue at Sardis, in western Asia Minor, incorporated in a prominent part of the city's civic complex and used long into late antiquity, shows the prominence Jews could have in a Roman city. Roman laws and precedents granted Jewish communities specific protections, exemptions, and recognized communal institutions, although these did not amount to a formal empire-wide category of "licensed religions." They included a hereditary Jewish leader in Palestine, the patriarch, who was acknowledged by the state until the early fifth century.

Imperial Christianity nevertheless changed the legal environment for Jews. Laws beginning with Constantine forbade Jews from circumcising or holding Christian slaves and discouraged conversion from Christianity to Judaism, while Christian councils prohibited Christians from observing Jewish festivals and sharing certain rituals. Christian preachers regularly denounced Judaism, and the language of anti-Jewish polemic grew harsher in these decades. Yet the same imperial law protected synagogues from arbitrary seizure or destruction. A law of 393 explicitly banned attacks on synagogues, and Jewish religious life continued. It would be inaccurate to imply that Judaism faded once Christianity gained imperial favor. The empire's Jews continued to build, worship, teach, and trade, and the relationship between Christians and Jews remained a mix of coexistence, competition, and, at times, violence.

Section 26.8

Christianization in Cities and Countryside

The phrase "Christianization" is used here to describe a set of overlapping processes, not a single transformation of belief. They included imperial patronage, the conversion of elites, the construction of churches, the expansion of episcopal organization, the conversion of families and households, the spread of monastic communities, legal restrictions on sacrifice, the introduction of a Christian calendar, the growth of saints' cults, the evangelization of rural areas, the closure, destruction, or conversion of some sacred sites, and the continuation and adaptation of older practices. These occurred at different speeds and to different degrees in different regions. An empire could become institutionally more Christian while much of its daily religious life remained mixed or locally distinctive.

Cities were the principal arena. Church buildings changed urban landscapes. Constantine's foundations, including the Lateran basilica and the church over the tomb of Peter at Rome, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre at Jerusalem, and churches in Constantinople, set a pattern. Under Constantius II, the first Hagia Sophia at Constantinople was dedicated in 360, and relics of apostles were brought to the city. In the following decades, basilicas rose in cities across the empire, funded by bishops, private donors, and the state. Christian processions, festivals, and funerals occupied public spaces. The conversion of elites gradually reoriented the patronage that had once gone to temples and civic buildings. This visual and social transformation was substantial, but archaeology shows that in many cities temples continued in use, sometimes long after the mid-fourth century, and that Christian building was often modest until wealth accumulated.

The countryside was different. Most people lived in villages and on estates, where the bishop's reach was limited and where Christian communities were slower to form. Conversion often depended on landowners, whose patronage could bring a church to their estate, or on charismatic figures such as monks. Martin of Tours, according to his biographer Sulpicius Severus, destroyed rural shrines and preached in the Gaulish countryside, and although the account is hagiographic, it reveals how a missionary bishop might have worked and how local resistance could look. In Egypt, monasteries were often the principal agents of Christian expansion. In North Africa, rural Christianity was strong, and in the Balkans and Asia Minor, the pattern varied from region to region. Rural religion frequently combined practices of shrine visiting, agricultural festivals, and healing rituals that clergy tried to redirect toward Christian forms, and sometimes tolerated as harmless custom. Historians debate how far to treat these continuities as "survivals" of paganism or as new Christian forms, and it is safest to describe them as adaptive practices in a changing religious environment.

Christianization also extended beyond the empire's frontiers, and the effects were political. Ulfila, a Christian of partly Cappadocian descent born among the Goths, was consecrated a bishop about 341 by Eusebius of Nicomedia and led a mission among the Goths north of the Danube. After persecution of Christian Goths, he and followers moved into Roman territory, and he translated parts of the Bible into Gothic. He belonged to the Homoian tradition, and his converts, whose story belongs to Chapter 27, carried that form of Christianity with them. Further east, the kingdom of Armenia adopted Christianity around the early fourth century, and the kingdoms of Aksum in Ethiopia and Iberia in the Caucasus later did so as well. In the Sasanian Persian empire, where a substantial Christian community lived, Roman imperial Christianity had a difficult side effect: Christians became suspect as possible allies of Rome, and persecution occurred under Shapur II in the fourth century. These examples show that Christian networks crossed imperial frontiers and were tied to geopolitics.

Historical Debate

How Did the Roman Empire Become Christian?

The question: Within about a century of Constantine's rise, Christianity moved from a persecuted minority to the religion favored by the emperor and increasingly the dominant public religion of the empire. What explains this change? Historians propose several overlapping explanations, and current scholarship generally combines them instead of choosing one.

Interpretation A: Imperial transformation. Constantine and his successors changed incentives through law, money, buildings, and prestige. Imperial favor made Christianity attractive to ambitious men, funded the church, and later penalized rival cults. This interpretation emphasizes the top-down force of state power.
Interpretation B: Social and institutional growth. Christian networks, bishops, families, ascetics, and charitable institutions expanded independently of imperial command. Christianity was already growing before Constantine, and its communal organization and care for the poor gave it distinctive strengths. This interpretation emphasizes bottom-up processes in local communities.
Interpretation C: Cultural adaptation. Christianity succeeded in part because communities translated inherited Roman social and cultural forms into Christian ones: patronage into episcopal leadership, civic benefaction into charity, festivals and cults of the dead into commemorations of martyrs, and classical education into Christian rhetoric. This interpretation emphasizes continuity and the hybrid character of the change.

Evidence: Supporters of Interpretation A cite imperial legislation, subsidies, and building programs. Supporters of B point to evidence of growth before 312, the role of monasteries and local churches, and the incompleteness of legislation's reach. Supporters of C point to the continuity of literary, architectural, and social forms in Christian settings. Every interpretation faces limits, since evidence for conversion of ordinary people is thin, and the sources are dominated by the clergy and imperial officials.

Why it matters: If imperial power was decisive, the story is one of political change. If social networks mattered most, Christianization would have proceeded even without imperial help, though probably more slowly. If cultural adaptation was central, then Christianity and Roman society changed each other. Current scholarship generally combines the three, and the balance may differ by region and by social group.

Section 26.9

Religious Conflict, Coercion, and Community Boundaries

Religious conflict in the fourth century took many forms, and much of it occurred among Christians. The most durable example is the Donatist controversy in Roman Africa. It originated in the aftermath of the Great Persecution of 303 to 305, when officials demanded that Christian clergy surrender sacred books. Some clergy complied and were called traditores, "those who handed over." In 311, Caecilian was consecrated bishop of Carthage by a bishop whom opponents accused of having been such a traditor. Rivals rejected the consecration as invalid and set up their own bishop, and after 313 the movement was led by Donatus, from whom the name Donatist derives. Donatists emphasized the holiness and integrity of the true church and rejected the legitimacy of ordinations and sacraments transmitted through clergy whom they regarded as traditores or as belonging outside that church. Their opponents, whom the Donatists called the party of Caecilian and who claimed the name Catholic, increasingly argued that sacramental efficacy came from Christ and was not invalidated by the personal unworthiness of a minister.

Constantine intervened early. Councils at Rome in 313 and Arles in 314 ruled against the Donatists, and after a brief period of coercion, he reversed course in 321 and allowed the dispute to continue. The controversy therefore became a case study in imperial involvement in church affairs, and it shows the same pattern seen in the doctrinal disputes: imperial action could not end the division. Donatism was especially strong in Numidia and could outnumber Catholic communities locally, but it was neither simply a rural nor a lower-class movement: Donatist bishops, urban congregations, landowners, and elites are all attested. Historians have long debated whether regional identity, rural social tensions, and resistance to imperial coercion help explain its durability, while recent scholarship cautions against reducing the schism to ethnic, nationalist, or class revolt. In the eyes of Catholic writers, it was also linked to bands of militant itinerant ascetics called Circumcellions. The evidence for these groups is almost entirely hostile, and historians debate how far they were a distinct movement of social protest or a Catholic caricature. Imperial coercion under Constans in the 340s deepened the divide, and the Donatists claimed the martyrs of those years. Julian's permission for exiled Donatists to return renewed the dispute. Optatus of Milevis, writing in the 360s and 380s, defended the Catholic position, and the conflict would reach its height in the time of Augustine, who became bishop of Hippo in the mid-390s and whose role belongs to later chapters. Donatism cannot be reduced to the question of whether "traitor priests" were valid, though that question was central. It also involved episcopal legitimacy, persecution memory, regional identity, and the relationship between church and empire.

The treatment of Priscillian shows another dimension of the period. Priscillian was an Iberian Christian teacher with an ascetic following, accused by some Spanish bishops of heresy and magical practices. After appeals to imperial authorities and a hearing before the usurper Magnus Maximus, he was executed with some followers at Trier around 385. It was the first execution of Christians for heresy by imperial authority, and prominent bishops, including Martin of Tours and Ambrose, protested it, though they were themselves not sympathetic to his teaching. The episode shows that some bishops were uncomfortable with the use of capital punishment against religious dissenters even as they supported other forms of coercion.

Violence also accompanied episcopal elections and confrontations between rival Christian parties in cities such as Rome, Alexandria, Constantinople, and Antioch, sometimes with the involvement of armed forces and sometimes drawing in monks and crowds. The disputes were not merely theological, since they involved control of churches and their resources, and so they were as much about property and public standing as about doctrine. Emperors dealt with these situations by exiling bishops, awarding churches to one faction, or appointing governors to restore order, and their interventions typically produced winners and losers.

Relations with adherents of other traditions could also involve coercion. In the decades after Constantine, imperial laws restricted sacrifice, though the extent and enforcement of the earliest such measures are uncertain. Individual Christians, including some bishops and monks, took part in the destruction or conversion of temples, sometimes with official approval and sometimes not. Jewish communities faced periodic hostility. In 388, at Callinicum on the Euphrates, a synagogue was burned by a Christian crowd, reportedly at the urging of the local bishop. Theodosius I ordered that the bishop rebuild it at his own expense. Ambrose wrote to the emperor to object, arguing that a Christian bishop should not be compelled to pay for a synagogue, and the emperor relented. The episode, examined further in Section 26.11, shows both the state's continuing role in protecting Jewish property and the growing willingness of bishops to challenge it. Modern historians debate how representative these episodes were and how much of the violence was popular and spontaneous, but they agree that community boundaries between religious groups in cities were increasingly contested.

Across all these cases, conflict cannot be reduced to theology. Political, economic, dynastic, regional, and social factors mattered. Church property and offices had value. Rivalry between cities, resentment of imperial officials, competition among elites, and local memories shaped the disputes. Religious language gave these tensions a vocabulary and moral urgency, and religious identity in turn became a powerful form of solidarity.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through Religious Change in the Fourth Century

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and they do not carry equal power or evidence.

A Nicene bishop: The council at Nicaea defended the full divinity of the Son, and the language of Nicaea must be defended even when emperors dislike it. Exile is a price to be borne, and the unity of the church rests on right belief, not on imperial convenience.
A Homoian Christian: The word homoousios is not found in scripture and risks confusing Father and Son. A formula that says the Son is like the Father honors both tradition and imperial hopes for peace, and those who call Homoians "Arians" misrepresent them.
An Egyptian monk: Life in the desert or in a Pachomian community is a way of serving God through discipline, work, and prayer, and it gives a monk standing that the great men of the city do not have. Bishops may be honored, but a holy man has his own authority.
A wealthy Christian widow and ascetic patron: Widowhood and asceticism offer a life free from the expectations of remarriage and household management, and her wealth can build a church, a hostel, or a monastery. Her independence has limits, and it depends on the approval of family and clergy.
A traditional cult priest or philosopher: The gods have protected the city for generations, and their rites are part of civic life and of a long intellectual tradition. The withdrawal of imperial support, and later restrictions on sacrifice, threaten festivals, livelihoods, and the shared culture of the city, though many friends and colleagues are Christian.
A Jewish resident of a city such as Antioch or Sardis: Roman law grants the community specific protections and protects its synagogue, yet Christian preachers denounce Judaism, laws restrict certain practices, and local crowds may become hostile. Communal life, study, and worship continue.
A rural household on an estate in Gaul or Anatolia: The family may attend a nearby shrine, honor a saint or an older spring, keep harvest festivals, and use inherited healing practices, without thinking that these habits conflict with what a visiting bishop or monk says. Its members leave few records of their own.
An imperial official charged with enforcing religious law: Laws come from the emperor, but the governor has limited staff, local elites to consult, and the risk of riot. He may enforce a decree vigorously, selectively, or hardly at all, depending on his own beliefs, his local relationships, and the direction of imperial favor.
Section 26.10

Theodosius I, Nicene Christianity, and the Council of Constantinople

The stabilization of the Nicene position came under Theodosius I. A Spanish-born general, he was named Augustus in the east by the western emperor Gratian in January 379, in the aftermath of the military disaster of 378 examined in Chapter 27. Theodosius was a committed Nicene Christian, though his baptism came only in the autumn of 380, following a serious illness at Thessalonica, and he was baptized by the bishop of that city, Acholius. Theodosius entered Constantinople in November 380 and expelled the Homoian bishop of the city, Demophilus, whose Homoian community had held the city's churches for decades. He installed Gregory of Nazianzus, the Nicene theologian, in his place.

Before that, on 28 February 380, Theodosius, together with Gratian and Valentinian II, had issued from Thessalonica an edict, addressed to the people of Constantinople, that declared support for a particular form of Christianity. This is the text known from its opening words as Cunctos populos, and it is preserved in the Theodosian Code. It has often been described as making Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire and outlawing paganism. Neither statement is accurate, as the Primary Source Window below shows. The edict endorsed the faith taught by Peter and maintained by Damasus of Rome and Peter of Alexandria, in which Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are of equal majesty within a Trinity. It commanded that its followers be called Catholic Christians and treated other Christians as holders of a heretical teaching, without giving them the right to call their assemblies churches. It was a measure directed at the internal divisions of the church. It did not create an "official religion" in a legal sense that later states would recognize, and it did not address traditional cult or Judaism at all.

Primary Source Window

The Edict of Thessalonica (Cunctos populos), 28 February 380 CE

Creator: Issued in the names of the emperors Gratian, Valentinian II, and Theodosius I · Date and place: 28 February 380, Thessalonica · Type: An imperial constitution, preserved in the Theodosian Code (Book 16, title 1), compiled in the 430s

Context: The edict was addressed to the population of Constantinople, where the Homoian church controlled the main churches and Nicene Christians were a minority. It was issued by a new eastern emperor, appointed the previous year, at a moment when Theodosius lacked a secure base in the city and was seeking to align imperial authority with the Nicene party, which had support in the west and among leading theologians. The law reached us through the Code's compilers, who arranged and sometimes edited imperial constitutions, so its wording and placement reflect later editorial choices.

Paraphrase of the edict, not a translation The emperors declare that all peoples governed by their clemency should hold the religion that the apostle Peter handed on to the Romans, and which the bishops Damasus of Rome and Peter of Alexandria currently uphold. That faith teaches one divinity of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in equal majesty and in a Trinity. Those who follow this teaching are to be called Catholic Christians. Others are judged senseless and are to bear the disgrace of heretical teaching. Their meeting places are not to be called churches, and they face divine punishment and the emperors' own measures.

Analysis: The edict defines a form of Christianity, not Christianity in general. It invokes the authority of two named bishops, of Rome and Alexandria, as standards of correct belief, a choice that reflects the Nicene alliance and that excludes the Homoian leaders then in control at Constantinople. It uses the language of command and of condemnation, but it specifies no detailed penalties and says nothing about traditional cult, sacrifice, or Jews. Its immediate effect at Constantinople came through Theodosius's own actions later in the year, when he expelled Demophilus and had the Nicene bishop installed. The edict shows how emperors began to use law to define religious membership, and it illustrates the developing use of "Catholic" as a label for the emperor's preferred community. It does not prove that the population followed the law, that Nicene Christians became a majority in Constantinople by 380, or that other religions ceased. It is prescriptive evidence, and prescription does not equal social reality. A single law also cannot show how consistently officials applied it.

The following year, in 381, Theodosius summoned bishops from the east to a council at Constantinople. About 150 bishops attended, all from the eastern provinces, and no western bishops or delegates from Rome participated. The council was first presided over by Meletius of Antioch, who died during its sessions, and Gregory of Nazianzus, who succeeded him and was then persuaded to resign amid disputes over the bishop's status. The bishopric of Constantinople went to Nectarius, a city official and a respected figure who was still unbaptized at the time of his selection, a detail that shows how an imperial city's needs and the emperor's preferences could shape ecclesiastical appointments. In an accompanying law of July 381, the emperor ordered that churches be handed over to bishops who were in communion with the Nicene leaders.

The council reaffirmed the faith of Nicaea and, in the form that later tradition preserved, addressed the divinity of the Holy Spirit, an issue that had come to the fore in the intervening decades. It also condemned a series of teachings, including those of Apollinaris of Laodicea, who was accused of denying that Christ had a full human mind, an issue that foreshadowed the christological controversies of the fifth century. Its third canon ruled that the bishop of Constantinople should have precedence of honor after the bishop of Rome, "because Constantinople is New Rome," a phrase that reflected the city's imperial status rather than any apostolic foundation and that Rome and Alexandria would later resist. The creed now used in many churches, usually called the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, is associated with this council, though its exact origin and the council's role in producing it are debated, and it is first securely documented at Chalcedon in 451. The council also did not immediately hold the status of an ecumenical council. That standing developed as the eastern and later western churches accepted its results.

Doctrinal definition in this period was thus a process, not a single event. Nicaea in 325, the debates of the middle decades, the Cappadocian synthesis, the edict of 380, and the council of 381 each contributed, and the outcome depended on theological argument, the choices of emperors, and the willingness of many bishops to accept the developing consensus. Homoian Christianity did not disappear in 381. It remained influential in the Balkans, among the Goths, and at the western imperial court in Milan, and it would remain a major form of Christianity among the Germanic peoples who entered the western provinces, a subject for Chapter 27.

Theodosius's legislation against traditional cult developed through a series of measures, not a single act. In 391 and 392, laws prohibited sacrifice and visits to temples, and one banned all forms of traditional worship even in private homes. The scope, enforcement, and effects of these laws have been debated. Some temples were destroyed or closed, and the destruction of the Serapeum at Alexandria in 391, led by the bishop Theophilus, became notorious. Other temples were converted or left to decay, and many stood for decades more. In many places, local officials had little incentive to enforce these laws, and modern historians view them as important signals of a changing climate more than as indicators of immediate universal change. In 394, at the Frigidus River, Theodosius defeated the western usurper Eugenius, who had been backed by the general Arbogast and by some non-Christian aristocrats. Christian writers described the victory as a religious triumph, and modern historians debate how far the conflict was really about religion. Theodosius died at Milan in January 395, ruling the whole empire, which he left to his two sons, Arcadius in the east and Honorius in the west.

Section 26.11

Bishops, Emperors, and the Social Power of the Church

The relationship between Ambrose of Milan and Theodosius I offers the best-documented illustration of episcopal power in the period and, at the same time, a warning against exaggerating it. Ambrose was born into a prominent Roman family, trained in law and rhetoric, and appointed governor of a northern Italian province with his seat at Milan. When the city's bishop died in 374, and a dispute between Nicene and Homoian factions threatened public order, Ambrose intervened as governor, and the people, according to the traditional account, acclaimed him bishop. He was baptized and consecrated within days. As bishop he became an energetic preacher, a writer of theological and pastoral works, a composer of hymns, and a forceful defender of Nicene Christianity against the Homoians who had the support of the court of Valentinian II.

In the confrontations with the imperial household in 385 and 386, Ambrose refused to yield a basilica to the Homoian congregation of the court, resisting demands that the emperor considered lawful, and he maintained that the emperor was within the church, not above it. He also opposed Symmachus's petition for the Altar of Victory and defended the bishop's right to speak on public matters. In 388, in the affair of Callinicum described earlier, he wrote to Theodosius and, according to his own account, reproached the emperor from the pulpit until he yielded. Ambrose's accounts of such events were written by Ambrose himself, in letters that circulated in his own interest, and historians read them with that fact in mind.

The most famous episode followed the massacre at Thessalonica in 390. After the murder of a military commander by a crowd in the city, imperial troops killed many inhabitants, and ancient sources speak of thousands, though the number is uncertain and probably exaggerated in later tradition. Ambrose wrote to Theodosius urging him to do public penance and reportedly withheld communion until he did. According to Ambrose's own funeral oration for the emperor in 395, Theodosius laid aside his imperial insignia and made a public act of contrition. Later Christian writers, including Theodoret and Rufinus, added a dramatic scene in which Ambrose barred the emperor from entering the church, and historians doubt these embellishments. What the episode showed was that an exceptionally influential bishop could use religious authority against an emperor within a shared Christian political culture. It did not show that the church had become more powerful than the state. The emperor remained emperor, Ambrose did not control imperial policy, and Theodosius's acceptance of penance, if the accounts are correct, was itself an act of imperial choice that may have enhanced his image as a pious ruler.

Beyond individual confrontations, the social power of the church lay in its resources and relationships. Church wealth grew from bequests, from gifts by emperors and aristocrats, and from the revenue of estates. Later sources, such as the sixth-century Book of the Popes, list lavish donations by Constantine to the Roman churches, but the compilation is much later, and its details must be treated with caution. Nevertheless, the archaeology of Roman basilicas and the documentary evidence for church property show substantial accumulation of resources. Bishops managed this wealth, but they also faced criticism. Ammianus mocked the luxury of the bishop of Rome, and some ascetics denounced clerical riches. Bishops directed church funds toward charity, buildings, and the ransoming of captives, but they also used them to reward supporters, cement alliances, and enhance their own status.

The church's networks of patronage gave it social reach. A bishop who could mediate between an urban community and an imperial governor, secure tax remission, feed the hungry in a famine, or protect a refugee could command loyalty. But his power was limited. He depended on the acquiescence of local elites, the approval of his clergy, the favor of the emperor, and the goodwill of his flock. He could be exiled, as Athanasius and many others were. He could face rivals within his own city. His words carried weight when they matched the sentiments of a crowd and lost force when they did not. The bishop was an important participant in a complex system of civic and imperial politics, not a substitute for it. Traditional municipal institutions and the imperial administration continued, and, as Chapter 27 shows, they would soon face challenges of a different kind.

Section 26.12

A Christianizing Empire, Not a Fully Christian Society

By 395, the Roman world had changed profoundly. Christianity had become extraordinarily powerful. The emperor favored a particular Nicene form of the faith, and imperial law increasingly used Christian language and restricted some traditional practices. Bishops held positions of substantial public authority in cities across the empire. Monasteries had become major institutions in Egypt, Palestine, Syria, and Asia Minor and were spreading in the west. Churches had transformed urban landscapes, and church wealth funded charity and building on a large scale. Doctrinal formulations developed in the fourth century, especially the doctrine of the Trinity, had become foundations of Christian teaching for the coming centuries.

Yet the Roman world was not religiously uniform. Christians still disagreed. Homoian Christianity remained strong in parts of the Balkans and at courts, and the Donatist church remained the church of many North Africans. Traditional cults continued in cities and in the countryside, where some temples remained in use and where local practice adapted to Christian frameworks. Jewish communities remained a visible part of Roman society, with synagogues, leaders, and scholars. Local religious habits were difficult for bishops to control. Regional variation was enormous: the Christianity of Egyptian villages, of Cappadocian aristocrats, of the Milanese court, and of Numidian Donatists differed in language, organization, and priorities. The image of a "Christian empire" is therefore best replaced by that of a Christianizing empire, one in which institutions and public culture were changing faster than everyday religious life.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Christianity and Roman Society, c. 337 and c. 395

What Changed

  • Around 337, imperial patronage was recent, and doctrinal settlement was unstable. By 395, a Nicene form of Christianity enjoyed firm imperial support, with councils and law reinforcing it.
  • Bishops in 337 had growing but uneven public authority. By 395, episcopal networks, church wealth, and the bishops' roles as preachers, patrons, and intermediaries were far more developed.
  • Monasticism, still emerging in 337, was established across the eastern provinces and increasingly in the west.
  • Christian language and concerns had begun to shape imperial law, and restrictions on sacrifice and some traditional cult practices had multiplied.
  • Church building, charitable institutions, and the cult of saints and relics had expanded markedly.

What Stayed the Same

  • Traditional religion persisted in cities and the countryside, and many Romans continued to participate in civic festivals and inherited practices.
  • Jewish communities persisted with synagogues and legal recognition, though in a changing legal and rhetorical climate.
  • Christian doctrine remained contested, and rival Christian communities kept their own institutions.
  • Regional variation remained enormous, and Greek, Latin, Syriac, and Coptic Christianity developed differently.
  • Church and state remained distinct institutions, though closely entangled, and bishops still operated within municipal and imperial systems.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban elites, bishops, clergy, and monks, and residents of cities, where church building, preaching, and legal change were most visible. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural households, small-town residents, and people outside elite networks, whose religious lives changed more slowly and are less well documented.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: assuming that a Christian empire was created by 395, and assuming that nothing significant had changed since Constantine.

The chapter should not end by claiming that Christendom had arrived. In this textbook, Christianity and Christendom are distinct. By 395, students should see an increasingly Christianized imperial political culture, not a unified medieval Christendom. The institutions, regions, and identities that would compose Latin Christendom and Orthodox Christendom were still to be formed, and the routes by which they emerged, examined in Parts V and VI, depended on developments that had scarcely begun. The church of this chapter was also becoming embedded in political conflicts that would soon intersect with migration, warfare, and the fragmentation of the western empire. Many of the peoples who entered the empire in the following decades were themselves Christians, often of the Homoian tradition transmitted by Ulfila's mission, so that religious difference would color relations between Romans and newcomers. Chapter 27 takes up that story, and so hands on a transformed Roman world in which the church was a major institution, though not one that had solved the problems of doctrine, authority, and coexistence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the development of Christianity in the Roman world between the death of Constantine in 337 and the death of Theodosius I in 395, reaching back to the Council of Nicaea of 325. Its central argument is that Constantine's patronage did not settle Christianity. It changed the conditions under which Christians disagreed, turning what had been disputes among communities into questions that emperors, officials, and crowds all had reason to care about. The church became stronger and more divided at once, and the tension between its power and its divisions runs through the entire period.

The chapter explained the controversy over the teaching of Arius and the Council of Nicaea, where the term homoousios was adopted to affirm that the Son shares the Father's being. It emphasized that Nicaea did not end the controversy and that the following decades saw shifting coalitions of Nicene, Homoian, Homoiousian, and Anomoean Christians, so that the label "Arian" is better treated as a polemical umbrella than as a description of a single group. Constantius II, whose reign demonstrates what imperial Christianity looked like after Constantine, convened councils, exiled bishops such as Athanasius, and promoted Homoian formulas, while the Cappadocian theologians developed vocabulary that allowed a Nicene consolidation.

The chapter examined the bishop as a new kind of public authority: teacher, arbitrator, patron, intermediary, builder, and political actor. It stressed that bishops operated within Roman civic society, that many came from the elite, and that they did not replace municipal or imperial government. It described the geography of episcopal prestige, from Rome and Milan in the west to Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem, and Constantinople in the east, and cautioned against projecting the medieval papacy or the later formal ranking of patriarchates back into the fourth century.

Monasticism and asceticism formed a further pillar of the church's development. The chapter distinguished eremitic from cenobitic forms, traced the roles of Antony, Pachomius, Basil, and Macrina, and treated the Life of Antony as a hagiographic source that reveals the ideals of its author as much as the life of its subject. It showed that monks remained connected to villages, cities, patrons, and bishops, that they could be instruments of both charity and violence, and that they created new forms of religious authority within and alongside episcopal structures. Women were prominent as ascetics, patrons, and community leaders, though within limits set by family and clergy.

The chapter turned to everyday religious life, using the household as a lens. Sermons and other sources show Christians organizing life around Sunday, fasting, baptism, burial, martyr cults, and almsgiving, while blending inherited practices that clergy criticized. Christian charity, expressed through poor relief and hospitals, offered a new model of social obligation. The reconstruction of a household in Antioch showed that Christianization looks less complete from the household than from the law code, and that clerical sources reveal preachers' aims as well as popular habits.

Julian's reign from 361 to 363 demonstrated that Christianization was neither complete nor irreversible. His attempt to revive and reorganize traditional cult, reduce Christian privileges, and rebuild the Jerusalem Temple failed, but it revealed that traditional religion, philosophical culture, and elite resistance remained alive. The chapter emphasized that "pagan" was a Christian umbrella term for many practices, that Jewish communities remained substantial and active, and that laws affecting Jews both restricted certain practices and protected synagogues.

Christianization was treated as a set of processes: imperial patronage, elite conversion, church building, episcopal organization, household conversion, monastic expansion, legal restrictions, calendar change, saints' cults, rural evangelization, and the adaptation of older practices. These occurred unevenly, with cities changing faster than the countryside. The chapter also noted how Christian networks crossed imperial frontiers, as in the Gothic mission of Ulfila and in Armenia and Persia, and it presented a debate over whether imperial power, social networks, or cultural adaptation best explains the empire's Christianization, concluding that current scholarship combines them.

Religious conflict was explored through the Donatist controversy in North Africa, the execution of Priscillian, urban violence over episcopal elections, and confrontations involving temples and synagogues. These cases showed that religious conflict cannot be reduced to theology, since property, regional identity, social tension, imperial policy, and communal memory all shaped it. Under Theodosius I, the Edict of Thessalonica of 380 and the Council of Constantinople of 381 consolidated a Nicene settlement in the east, while a series of laws in 391 and 392 restricted traditional cult. The chapter warned against the claim that in 380 Christianity became the official religion and other cults were banned, since the edict addressed divisions among Christians and later legislation was uneven in effect.

Finally, the encounter between Ambrose and Theodosius illustrated both the growing public authority of bishops and its limits. The chapter concluded that by 395 the empire was Christianizing, not fully Christian: Nicene Christianity enjoyed powerful imperial backing, bishops and monasteries were major institutions, and churches had transformed cities, yet doctrinal disagreement, traditional cult, Jewish communities, and local religious variation persisted. This transformed Roman world, and not a completed Christendom, is what the chapter hands to Chapter 27, which examines how it encountered migration, warfare, accommodation, and the formation of successor states.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Homoousios
A Greek term meaning "of the same being" or "of one substance," used at Nicaea to affirm that the Son shares the divine being of the Father; it was controversial because it was not scriptural and could be read in more than one way.
Nicene
Describing those who upheld the language of the Council of Nicaea; a party label that solidified across the mid-fourth century and later became the basis of Trinitarian orthodoxy.
Homoian
Describing the position, promoted by Constantius II and Valens, that the Son is "like" the Father, without the language of "substance"; often called "Arian" by opponents.
Homoiousian
Describing the position that the Son is of "similar substance" to the Father, intermediate between Nicene and Homoian positions.
Anomoean
Describing a radical position, associated with Aetius and Eunomius, that the Son is "unlike" the Father in essence.
Trinity
The Christian doctrine that God is one being in three persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, elaborated in the controversies of the fourth century.
Creed
A concise statement of Christian belief, adopted by a council or used in worship and instruction.
Council
A formal assembly of bishops, often summoned by an emperor, to settle disputes, define doctrine, or regulate church discipline; only later were some called ecumenical.
Canon
A rule of church discipline or organization issued by a council, distinct from a doctrinal creed.
Bishop (episkopos)
The leading official of a Christian community in a city, responsible for teaching, ordination, discipline, and, increasingly, charity and public representation.
See
The seat or jurisdiction of a bishop, from Latin sedes; a "major see" is a city whose bishop held particular prestige.
Metropolitan
The bishop of a provincial capital who had recognized precedence over the other bishops of the province.
Episcopal audience
The practice, recognized by imperial law, of parties bringing disputes to a bishop for arbitration; its scope and formal status remain debated.
Catechumen
A person receiving instruction in preparation for baptism; many Christians in this period remained catechumens for long periods.
Asceticism
The disciplined practice of renunciation, such as fasting, celibacy, and poverty, for religious purposes.
Eremitic
Describing solitary or loosely grouped ascetic life, from the Greek for "desert."
Cenobitic
Describing communal monastic life under a shared discipline, associated with Pachomius and Basil.
Hagiography
Writing about saints, such as the Life of Antony, that combines biography with religious purpose and must be read critically.
Schism
A division within the church over authority or discipline, as with the Donatists, distinct from a dispute over doctrine, though the two often overlapped.
Heresy
A teaching condemned as false by a church authority; a label that reflected the outcome of struggles, not an objective description.
Orthodoxy
Doctrine accepted as correct by an authoritative body; the boundaries of orthodoxy in this period were formed during the controversies themselves.
Traditor
A cleric accused of having handed over scriptures to persecutors, central to the Donatist dispute.
Pagan
A Christian umbrella term for adherents of inherited religious practices, not a self-designation of a unified religion.
Christianization
The multifaceted, uneven process by which Christian institutions, practices, and identities spread through Roman society.
Theodosian Code
A compilation of imperial constitutions since 312, published in the 430s, a chief source for late Roman religious legislation.
Cunctos populos
The opening words of the Edict of Thessalonica of 380, which endorsed a Nicene form of Christianity.
New Rome
A title for Constantinople, invoked at the Council of 381 to justify the honor accorded its bishop.
Circumcellions
A name given by Catholic writers to itinerant militants associated with Donatism; the evidence for them is hostile and debated.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how one of the great transformations of the ancient world actually occurred. The movement of Christianity from a minority religion to a central institution of Roman public life was not the result of a single conversion or a single law. It emerged from the interaction of imperial decisions, theological argument, the growth of episcopal and monastic institutions, the work of households and local communities, and the adaptation of Roman social forms. Understanding this complexity guards against the simple narrative of persecution followed by triumph, and it helps explain why later medieval Christianity took the varied forms that it did.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern of Roman history: institutions and identities changed while old structures continued. The bishop resembled the civic patron in many respects, the martyr shrine echoed older cults of the dead, and the charitable network built on and redirected older habits of benefaction. At the same time, the conflicts examined here, over doctrine, over episcopal legitimacy, and over community boundaries, established patterns that would recur in later European history, including the entanglement of religious authority with political power and the use of doctrinal labels as markers of identity. These are patterns, not inevitabilities, and the contingency of the fourth-century outcomes is a reminder that later religious settlements were also historically contingent.

For Volume I, the chapter shows how the Roman world of Late Antiquity was transformed from within before it was transformed from without. The church that emerged by 395 was a powerful institution embedded in the empire, but it was neither uniform nor complete, and it had not yet become the Christendom of the Middle Ages. Chapter 27 takes up the encounter between this transformed Roman world and the migrations and military pressures on its frontiers, and Chapter 28 examines the end of western imperial rule. Volume II will show how, in the west, the church's institutions and networks became foundations of new political orders in Part V, while in the east, the Roman state and Orthodox Christianity continued in forms traced in Part VI.

Finally, the chapter invites students to think carefully about sources and about the differences between law and life, prescription and practice, and the voices that survive and those that do not. Bishops, emperors, and monks wrote most of the surviving evidence. The households, villages, Jewish communities, and traditional worshippers who shared their world speak more faintly. Learning to read the sources critically, and to recognize what they cannot show, is one of the essential skills of the historian, and it will be needed throughout the remaining chapters of this textbook.
