Art, Architecture, and Renaissance Patronage

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 25 March 1436, Pope Eugenius IV consecrated the cathedral of Florence beneath a dome that no one had been certain could be built. The dome was brick and stone, raised without a full wooden support structure, and it was the work of a goldsmith turned architect, a city government willing to pay for it, a guild that managed the cathedral works, and hundreds of masons, carpenters, and laborers whose names are mostly lost. The Renaissance in art was made by exactly this kind of combination: skill, money, devotion, civic pride, and negotiation.

The consecration of 1436 is a useful place to begin because it contains, in a single event, many of the themes of this chapter. The cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore had been begun in 1296, long before anything now called the Renaissance, and its Gothic nave and Italian medieval design were not abandoned when Filippo Brunelleschi proposed his solution for covering the enormous crossing. The dome was a technical and aesthetic triumph, but it was also an institutional one. It depended on the Opera del Duomo, the board that administered the cathedral's construction, on the Arte della Lana, the wool guild that supervised that board, on public taxation and private gifts, on a competition for designs, and on years of meticulous organization of materials and labor. The pope who blessed it had been driven out of Rome and was then residing in Florence. Art, architecture, religion, finance, and politics arrived together.

This chapter asks how Renaissance artists transformed the visual representation of the world, and how religious institutions, political authorities, patrons, and working practices shaped those transformations. Its first claim is that the transformation was gradual and cumulative rather than a sudden invention of realism. Painters of the fourteenth century already experimented with emotion, bodily weight, and architectural setting. Medieval builders already understood geometry and engineering. What changed across roughly the years 1300 to 1520 was the combination, in certain Italian cities, of intensified observation of nature, close study of the surviving remains of Roman antiquity, mathematical methods for organizing space, new demand from competing patrons, and an unusually dense culture of workshops in which techniques were passed on, tested, and rivaled.

The chapter's second claim is that Renaissance artworks must be understood as made objects with functions. An altarpiece was a liturgical and devotional instrument. A fresco cycle in a chapel advertised a family's piety and its claim to burial space. A bronze statue on a public building represented a guild's wealth and the city's identity. A palace façade told neighbors who held power. To explain a work one must ask who ordered it, who paid, who made it, where it stood, what it was meant to do, and what a viewer of the time would have brought to the act of looking. The chapter therefore gives sustained attention to specific works, their dates, settings, media, and the documentary evidence, such as contracts, account books, letters, tax records, and early biographies, that allow historians to reconstruct them.

The third claim concerns patronage. Popular accounts often reduce Renaissance art to the generosity of the Medici family. The Medici mattered a great deal, but they did not finance the Renaissance. Guilds, confraternities, cathedral boards, monasteries, republican councils, merchant families, bishops and popes, princely dynasties, and individual women such as Isabella d'Este all commissioned, bargained, interfered, and sometimes failed to pay. Artists, in turn, were not passive instruments. They competed for commissions, negotiated terms, raised the status of their crafts through theory and display, and sometimes refused or abandoned works. Patronage was a relationship, and its terms changed from city to city.

The chapter takes four geographic and social settings in turn: the republican and guild centered environment of Florence, the princely courts of Milan, Mantua, Ferrara, Urbino, and Naples, the merchant and confraternity culture of Venice, and the papal city of Rome in the early sixteenth century. It then follows the three celebrated figures of the High Renaissance, Leonardo da Vinci, Raphael, and Michelangelo, together with the architect Donato Bramante, as individuals working within these systems rather than above them. It closes by asking how far the traditional canon of Renaissance art, a story of progress from Giotto to Michelangelo, was shaped by later writers, and what that canon leaves out.

This chapter belongs to Part IX, Renaissance Europe, and to Volume III. Chapter 56 examined the Italian city states whose wealth and rivalry created the setting for these artistic developments, and Chapter 57 traced humanism and the recovery of classical learning. Here those political and intellectual conditions are assumed, and the focus falls on images, buildings, and sculptures themselves. Chapter 59 will turn to the northern Renaissance and Christian humanism, including the major painters of the Low Countries and Germany, and Chapter 60 will consider political thought, printing, and the longer Renaissance legacy. Where northern techniques, such as oil painting, entered Italian practice, they are noted briefly here, but their fuller treatment belongs to Chapter 59.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain Renaissance artistic innovation as a gradual transformation of medieval and Trecento traditions rather than a sudden invention of realism.
  2. Trace the development of representations of space and the human body from Giotto and Duccio through Masaccio, Piero della Francesca, and the High Renaissance.
  3. Describe how linear perspective was developed in Florence, formalized by Alberti, and applied in painting, while recognizing its limits as a universal goal.
  4. Analyze how frescoes, panel paintings, bronzes, and marble sculptures were actually made, and why materials shaped artistic possibilities and costs.
  5. Explain the engineering, financing, and organization of Brunelleschi's dome and the architectural thought of Brunelleschi and Alberti.
  6. Compare the artistic environments of Florence, Venice, Rome, and the Italian princely courts, including their patrons, institutions, and preferred forms.
  7. Interpret patronage as a relationship of wealth, devotion, public authority, social ambition, craftsmanship, and negotiation rather than simple generosity.
  8. Assess a surviving contract as historical evidence for payment, collaboration, religious requirements, and the gap between agreement and result.
  9. Evaluate the contributions and limits of Leonardo, Bramante, Raphael, and Michelangelo within their workshops, commissions, and papal or princely settings.
  10. Distinguish documented commissions, workshop contributions, later tradition, and disputed attributions when discussing famous works.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether Renaissance art represented a decisive break with the Middle Ages, and the role of Vasari in shaping the traditional canon.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what ways did Renaissance artists build on medieval and Trecento achievements, and in what ways did they depart from them?
  2. How did the study of ancient Roman remains and of nature change what artists could depict, and what limits did those sources impose?
  3. Why did mathematical perspective develop in early fifteenth century Florence, and why was it not adopted everywhere or by everyone?
  4. How did materials, such as pigments, panel, plaster, bronze, and marble, constrain artistic decisions and shape the cost and meaning of works?
  5. How did guilds, confraternities, cathedral boards, merchant families, princes, and popes differ as patrons, and why did those differences matter for the art produced?
  6. What can contracts, account books, and letters tell historians about the real conditions under which famous works were made, and what can they not tell us?
  7. Why did Florence, Venice, Rome, and the courts develop distinctive artistic cultures, and how did artists move among them?
  8. How should historians weigh the achievements of individual masters against the collaborative labor of workshops?
  9. To what extent is the familiar story of Renaissance art as a progress toward the High Renaissance a product of later interpretation?

Historical Context

Chapter 56 established the economic and political setting of this chapter. Italian city states such as Florence, Venice, Milan, and Genoa had grown rich from textile manufacture, banking, long distance commerce, and the management of credit, building on the commercial revival described in Chapter 43. The catastrophe of the Black Death of 1347 to 1351, examined in Chapter 50, reduced populations sharply, altered labor and inheritance, and, in some historians' interpretation, concentrated wealth in surviving families and institutions, though the effects on art and patronage remain debated. By the late fourteenth century, a dense urban society of guilds, confraternities, parish churches, monasteries, and governing councils was accustomed to spending on buildings and images as a form of religious expression and public display.

Chapter 57 traced the humanist recovery of classical learning, including the study of Latin and Greek texts, rhetoric, and civic ideals. The present chapter applies that history to the visual world. Humanist scholars supplied vocabulary, texts such as the Roman architect Vitruvius, and patrons or friends who valued antiquity. Yet artists also learned directly from the physical remains of Rome, from sarcophagi, statues, coins, and ruins, and from their own workshops, which depended on practical, often secret, knowledge of materials and methods. Art was a craft tradition before it was an intellectual one, and the intellectual claims made on its behalf in the fifteenth century were part of a deliberate effort to raise its status.

Religion remained the primary framework for artistic production. The late medieval church, examined in Chapter 53 and again in Chapter 61, was an enormous patron through cathedrals, parish churches, monasteries, mendicant orders such as the Franciscans and Dominicans, and the lay confraternities that organized devotion, charity, and burial. Most surviving Renaissance works were made for sacred settings or devotional purposes. Even secular subjects, such as portraits, mythological scenes, and civic allegories, were commonly produced by artists who spent most of their time on altarpieces and frescoes. Students should not imagine a Renaissance that replaced Christian art with pagan or secular art. It expanded the range of subjects while continuing to rely overwhelmingly on religious commissions.

Evidence for the period is unusually rich, though uneven. Surviving works are the principal evidence, but many were damaged, repainted, moved, cut up, or lost, including Brunelleschi's perspective panels and many early frescoes. Documents include contracts, guild statutes, tax declarations such as the Florentine catasto of 1427, account books, letters among artists and patrons, and records of building committees. Narrative sources include Lorenzo Ghiberti's commentaries, Alberti's treatises on painting, sculpture, and architecture, Leonardo's notebooks, and, above all, Giorgio Vasari's Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and Architects, first published in 1550 and revised in 1568. Vasari is indispensable and biased: he wrote as a Tuscan, mostly Florentine, artist and courtier, and shaped the idea of a progressive revival culminating in Michelangelo. Historians use him with caution and check his claims against documents and the works themselves.

Finally, the geographic emphasis of this chapter is Italian, which reflects the scope of the assigned subject and not a claim that art elsewhere in Europe was secondary. Contemporary painters in the Low Countries, such as Jan van Eyck, and in the German lands were pursuing equally sophisticated innovations, and their techniques and compositions reached Italy through trade, courts, and the movement of works and artists. These exchanges are noted where they affect Italian practice and examined fully in Chapter 59. The chronological emphasis is approximately 1300 to 1520, which is a convenient instructional frame and not a rigid boundary, since Italian artistic activity continued after 1520 and many of the developments traced here began earlier.

Section 58.1

Renaissance Art: Innovation and Medieval Continuity

The word "Renaissance," meaning rebirth, was applied to the arts by writers who believed that painting, sculpture, and architecture had declined after antiquity and revived in their own time. Giorgio Vasari gave this idea its classic form in the sixteenth century, and the nineteenth century historian Jacob Burckhardt later made the Italian Renaissance a defining moment of European modernity. Modern historians keep the label as a useful analytical convention but dispute much of the story it implies. Medieval artists did not lack skill, and Renaissance artists did not abandon medieval religion, craft traditions, or iconography. As Chapter 57 explained for humanism, the period involved selective, creative recovery of antiquity combined with continuous inheritance from the immediate past.

Several developments are nonetheless distinctive of the period roughly 1300 to 1520 in Italy. Artists represented bodies with greater weight and anatomical coherence. They organized pictorial space, increasingly through geometry. They adopted classical architectural elements, such as columns, entablatures, arches, and domes, in systematic rather than occasional ways. They developed new forms of sculpture, including freestanding figures and large scale bronze work, and new genres, including the individual portrait and the mythological painting. And they, together with writers, claimed a higher intellectual and social status for the arts. None of these changes occurred everywhere simultaneously. Florence, Siena, Padua, Venice, Milan, Urbino, and Rome followed different paths, and their artistic relations involved borrowing and rivalry.

Three cautions guide this chapter. First, "realism" is not a single standard. Medieval painters used gold grounds, symbolic scale, and stylized figures deliberately, to convey sacred meaning, not because they could not do otherwise. Renaissance naturalism served particular purposes, and it coexisted with idealization, convention, and symbolism. Second, categories such as Early and High Renaissance are interpretive and retrospective. The terms Trecento, Quattrocento, and Cinquecento, which refer to the fourteenth, fifteenth, and sixteenth centuries in Italian usage, are simpler chronological markers. Third, the artists associated with this period were not working toward a predetermined summit. They solved immediate problems for specific patrons, and many of their contemporaries made quite different choices.

Section 58.2

Giotto, Duccio, and the Fourteenth Century Artistic Inheritance

The painting of the Trecento in Florence, Siena, and Padua provides the foundation for later developments. Its principal inherited resource was the Byzantine and medieval Italian tradition sometimes called the maniera greca, or Greek manner, in which icons and altarpieces used gold backgrounds, elongated figures, and fixed compositional types. Chapter 40 and Chapter 41 examined the cultural and political relations between Latin and Greek Christendom, and these relations remained important for art, since Italian painters knew Byzantine icons and, through Venice and other ports, Byzantine craftsmen. Fourteenth century painters did not reject this tradition so much as stretch it, introducing greater volume, expression, and narrative incident.

Giotto di Bondone, born in or near Florence, is credited by Vasari, and by writers well before him, with a decisive change in painting. His surviving works, though subject to attribution questions, include the fresco cycle of the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua, painted about 1303 to 1305 for the banker Enrico Scrovegni, whose family fortune had been tainted by accusations of usury and who built the chapel in part as a pious expression and a memorial. In scenes from the lives of the Virgin and Christ, Giotto placed solid, heavy bodies within shallow but legible architectural settings. Figures turn their backs, bend, mourn, and react to one another, so that the narrative is conveyed through gesture and grouping. The famous Lamentation arranges mourners around the body of Christ under a diagonal slope that directs the eye to the central grief. Historians stress that this achievement was not the discovery of perspective, which came later, but a new command of mass, emotion, and dramatic organization.

In Siena, Duccio di Buoninsegna created in 1308 to 1311 the Maestà, a large double sided altarpiece for the cathedral. The front showed the enthroned Virgin and Child among saints and angels, and the back contained numerous small scenes of Christ's Passion. The work is more indebted to Byzantine form than Giotto's, with its luminous gold and delicate color, but its narrative panels display notable inventiveness in space and feeling. Siena's civic government and cathedral officials paid considerable sums, and the altarpiece's procession to the cathedral, recorded in later tradition, indicates its public meaning. Simone Martini, with Lippo Memmi, painted the Annunciation of 1333 for Siena Cathedral in a courtly, linear, and richly decorative style, and the brothers Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti explored space and observation more strongly. Ambrogio's Allegory and Effects of Good and Bad Government, painted in 1338 to 1339 in Siena's Palazzo Pubblico, depicted both abstract virtues and a recognizable city and countryside, and it served a government of nine magistrates as a visual statement of civic ideals.

The Black Death of 1348, discussed in Chapter 50, killed both Lorenzetti brothers, according to traditional accounts, and altered the circumstances of artistic production. Some scholars argue that later fourteenth century painting turned to more conservative, devotional, and hieratic forms. Others caution that the evidence for a clear stylistic change caused by plague is limited, and that economic recovery and continuing demand shaped works in complex ways. What is clear is that by about 1400 Italian painters had inherited a repertoire of narrative gesture, architectural setting, and fresco practice from which the next generation could depart.

Section 58.3

Classical Antiquity, Nature, and Artistic Observation

Italy was physically filled with the remains of Rome. Triumphal arches, temples, theaters, baths, columns, and sarcophagi survived in cities across the peninsula, often reused, buried, or incorporated into later buildings, and they provided a concrete, visible link with antiquity that northern Europe lacked to the same degree. Medieval artists had always been aware of these remains and had often imitated them, as Chapter 31 noted for the Carolingian period. What changed in the fifteenth century was the intensity and systematic quality of attention. Artists measured ancient buildings, drew statues and reliefs, and tried to understand the rules that governed proportions and orders. Brunelleschi and Donatello were reported by early biographers to have studied ruins in Rome, though the details of those visits are uncertain and later embellished.

Antiquity was not a single thing. Artists encountered Roman copies of Greek works, imperial portraiture, funerary reliefs, coins, engraved gems, and architectural fragments, and they interpreted them through the filter of their own interests and the texts supplied by humanists. They did not have access to the polychromy, or painted color, that originally covered much ancient sculpture, and their admiration for white marble reflects the condition in which works survived and not Roman practice. Few Greek originals were accessible, and Greek art was known mainly through Roman versions and written descriptions. The Renaissance image of the classical past was therefore a reconstruction. Understanding this helps explain why Renaissance classicism was creative rather than archaeological, and why it frequently combined ancient forms with Christian subjects.

Observation of nature was equally important, and it too had medieval precedents. Gothic sculptors had carved naturalistic foliage, and fourteenth century painters had observed light and gesture. In the fifteenth century, artists intensified the study of the human body through drawing from models and, increasingly, through anatomical knowledge. Alberti urged painters to understand the structure beneath the skin, and later artists, notably Leonardo and Michelangelo, pursued anatomy through dissection, a practice that depended on access to bodies, medical institutions, and the cooperation of physicians and other authorities. Although practical and institutional constraints limited what artists could examine, there was no general Church prohibition on human dissection. Plants, animals, drapery, and landscape were also drawn from life. Studio practice, built on drawing, stored this knowledge in model books and sketchbooks that were shared and recycled within workshops.

Section 58.4

Linear Perspective and the Representation of Space

Painters before the fifteenth century did not lack spatial representation. Giotto's chapels, Duccio's interiors, and Ambrogio Lorenzetti's tiled floors show an effort to suggest depth, using overlap, diminishing size, and converging lines. What was new in early fifteenth century Florence was a mathematical method for constructing space on a flat surface so that all the receding lines of a scene appeared to converge at a single point, with objects scaled in consistent proportion. This method is called linear perspective, and its development involved architecture, geometry, and optics as much as painting.

Historical Vocabulary
Linear Perspective

Linear perspective is a geometrical system for representing three dimensional space on a two dimensional surface. Parallel lines that recede from the viewer are drawn so that they converge at a vanishing point, usually placed on the horizon line at the viewer's eye level, and objects diminish in size at calculated rates. Fifteenth century Florentines used the terms prospettiva and, in Alberti's account, the picture as a window through which one looks at a scene.

The system matters because it changed how painters planned compositions, allowed architecture and figures to be positioned in a coherent measurable space, and gave artists a claim to mathematical knowledge. It should not be confused with "realism" as such. Perspective is one convention among others, and it presumes a single fixed viewing point. Many Renaissance and later painters adapted it or used it selectively, and other traditions in Islamic, Chinese, and Byzantine art organized space differently for their own purposes.

According to the biography of Brunelleschi attributed to Antonio Manetti, written decades afterward, Brunelleschi conducted perspective experiments around 1415 to 1420 using painted panels of Florentine buildings, including the Baptistery, viewed through a hole in the panel and compared with a mirror. The panels have not survived, and the exact procedure and date remain uncertain. Even so, historians generally accept that Brunelleschi, who combined goldsmith training, geometry, and architectural interest, played a central role in formulating the method. It would be a mistake to say that he invented all perspective, or that earlier painters had no spatial skill. He contributed a rigorous construction technique associated with the single vanishing point.

The theory was set out by Leon Battista Alberti in De pictura (On Painting), composed in Latin in 1435 and in an Italian version in 1436, the latter dedicated to Brunelleschi. Alberti explained how to construct the pictorial surface using a grid, how to determine the diminution of floor tiles, and how composition, color, and the figures' relation to a narrative (istoria) should be organized. His book is both a technical manual and a humanist argument that painting is a liberal art grounded in geometry, rhetoric, and observation. It circulated in manuscript to artists and patrons, and its influence is visible, though the extent to which individual painters read it is difficult to establish.

Masaccio is credited with the first major application of the system to monumental painting, most clearly in the Holy Trinity at Santa Maria Novella, painted about 1425 to 1427. The fresco presents the Trinity, the Virgin, Saint John, and two kneeling donors within an illusionistic barrel vaulted chapel, with the vanishing point located at about the eye level of a standing viewer. Piero della Francesca later wrote a treatise on perspective in painting and used geometry to create calm, luminous compositions. Perspective spread through workshops and theoretical writings, but it was never a universal goal. Venetian painters, as discussed below, often emphasized color and atmosphere, and many artists adapted the rules to the demands of altarpiece, fresco, or devotional image. Scholars have also noted that the Florentine enthusiasm for perspective was connected to practical concerns of commerce and measurement, such as surveying and accounting, a suggestion that remains influential but is debated.

Section 58.5

Materials, Techniques, and the Making of Renaissance Art

An artwork's materials determined its price, durability, and the choices open to its maker, and contracts often specified them in detail. Painting on panel began with wood, commonly poplar in Tuscany, which carpenters assembled and which the painter prepared with layers of gesso, a mixture of gypsum and animal glue, polished to a smooth surface. Gilding applied thin leaf, which could be burnished and tooled, over a reddish clay layer called bole. Pigments were drawn from many sources and some were extraordinarily costly. Ultramarine, made from lapis lazuli mined in what is now Afghanistan and imported through Mediterranean trade, could cost more than gold, so patrons sometimes insisted on its use in the Virgin's robe while artists protested at the expense. Cheaper blues such as azurite, reds such as vermilion and lake, and greens such as verdigris were also widely used, and many pigments were imported through Venice and other commercial centers.

Historical Vocabulary
Fresco and Tempera

Fresco (from the Italian for "fresh") is a technique of painting with pigments mixed with water on wet lime plaster, the intonaco. As the plaster dries, a chemical reaction binds the pigment into the wall. Because the plaster must be wet, painters worked in sections called giornate, or "day's work," whose joins can often be seen today. Painters transferred designs from drawings or full scale cartoons to the wall, often by pricking the outlines and dusting them with charcoal, or by incising them. An underdrawing in red earth, a sinopia, sometimes guided the design.

Tempera usually refers to egg tempera, in which pigments are bound with egg yolk, producing a quick drying, matte, precise medium suited to panels and to details added over fresco after drying. Both techniques demanded planning and discipline, since errors were difficult to correct. Their qualities help explain the clear outlines and luminous flatness of many Quattrocento works, and the appeal of oil painting, which dried slowly and allowed blending.

Oil painting, in which pigments are suspended in drying oils such as linseed, was not invented by Italians, and it existed before the fifteenth century. Netherlandish artists such as Jan van Eyck and Robert Campin refined its techniques and demonstrated new possibilities for glazing, color, and the representation of surfaces, and their example reached Italy through imported works, traveling painters, and merchants. Italian artists adopted it gradually. The Sicilian painter Antonello da Messina, who may have visited Venice in 1475 to 1476, was important in transmitting the technique to Venice, where it flourished by the early sixteenth century. Oil allowed layered glazes, soft transitions, and rich color. Its fuller discussion belongs to Chapter 59, but students should notice here that Italian art owed technical debts to the north.

Sculptors worked in different materials with different constraints. Marble came from quarries such as those at Carrara and was extracted, transported, and bought at considerable expense. A carver removed material and could not add it back, so the choice and condition of the block shaped the design, as Michelangelo's David later demonstrated. Bronze required the lost wax process, in which a wax model was encased in a mold, melted out, and replaced by molten metal, and it demanded foundry expertise, large amounts of metal, and often a team of specialists. Bronze doors and statues were among the most expensive artistic commissions of the century. Drawing, finally, was a basic tool of every craft. Silverpoint, pen and ink, chalk, and charcoal were used on prepared paper, vellum, or boards, and drawings recorded ideas, prepared compositions, and trained apprentices.

Architects relied on drawings, scale models in wood or clay, and the practical knowledge of master masons. Brick, stone, lime, timber, and iron were obtained from specific suppliers, and construction was organized by building administrations that purchased materials, hired labor, and kept accounts. The cost and logistics of building, and the dependence of design on available skills and equipment, explain why architectural innovation often followed patterns of local resources and institutional capacity.

Section 58.6

Masaccio, Fra Angelico, and Early Renaissance Painting

Tommaso di Ser Giovanni, called Masaccio, was a Florentine painter who died in 1428, probably at the age of twenty-six, after a brief and astonishing career. His surviving works include the Holy Trinity and the frescoes of the Brancacci Chapel in Santa Maria del Carmine. The chapel was commissioned by Felice Brancacci, a wealthy merchant and diplomat, and was decorated about 1424 to 1428 in collaboration with Masolino da Panicale, an older painter whose gentler style contrasts with Masaccio's. The chapel's scenes from the life of Saint Peter include The Tribute Money, in which Christ instructs Peter to pay a tax collector, and the Expulsion from the Garden of Eden, in which Adam and Eve display grief with strikingly physical directness. The frescoes were left unfinished after Brancacci was exiled in 1436 and were completed in the 1480s by Filippino Lippi. Their combination of weighty figures, consistent light from a single direction, and perspectival architecture influenced generations of painters, including Michelangelo, who is said to have drawn there.

The Holy Trinity was a prominent commission in Santa Maria Novella, probably paid for by a patron whose identity remains uncertain, and its kneeling donors outside the architectural space identify lay devotion within the sacred scene. The painted skeleton below, with its inscription reminding viewers of mortality, links the spatial innovation to a message about death and salvation. Such features show that perspective was used to intensify a religious meaning, not to replace it.

Fra Angelico, a Dominican friar born Guido di Pietro, represents a different path. He painted altarpieces and frescoes for the convent of San Marco in Florence in the late 1430s and 1440s, including the cell paintings that supported the meditation of friars and the well known Annunciation at the head of a stairway. His work combines the new spatial understanding with luminous color, delicate figures, and devotional restraint. The restoration of San Marco was funded in part by Cosimo de' Medici, whose patronage extended to monastic institutions, and this relationship illustrates how a family might connect personal piety, social standing, and civic reputation. Fra Angelico also worked for popes in Rome, and his career is a reminder that religious painting remained at the center of the Quattrocento.

Piero della Francesca, working from his native Borgo San Sepolcro and at the courts of Urbino and Rimini, brought geometry to painting with exceptional rigor. His Flagellation, whose date and meaning remain debated, places a distant scene of Christ's torment in an architectural space measured with precision while three foreground figures converse in a contemporary setting, and scholars have proposed several interpretations of their identities. The Montefeltro Altarpiece of about 1472 to 1474 and the double portrait of Federico da Montefeltro and Battista Sforza show his command of light, form, and landscape. He wrote treatises on perspective and mathematics, and his career shows the Early Renaissance reaching beyond Florence into central Italy's courts.

Section 58.7

Ghiberti, Donatello, and Renaissance Sculpture

Sculpture in Florence developed in public view, on cathedral, guild, and civic buildings. In 1401, the Arte di Calimala, the guild of cloth finishers and merchants responsible for the Baptistery, organized a competition for a new pair of bronze doors, inviting several sculptors, among them Filippo Brunelleschi and Lorenzo Ghiberti, to create a trial relief of the Sacrifice of Isaac. The jury, composed of experts and officials, chose Ghiberti, whose trial panel was cast in fewer pieces, and Brunelleschi, according to later accounts, turned to architecture. Ghiberti's doors, completed in 1424, depict scenes from the New Testament in a quatrefoil framework still indebted to Gothic design. He received a second commission for the east doors, made from 1425 to 1452 and later called the "Gates of Paradise," whose ten large rectangular panels used perspectival space and remarkably deep relief. A workshop of many assistants, including some who became important artists, cast and finished the work, and Ghiberti's own commentaries preserve his reflections on antiquity and artistic history.

Donatello (Donato di Niccolò di Betto Bardi) was among the most inventive sculptors of the century. Trained as a goldsmith and in Ghiberti's workshop, he made marble and bronze figures for Orsanmichele, the grain market church whose exterior niches were assigned to the major guilds, each of which commissioned a statue of its patron saint. His Saint George, made about 1415 to 1417 for the armorers' guild, shows a young warrior in firm, balanced stance whose alert expression is part of the work's lasting reputation. His prophets for the cathedral campanile, his wooden Magdalene, and his work in relief, in which he developed the very shallow rilievo schiacciato, or "flattened relief," show an extraordinary range.

His bronze David, a life size nude figure of the biblical hero standing on Goliath's head, is one of the best known works of the period, yet its date, patron, and original setting remain disputed. It is generally associated with the Medici household, and may have stood in the courtyard of their palace, though scholars place its creation anywhere from the 1430s to the 1460s. The work revives the freestanding bronze nude, previously unfamiliar since antiquity, and employs contrapposto, the relaxed, weight shifting stance in which the body's axes oppose each other. Whether it was intended primarily as a civic symbol, a Medici statement, or an exercise in classical form is debated, and responsible discussion acknowledges that evidence is incomplete. Later in life Donatello worked in Padua, where his equestrian bronze Gattamelata, cast about 1447 to 1453, commemorated a mercenary commander in a form recalling Roman imperial monuments, and for the Basilica of Saint Anthony he created a high altar with bronze reliefs and figures.

Sculpture also flourished beyond Florence, from the work of Jacopo della Quercia in Siena and Bologna to Venetian tomb monuments and Neapolitan court commissions. Funerary monuments in particular, set in churches, advertised families' status and religious hopes. Female sculptors were rare, and documentation of them is thin. Properzia de' Rossi of Bologna, active in the early sixteenth century, is among the few women with documented work in marble, but her career is known mainly from later sources and from a small number of works. The material and social demands of carving, casting, and guild membership limited women's participation.

Section 58.8

Brunelleschi and the Transformation of Florentine Architecture

The cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore was begun in 1296 under Arnolfo di Cambio, and by the early fifteenth century its nave was substantially built, leaving a huge octagonal opening above the crossing, about forty-five meters across, to be covered. The problem was both structural and financial. A conventional dome of that size would need enormous timber centering, a support structure, and the supply of suitably large timbers was a serious obstacle. The Opera del Duomo held a competition around 1418, and in 1420 Brunelleschi, together with Ghiberti who was initially associated with the project, was engaged. Their rivalry and the division of responsibility are recorded in documents and in anecdotes of uncertain reliability.

Brunelleschi's design used a double shell, with an inner dome carrying the load and an outer one protecting it from the weather, and ribs to connect them. Stone and iron chains bound the structure at various levels to resist outward thrust, and bricks laid in a herringbone pattern helped the courses support themselves as they rose. Machines devised for the project, including ox powered hoists for lifting stone and brick, drew the admiration of contemporaries. The dome's structure was completed to its summit by 1436 and consecrated on 25 March of that year. The marble lantern that crowns it, designed by Brunelleschi, was built after his death in 1446, with completion in about 1461. The dome, therefore, should not be confused with the cathedral as a whole, which remained a largely Gothic building with a later façade, and the dome itself combined Gothic pointed profile and classical sensibility.

Brunelleschi also designed buildings that demonstrate his command of classical vocabulary and clear geometric proportion. The Ospedale degli Innocenti, a foundling hospital begun in 1419 and funded by the silk guild, features a loggia with slender columns, round arches, and modular bays. The church of San Lorenzo, begun in the 1420s with the support of the Medici family and the parish, and the Old Sacristy, associated with Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici, display a rational interior of gray stone articulated against white plaster. The Pazzi Chapel, begun later and finished after his death, shows a similar clarity. These buildings demonstrate that Renaissance architecture was not simply revived Roman building. Brunelleschi reinterpreted Romanesque Tuscan forms, Roman remains, and Gothic engineering to create something new.

The social context is essential. The dome belonged to a republican city proud of its independence, and public finance, guild oversight, and supervised competition made it possible. The Innocenti project reflected both charity and guild prestige, since a hospital for abandoned infants served a pressing social need in a city with many such children, a fact visible in the institution's later records. The Medici support for San Lorenzo reflected family devotion and political reputation. Each case shows architecture serving communal and private purposes together, and illustrates how building required sustained administration of labor, materials, and finance.

Section 58.9

Leon Battista Alberti and Classical Architectural Design

Leon Battista Alberti, born in Genoa in 1404 to an exiled Florentine family and educated in humanist circles, was a writer, scholar, and designer rather than a trained builder. He wrote treatises on painting, on sculpture, and on architecture. De re aedificatoria (On the Art of Building), composed about 1443 to 1452 and not printed until 1485, was modeled on the work of the Roman architect Vitruvius but corrected and extended it by drawing on Alberti's own observation of ancient ruins. He insisted that beauty arose from harmonious proportions and from the appropriate relationship of every part to the whole, and he discussed the classical orders, the uses of different building types, and the principles of urban planning, structure, and decoration. Because his treatise appeared before printed illustrations became common, its influence developed through manuscripts and later through the sixteenth century printed treatises discussed in Chapter 60 in connection with printing.

His own designs show how he applied classical principles to existing needs. The Palazzo Rucellai in Florence, built for the wealthy merchant Giovanni Rucellai during the middle decades of the fifteenth century, has a façade traditionally attributed to Alberti, with superimposed pilasters of different orders over three stories, an arrangement recalling the Roman Colosseum, while the building incorporated older houses behind a unified front. The palace was built in phases, and scholars continue to debate its chronology, some placing parts of the work in the 1460s, and the respective roles of Alberti and the builder Bernardo Rossellino. At Santa Maria Novella, Alberti completed the façade between about 1456 and 1470, also for Rucellai, harmonizing the older Gothic and Romanesque church with a geometric marble design and large volutes that connect the nave to the aisles. At Rimini, he transformed the church of San Francesco into the Tempio Malatestiano, begun about 1450, for the ruler Sigismondo Malatesta, wrapping the Gothic structure in a triumphal arch design that served the patron's dynastic glorification. At Sant'Andrea in Mantua, commissioned by Ludovico Gonzaga and begun in 1472, the year of Alberti's death, a monumental nave and façade evoked Roman basilicas and triumphal arches.

Alberti's work illustrates both the strengths and the limits of architectural theory. He rarely supervised construction personally, and executed buildings depended on local master builders who followed his drawings or models, adapted them, and finished them after his death. The relationship between a humanist treatise and a completed church was therefore mediated by craftspeople, budgets, and patron preferences. Students should not imagine a Renaissance in which learned theory replaced craft practice. The two interacted, and the status of the architect as an intellectual rather than a master mason was a development that took time and was never complete.

Section 58.10

Florence: Guilds, the Medici, and Civic Patronage

Florentine art was shaped by a society in which corporations, families, and religious bodies competed to make their presence visible. Chapter 56 described the republic's government, with its guild based citizenship and its recurring struggles among powerful families. For art, the important point is that many commissions were public or semi public. The Arte di Calimala sponsored the Baptistery doors. The Arte della Lana oversaw the cathedral works. The Arte della Seta funded the Innocenti. The major guilds each commissioned a statue for Orsanmichele, and their choices of artists, subjects, and materials contributed to rivalry and civic identity. Guild statutes, deliberations, and payments survive in archives and allow historians to trace commissions with remarkable precision.

Historical Vocabulary
Patronage and Commission

Patronage is the support, financial and social, that individuals, families, corporations, or institutions gave to artists and architects. A commission is a specific order for a work, usually governed by an agreement, often a written contract, that specified subject, materials, size, price, deadlines, and sometimes the artist's own participation. Historians use the terms to analyze a relationship of mutual dependence, in which patrons sought devotion, prestige, memorial, political advantage, or pleasure, and artists sought income, reputation, and opportunity.

The modern picture of a wealthy individual freely funding genius is misleading. Many patrons were groups, such as guilds, confraternities, or cathedral boards. Patrons often constrained design, and artists bargained, delayed, or sued. The word "patron" was less common in contemporary documents than words for the specific agreement, and Renaissance people also spoke of obligation, honor, and magnificence. Patronage also involved the display of wealth as a virtue, a theme Chapter 57 touched on in humanist writing about magnificence and civic generosity.

Confraternities, lay brotherhoods devoted to prayer, charity, burial, and festivals, were among the most numerous patrons in Italian cities. Their members included artisans, merchants, and professionals, and they commissioned altarpieces, banners, processional images, and chapels. Families also sponsored chapels in churches, often securing burial rights and the right to display their arms and portraits. Such commissions blurred piety and status. Gifts to the church were understood as acts of charity and as claims to social memory, and criticism of such display, which grew in the sixteenth century, appears in the later chapters on reform.

The Medici were extraordinarily influential, but their role must be proportioned. Cosimo de' Medici, who led Florence's politics from 1434 until his death in 1464, supported San Lorenzo, San Marco, and the Medici palace designed by Michelozzo, and he and his family commissioned works from Donatello, Fra Angelico, Fra Filippo Lippi, and others. Lorenzo de' Medici, who led from 1469 to 1492, collected antiquities and gems, supported poets and philosophers, and patronized artists including Botticelli, whose Primavera and Birth of Venus, produced in the 1480s, were made for Medici circles and relatives, with the exact patron and interpretation of the first still debated. Lorenzo also arranged for Florentine artists to work abroad, in part as diplomatic gestures. Yet the Medici did not finance most of what Florentines saw. Much of the city's art was paid for by guilds, parishes, confraternities, and other families such as the Strozzi, Rucellai, Sassetti, Tornabuoni, and Brancacci. The Medici were expelled in 1494 when French invasion destabilized their regime, and the republic that followed, described in Chapter 56, commissioned public works, including the commissions that brought Leonardo and Michelangelo into competition in the early sixteenth century.

Money underlay all of this. Florentine wealth rested on cloth manufacture, banking, and trade, and its florin, minted in gold likely obtained through trade routes extending to West Africa and elsewhere, served as a standard coin across Europe. Jewish moneylenders and bankers operated in Tuscan towns under licenses and, though largely excluded from guilds and civic commissions, participated in the credit economy that made much spending possible. Historians such as Richard Goldthwaite have argued that the rise in demand for art and buildings reflected an expanding market for material goods and display, not only noble taste. Wealthy families spent on palaces, chapels, furniture, and ceramics, and a competitive market encouraged artists to specialize and innovate.

Section 58.11

Religious Images, Sacred Spaces, and Devotional Art

An altarpiece was not merely a decoration. It stood on or behind an altar where the Mass was celebrated, framing the sacrament, and it communicated the identity of the church, order, or confraternity that owned it. Many were polyptychs, with multiple panels in architectural frames, which gradually gave way in the fifteenth century to unified single panels, sometimes called sacre conversazioni, in which the Virgin and Child sit with saints in a shared space. The shift reflects changing taste and spatial thinking, and it was not abrupt. Frames, often carved and gilded by specialist craftsmen, were part of the object and sometimes cost as much as the painting.

Fresco programs in chapels, chapter houses, and cloisters presented sacred narratives for contemplation. Dominican and Franciscan convents commissioned images to assist prayer. Domenico Ghirlandaio's frescoes in the Sassetti Chapel in Santa Trinita and the Tornabuoni Chapel in Santa Maria Novella, painted in the 1480s and 1490s, placed biblical events in contemporary Florentine settings and included portraits of donors and relatives, showing how sacred stories could carry family claims. Private devotion also generated images. Small panels of the Madonna and Child, crucifixes, and domestic altars were used in bedrooms and household chapels, and painted or sculpted images supported prayer, protection, and the cultural expectations of marriage and childbirth. Cassone chests and birth trays, though not strictly religious, were painted for domestic and ceremonial occasions.

Religious demand shaped style. Preachers such as the Dominican Savonarola, who dominated Florentine religious life in the 1490s and denounced vanity, encouraged some citizens to destroy luxury goods, including works of art, in the so called bonfires of 1497, though the extent of destruction and the involvement of artists are complicated matters. Devotional movements of the fifteenth century, including the cult of the Holy Name, the Passion, and the Virgin, encouraged particular kinds of images. Chapter 61 will examine the late medieval church and demands for reform, and Chapter 65 will consider how Catholic reform later influenced the regulation of religious art. For the period of this chapter, religious images served believers' devotion, and artists who made them were believers working within doctrinal and liturgical boundaries.

Sacred art was also international. Altarpieces, devotional panels, and carved works were exported through commercial networks, and Italian churches contained images from the Low Countries, such as the Portinari Altarpiece by Hugo van der Goes, brought to Florence about 1483 for the Medici bank's agent, Tommaso Portinari. Venetian and Genoese merchants carried icons from Crete, whose workshops produced Greek style panels for Orthodox and Catholic customers, a reminder that Byzantine tradition persisted as a living practice, not merely a source of inheritance. Jewish and Muslim communities in Italy did not participate in the Christian tradition of religious figural painting, but Jewish communities developed their own artistic culture in manuscripts and synagogue objects, and Islamic textiles, ceramics, and carpets from the Mamluk and Ottoman worlds appear in Italian paintings as luxury goods that suggest broader trade.

Section 58.12

Workshops, Apprenticeship, Contracts, and the Status of Artists

Most Renaissance art was made in the bottega, a workshop headed by a master who owned the premises, held contracts, trained apprentices, and employed assistants. Painters in Florence normally belonged to the guild of physicians and apothecaries (the Arte dei Medici e Speziali), which also included those who traded in pigments, and a separate company, the Compagnia di San Luca, formed in 1339 as a devotional and mutual aid association. Sculptors and architects often belonged to the stone and wood workers' guild, and goldsmiths to the silk guild. Guild membership allowed an artist to take commissions and enroll apprentices, and in many cities it regulated quality and competition. Venice and Siena had their own arrangements, as did the courts, where the artist might be a salaried member of the household.

Training began young. A boy, typically between about ten and fourteen, entered a master's workshop under a contract with his family, often living in the master's house. He learned to grind pigments, prepare panels, draw, copy the master's models, and eventually paint minor passages. Assistants and journeymen carried out much of the work on larger commissions. Verrocchio's Florentine workshop, which trained Leonardo and produced painting, sculpture, metalwork, and decorations, illustrates the breadth of work and the collaborative habits of such shops. Training in a workshop was practical, but it also included drawing from models, studying antiquities, and, increasingly, exposure to mathematics and writing. Artists such as Alberti, who came from humanist circles, helped push the idea that the arts required learning.

Contracts survive in large numbers and reveal the terms of work: subject, size, materials, price, schedule, and often the requirement that the master paint important parts himself. They show that artists were bound to expectations about quality, that patrons could and did monitor progress, and that payment was commonly staged. They also show disagreement. Artists sued patrons who withheld payment, and patrons sued artists who failed to deliver. An important development was the changing status of artists. Painters, sculptors, and architects began to claim that their work was intellectual as well as manual, supported by treatises, by the classical comparison of painting to poetry, and by the Italian concept of disegno, meaning both drawing and design, the mental plan behind every art. By the sixteenth century, famous artists such as Michelangelo and Raphael were treated as nearly courtly figures, though that elevated treatment was not shared by most craftsmen.

Primary Source Window

The 1483 Contract for The Virgin of the Rocks, Milan

Creators: The Confraternity of the Immaculate Conception, Milan, with Leonardo da Vinci and the brothers Evangelista and Ambrogio de Predis · Date: 25 April 1483 · Type: A notarial contract for a painted and gilded altarpiece, with a related carved frame, for the chapel of the confraternity in the church of San Francesco Grande

Context: The confraternity, devoted to the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception of the Virgin, which was a subject of theological discussion in the period, commissioned an altarpiece for its chapel. It engaged the carver Giacomo del Maino for the elaborate frame and the three painters for the panels and their gilding and finishing. Leonardo had arrived in Milan about 1482, and this was an early Milanese commission.

Paraphrase of the contract's main terms The agreement identified the three painters as jointly responsible and set out the central subject of the Virgin and Child with angels, together with side panels depicting angels playing and singing. It specified the colors and gilded decoration the work was to contain, including costly materials, and set a completion date near the end of the year. The agreement provided for payment of 800 lire, together with a further sum to be assessed, and the financial arrangements subsequently became the subject of a prolonged dispute. This summary is a paraphrase of the documented terms and not a translation of the text.

Analysis: The contract shows that a celebrated painting began as a business arrangement with precise requirements. Religious meaning, materials, and cost were fixed in advance, and the painters were jointly accountable. It reveals collaboration, since Leonardo shared the commission with the de Predis brothers, who were established Milanese painters, and documents show that Ambrogio de Predis worked on associated panels. Subsequent records show a dispute. The painters later argued that the price had not covered the costs, and the confraternity and artists negotiated for years. Two versions of the composition survive, one in the Louvre and one in the National Gallery in London, and scholars continue to debate their dates, the roles of Leonardo and his collaborators, and how the dispute and the pictures relate to each other.

The contract does not tell historians how Leonardo conceived the composition, whether he painted all of it, or how contemporaries judged the result. It tells us what the patron wanted and the framework in which the work was made, and it should be read together with the paintings themselves and later records.

Multiple Perspectives

The Commission: Patrons, Artists, Clergy, and Assistants

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on patterns visible in contracts and records. They are not quotations from named historical individuals, and the groups did not hold equal power.

From the perspective of a confraternity or other lay patron: A commission fulfilled religious obligation and group identity. The members wanted a work of visible quality, appropriate doctrine, and costly materials that honored the Virgin and the brotherhood, delivered on time and within a budget that came from member dues and bequests.
From the perspective of the master artist: The contract secured income and prestige but could also limit creative choice and leave the artist financially exposed if materials proved more expensive than expected. Reputation depended on pleasing patrons while demonstrating distinctive skill, and disputes over price could be pursued through petitions.
From the perspective of clergy or religious supervisors: The altarpiece had to conform to doctrine and liturgical use. Friars or priests who hosted the chapel cared about the work's placement, theological correctness, and its effect on worshippers, and they sometimes held rights over the space.
From the perspective of workshop assistants, carvers, and gilders: They performed much of the time consuming labor, such as preparing panels, carving, gilding, and underpainting, usually without individual recognition. Their wages were low and their names rarely survive, but their skills determined the quality of the finished object.
Everyday Life and Social History

Working and Learning Inside a Renaissance Workshop

The workshop was usually a combined workplace and household, located on a street or near a church, where the master and his family lived and where apprentices ate, slept, and worked. The day began with preparation. Boys ground pigments by hand on a stone slab, boiled glue for gesso, cleaned brushes made from animal hair, prepared panels with layers of gesso, and kept the fire and materials in order. They copied drawings and models kept in the shop, learned to draw hands, drapery, and heads, and gradually painted backgrounds, borders, and secondary figures. The master controlled quality, designed compositions, and took charge of the most visible parts.

Because paint and plaster dry at different rates and panels need time to settle, work followed schedules dictated by materials and seasons. Fresco painters worked quickly on wet plaster and moved scaffolds from section to section, often in cold chapels. Light conditions, religious holidays, and commissions' deadlines affected the working year. Workshops also made lower cost goods, such as painted chests, banners, coats of arms, and decorations for festivals and weddings, which provided steady income. Illness, plague, and fires were constant risks. Family relationships shaped workshops, since sons often inherited the master's tools and drawings, wives and daughters kept accounts or assisted in preparation, and brothers collaborated. The Bellini family of Venice, the della Robbia sculptors of Florence, and the Lippi family show how workshops became family enterprises.

Section 58.13

Princely Courts and the Art of Political Power

While Florence and Venice were republics, much of Italy was ruled by dynasties whose legitimacy depended on military success, imperial or papal recognition, and public display. Chapter 56 described the political conditions of these states. For art, courts offered a different pattern of patronage. A ruler might employ a painter as a court servant with a salary, housing, and obligations to decorate palaces, design festivals, paint portraits, and supply diplomatic gifts. Courts competed for the best artists and humanists, and artists moved among them, taking techniques and models with them.

In Mantua, the Gonzaga hired Andrea Mantegna, trained in Padua and deeply influenced by Donatello and ancient sculpture, who served the family from about 1459 until his death in 1506. His Camera degli Sposi (Bridal Chamber), painted about 1465 to 1474 in the Ducal Palace for Ludovico Gonzaga, shows the family and court in illusionistic space, including a ceiling oculus with an apparent opening to the sky. Mantegna later painted the Triumphs of Caesar for the Gonzaga, a series reflecting the court's interest in Roman history. In Ferrara, the Este family, notably Borso and Ercole I, sponsored the decoration of the Palazzo Schifanoia, where Francesco del Cossa and others painted the Hall of the Months in the 1470s with astrological, courtly, and agricultural scenes, and the court drew on Netherlandish art as well as Italian. In Urbino, Federico da Montefeltro, a successful mercenary captain who ruled from 1444 to 1482, built the Palazzo Ducale and filled it with books, paintings, and a small study, the studiolo, decorated with inlaid wood panels that depicted books, instruments, and symbols of learning. He employed Piero della Francesca, Netherlandish painters, and architects including Luciano Laurana, and the court became a model of cultivated rule.

In Milan, the Sforza dynasty, which had seized power in 1450, commissioned buildings, fortifications, and art, and Ludovico Sforza, called il Moro, ruled in the 1480s and 1490s with the help of artists and engineers, including Leonardo. In Naples, Alfonso V of Aragon, who conquered the kingdom in 1442, ordered a triumphal arch for the Castel Nuovo, built in the 1450s and later, drawing on Roman imperial models and demonstrating how a dynastic conqueror could appropriate the vocabulary of antiquity. Courts also used art for marriage alliances and diplomatic relations, sending portraits, medals, and gifts.

Isabella d'Este (1474 to 1539), daughter of Ercole I of Ferrara and wife of Francesco II Gonzaga of Mantua, is the best documented woman patron of the period. She assembled a study and an adjoining small room, a grotta, with paintings by Mantegna, Perugino, Lorenzo Costa, and later Correggio, along with antiquities, instruments, books, and objects. Her letters survive in great number and show a determined collector who instructed artists in extraordinary detail, pressed them for results, and sometimes failed to get what she wanted. She asked Leonardo, who drew her portrait in 1499 to 1500 while visiting Mantua, to paint it or another picture, and he never did, and she sought works from Giovanni Bellini, who resisted her requests for a fixed subject. Her patronage shows that women could shape art, particularly in elite circles, and that patron and artist negotiated rather than one simply commanding.

Section 58.14

Venetian Renaissance Painting: Color, Light, and Commerce

Venice was a maritime republic governed by a patrician elite, with a doge as ceremonial head and a complex set of councils. Its wealth came from trade with the eastern Mediterranean, Egypt, and the Black Sea, and its artistic culture reflected that position. Byzantine mosaics in San Marco, Islamic goods in markets, Gothic palaces on the Grand Canal, and Greek icons in the city formed a visual environment distinct from Florence. Venetian painters did not simply follow the Florentine path. They worked in a setting with strong state ceremonial, lay confraternities called scuole, which commissioned large cycles of narrative paintings for their meeting halls, and a mercantile clientele eager for portraits and devotional pictures.

Giovanni Bellini (about 1430 to 1516), from a family of painters, trained with his father Jacopo and was influenced by his brother in law Mantegna, and by Antonello da Messina's example in oil painting. His altarpieces, such as the San Giobbe Altarpiece of the 1480s and the San Zaccaria Altarpiece of 1505, set saints in luminous architectural settings, and his Portrait of Doge Leonardo Loredan of about 1501 to 1502 combines official dignity with careful individual observation. He ran a major workshop that trained Giorgione and Titian. Vittore Carpaccio, active about 1490 to 1525, painted narrative cycles for the scuole, including the Legend of Saint Ursula for the Scuola di Sant'Orsola in the 1490s, which includes detailed depictions of Venetian and eastern settings, costumes, and ships, valued by historians as visual evidence of the city's life, although such images are stylized and should not be read as photographs.

Giorgione (about 1477 to 1510), who died young, is a figure of considerable mystery, and many works attributed to him are debated. The Tempest, about 1506 to 1508, set a soldier and a nursing woman in a landscape under a stormy sky, and its subject remains uncertain, which is itself historically revealing of the market for pictures whose meaning was left open for cultured private owners. Titian (about 1488 to 1576), whose long career extends beyond this chapter, painted in the 1510s the Assumption of the Virgin for the Franciscan church of the Frari, completed in 1518, whose large scale, bright color, and dynamic figures announced a new grandeur. Venetian painters used oil on canvas increasingly, a support well suited to a damp climate and to large works that could be rolled and transported, and they explored color and atmosphere to create form, a quality sometimes contrasted with Florentine emphasis on drawing, though the contrast is a generalization and not a rule.

Venice's international position encouraged exchange. German merchants resided at the Fondaco dei Tedeschi, and Albrecht Dürer, discussed in Chapter 59, visited Venice in 1505 to 1507, absorbing and influencing local painting. Venetians traded and fought with the Ottomans, and Gentile Bellini's 1479 to 1481 residence at Mehmed II's court, where he painted the sultan's portrait, shows how art functioned within diplomacy between a Christian republic and an Islamic empire. The republic also created the Jewish ghetto in 1516, a setting that confined a community while also preserving its commercial and cultural life, and Jewish residents contributed to the city's economy and printing, though not as patrons of the figural art discussed here.

Section 58.15

Leonardo da Vinci: Observation, Experiment, and Artistic Practice

Leonardo da Vinci was born in 1452 in or near the town of Vinci, the illegitimate son of a notary, and entered the Florentine workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio in the 1460s. There he learned painting, sculpture, metalwork, and mechanical skills. The Baptism of Christ, made in Verrocchio's shop in the 1470s, is traditionally said to include an angel painted by Leonardo, and the claim is widely accepted, though workshop collaboration makes certainty difficult. His early independent works include the Annunciation and the portrait of Ginevra de' Benci. His unfinished Adoration of the Magi, commissioned in 1481 by the monks of San Donato a Scopeto, was left behind when he went to Milan about 1482, and it shows his elaborate preparatory drawing and his interest in crowds, emotion, and perspective.

In Milan, working for Ludovico Sforza and for other patrons, Leonardo served as a painter, engineer, designer of festivals, and court entertainer. He proposed military machines and canal projects in a famous letter of self presentation, though much of what he proposed was unrealized. His model for an enormous bronze equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza was never cast, and the clay model was destroyed when French troops captured Milan in 1499. His surviving paintings from the period include The Virgin of the Rocks, discussed above, and The Last Supper, painted about 1495 to 1498 on the refectory wall of the Dominican convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie. The picture shows Christ at the moment he announces a betrayal, with the apostles responding in groups of three. The perspective converges behind Christ's head, and the composition links gesture, space, and narrative with clarity. Leonardo, however, did not use traditional fresco. He experimented with oil and tempera on a dry wall, a technique that gave him time and control but proved unstable, and the painting began to deteriorate within decades. What viewers see today reflects centuries of damage, restoration, and the major cleaning completed in 1999.

Leonardo's notebooks, written in mirror script on thousands of surviving pages, record studies of anatomy, optics, water, plants, geology, flight, machines, and painting. They were not published in his lifetime and had limited influence on his contemporaries, though some drawings and ideas circulated through pupils. He reported dissecting human bodies, and his surviving anatomical drawings of the skull, muscles, heart, and fetus are remarkable, though claims about the exact number of dissections rest partly on later accounts. He sought to understand how light and shadow shape appearance, developing chiaroscuro, the modeling of form through light and dark, and sfumato, the soft blending of tones without hard outlines, qualities visible in the Mona Lisa, begun about 1503 in Florence and kept with him until his death. The identity of the sitter has long been identified, on the basis of early sources, as Lisa Gherardini, wife of Francesco del Giocondo, though the painting's purpose and completion remain debated.

Leonardo returned to Florence in 1500, worked briefly for Cesare Borgia as a military engineer, and in 1503 received a major civic commission, the Battle of Anghiari, for the Council Hall of the Palazzo della Signoria, where Michelangelo was commissioned to paint a companion scene. Leonardo received the commission and prepared substantial studies, but the project remained unfinished. Accounts of his experimental technique describe technical difficulties. The extent of any completed wall painting, its subsequent fate, and whether physical remains survived later renovations of the hall are disputed, though copies and drawings preserve something of the design. Leonardo moved again, to Milan, Rome, and finally to France, where Francis I gave him a residence at Amboise, and he died there in 1519. He completed relatively few paintings. His influence rested on those works, on his drawings, and on followers in Milan, whose style, a variant of his own, is called Leonardesque. Leonardo's example should not be mistaken for a universal model of Renaissance artists. He was extraordinary in curiosity and unusual in leaving works unfinished, a pattern that frustrated patrons.

Section 58.16

Bramante and High Renaissance Architecture in Rome

Donato Bramante, born about 1444 near Urbino, began as a painter and absorbed the architectural and perspectival interests of the Urbino court. In Milan from the 1470s he worked for Ludovico Sforza, producing an illusionistic choir for Santa Maria presso San Satiro, a shallow space made to look deep through painted and relief perspective, and contributed to the church of Santa Maria delle Grazie. After the French conquest of Milan in 1499 he moved to Rome, where he found new opportunities under Pope Alexander VI and then Julius II.

His most celebrated early Roman work is the Tempietto, a small circular, domed shrine in the courtyard of San Pietro in Montorio, built about 1502 to 1510 for the Spanish monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella, to mark the traditional site of Saint Peter's crucifixion. The building uses the Doric order, a ring of columns, and a calm, geometric form, and it was admired as a model of classical harmony, though its small size and unusual circumstances mean it did not simply function as a template. Bramante also designed the Belvedere Courtyard at the Vatican, a large terraced space linking the papal palace to a villa, which employed ramps, stairs, and loggias on a monumental scale.

Julius II, pope from 1503 to 1513, intended to rebuild the basilica of Saint Peter, the late antique church founded under Constantine, now in poor condition. Earlier popes had considered repairs. Julius chose a larger plan, and the foundation stone of the new church was laid on 18 April 1506. Bramante's design is generally understood as a centralized, Greek cross scheme with a great dome, though its details are known mainly from drawings and medals and were changed by successors. Demolition of parts of the old basilica offended some contemporaries and destroyed ancient monuments. The project's cost led to extensive fundraising, including the sale of indulgences, a financial and religious practice that later became a major point of controversy, as Chapter 62 explains. After Bramante's death in 1514, Raphael, Antonio da Sangallo, Michelangelo, and others altered the design across the century. The basilica illustrates how papal ambition, classical architecture, and fiscal strategy interacted, and how a project could outlast its originator and become the product of many hands.

Section 58.17

Raphael and the Artistic Culture of Papal Rome

Raffaello Sanzio was born in Urbino in 1483, the son of Giovanni Santi, a painter and court poet, and learned painting in his father's shop and in the circle of Perugino, whose calm, harmonious compositions influenced him strongly. His early altarpieces, such as the Marriage of the Virgin of 1504, show this training. In Florence from about 1504 to 1508, he studied Leonardo and Michelangelo and produced a series of Madonnas that combined grace with spatial clarity and found a wide market among Florentine families. His success reflected not only talent but a talent for management, since he cooperated well with patrons and built an effective workshop.

In 1508 Raphael went to Rome, possibly at the recommendation of Bramante, and Julius II engaged him to decorate rooms in the Vatican palace, the Stanze. In the Stanza della Segnatura, painted about 1509 to 1511 and probably intended for the pope's library and perhaps for the signing of papal documents, he created frescoes representing the major fields of learning: theology, philosophy, poetry, and law. The School of Athens shows ancient philosophers and mathematicians gathered in a grand classical building recalling Roman architecture and, perhaps, Bramante's plans for Saint Peter's. Plato and Aristotle stand at the center, with Plato pointing upward and Aristotle gesturing outward, while figures including Pythagoras, Euclid, and Heraclitus occupy the surrounding space. Many of the figures' identities are debated. Tradition proposes that Plato resembles Leonardo and Heraclitus Michelangelo, but such identifications rest on later accounts and are uncertain. The fresco unites humanist interest in antiquity, perspectival architecture, and a papal setting, though it should be understood within a Christian program that also included the Disputa on theology. It does not show that the Renaissance papacy had turned away from Christianity.

Raphael's later career involved portraits, such as that of Baldassare Castiglione, author of The Book of the Courtier, and of Pope Leo X with two cardinals, along with tapestry designs for the Sistine Chapel in 1515 to 1516, villa decoration for the banker Agostino Chigi at the Farnesina, and architecture. In 1514 he became architect of Saint Peter's. He ran a large workshop, whose members, including Giulio Romano and Gianfrancesco Penni, executed much of the work on later commissions, and he worked with the engraver Marcantonio Raimondi to circulate his designs in print, an early example of how reproduction could spread an artist's style, a theme developed in Chapter 60. He died on 6 April 1520, aged thirty-seven. The scale of his output and the delegating practices of his shop highlight why attributions, especially of late works, require careful study of documents and technique.

Section 58.18

Michelangelo: Sculpture, Painting, and the Ideal of Artistic Achievement

Michelangelo Buonarroti, born in 1475 in Caprese, trained briefly in the Florentine workshop of Domenico Ghirlandaio and then, according to traditional accounts, studied sculpture in a garden near San Marco associated with the Medici, though the details of this early period are known mainly from his biographers, Vasari and Ascanio Condivi, both of whom wrote with his involvement or favor. He saw himself primarily as a sculptor. His Pietà, carved in Rome about 1498 to 1500 for the French cardinal Jean de Bilhères, shows the Virgin holding the dead Christ with a smoothness and monumentality that astonished viewers. It is the only work Michelangelo signed, and the signature on the Virgin's sash is mentioned in biographical tradition as a response to doubts about its authorship.

Returning to Florence, he received in 1501 a commission from the Opera del Duomo and the wool guild for a David from a large block of marble that earlier sculptors had begun and abandoned. The statue, carved in 1501 to 1504, more than five meters tall, shows the youth before the battle, tense and watchful, a departure from Donatello's victorious David. A committee of artists, including Leonardo and Botticelli, discussed where it should stand, and it was placed at the entrance of the Palazzo della Signoria in September 1504, where it took on meaning for the republic that had expelled the Medici. In Rome in 1505, Julius II commissioned a large tomb for himself, an ambitious project that was repeatedly reduced and not completed as planned until decades later, producing a long dispute that Michelangelo called the tragedy of the tomb.

Julius then asked Michelangelo to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, the papal chapel built by Sixtus IV. The artist, who protested that painting was not his profession, worked from 1508 to 1512, and the ceiling was unveiled on 1 November 1512. It presents scenes from Genesis, including the Creation of Adam, framed by an illusionistic architectural structure, surrounded by prophets and sibyls, and accompanied by nude youths, the ignudi. The project required scaffolding, assistants for plaster preparation and cartoons, and fresco work in sections, and surviving drawings and letters record the physical strain and financial disputes. Michelangelo employed assistants, though he controlled design and executed much of the painting himself, which contributed to his reputation for solitary achievement. His work centered on the nude male body as an expression of spiritual and physical power, an approach rooted in classical models but used in Christian subject matter.

Michelangelo's reputation developed quickly. Contemporaries called him divino, and Vasari made him the culmination of artistic progress. Yet his relations with patrons were often stormy, as he quarreled with Julius II and later popes, abandoned works, and negotiated about payment and scope. His career continued long after 1520, including the Medici Chapel, the Last Judgment, and architectural work at Saint Peter's, belonging to a later period of Italian art affected by religious reform and political crisis. Within the period of this chapter, he is best understood as a man whose ambition, skill, and difficult temperament made him exceptional, but whose works were commissioned, financed, and constrained by republics, guilds, cardinals, and popes.

Section 58.19

The High Renaissance and the Transformation of European Artistic Culture

The term High Renaissance usually denotes the period from about the 1490s to 1520, when Leonardo, Bramante, Raphael, and Michelangelo produced works admired for balance, monumentality, and mastery of the human figure. The label comes largely from Vasari and later critics, who regarded these artists as achieving a perfection toward which earlier art had striven. Modern historians use the term with caution, because it implies an ascending line that diminishes other artists and traditions, among them Venetian painters, Netherlandish contemporaries, and many Italian artists working outside the dominant centers, as well as later developments sometimes dismissed as decline. Italian art after 1520, in cities from Florence to Venice, took many directions, and the death of Raphael does not mark the end of the Renaissance.

Several changes in the broader culture of art are nevertheless clear. The status of leading artists rose, and they were increasingly seen as learned creators and treated as companions of princes and prelates. Artistic theory expanded, as treatises on painting, sculpture, and architecture multiplied. Print, discussed in Chapter 55 and Chapter 60, spread images and designs beyond local markets. Collecting of antiquities and contemporary works grew, and artists traveled more widely. Religious demand continued, but the Reformation, which Chapter 62 begins to examine, would soon transform patronage across much of northern Europe, reducing church commissions and encouraging different forms of art. Italy's artistic prestige influenced courts from France to Spain, and Francis I of France, who hosted Leonardo, and Charles V, a Titian patron, illustrate the export of Italian taste, as does the wider Habsburg dynastic patronage that Chapter 75 places in its political setting.

Equally important are the limits of the canon. The traditional story privileges a short list of male painters, sculptors, and architects in a few Italian cities. It underemphasizes the roles of workshops, craftspeople, women patrons, convents, and regional schools in places such as Naples, Siena, Bologna, and the Veneto. It also minimizes artistic exchange with the Low Countries, the eastern Mediterranean, and the Islamic world, and it overlooks the enormous number of anonymous or lesser works that formed the visual experience of most people. Students should learn the great works, and should also recognize that the framework in which those works are celebrated was itself constructed, partly by Vasari and partly by later museums and universities.

Historical Debate

Was Renaissance Art a Decisive Break with Medieval Art?

The question: Did artists in fifteenth and early sixteenth century Italy create a fundamentally new relationship to nature, antiquity, and the individual artist, or did they develop and transform medieval traditions that continued alongside them?

Interpretation A, a significant rupture: Following Vasari and Burckhardt, many scholars argued that Renaissance artists revived classical standards, discovered perspective and anatomy, and asserted individual creative authority, leaving behind a medieval art governed by convention and anonymity. They point to developments such as mathematical perspective, the classical orders, the nude, and the portrait as evidence of novelty.
Interpretation B, continuity and transformation: Many recent historians emphasize that medieval art was inventive and naturalistic, that religious function, workshop practice, and patronage patterns continued, that classical elements had appeared earlier, and that Renaissance artists often combined old and new. Scholars such as Michael Baxandall have stressed how the visual habits of fifteenth century viewers, shaped by commercial and religious culture, informed painting, a view that links art to its society and not to a heroic rupture.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same works, contracts, treatises, and biographies, but differ in emphasis. Interpretation A relies on innovations in perspective, anatomy, and classical architecture and on the claims of Alberti and Vasari. Interpretation B relies on patronage records, continuities in iconography and technique, and the experience of non Italian art.

Why it matters: How the question is answered affects whether the Renaissance is understood as the beginning of modernity or as one period of creative change in a long history of European art, and whether it centers Italy alone or a wider European network.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Medieval and Renaissance Visual Traditions

What Changed

  • Mathematical perspective and anatomical study changed how space and bodies were represented
  • Classical architectural orders, proportion, and forms were used systematically
  • Artists acquired greater recognition, wrote treatises, and sometimes signed works
  • Oil painting, canvas, portraiture, and mythological subjects expanded the repertoire

What Stayed the Same

  • Religious institutions and devotion remained the largest source of commissions
  • Workshops, apprenticeship, and guild regulation continued to structure labor
  • Contracts, costly materials, and negotiated payment governed production
  • Families, corporations, and rulers used art to display status and legitimacy

Who experienced what: Leading artists and elite patrons experienced the greatest change in status and opportunity. Ordinary worshippers, assistants, and most craftspeople experienced greater continuity, since parish images, devotional objects, and building trades changed more slowly.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both avoids treating the Renaissance as a clean break while still explaining why contemporaries perceived novelty.

Cause and Effect

How Patronage, Technical Experiment, and Competition Influenced Artistic Change

Long term causes

Urban wealth from trade and banking, dense religious institutions, guild and confraternity structures, the survival of Roman remains, and the craft traditions of workshops created sustained demand and skill.

Short term causes

Competitions such as the Baptistery doors and dome, rivalry among guilds, families, and courts, humanist interest in antiquity, and ambitious papal programs under Julius II pressed artists to innovate.

Immediate effects

New techniques of perspective, bronze casting, and fresco appeared, major buildings and cycles were made, and artists gained greater bargaining power and visibility.

Long term consequences

A broader European tradition of academic training, artistic theory, collecting, and the canon emerged, while later religious reform and political upheaval changed the conditions of patronage.

Historical complexity: No single cause accounts for the changes. Competition could spur experiment but also encourage conservatism, since patrons often wanted proven formulas. Technical failures, such as Leonardo's wall paintings, show that experiments carried risks. The outcome depended on the interaction of institutions, money, skill, and individual choices.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined Renaissance art, architecture, and patronage between roughly 1300 and 1520 as a gradual and collaborative transformation. Its central argument is that artistic innovation arose from the interaction of craft skill, observation of nature, study of antiquity, mathematical method, and the demands of patrons. The traditional image of a sudden revival of classical realism was replaced with a more complex picture of continuity with medieval traditions and selective change.

The Trecento masters Giotto, Duccio, Simone Martini, and the Lorenzetti brothers inherited Byzantine and medieval forms and expanded narrative, emotion, and spatial suggestion. In early fifteenth century Florence, Brunelleschi, Alberti, and Masaccio developed and applied linear perspective, the formal system for representing space geometrically, though earlier painters had already explored depth, and perspective never became a universal standard.

The chapter explained how art was physically made, showing that materials such as gesso, tempera, fresco plaster, ultramarine, marble, and bronze constrained design and cost. Oil painting, refined by Netherlandish painters, entered Italian practice gradually, notably through Venice, a reminder that innovation was international. Understanding materials also clarified collaborative production and why attribution requires careful distinction of documents, workshops, and later tradition.

Florentine sculpture and architecture illustrated the civic character of early Renaissance art. Ghiberti's doors and Donatello's statues arose from guild sponsored competitions and commissions, and Brunelleschi's dome, consecrated in 1436, depended on engineering, public funding, and the administration of the Opera del Duomo. Alberti linked humanist learning with architectural design, though his buildings relied on local builders and patrons.

Patronage emerged as a central theme. Guilds, confraternities, cathedral boards, families such as the Brancacci, Rucellai, and Medici, courts, and popes all commissioned works for religious, civic, and dynastic purposes. The Medici were prominent, but the idea that they financed the Renaissance was rejected. Patronage involved negotiation, competition, and disputes, as the 1483 contract for The Virgin of the Rocks demonstrates.

The chapter examined workshops as places of apprenticeship, collaboration, and family enterprise, and it described the changing status of artists, who increasingly claimed intellectual standing through disegno and theory. Women were largely excluded from formal training and guilds, though they acted as patrons, such as Isabella d'Este, and as participants in convents and family workshops. Documented women artists were few, and their careers must not be inflated.

Regional comparison showed distinct artistic cultures. Florence emphasized civic and guild commissions and drawing. The courts of Mantua, Ferrara, Urbino, Milan, and Naples used art for dynastic display, with artists like Mantegna and Piero serving rulers. Venice, shaped by commerce, scuole, and contact with the Byzantine and Ottoman worlds, developed color and atmosphere in the work of the Bellini, Giorgione, and Titian.

The High Renaissance in Milan, Florence, and Rome was shown through Leonardo, Bramante, Raphael, and Michelangelo. Leonardo's observation and experimentation, Bramante's classical architecture and the beginning of the new St. Peter's, Raphael's workshop and Vatican commissions, and Michelangelo's sculpture and Sistine ceiling were all analyzed as responses to particular commissions and patrons, especially Julius II. Their achievements were significant, but they were not the only path.

The chapter also stressed religious context. Altarpieces, frescoes, chapels, and devotional images remained central, and Renaissance art did not represent a rejection of Christianity. Contacts with the Orthodox world, the Ottoman Empire, the Islamic Mediterranean, and northern Europe contributed materials, techniques, and ideas, and Jewish communities participated in the credit economy and the cultural life of Italian cities.

Finally, the chapter evaluated the concept of the High Renaissance and the canon. Vasari's influential narrative shaped the story of progress from Giotto to Michelangelo, and modern scholarship has complicated that story by emphasizing continuity, collaboration, patronage, and diversity. Chapter 59 now turns to the northern Renaissance and Christian humanism, where parallel developments in painting, printing, and religious criticism took different but connected forms.

Reconstructing a Renaissance Commission

This project asks you to study one Renaissance work or building as the outcome of a commission, using library and reference resources to reconstruct who ordered it, who made it, how it was funded, and how it was used.

  1. Choose a work. Select a work discussed in the chapter, such as the Brancacci Chapel, the Baptistery doors, the Palazzo Rucellai, the Camera degli Sposi, or the Sistine ceiling.
  2. Identify the evidence. Locate published editions or reliable summaries of contracts, account books, letters, or biographies relevant to the work, and note which evidence is contemporary and which later.
  3. Reconstruct the commission. Explain the patron's motives, the artist's role, the materials, the cost, and any disputes.
  4. Assess attribution and later history. Note questions of workshop participation, restoration, damage, and changing interpretation.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4 to 6 page essay or equivalent presentation, explain what the commission reveals about patronage, religion, and society, using careful language where evidence is incomplete.

Library resources to use: museum collection catalogues, historical atlases of Italy, scholarly surveys of Renaissance art, documentary collections, and the MGU History Library reference materials on Italian art history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a culture's most admired achievements were made through ordinary instruments: contracts, guild statutes, workshop labor, pigments bought from merchants, and negotiations among patrons who wanted prestige and salvation. The Renaissance, in this account, is neither a miracle nor a myth, but a set of historically explicable changes in how images and buildings were conceived, financed, and understood.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in Volume III. Commercial wealth, urban competition, and religious institutions created demand, and new forms of knowledge, from geometry to anatomy to archaeology, supplied methods. Artistic prestige became a resource of states, as popes and princes used images and buildings to claim legitimacy. These patterns anticipate the political uses of culture in later chapters and the global expansion of European artistic and material networks examined in Part XI.

Its legacy was long. The idea of the artist as an intellectual creator, the academy, the museum, and the canon of masterworks all descended from this period, as did the architectural vocabulary of columns, domes, and façades that spread across Europe and beyond. At the same time, much of what was most characteristic of the age, including devotional practice, workshop collaboration, and the dependence on patrons, continued with little change. The chapter's balance between innovation and continuity is a model for reading the Renaissance as a whole.

Chapter 59 now turns north, where painters such as van Eyck and Dürer, printers, and humanists like Erasmus pursued their own innovations, often in dialogue with Italy. Understanding the Italian case in its institutional and material detail prepares students to see both the connections and the differences that marked European cultural history in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.
