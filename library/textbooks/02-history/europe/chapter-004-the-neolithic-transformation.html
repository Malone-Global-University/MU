The Neolithic Transformation

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Sometime after roughly 7000 BCE, on the plains of what is now Greece, and then across the following four millennia in ever widening circles, communities across the landmass this textbook calls Europe abandoned, modified, or blended with an older way of life that had sustained human beings for tens of thousands of years. They began, in varying combinations and at markedly different speeds, to plant crops, tend herds, and live in permanent farming villages that became, across the following centuries, increasingly widespread and increasingly the dominant form of settlement across most of the continent. No single event marks this change. It was, instead, one of the most consequential transformations in the whole of European history, and one that took millennia to unfold.

Picture a small cluster of rectangular timber houses standing beside a stream on the fertile plain of Thessaly, in what is now central Greece, sometime around 6500 BCE. Inside one house, a family stores grain in clay bins sunk into the floor, grain grown from seed that ultimately traces back, through generations of farmers moving slowly westward, to wild grasses first cultivated many centuries earlier and a considerable distance to the east, in the hill country of the Fertile Crescent. Outside, sheep and goats, animals not native to Europe, graze on stubble left from the last harvest. A cemetery lies nearby, holding the bodies of people who, unlike their Paleolithic ancestors described in Chapter 3, expected to live, work, marry, raise children, and die within sight of the same fields and the same houses, generation after generation.

Picture, at almost the same moment, a very different scene several hundred kilometers to the north and west, along a river valley in what is now northern Germany or Poland. Here, hunter gatherer communities descended from the Mesolithic populations discussed at the close of Chapter 3 continued to fish, hunt, and gather much as their ancestors had for centuries, largely unaware of, or perhaps only distantly aware of, the new way of life spreading slowly toward them from the southeast. For these communities, the Neolithic transformation, when it eventually arrived, would come not overnight but across generations, sometimes through direct contact with farming newcomers, sometimes through the gradual adoption of new crops, animals, and tools by people who remained, in most other respects, recognizably continuous with the foraging societies that had inhabited the same territory for thousands of years. It is worth noting from the outset that some of these Mesolithic communities were themselves already substantially settled, occupying favorable coastal, riverine, or lakeside locations year round and practicing their own forms of food storage, so that permanence of settlement and the deliberate storage of food were not, by themselves, innovations that farming introduced to Europe for the first time; farming instead made a much more thoroughgoing and widespread version of both practices possible.

This chapter examines both of these processes together, because neither can be understood in isolation. The spread of farming across Europe, a process archaeologists and historians call Neolithization, was neither instantaneous nor uniform. It did not begin in Europe at all, but arrived from the Fertile Crescent of southwestern Asia, where wild cereals and legumes were first cultivated and animals first domesticated. It moved into Europe along at least two identifiable general routes, a Mediterranean sea route linking the Aegean, the Adriatic, and the Iberian coast, and a continental route following the Danube and its tributaries deep into central Europe. Along each route it encountered resident hunter gatherer populations whose responses ranged from wholesale replacement, to gradual absorption, to sustained coexistence and eventual, sometimes selective, adoption of farming practices. And even after farming arrived in a given region, older ways of gathering, hunting, and fishing did not simply vanish; in many places they persisted alongside cultivated fields and herded animals for centuries.

This chapter also asks students to resist a temptation common in popular accounts of this period: the assumption that the shift from foraging to farming was an obvious improvement that any rational community would readily adopt once it became available. The archaeological and skeletal evidence examined later in this chapter complicates that assumption considerably. Early farming populations in many regions show evidence of poorer average health, shorter stature, and increased exposure to certain infectious diseases compared to the hunter gatherer populations that preceded them, even as those same farming populations grew substantially larger in overall numbers. Farming supported much higher population densities, more widespread permanent settlement, larger food stores, and new opportunities for specialization and social differentiation. These conditions became important foundations for many later European societies, but they did not make metallurgy, hierarchy, cities, or states inevitable.

Understanding why farming spread, how it spread, what it changed, and what it did not change is therefore essential to understanding the deeper roots of European history. The Neolithic transformation did not itself produce states or writing, while early copper working emerged during the Neolithic in limited parts of southeastern Europe and is examined more fully in Chapter 5. It did, however, create the demographic density, the fixed settlements, and the accumulated surplus, along with, in some regions, the beginnings of social distinction in wealth and status, conditions quite different from those available to the mobile foraging societies of the Paleolithic and Mesolithic periods examined in Chapter 3, and conditions that opened possibilities, without guaranteeing outcomes, for the monument building, metalworking, and eventually cities and states examined in later chapters.

This chapter proceeds region by region and theme by theme: it begins with the origins of farming outside Europe, in the Fertile Crescent, traces its two principal routes of transmission into the continent, examines the crops and animals that farming communities relied upon and adapted to different European environments, describes the villages and households in which Neolithic Europeans lived, considers what skeletal and archaeological evidence reveals about demographic change and human health, and closes by examining the long frontier zones where farming and foraging ways of life coexisted, sometimes for many centuries, before farming eventually became the dominant, though never universal, way of life across most of the continent.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what archaeologists mean by the Neolithic transformation, and distinguish it from the mere adoption of new tools or pottery.
  2. Trace the origins of plant and animal domestication in the Fertile Crescent and explain why this region, rather than others, produced the earliest identifiable agricultural economies relevant to Europe's Neolithic.
  3. Describe the two principal routes by which farming spread into Europe, the Mediterranean maritime route and the Danubian continental route, and compare their chronology and character.
  4. Identify the major domesticated plants and animals introduced into Europe during the Neolithic period and explain how farming communities adapted them to different regional environments.
  5. Analyze the archaeological evidence for Neolithic village life, household organization, and settlement permanence.
  6. Evaluate the skeletal and demographic evidence for the health consequences of the transition to farming, and explain why population growth and individual health did not necessarily move together.
  7. Compare the differing interpretations historians and archaeologists have offered for how farming spread, whether primarily through migrating farming populations, the diffusion of ideas and techniques among existing populations, or some combination of both.
  8. Describe the long coexistence of farming and foraging communities along the frontiers of Neolithic expansion, particularly in northern and northeastern Europe.
  9. Explain the origins of Neolithic funerary practice and early monument building, and connect them to the longer development examined in Chapter 5.
  10. Assess the strengths and limitations of the evidence, archaeobotanical, zooarchaeological, skeletal, genetic, and isotopic, historians use to reconstruct the Neolithic transformation.
  11. Contextualize the European Neolithic within the wider Neolithic transformations occurring across southwestern Asia and the eastern Mediterranean.
  12. Avoid anachronistic assumptions that farming represented an inevitable or straightforwardly superior improvement over foraging ways of life.

Historical Context

Chapter 3 traced the long history of Paleolithic and Mesolithic populations across Europe, from the earliest hominin presence on the continent through the highly skilled, mobile, and often remarkably successful hunter gatherer societies that inhabited post glacial Europe by the beginning of the Holocene epoch, described in Chapter 2. Those societies, for tens of thousands of years, obtained their food entirely through hunting wild animals, fishing, and gathering wild plants, without cultivating crops or keeping domesticated herds. By the era with which this chapter begins, broadly the eighth and seventh millennia BCE, some of these Mesolithic communities in southeastern Europe had already developed rich, complex, and in some cases quite settled ways of life adapted to particular local environments, coastal fishing communities, riverine settlements, and forest dwelling groups exploiting a wide range of seasonal resources.

The change examined in this chapter did not originate in Europe. It began several centuries earlier, and a considerable distance away, in a region of southwestern Asia commonly called the Fertile Crescent, an arc of hill country and river valleys stretching from the eastern Mediterranean coast through parts of what is now southeastern Turkey, northern Syria, and Iraq, and down into the valley of the Jordan River and beyond. In this region, beginning by roughly 9500 to 8500 BCE according to the archaeobotanical evidence discussed further below, human communities began deliberately planting and harvesting wild cereals such as einkorn and emmer wheat and barley, and, somewhat later, domesticating legumes including lentils, peas, and chickpeas. At a broadly similar period, communities in the same general region began managing and eventually domesticating sheep, goats, and, somewhat later, cattle and pigs.

Historians and archaeologists do not fully agree on why agriculture developed first in this particular region rather than in comparably fertile areas elsewhere, and this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines one important dimension of the broader scholarly discussion connected to this question. What is well established, however, is that the Fertile Crescent possessed a remarkable natural concentration of wild plant and animal species suitable for domestication, wild wheat and barley varieties with genetically favorable characteristics for cultivation, and wild sheep, goat, and cattle populations amenable to human management, a combination not equally available in every world region. Once established in this core zone, farming economies spread outward across the following millennia in several directions, southward and eastward into the wider Near East, and, of central importance to this chapter, northwestward across Anatolia and, from there, into Europe.

This chapter's central task is to trace that northwestward spread, from its arrival in southeastern Europe by approximately the beginning of the seventh millennium BCE through its gradual extension, over the following roughly four thousand years, across virtually the entire continent, with the notable and historically significant exception of the far north, where farming arrived very late or, in some regions, scarcely at all before the modern era. This process unfolded unevenly, at different speeds in different regions, through different combinations of migration and local adoption, and with markedly different consequences for the resident hunter gatherer populations who encountered it.

The evidence available for reconstructing this transformation differs considerably from the evidence available for the Paleolithic societies examined in Chapter 3. Archaeobotanists, specialists who study the remains of ancient plants, particularly carbonized seeds and grains preserved by chance burning, identify which crops were cultivated in a given settlement and trace how those crops changed genetically as farmers selected, consciously or unconsciously, for favorable traits across many generations. Zooarchaeologists, who study animal bones recovered from archaeological sites, distinguish wild from domesticated animals through changes in bone size, shape, and herd composition, since herds managed by human beings typically show different age and sex profiles than wild populations subject only to natural predation. In recent decades, historians and archaeologists have also gained access to increasingly sophisticated genetic evidence, drawn from ancient DNA extracted from human skeletal remains, which allows researchers to trace population movements and estimate the genetic relationship between early European farmers and both their Fertile Crescent predecessors and the Mesolithic hunter gatherer populations they encountered. Stable isotope analysis of human bone and tooth enamel provides further evidence about individual diet, mobility, and, in some cases, the geographic region in which a person spent their childhood, since certain isotopes absorbed from local water and food sources become fixed in developing teeth and can later be measured by researchers. This chapter draws on all of these forms of evidence, while remaining attentive, as the Master Instructions governing this textbook require, to the limitations and ongoing scholarly debates surrounding each.

Section 4.1

Why Farming Changed Everything, and Why It Was Not Inevitable

The term Neolithic, derived from Greek roots meaning "new stone," was coined by nineteenth century archaeologists to describe a period distinguished, in their original classification, chiefly by the appearance of polished, rather than merely chipped, stone tools. Modern archaeology has substantially revised this original definition. What actually distinguishes the Neolithic from the preceding Mesolithic period, in most current scholarly usage, is not stone tool technology as such but the presence of a farming economy, communities that obtained a significant portion of their food through the deliberate cultivation of domesticated plants and the management of domesticated animals, rather than relying entirely on hunting, fishing, and gathering wild resources.

This distinction matters because it clarifies what this chapter is, and is not, examining. The Neolithic transformation was not primarily a change in tools, pottery styles, or material culture, although all of these did change substantially during the period this chapter covers. It was, at its core, an economic and social transformation: a shift in how human communities obtained their food, organized their labor, related to particular pieces of land, and structured their households and settlements. Pottery, polished stone tools, and other material innovations frequently accompanied this transformation and provide archaeologists with much of their evidence for tracing it, but they are symptoms of the underlying economic change rather than its defining feature. This equation of "Neolithic" with a farming economy works reasonably well for most of southeastern, central, and western Europe, the primary focus of this chapter, but students should be aware that archaeologists working in northeastern Europe sometimes apply the term "Neolithic" to pottery-using communities who had not yet adopted agriculture at all, since in parts of the eastern Baltic, examined in Section 4.9, pottery appears among populations that continued to rely substantially on hunting, fishing, and gathering for many centuries afterward. Students should therefore treat "Neolithic" as a label whose precise meaning can vary somewhat by region rather than as a perfectly uniform category across the whole continent.

It is tempting, viewing this transformation from a modern vantage point in which farming has long since become the near universal basis of human subsistence, to treat its spread as an obviously beneficial and essentially inevitable development, one that any community would readily adopt once it became available. The evidence examined throughout this chapter complicates that assumption substantially. Skeletal evidence from many regions indicates that early farming populations were, on average, somewhat shorter in stature than the Mesolithic populations that preceded them, a pattern generally interpreted as reflecting a less varied and, in some respects, less nutritionally complete diet compared to the broad range of wild resources available to skilled foragers. Living in permanent, densely occupied settlements alongside domesticated animals also appears to have increased exposure to certain infectious diseases, some of which, according to genetic and epidemiological research on pathogen origins, likely first passed to human populations from closely managed livestock. Sustained agricultural labor, particularly the repetitive tasks associated with grain processing, also left skeletal markers, in some populations, associated with hard physical strain not equally documented among Mesolithic foraging populations.

At the same time, farming enabled something foraging economies, however skilled and successful, generally could not: sustained population growth well beyond what a comparable area of wild resources could support through hunting and gathering alone. Cultivated fields and managed herds, whatever their costs to individual health, produced far more calories per unit of land than wild resources typically could, particularly once farmers had developed effective techniques for storing surplus grain across the lean months of the year. This combination, a subsistence strategy that may have reduced individual health and stature while dramatically increasing sustainable population density, helps explain both why farming spread as extensively as it eventually did and why its adoption was neither immediate nor uncontested wherever it appeared. Communities encountering farming for the first time faced a genuine choice, weighed against their own particular local resources, and this chapter treats their varied responses, adoption, adaptation, and, in some regions, sustained resistance or delay, as historically significant rather than as a foregone conclusion.

Section 4.2

Origins in the Fertile Crescent and the Question of Independent Invention

The domesticated plants and animals that eventually reached Europe did not originate there. They were first domesticated in the Fertile Crescent, an arc of hill country and river valleys in southwestern Asia where, beginning by approximately 9500 to 8500 BCE, communities began deliberately planting and harvesting wild cereals, chiefly einkorn wheat, emmer wheat, and barley, whose wild ancestors grew naturally across parts of this region. Archaeobotanical evidence, particularly the identification of morphological changes in preserved grain, such as the loss of the brittle stalk structure that allows wild grasses to disperse their seed naturally, a trait unfavorable to a farmer trying to harvest a field intact, allows researchers to trace the gradual, generations long process by which repeated human selection transformed wild cereals into domesticated crops dependent on human cultivation for their continued propagation.

Within a few centuries of this initial plant cultivation, communities in broadly the same region began a parallel process with animals, managing and eventually domesticating sheep and goats, whose wild ancestors, respectively the mouflon and the bezoar goat, inhabited the hill country of this same general area, followed somewhat later by cattle, domesticated from wild aurochs, and pigs, domesticated from wild boar. Zooarchaeological evidence for this process includes changes in herd age and sex composition, since a managed herd typically shows a much higher proportion of young males slaughtered for meat, with breeding females kept longer, than a wild population subject to natural predation, along with gradual reductions in body size that frequently, though not universally, accompany domestication.

Agriculture also developed independently in several other regions of the world, including parts of East Asia, the Americas, and sub-Saharan Africa, in each case involving different crops, different animals, and different chronologies, a broader global history that lies outside the scope of this European history textbook. Within this wider comparative picture, historians and archaeologists continue to debate why this particular combination of cereal cultivation and animal domestication developed so early and so intensively in the Fertile Crescent specifically, since it was this Fertile Crescent complex of crops and animals, rather than any of these other independent centers, that eventually spread into Europe, a question this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines from a related angle. What is well established is that the region possessed an unusually favorable concentration of wild species suited to domestication, several distinct wild cereal and legume species growing in overlapping ranges, alongside several large mammal species amenable to human management, a combination that provided early farming communities with the raw biological material for a balanced agricultural economy combining carbohydrates, protein, and, eventually, animal labor and secondary products such as milk and wool, discussed further in Section 4.6.

Once established, this Fertile Crescent farming economy did not remain confined to its place of origin. Over the following two thousand years, it spread outward in multiple directions, southward and eastward into the wider Near East and eventually South Asia, and, of direct relevance to this chapter, northwestward across Anatolia. By approximately 7000 BCE, farming communities using this same basic combination of domesticated wheat, barley, sheep, goats, and, increasingly, cattle, had become established at Aegean coastal sites and, from there, within the conventional geographic boundary of Europe discussed in Chapter 1. This chapter's remaining sections trace that northwestward spread in detail, but its origin outside Europe entirely is essential to understanding why the European Neolithic must be examined as part of a wider Eurasian and Mediterranean process, not as an independent European invention.

Section 4.3

Routes of Transmission into Southeastern Europe

The earliest well documented farming settlements within the conventional geographic boundary of Europe appear on the Greek mainland by approximately 6700 to 6400 BCE, with sites such as Franchthi Cave and Sarakenos Cave providing some of the earliest evidence and the fuller sequence of Thessalian tell settlements generally dated somewhat later, to roughly 6500 to 6400 BCE. This chronology is later than an older generation of scholarship once proposed, and students should be aware that absolute dates for the earliest Aegean Neolithic continue to be refined as new evidence accumulates. Cyprus followed a substantially earlier and largely separate trajectory: Pre-Pottery Neolithic activity there reaches back into the ninth millennium BCE, with imported domesticated cattle documented by roughly 8500 BCE and domesticated sheep by roughly 7900 BCE, a sequence that reflects Cyprus's particular position along early maritime routes out of the Fertile Crescent zone rather than a development directly parallel to, or predictive of, the timing of farming's arrival on the Greek mainland. Sites such as those excavated at Nea Nikomedeia in Macedonia and various tell settlements, mounded sites formed by the long accumulation of successive mudbrick building layers, across Thessaly reveal small farming villages growing wheat and barley, keeping sheep, goats, and, somewhat later, cattle and pigs, and living in rectangular mudbrick or timber framed houses, evidence discussed further in Section 4.7.

From this Aegean and Thessalian core, farming spread over the following several centuries northward and westward across the Balkan Peninsula, reaching parts of Macedonia and Bulgaria by roughly 6500 to 6000 BCE and, somewhat later, penetrating further into the Balkan interior and toward the Danube basin, a region of particular importance for the continental route of Neolithization examined in Section 4.5. This early Balkan farming expansion moved through a landscape already inhabited by Mesolithic hunter gatherer populations, and archaeologists continue to debate, region by region, the relative importance of migrating farming populations displacing or absorbing existing foragers, compared to existing Balkan populations themselves adopting farming practices and domesticated species through contact and exchange with farming neighbors, a question examined more fully in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

The archaeological record for this early Balkan Neolithic includes not only settlement remains and agricultural evidence but also early pottery traditions, including painted wares in some regions, and figurines, frequently interpreted as representing fertility, ancestral veneration, or other ritual concerns, though historians and archaeologists should exercise caution in attributing specific religious meaning to material culture whose original significance to its makers cannot be recovered with certainty from archaeological evidence alone. What is clearer is that these early Balkan farming communities established the demographic and agricultural base from which two broadly distinguishable further routes of expansion, the Mediterranean coastal route examined in Section 4.4 and the Danubian continental route examined in Section 4.5, subsequently carried farming across the remainder of the continent.

Section 4.4

The Mediterranean Route and the Western Sea Lanes

Beginning by roughly 6000 to 5500 BCE, farming spread rapidly westward along the northern coastline of the Mediterranean Sea, a process closely associated by archaeologists with a distinctive pottery tradition known as Cardial or Impressed Ware, named for the practice of decorating pottery surfaces by pressing the edge of a cockle shell, of the genus Cardium, or other implements into wet clay before firing. Sites bearing this pottery tradition appear in rapid succession along the coastlines of Italy, southern France, and Iberia, reaching the Atlantic coast of Portugal by approximately 5500 BCE, a pace of expansion that most archaeologists interpret as evidence for maritime transport, since the speed and coastal distribution of these early sites would be difficult to explain through purely overland movement alone.

This maritime route illustrates a theme introduced in Chapter 2: the Mediterranean Sea functioned historically as a connective corridor rather than a barrier, and the Neolithic transformation provides one of the earliest large scale demonstrations of this pattern in the archaeological record. Early Mediterranean farmers appear to have moved by boat along the coastline and among offshore islands, including Corsica, Sardinia, and the Balearic Islands, establishing small farming settlements, often initially in caves or rock shelters before shifting toward open air village sites, adapted to the Mediterranean climate zone described in Chapter 2, with its hot, dry summers and mild, wetter winters, and to crops such as wheat, barley, and, in various regions, early cultivation of legumes and, somewhat later, fruit trees suited to this climate.

The relationship between these arriving Mediterranean farming communities and resident Mesolithic populations along the Iberian, French, and Italian coastlines appears, based on current genetic evidence, to have begun in most areas with a comparatively abrupt arrival of farming populations themselves, carrying ancestry derived substantially from Anatolian and Aegean farming groups, rather than with existing Mesolithic communities independently and gradually adopting farming on their own initiative. In Iberia specifically, genomic evidence indicates that incoming Cardial farmers subsequently admixed with resident hunter gatherers to a variable and, in some early western Cardial groups, unusually high degree, producing regional populations with a distinctive blend of farming and foraging ancestry. This admixture followed the arrival of farming populations rather than representing a case of indigenous foragers adopting agriculture without any accompanying migration, a distinction developed further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Section 4.5

The Danubian Route and Linear Pottery Farmers of Central Europe

The second principal route by which farming spread into Europe followed an inland, riverine path rather than a maritime one, moving from the Balkan farming zone described in Section 4.3 northward and westward along the Danube and its tributaries, the great central European river corridor introduced in Chapter 2. Beginning by approximately 5500 BCE, archaeologists identify a remarkably rapid and remarkably uniform farming expansion across much of central Europe, associated with an archaeological culture conventionally called, using the German term coined by early twentieth century archaeologists, Linearbandkeramik, or Linear Pottery culture, commonly abbreviated LBK, named for its characteristic pottery decorated with incised linear and curvilinear bands.

Linear Pottery farming communities expanded, within only a few centuries, from the middle Danube basin across a vast swath of central Europe encompassing much of modern Hungary, Austria, the Czech lands, Slovakia, Poland, Germany, and eventually into the Rhine valley, the Low Countries, and the Paris Basin in northern France, discussed in Chapter 2 as a historically important lowland region. This expansion followed, with notable consistency, particular soil types, especially the fertile loess soils, the wind deposited silt described in Chapter 2 as especially well suited to early agriculture, suggesting that Linear Pottery farmers actively sought out land with favorable agricultural characteristics rather than settling indiscriminately across the landscape.

The pace and consistency of this Danubian expansion have led many archaeologists to interpret it as substantially driven by the movement of farming populations themselves, a process sometimes called demic diffusion, migrating populations carrying their agricultural economy, material culture, and, to a significant degree based on current genetic evidence, their own genetic ancestry into new territory, rather than existing central European hunter gatherer populations independently adopting farming through contact alone. Genetic evidence from Linear Pottery period skeletal remains generally shows substantial ancestral continuity with earlier Aegean and Anatolian farming populations, supporting this migration centered interpretation for the Danubian route specifically, even though, as Section 4.4 explained, the Mediterranean route shows more regional variation.

Linear Pottery communities lived in large timber longhouses, examined further in Section 4.7, arranged in small to medium sized villages, and their material culture, pottery style, house construction, and settlement pattern, shows remarkable uniformity across an enormous geographic range, a pattern archaeologists have interpreted as evidence for strong social and economic networks connecting Linear Pottery communities across considerable distances, possibly including systems of exchange, kinship, and shared cultural practice that helped maintain this uniformity despite the physical spread of settlement across many hundreds of kilometers. This uniform, rapidly expanding Danubian farming system eventually reached, and in various regions interacted extensively with, the Mesolithic hunter gatherer populations of northern and northeastern Europe, an encounter examined in Section 4.9.

Section 4.6

Domesticated Plants and Animals in the European Landscape

Wherever it took root, Neolithic farming in Europe depended on a core package of domesticated species originally derived from the Fertile Crescent, gradually adapted, modified, and supplemented as farming communities encountered new environments across the continent. Emmer and einkorn wheat and barley remained the principal cultivated cereals across most of the European Neolithic, though their relative importance varied regionally according to soil and climate, with barley, generally more tolerant of poorer soils and shorter growing seasons, becoming comparatively more important in northern and upland regions than in the Mediterranean zones described in Section 4.4. Legumes, including lentils and peas, supplemented cereal cultivation across much of the continent, providing dietary protein and, agronomically, contributing nitrogen to soils through processes not understood in scientific terms by Neolithic farmers themselves but exploited, evidently, through accumulated practical experience.

Among domesticated animals, sheep and goats, particularly well suited to the Mediterranean climate zone, remained especially important in southeastern Europe and the Mediterranean coastal regions discussed in Section 4.4, while cattle assumed greater relative importance across much of central and northern Europe, where they provided not only meat but, in time, traction for pulling simple ploughs and carts. Archaeologists once described the exploitation of domesticated animals for products and services beyond meat, including milk, wool, and animal labor, as a single, comparatively late "secondary products revolution" occurring near the end of the Neolithic and into the Bronze Age examined in Chapter 5. More recent evidence has substantially revised this picture by showing that these developments had markedly different chronologies rather than arriving together as one late package. Chemical residue analysis indicates that dairying was already practiced in the Near East and southeastern Europe by the seventh millennium BCE, considerably earlier than the older model proposed, while direct physical evidence of ploughing, in the form of preserved plough marks beneath later monuments, reaches back to the very beginning of the fifth millennium BCE in parts of temperate Europe. Wool production, by contrast, does appear to have developed later, closer to the timing the older model proposed. Historians should therefore treat the secondary products revolution as a useful historiographical label for a family of related innovations rather than as evidence for a single, uniform, late-Neolithic turning point. Pigs, requiring less open grazing land than cattle or sheep and thriving in wooded environments, proved particularly well suited to the forested landscapes of temperate Europe described in Chapter 2, and their remains appear in substantial numbers at many central European Neolithic sites.

Adapting this originally Near Eastern agricultural package to the considerably more varied and, in many regions, cooler and wetter European environment required real agronomic adjustment. Farmers in northern and upland regions faced shorter growing seasons, different pest and disease pressures, and soils considerably different from those of the Fertile Crescent or even the Mediterranean basin, and archaeobotanical evidence suggests that European Neolithic farmers gradually selected locally adapted crop varieties and adjusted their farming techniques, including timing of planting and harvest, accordingly. This process of adaptation continued well beyond the period covered by this chapter, but its beginnings lie in the Neolithic communities examined here, whose success in extending a Near Eastern agricultural system across such a wide range of European environments represents a substantial, if often underappreciated, technical achievement.

Section 4.7

Villages, Households, and the Architecture of Settlement

One of the most historically significant consequences of the Neolithic transformation was the emergence of settled, or sedentary, village life on a scale and with a permanence not generally characteristic of the Mesolithic communities examined in Chapter 3, some of which had achieved considerable, though usually more seasonal or semi permanent, settlement stability of their own. Neolithic farming villages varied considerably in size, layout, and architecture across different regions and periods, but certain broad patterns recur across much of the evidence.

In southeastern Europe, the tell settlements mentioned in Section 4.3, formed through the long accumulation of successive layers of collapsed and rebuilt mudbrick housing on the same location across many generations, sometimes centuries, indicate a striking degree of attachment to particular settlement sites, communities that rebuilt directly atop their own ancestors' houses repeatedly rather than relocating, a pattern archaeologists interpret as reflecting both the practical convenience of established fields and water sources and, quite possibly, social or symbolic attachment to a particular place and its accumulated history, though this latter interpretation, since it concerns beliefs rather than directly observable behavior, should be treated with appropriate caution as an inference rather than a certainty.

In central Europe, the Linear Pottery farming communities examined in Section 4.5 built distinctive timber longhouses, rectangular structures sometimes reaching considerable length, tens of meters in some documented cases, framed by rows of substantial timber posts whose postholes remain detectable archaeologically long after the timber itself has decayed. These longhouses likely sheltered extended family or multi generational household groups, along with, in some interpretations, stored grain and possibly livestock during parts of the year, though the precise combination of domestic, storage, and animal housing functions within a single longhouse structure remains a subject of ongoing archaeological investigation and regional variation.

Across most Neolithic settlements, regardless of region, archaeologists find evidence of grain storage facilities, whether storage pits, raised granaries, or storage vessels, a material reflection of the fundamental Neolithic strategy of accumulating surplus from a single annual or seasonal harvest to sustain a community across the remainder of the year, a strategy that some Mesolithic foraging communities had already practiced on a smaller scale, through caching and other forms of food storage, but that became structurally central and vastly expanded in scale within farming economies, which depended on storing the yield of a single annual or seasonal harvest far more heavily than foraging economies typically relied on stored surplus of wild resources. This capacity to store surplus grain across the year, while requiring real technical skill in construction and pest management to prevent spoilage and loss, provided the demographic and economic foundation for the population growth examined in Section 4.8 and for the social differentiation in wealth and status that, as the striking evidence from Varna examined in Section 4.10 demonstrates, had already become pronounced in some later Neolithic and Copper Age communities of southeastern Europe well before it became a more widespread feature of European society in the following Bronze Age, examined in Chapter 5.

Section 4.8

Demography, Health, and the Costs of Farming

The Neolithic transformation produced, across the roughly four thousand years this chapter surveys, a substantial and sustained increase in the human population of Europe, a pattern documented through the increasing number, size, and density of archaeological settlements dating to this period compared to the preceding Mesolithic. This population growth reflected farming's capacity, discussed in Section 4.1, to produce substantially more calories per unit of land than hunting and gathering alone could typically sustain, particularly once effective grain storage, examined in Section 4.7, allowed communities to survive lean months and unfavorable years without the population being sharply reduced by seasonal scarcity in the way that may have periodically constrained some Mesolithic foraging populations.

At the same time, skeletal evidence recovered from Neolithic cemeteries across many parts of Europe reveals a more complicated picture of individual human welfare than population growth alone would suggest. Comparative studies of skeletal remains indicate that many early farming populations were, on average, somewhat shorter in adult stature than the Mesolithic populations that had inhabited the same or nearby regions previously, a pattern generally attributed to a diet more heavily dependent on a narrower range of cultivated staple foods, particularly cereals, compared to the broader and more nutritionally varied diet available to skilled foragers exploiting multiple wild plant and animal resources across different seasons. Dental evidence from Neolithic populations frequently shows increased rates of dental caries, associated with greater consumption of starchy cultivated grains, compared to Mesolithic populations whose diet included less concentrated carbohydrate.

Living in permanent, often relatively densely occupied settlements, frequently in close proximity to domesticated animals, also appears to have increased Neolithic populations' exposure to certain infectious diseases. Modern genetic and epidemiological research into the evolutionary origins of various human pathogens has identified several diseases whose transmission to human populations is associated with close contact with domesticated livestock, a pattern consistent with the archaeological evidence for increased infectious disease burden in some, though not all, studied Neolithic populations compared to their Mesolithic predecessors. Skeletal evidence from various Neolithic sites also documents markers associated with sustained heavy physical labor, particularly patterns of bone and joint stress linked, in some interpretations, to repetitive grain processing tasks such as grinding grain using stone querns, labor that in many, though not all, documented societies of this period appears to have fallen disproportionately on women, based on comparative skeletal wear patterns, though historians should exercise appropriate caution in generalizing this pattern uniformly across every Neolithic society and region.

Taken together, this evidence supports a more nuanced historical judgment than a simple narrative of agricultural progress would suggest. Farming enabled substantially larger human populations to inhabit Europe than hunting and gathering alone could typically have sustained, and this demographic expansion, along with greater sedentism and larger stores of food, created conditions that became important foundations for many later European societies. At the same time, the individual human costs of this transition, in stature, dental health, infectious disease exposure, and physical labor, were real, and historians should resist treating the Neolithic transformation as an unambiguous improvement in individual quality of life, or as a step on a predetermined path toward states, hierarchy, metallurgy, or urban life, none of which farming made inevitable even where it did make them possible.

Section 4.9

Hunter-Gatherer Persistence and the Frontier of Farming

Farming did not spread across Europe as a single, uniform wave sweeping aside all resident populations in its path. As earlier sections of this chapter have already indicated, the pace, character, and demographic impact of Neolithization varied considerably by region, and in several parts of the continent, particularly the North European Plain, southern Scandinavia, and much of the Baltic region, Mesolithic hunter gatherer communities continued their existing way of life for many centuries, in some cases over a millennium, after farming had already become firmly established in neighboring regions to the south.

The boundary zone between expanding Linear Pottery farming communities, examined in Section 4.5, and the Mesolithic hunter gatherer populations of the North European Plain and southern Scandinavia has been studied particularly closely by archaeologists, in part because it offers unusually rich evidence for the varied ways farming and foraging societies could interact over an extended period. In some areas, archaeological and genetic evidence suggests farming communities and neighboring hunter gatherer groups maintained largely separate identities and settlement areas for centuries, with only limited exchange of goods, and perhaps genetic exchange through occasional intermarriage, across the frontier between them. In other areas, evidence suggests more substantial interaction, including the selective adoption by hunter gatherer communities of particular Neolithic elements, certain domesticated animals, pottery styles, or polished stone tool types, without a full transition to a farming economy, a pattern sometimes described by archaeologists as the availability model of Neolithization, in which resident foraging populations adopted specific useful elements of the farming package selectively and gradually, on their own terms and according to their own assessment of local advantage, rather than wholesale replacing their existing subsistence strategy immediately upon contact.

The eventual, though considerably delayed, spread of farming into Scandinavia and the Baltic region did not follow a single uniform pattern, and current genetic evidence indicates that these two neighboring regions should be examined separately rather than treated as a single case. In Denmark, farming arrived at approximately 3950 BCE with what ancient genomic evidence indicates was a comparatively abrupt population turnover, incoming farmers contributing the great majority of ancestry with only a limited genetic contribution from resident Mesolithic hunter gatherers, a pattern broadly consistent with the demic diffusion model discussed in this chapter's Historical Debate feature. Farther east, however, the Baltic region followed a markedly different trajectory: hunter gatherer ancestry and foraging based economies persisted there for many centuries longer, and communities across much of the eastern Baltic continued to rely substantially on hunting, fishing, and gathering, sometimes alongside the adoption of pottery and, later, some domesticated species, well after farming had become the dominant subsistence strategy in Denmark and much of the North European Plain to the south.

Even in regions where farming eventually became dominant, hunting, fishing, and gathering continued to supplement agricultural subsistence for many communities, particularly in coastal, riverine, and forested areas where wild resources remained abundant and reliable, a reminder that the transition examined in this chapter should be understood as a gradual and often partial, and regionally quite variable, shift in the balance of subsistence strategies rather than an abrupt and total replacement of one way of life by another everywhere across northern Europe.

Section 4.10

Belief, Death, and Monumental Beginnings

The Neolithic transformation also produced significant changes in how European communities treated their dead and, during the later millennia of the European Neolithic, specifically across the fifth and fourth millennia BCE, in how they began to shape the landscape through deliberate monumental construction, developments that anticipate, without yet fully constituting, the megalithic monument building examined more fully in Chapter 5.

Formal cemeteries were not a Neolithic invention. Substantial Mesolithic cemeteries, including those documented at Zvejnieki in the eastern Baltic, Skateholm in southern Scandinavia, and Yuzhniy Oleniy Ostrov in northwestern Russia, demonstrate that some hunter gatherer communities had already developed elaborate formal burial practices well before farming arrived in their regions. What changed with the spread of farming was less the existence of cemeteries as such than their increasing prevalence across most of the continent and, in many regions, their association with the settled, multi-generational communities examined in Section 4.7. Across much of Neolithic Europe, archaeologists find formal cemeteries associated with settled farming communities, containing burials, in many though not all regions, accompanied by grave goods such as pottery vessels, stone tools, or personal ornaments, evidence that has allowed researchers to study, with appropriate methodological caution, patterns of social differentiation, gender, age, and community organization within Neolithic populations. In some regions and periods, grave goods and burial treatment appear relatively uniform across a cemetery population, suggesting comparatively limited social hierarchy, while in other regions and later periods, increasing variation in grave goods and burial elaboration suggests the gradual emergence of social distinction. This trend became strikingly pronounced in parts of southeastern Europe well before the Bronze Age proper: the cemetery at Varna, in what is now Bulgaria, dated to approximately 4550 to 4325 BCE and therefore falling within the later, Copper Age phase of the southeastern European Neolithic, contains burials of dramatically unequal wealth, including some of the earliest known large scale deposits of worked gold anywhere in the world alongside graves furnished with little or nothing at all. Varna demonstrates that pronounced social and economic inequality had already become visible in at least some fifth-millennium BCE European societies, well before the more widely documented Bronze Age hierarchies examined in Chapter 5, even though such pronounced inequality was not yet the general pattern across most of Neolithic Europe.

Monumental funerary construction in western Europe began somewhat earlier than older scholarship once proposed. Recent excavation and dating at Le Plasker, part of the wider megalithic complex at Carnac in Brittany, has identified monumental funerary activity dating to approximately 4720 BCE, with continued standing-stone construction in the same area across roughly 4680 to 4250 BCE, placing the earliest such monuments within the first half of the fifth millennium BCE rather than at its end. From this early Breton core, and quite possibly through parallel independent developments elsewhere, more widespread and increasingly elaborate megalithic traditions, structures archaeologists generally group under the term megalithic, including chambered tombs, in which a stone lined burial chamber was covered by an earthen or stone mound, and long barrows, elongated earthen mounds covering one or more burials or chambers, spread across the British Isles, Iberia, and Scandinavia over the later fifth and fourth millennia BCE. These monuments required substantial coordinated labor to construct, evidence, in the interpretation of most archaeologists, for organized communal effort, whether motivated primarily by religious belief, ancestor veneration, territorial marking, or some combination of these and other purposes that cannot be recovered with certainty from the physical evidence alone.

Historians and archaeologists should exercise particular caution when interpreting the religious or ideological meaning of Neolithic material culture, including figurines, monument orientation, and burial practice, since the belief systems of Neolithic communities left no written record and can only be inferred, often speculatively, from physical remains whose original symbolic significance to the people who created them cannot be directly recovered. This chapter therefore describes the material evidence for Neolithic funerary and monumental practice while resisting confident claims about specific religious beliefs, a caution that will recur in Chapter 5's fuller treatment of megalithic monument building and the more elaborate Bronze Age funerary and religious evidence that follows the developments introduced here.

Section 4.11

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the Neolithic transformation, the shift from hunting and gathering to farming, from its origins in the Fertile Crescent of southwestern Asia through its gradual, uneven, and regionally varied spread across Europe over roughly four thousand years. Before proceeding to Chapter 5's examination of the Bronze Age, students should be able to demonstrate command of this chapter's central concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: farming did not originate in Europe but was introduced from the Fertile Crescent, where wild cereals and legumes were first cultivated and sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs first domesticated. Farming reached Europe by at least two broadly distinguishable routes, a Mediterranean maritime route associated with Cardial or Impressed Ware pottery and a continental Danubian route associated with the Linear Pottery culture, each with its own chronology and character. The spread of farming involved varying combinations of migrating farming populations and the adoption of farming elements by existing Mesolithic populations, a balance that differed by region and remains, in its precise regional weighting, a subject of ongoing scholarly investigation. Farming enabled substantial population growth while, according to skeletal evidence from many regions, also reducing average stature, increasing certain infectious disease burdens, and imposing new patterns of physical labor, a complex balance of costs and benefits that should not be reduced to simple progress. Hunter gatherer communities in northern and northeastern Europe continued their existing way of life for many centuries after farming had become established elsewhere, though the manner of farming's eventual arrival varied sharply even within this northern zone, from the comparatively abrupt population turnover documented in Denmark to the far longer persistence of hunter-gatherer ancestry and foraging economies in the eastern Baltic. Formal cemeteries were not a Neolithic invention, since some Mesolithic communities already practiced elaborate burial, but cemeteries became considerably more widespread and, in many regions, took on new forms associated with settled farming life, and, in its later centuries, the Neolithic period produced some of the earliest known monumental funerary architecture in Europe and, at sites such as Varna, striking evidence of social and economic inequality, developments whose significance expands considerably in the Bronze Age examined in Chapter 5.

Primary Source Window

Archaeological Evidence from Çatalhöyük, c. 7100–5950 BCE

Creator: Not applicable in the conventional sense; this entry examines material archaeological evidence rather than a textual source · Date: The settlement was occupied across roughly a millennium, from approximately 7100 to 5950 BCE · Type: A large, densely built early farming settlement in central Anatolia, excavated and studied by archaeologists since the mid twentieth century

Context: Çatalhöyük, located in central Anatolia and therefore, by the conventional geographic boundary discussed in Chapter 1, situated just outside Europe proper, nonetheless offers historians and archaeologists an unusually well preserved and extensively studied window onto a broadly contemporaneous farming society of the wider Neolithic world from which the settlements of Thessaly and the Balkans, examined in Section 4.3, ultimately emerged. Ancient DNA evidence indicates that the community at Çatalhöyük itself developed with substantial genetic continuity from earlier local Anatolian hunter gatherer populations, while the farming populations that expanded into the Aegean and, from there, into Europe trace their ancestry to broader western Anatolian and Aegean populations related to, but not necessarily descended directly from, the specific community at Çatalhöyük. The site should therefore be understood as an important comparative example of a contemporaneous central Anatolian farming society rather than as a demonstrated direct ancestor of any specific European settlement. The settlement consisted of densely packed mudbrick houses, entered through openings in their roofs rather than through ground level doors, with houses often rebuilt directly atop earlier houses across many generations.

Archaeological excavation at Çatalhöyük has recovered evidence of domesticated wheat, barley, and legumes, alongside managed sheep and goats and, in the settlement's later phases, cattle, together with burials placed beneath the floors of houses, wall paintings and plastered bull horns installed within some domestic interiors, and figurines whose precise ritual or symbolic significance remains a matter of ongoing archaeological interpretation rather than settled certainty.

Analysis: This evidence is valuable to students of this chapter because it illustrates, in exceptional physical detail, the kind of settled, densely populated farming community that developed in the wider region from which European Neolithic culture ultimately derived, including patterns of house rebuilding, domestic burial, and elaborate interior decoration that find echoes, though not exact parallels, in the tell settlements of Thessaly discussed in Section 4.3. At the same time, students should recognize that Çatalhöyük lies outside conventional geographic Europe, and its evidence should be used to illuminate the broader Neolithic world from which European farming emerged rather than treated as direct evidence for any specific European community.

Historical Debate

Migration and Admixture: How Did Farming Actually Spread?

The question: As farming spread across Europe, how much of that spread involved the movement of farming populations themselves, carrying their agricultural economy, material culture, and genetic ancestry into new territory, compared to existing Mesolithic hunter gatherer populations adopting farming techniques and domesticated species through contact alone, without population movement? For decades this question was posed as a fairly even contest between two named alternatives, demic diffusion and cultural diffusion. Large scale ancient DNA research over the past decade has substantially resolved the broad outline of this question while leaving important regional and chronological details still under active investigation.

What ancient DNA has established: Genome-wide evidence from hundreds of Neolithic skeletons now shows that demic diffusion, the migration of farming populations themselves, was the principal mechanism by which agriculture reached most of southern, central, and western Europe. Early farmers across these regions typically derive the substantial majority of their ancestry, often more than ninety percent, from a population related to Anatolian and Aegean farmers, rather than from local Mesolithic hunter gatherers. This applies to both the Mediterranean route examined in Section 4.4 and the Danubian route examined in Section 4.5.
What remains genuinely debated: The open historical questions now concern the pace, direction, and regional variability of admixture between incoming farmers and resident hunter gatherers after initial farming settlement, why some regions and time periods show markedly higher hunter-gatherer genetic contribution than others, how social and marital practices between farming and foraging communities shaped this admixture, and how quickly, once established, admixture proportions changed across subsequent centuries. Regions such as the eastern Baltic, examined in Section 4.9, retained substantially higher hunter-gatherer ancestry and delayed the shift to farming far longer than regions such as Denmark, where genetic evidence points to a comparatively abrupt population turnover.

Evidence: The genetic evidence now available is considerably more extensive and more direct than the archaeological and skeletal evidence historians relied upon in earlier decades, and it has shifted the scholarly consensus decisively toward migration as the dominant mechanism, while archaeological, isotopic, and skeletal evidence continue to refine understanding of the specific regional and chronological patterns of subsequent admixture.

Why it matters: Recognizing migration as the principal mechanism does not erase the historical significance of resident hunter gatherer populations, who in most regions did not simply disappear but became incorporated, over time and in locally variable proportions, into the ancestry of later Neolithic and Bronze Age European populations. How historians describe this process, as replacement, as absorption, or as a variable mixture of both, shapes how the relationship between farming newcomers and existing populations should be narrated for any specific region.

Student task: Using the regional evidence described in Sections 4.4, 4.5, and 4.9, compare the genetic evidence for Denmark with that for the eastern Baltic, and explain what social, economic, or demographic factors might account for such different admixture outcomes between two broadly similar northern European regions.

Historical Vocabulary
Neolithic and Neolithization

The Neolithic is the archaeological period, following the Mesolithic examined in Chapter 3, generally defined not by its originally intended meaning of polished stone tools but by the presence of a farming economy based on domesticated plants and animals. Neolithization refers specifically to the historical process by which farming economies spread into and across a given region, a process this chapter has shown to have been gradual, regionally variable, and far from uniform across the roughly four thousand years it took for farming to reach most of Europe.

Historical Vocabulary
Domestication

Domestication refers to the gradual, generations long process by which human selection alters the genetic characteristics of a wild plant or animal species, producing a domesticated form dependent on human management for its continued propagation and typically differing from its wild ancestor in traits favorable to human use, such as a non-shattering seed head in cereals or reduced aggression and altered herd behavior in livestock. Domestication should be distinguished from mere taming of individual wild animals, which does not involve genetic change across generations, and from opportunistic human use of wild plants or animals, which does not involve deliberate management or selective breeding.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Transition to Farming

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across many sources of evidence. They are not quotations from any named historical individual, since no written record survives from this period.

From the perspective of a member of an early Thessalian farming household: Life centers on the annual cycle of planting, tending, and harvesting grain, on the labor of grinding stored wheat and barley, and on a settlement that has stood, rebuilt again and again, on the same mound of earth for as long as anyone can remember, a permanence quite different from the seasonal movement described by earlier generations.
From the perspective of a Mesolithic forager encountering farming neighbors on the North European Plain: The newcomers' fields and penned animals occupy land that has long supported good hunting and gathering, and while some of their pottery, and eventually some of their animals, prove useful to adopt, there is no obvious reason to abandon a way of life that has reliably supported this community for many generations, at least not yet.
From the perspective of a Linear Pottery farmer newly settled along the Rhine valley: Selecting land with the right soil, the pale, easily worked earth that yields good harvests, matters more than almost anything else, and the community's ties to farming villages many days' travel away, sharing similar houses, similar pottery, and similar customs, matter as much to daily life as ties to the nearest hunter-gatherer neighbors.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist studying Neolithic skeletal remains: The evidence resists a simple story of progress: population grew substantially, but individual skeletons often show reduced stature, more dental decay, and signs of repetitive physical strain compared to Mesolithic remains from the same region, a reminder that historical change can bring benefits and costs unevenly distributed across a population and across time.
Continuity and Change Over Time

What Farming Changed and What It Did Not

What Changed

  • Subsistence shifted, over time and unevenly by region, from reliance on wild resources to reliance on cultivated crops and managed herds
  • Settlement patterns shifted toward greater permanence, with some communities occupying the same site for many generations
  • Population density and total population size increased substantially compared to the Mesolithic period
  • Average stature and certain measures of skeletal health declined in many, though not all, studied populations
  • Formal cemeteries, already practiced by some Mesolithic communities, became more widespread and, in many regions, took on new forms associated with settled farming life, and monumental funerary architecture began to appear in parts of western Europe

What Stayed the Same

  • Hunting, fishing, and gathering continued to supplement, and in some regions substantially sustain, subsistence long after farming's initial arrival
  • Many Mesolithic populations, particularly in northern Europe, continued their existing way of life largely unchanged for many centuries after farming reached neighboring regions
  • Regional environmental diversity, discussed in Chapter 2, continued to shape which crops, animals, and farming techniques proved viable in a given area
  • Stone remained the overwhelmingly dominant material for tools throughout the Neolithic, though small scale copper working appears in parts of the Balkans by roughly 6000 BCE, with secure evidence for copper smelting by around the turn of the sixth to fifth millennium BCE, an early and regionally limited development that Chapter 5 examines in full as part of the broader transition to the Bronze Age

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating the Neolithic transformation as either a sudden, total revolution or as historically insignificant; it was instead a slow, uneven, and partial transformation whose consequences accumulated over many centuries and created conditions that helped make the Bronze Age developments examined in Chapter 5 possible, without making them inevitable.

Cause and Effect

Why Farming Spread Unevenly across Europe

Long-term causes

The Fertile Crescent's unusual concentration of domesticable wild cereals and animals produced a farming economy capable of sustaining far higher population densities than foraging alone, creating sustained demographic pressure favoring further territorial expansion over many centuries.

Short-term causes

Specific regional factors, including soil quality, climate, the density and social organization of resident hunter-gatherer populations, and the availability of maritime or riverine transport corridors, shaped the pace and character of Neolithization differently in different parts of the continent.

Effect

Farming reached southeastern Europe by roughly 6500 BCE but did not become established in Scandinavia until roughly 4000 BCE or later, a gap of well over two thousand years across a landmass of modest overall size.

This chapter's caution

Uneven spread was not simply a matter of some regions being more "receptive" to progress than others; resident populations in every region made historically contingent choices shaped by their own local resources, social organization, and assessment of relative advantage.

Historical complexity: The same underlying agricultural package produced very different regional outcomes depending on local environmental conditions and the specific character of the resident population's own way of life, a reminder against treating Neolithization as a single explanatory story rather than a family of related regional processes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in a Neolithic Farming Village

The Neolithic transformation reshaped daily life at a scale that political and economic summaries alone cannot fully convey. Where Mesolithic foragers examined in Chapter 3 organized their days and seasons around the movement and availability of wild resources, Neolithic farmers organized theirs around the agricultural calendar, plowing or tilling fields, sowing seed at the appropriate season, weeding and protecting growing crops from animals and pests, harvesting at the critical moment when grain was ripe but not yet lost to weather or spoilage, and processing and storing the harvest to last through the year ahead. This calendar imposed a different rhythm of labor and risk than foraging life, concentrating intense seasonal labor, particularly at harvest, alongside periods of lower agricultural demand that could be devoted to other tasks, including tool making, textile production, and, increasingly across the Neolithic, herding.

Household organization also changed. The longhouses and mudbrick dwellings examined in Section 4.7 housed family or kin based groups whose members likely shared responsibility for specific agricultural and domestic tasks, grinding grain, tending animals, and processing hides, wool, or plant fibers into cloth and other goods, though the precise division of labor by age and gender remains, for this period, a subject historians and archaeologists reconstruct cautiously from skeletal evidence, tool wear patterns, and comparative anthropological study of later farming societies, rather than from any direct written testimony. Village social life, structured around shared fields, shared labor at key points in the agricultural calendar, and, in many communities, shared burial grounds, created forms of community interdependence and, potentially, community obligation that differed from the more flexible band structures of many Mesolithic foraging societies, developments whose fuller social and political consequences become more visible in the Bronze Age evidence examined in Chapter 5.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Neolithic transformation, the gradual, uneven, and historically consequential shift from hunting and gathering to farming that reshaped human life across the European landmass over roughly four thousand years. Farming did not originate within Europe. It began in the Fertile Crescent of southwestern Asia, where communities first cultivated wild cereals and legumes and domesticated sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs, producing an integrated agricultural package that subsequently spread outward across the wider Near East and, by approximately 6700 to 6400 BCE, into the Greek mainland and southern Balkans, with the fuller Thessalian tell sequence generally dated to roughly 6500 to 6400 BCE, the earliest identifiable farming zone within conventional geographic Europe.

From this Aegean and Balkan core, farming spread along two broadly distinguishable routes examined in detail in this chapter: a Mediterranean maritime route, associated with Cardial or Impressed Ware pottery, that carried farming rapidly along the northern Mediterranean coastline from Italy to Iberia by roughly 5500 BCE, and a continental Danubian route, associated with the Linear Pottery culture, that carried farming with remarkable speed and material uniformity along the Danube corridor into central Europe from approximately 5500 BCE onward. These two routes differed considerably in pace and in their relationship to resident Mesolithic populations, a variation this chapter's Historical Debate feature examined by showing how ancient DNA evidence has established migration of farming populations, demic diffusion, as the principal mechanism by which agriculture reached most of southern, central, and western Europe, while leaving the pace and regional variability of subsequent admixture between incoming farmers and resident hunter gatherers as the genuinely open historical question.

The chapter examined the specific domesticated plants and animals that constituted the Neolithic agricultural package, wheat, barley, and legumes alongside sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs, and traced how European farming communities adapted this originally Near Eastern package to the considerably more varied climate and soil conditions described in Chapter 2. It examined the archaeological evidence for Neolithic settlement, including the tell villages of southeastern Europe and the longhouse settlements of Linear Pottery central Europe, evidence that documents a substantial shift toward settled, often remarkably permanent, village life.

A central theme of this chapter has been the complicated relationship between farming's demographic success and its costs to individual human health and welfare. Skeletal evidence from many regions indicates that farming populations, while growing substantially larger than the Mesolithic populations that preceded them, often experienced reduced average stature, increased dental decay, greater exposure to certain infectious diseases, and new patterns of physical strain associated with agricultural labor. This chapter has treated this evidence as a caution against narrating the Neolithic transformation as a simple story of progress, while still recognizing its transformative and, in the long run, foundational significance for subsequent European history.

The chapter also examined the extended frontier zones, particularly across the North European Plain and the Baltic region, where Mesolithic hunter gatherer populations continued their existing way of life for many centuries after farming had become established elsewhere. This chapter emphasized that this delayed transition did not follow a single pattern even within northern Europe: Denmark's genetic evidence points to a comparatively abrupt population turnover associated with incoming farmers, while the eastern Baltic shows far greater and more prolonged continuity of hunter-gatherer ancestry and foraging economies, with some communities adopting particular elements of the farming package selectively and gradually rather than experiencing an immediate or uniform transition. Finally, the chapter examined the increasing prevalence of formal cemeteries, themselves already practiced in some Mesolithic societies, alongside, in the Neolithic's later centuries, some of the earliest known monumental funerary architecture in Europe and striking early evidence of social inequality at Varna, developments that anticipate the more elaborate megalithic monument building and increasingly hierarchical societies examined in Chapter 5.

These themes, the non-European origin of farming, its uneven and regionally varied spread, the genuine costs as well as benefits of the transition, the long persistence of foraging alongside farming, and the beginnings of monumental and funerary complexity, together establish the demographic, economic, and social foundation upon which the Bronze Age societies examined in the following chapter would build.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Neolithic
The archaeological period following the Mesolithic, defined chiefly by the presence of a farming economy based on domesticated plants and animals, rather than by its original nineteenth century definition based on polished stone tools; in parts of northeastern Europe, however, the term is sometimes applied to pottery-using foraging communities who had not yet adopted agriculture, so its precise meaning can vary somewhat by region.
Neolithization
The historical process by which farming economies spread into and across a given region, a process this chapter has shown to be gradual, regionally variable, and non-uniform.
Domestication
The gradual, multi-generational process by which human selection alters the genetic characteristics of a wild plant or animal species into a form dependent on human management.
Fertile Crescent
An arc of hill country and river valleys in southwestern Asia where wild cereals and legumes were first cultivated and sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs first domesticated, beginning by roughly 9500 to 8500 BCE.
Archaeobotany
The scientific study of ancient plant remains, particularly carbonized seeds and grains, used to identify cultivated crops and trace genetic changes associated with domestication.
Zooarchaeology
The scientific study of animal bones recovered from archaeological sites, used to distinguish wild from domesticated animals and reconstruct herd management practices.
Ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from ancient skeletal remains, increasingly used by researchers to trace population movements and ancestry relationships among prehistoric populations.
Isotope analysis
A scientific technique measuring stable isotopes preserved in human bone and tooth enamel, used to reconstruct ancient diet and individual mobility or geographic origin.
Einkorn and emmer wheat
Early domesticated wheat varieties first cultivated in the Fertile Crescent and among the principal cereal crops of the European Neolithic.
Legumes
Domesticated plants including lentils, peas, and chickpeas, cultivated alongside cereals in the Fertile Crescent and subsequently across much of Neolithic Europe.
Cardial or Impressed Ware
A pottery tradition, decorated using shell edges or other impressed tools, associated with the rapid spread of farming along the northern Mediterranean coastline from roughly 6000 BCE.
Linear Pottery culture (LBK)
The archaeological culture, named for its incised linear pottery decoration, associated with the rapid expansion of farming along the Danube corridor into central Europe from approximately 5500 BCE.
Longhouse
A large rectangular timber structure characteristic of Linear Pottery settlements, likely housing extended family or kin-based household groups.
Demic diffusion
A model of Neolithization in which farming spreads primarily through the migration of farming populations themselves, carrying their economy, culture, and genetic ancestry into new territory.
Cultural diffusion
A model of Neolithization in which farming spreads primarily through the adoption of farming techniques and domesticated species by existing populations, without large-scale population movement.
Mesolithic
The archaeological period preceding the Neolithic, characterized by hunting, fishing, and gathering economies, examined in Chapter 3.
Sedentism
The practice of living in permanent, fixed settlements rather than moving seasonally, a pattern that became far more widespread with the Neolithic transformation.
Surplus
Agricultural production beyond immediate consumption needs, storable across the year and foundational to Neolithic population growth and, later, social differentiation.
Chambered tomb
A type of megalithic funerary monument in which a stone-lined burial chamber is covered by an earthen or stone mound, appearing in western Europe from roughly 4000 BCE.
Long barrow
An elongated earthen mound covering one or more Neolithic burials or funerary chambers.
Frontier zone
A region of prolonged contact and interaction between expanding farming populations and resident hunter-gatherer communities, examined particularly in connection with the North European Plain and Scandinavia.
Secondary products revolution
A historiographical model describing the exploitation of domesticated animals for products and services beyond meat, including milk, wool, and traction; current evidence shows these component innovations had staggered chronologies rather than arriving together as one late package, with dairying documented by the seventh millennium BCE and ploughing by the early fifth millennium BCE, while wool exploitation developed later.
Tell settlement
A mounded archaeological site formed through the long accumulation of successive layers of mudbrick construction on the same location across many generations, characteristic of southeastern European and Near Eastern early farming villages.
Availability model
An archaeological model of Neolithization in which resident foraging populations selectively and gradually adopt particular useful elements of the farming package on their own terms, rather than adopting a full farming economy immediately upon contact.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced one of the most fundamental transformations in the whole of European history, a transformation that unfolded slowly, unevenly, and at a pace almost impossible to perceive within a single human lifetime, yet whose cumulative consequences reshaped nearly every aspect of how people across the continent lived, ate, built their homes, organized their labor, and, eventually, buried their dead. Farming did not arrive as a single announced revolution. It arrived, region by region, over roughly four thousand years, carried by migrating farmers in some places and adopted gradually by existing populations in others, encountering along the way hunter gatherer communities whose own long and successful history, examined in Chapter 3, should not be forgotten simply because farming eventually became the dominant way of life across most of the continent.

This chapter has also insisted on a historical honesty that popular accounts of agricultural "progress" often omit: the shift to farming carried real costs for the individual human beings who lived through it, costs measurable in reduced stature, increased disease, and new patterns of hard physical labor, even as it enabled the larger populations, permanent settlements, and accumulated surplus that created conditions important to many later societies examined in this textbook, from Bronze Age metalworking to medieval kingdoms to modern industrial states, without predetermining those later developments. Understanding this dual character, real cost alongside real transformation, is a habit of historical thinking students should carry forward into every subsequent chapter.

The demographic growth, settled village life, grain storage, and early social distinction examined in this chapter, visible in stark form already at Varna by the mid-fifth millennium BCE, created important conditions for the next major transformation in European prehistory: the appearance of more widespread metalworking, expanded trade networks, more pronounced social hierarchy, and the great megalithic monuments that would come to define the Bronze Age landscape examined in Chapter 5. That chapter builds directly on the farming villages, domesticated herds, and funerary traditions this chapter has described, tracing how the demographic and economic changes begun by Neolithic farmers fed into, without predetermining, a new and, in important respects, more unequal chapter of European prehistory.
