Roman Society, Slavery, Family, and Citizenship

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

A person's place in the Roman world was never fixed by one fact. Freedom or bondage, citizenship or its absence, birth or manumission, wealth, sex, age, and position within a household all combined, and Mediterranean conquest rearranged those combinations without destroying the older institutions that gave them meaning.

Around the middle of the second century BCE, Marcus Porcius Cato, a senator and former consul, composed a manual on running a farm. The work survives as De Agricultura, and it opens a window onto a Roman landowner's world with unusual bluntness. Cato advises the owner who arrives at his estate to pay respects at the household shrine and then, if possible, walk over the whole property the same day, since a farm inspected in person is a farm better managed. The instructions that follow concern vines, olives, oxen, tools, contracts, and the people who did the work: an overseer, an overseer's wife, hired laborers, and enslaved men and women whose rations, clothing, and usefulness Cato calculates with the same attention he gives to the ploughs.

The manual is a good place to begin because it shows in one small text how many dimensions of Roman society intersected on a single farm. A citizen landowner exercised legal authority over a household that included his wife, his children, his dependents, and his property, human and otherwise. He owed obligations to friends, clients, and the state, and he expected profit from an agricultural enterprise linked to markets, which were in turn shaped by roads, cities, and armies. Chapter 17 explained how Rome acquired and governed a Mediterranean empire. This chapter asks a different question: what kind of society existed inside that expanding Roman world, how did status work within it, and how was conquest changing the daily lives of the people who lived there?

The central problem is what determined a person's place in Roman society, and how conquest reshaped households, labor, wealth, mobility, and citizenship without erasing older Roman institutions. The answer cannot be reduced to a single opposition. By the second century BCE the old division between patricians and plebeians, so prominent in the political struggles examined in Chapter 15, no longer served as the principal map of Roman society. Wealth, elite ancestry, citizenship, enslavement, and household position offered better guides, and they overlapped in complicated ways. A rich freedman might hold more property than a poor freeborn citizen, yet he stood lower in legal and social esteem. An elite woman might manage large estates through agents, yet she could not vote or hold magistracies.

Two themes run through the chapter. The first is slavery. Slavery was very old in the Roman world, but the conquests of the third and second centuries BCE increased its scale, its geographic reach, and its economic importance in Italy, and the chapter treats it as a major subject rather than a footnote to agriculture. The second is citizenship. Rome extended its political power much further than it extended its political membership, and Chapter 16 showed how differentiated status became an instrument of Italian expansion. Here the same question is asked from below, from the perspective of the person living under the system: what did citizenship give, and what did its absence cost?

Throughout, the chapter distinguishes among three layers that are often confused: the legal rule, the social expectation, and the actual practice. Roman law and Roman moralists described a world of strong paternal authority, dutiful wives, deferential clients, and obedient slaves. Real households negotiated, evaded, and reinterpreted those ideals. The evidence for that negotiation is uneven, and much of it was created by elite men, so the chapter also considers whose voices survive and whose do not. The voices of enslaved people are the most difficult to recover, and honest textbook writing must say so plainly.

The chapter belongs to a sequence within Part III. Chapter 15 examined how the Republic governed itself, Chapter 16 how Rome conquered Italy and survived Carthage, and Chapter 17 how it acquired and governed Mediterranean power. Chapter 18 treats conquest as background to social change, while Chapter 19 will examine how social and imperial pressures became political crisis. The present chapter therefore ends with tensions rather than a collapse. Roman society by the mid second century BCE was richer, more unequal, more dependent on enslaved labor, more mobile, and more politically complicated than the Republic of earlier generations, but none of that made later civil conflict inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Roman status was multidimensional, and why the patrician and plebeian distinction was no longer the principal social division by the second century BCE.
  2. Distinguish among senators, nobiles, equestrians, ordinary citizens, freedpeople, enslaved people, Italians without Roman citizenship, and provincials.
  3. Define familia, domus, paterfamilias, and patria potestas, and explain why legal formulations of paternal power do not simply describe everyday practice.
  4. Analyze marriage, dowry, guardianship, divorce, and women's property, distinguishing legal rule, social expectation, and lived practice.
  5. Describe Roman childhood, education, and the life course, and compare elite and nonelite experience.
  6. Trace how Mediterranean conquest changed the scale and distribution of slavery, and explain the range of conditions enslaved people experienced.
  7. Explain manumission and the position of freedpeople, including continuing obligations to former owners and the different status of their children.
  8. Evaluate the traditional argument that conquest destroyed the Roman small farmer, and identify which parts of that argument require qualification.
  9. Compare rural and urban experience, and contrast elite residences with the precarious housing and work of poorer Romans.
  10. Assess Roman citizenship, Latin status, allied Italian status, and provincial status from the perspective of those who lived under them.
  11. Interpret the strengths and limits of legal texts, inscriptions, archaeology, comedy, and elite prescriptive writing as evidence for Roman social life.
  12. Connect social change after conquest to the political questions examined in Chapter 19, without treating the later crisis as inevitable.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did conquest change Roman society, and how can historians avoid treating those changes as a single deterministic chain?
  3. In what ways did the Roman household function as a legal, economic, religious, and social institution, and how did enslaved people fit within it?

Historical Context

By about 200 BCE Rome had defeated Carthage in the Hannibalic War, controlled the Italian peninsula through a network of colonies, alliances, and dependent communities, and begun the overseas conquests that Chapter 17 examined. Over the next half century Roman armies fought in Greece, Macedonia, Iberia, and North Africa, and Rome organized provinces in Sicily, Sardinia and Corsica, the Iberian peninsula, Macedonia, Africa, and later Asia. The political system that directed these efforts, examined in Chapter 15, remained recognizably republican: annually elected magistrates, a Senate dominated by former magistrates, and popular assemblies in which citizens voted. Chapter 18 does not retell that constitutional history. It asks what sort of people lived under it.

Rome at this date was a society with deep continuities. The household was the basic unit of property, religious observance, and social reproduction. Land was the most prestigious form of wealth. Military service was tied to property and citizenship, since Roman armies were composed largely of citizen farmers and of allied Italian contingents who served under treaty obligations. Elite families competed for office and glory within a framework of inherited reputation, and the Senate's leading figures came disproportionately from families with consular ancestors. Slavery existed, and had for centuries, as did various forms of dependency and patronage.

What was changing was the scale and character of resources moving through this society. War produced booty, indemnities, tribute, and, above all, captives. Provinces generated taxes and contracts that were let to Roman businessmen. Roman soldiers spent long periods overseas, and Roman commanders and administrators encountered the wealth, art, and customs of Greek cities and Hellenistic kingdoms. Traders, craftsmen, and migrants moved toward Rome and other Italian centers. Greek culture, already present in southern Italy, became fashionable in elite circles and provoked both enthusiasm and anxious resistance from moralists who feared that luxury would corrupt Roman discipline. Historians should treat that moralizing critically, since anxiety about luxury was itself a political language used in elite rivalries.

Modern scholarship generally regards the period between the Second Punic War and the 130s BCE as one of substantial social transformation but not of collapse. Population, wealth, and urban size increased in many regions, and elite consumption grew conspicuously. The scale of enslavement increased. Many of the political problems later associated with the Gracchi, including land, army recruitment, and Italian demands for recognition, have roots in these changes, but the connection was mediated by political decisions, institutions, and contingency. Students should therefore resist reading second century society as merely a prelude to the later crisis, and should treat it as a functioning society with its own logic, tensions, and variations.

Evidence and Its Limits

Social history poses evidentiary problems different from those of military or constitutional narrative. A battle leaves a place, a date, and an account of some kind. A household leaves fragments. Historians of second century BCE Roman society work with an uneven set of sources, each of which illuminates some groups and obscures others, and the chapter's arguments depend on reading each with attention to who produced it and why.

Legal texts and later jurists. Roman law provides essential vocabulary for household, guardianship, marriage, inheritance, and manumission. But much of the surviving legal literature was written by jurists of the imperial period, especially the second and third centuries CE, and was preserved in sixth century compilations under the Byzantine emperor Justinian. Those texts describe legal concepts that sometimes reach back to the Republic, and they may preserve older rules, but they must be labeled as later evidence when used for republican practice. Early legal material, such as the fragmentary Twelve Tables traditionally dated to the mid fifth century BCE, survives only in quotations and later reconstruction. Law also describes rules and disputes, not the ordinary negotiations that never reached a court.

Inscriptions and epitaphs. Tombstones, dedications, and public inscriptions record names, offices, family relationships, occupations, and sometimes the sentiments of survivors. They are among the few sources in which freedpeople and nonelite citizens speak, if briefly and conventionally, in their own commemorative language. Their limits are equally clear. Inscriptions survive unevenly, and in the second century BCE they are fewer than in later periods, since the habit of commemorating oneself in stone expanded greatly under the empire. Those who could afford stone are also not typical of the entire population.

Archaeology. Excavations of houses, farms, workshops, cemeteries, and rural landscapes provide evidence that literary authors ignore. Field surveys, which record scatters of pottery and building material across the landscape, have transformed debates over Italian rural settlement. Archaeology can show settlement patterns and material standards but rarely identifies individual legal status, and survey data are notoriously difficult to date with precision. Pompeii and Herculaneum are extraordinarily rich, but students should be careful. Most of the preserved urban fabric reflects the late republic and, above all, the first century CE, and Pompeii cannot be treated as a photograph of Roman society in the second century BCE. A few houses there, such as the large house known as the House of the Faun, belong to the second century BCE and provide valuable evidence for wealthy households in a Campanian town, but a Campanian town under Roman influence was not Rome.

Comedy and literature. The plays of Plautus and Terence, produced in the early and middle second century BCE, are the oldest substantial Latin literature and offer vivid portrayals of fathers, sons, slaves, courtesans, and traders. But they are adaptations of Greek New Comedy, with Greek settings and stock characters, so they mix imported convention with Roman audience expectations. The clever enslaved trickster of comedy, for instance, tells us as much about theatrical tradition and audience fantasy as about actual relations between owners and enslaved people.

Prescriptive authors and later narrators. Cato's manual and his surviving fragments reveal elite ideals. Livy, writing under Augustus, and Plutarch, writing in the early second century CE, preserve details about second century events and about Cato that derive partly from earlier sources now lost, but they write from later perspectives and with moral and political purposes of their own. Polybius, a contemporary Greek observer, is valuable because he was neither a Roman senator nor a later moralist, though his own vantage was that of an elite Greek associated with the Roman aristocracy. Census figures, transmitted through later authors, offer numbers for adult male citizens, but their definition and reliability are contested, as Section 18.8 explains.

The general lesson is that nearly every source for this period reflects the perspective of free, male, propertied authors, or of those who wrote for them. Historians reconstruct the experience of women, poorer citizens, freedpeople, and especially enslaved people mainly through indirect evidence and inference, and the chapter marks the limits where they arise.

Section 18.1

Roman Society after Mediterranean Conquest

Chapter 17 ended with Rome as the dominant power of the Mediterranean, exercising direct rule through provinces and indirect influence through alliances and diplomacy. For the people living in Italy, this achievement was not simply a matter of maps. It arrived as a series of concrete changes: soldiers absent for campaigns in Spain or Macedonia, returning veterans with modest booty, magistrates displaying spoils, ships unloading goods and captives at Italian ports, and new opportunities for contractors and traders who supplied armies and collected provincial revenues.

Conquest generated resources on an unprecedented scale. Indemnities from Carthage and from Hellenistic kingdoms, tribute and taxes from provinces, and the proceeds of plunder flowed through the Roman treasury and into the hands of commanders and their entourages. Roman elites used these resources to build temples, roads, porticoes, and basilicas, to fund games, and to acquire land and slaves. At the same time, they were exposed to the material culture of Greek cities and of Hellenistic courts: sculpture, silverware, furniture, cuisine, rhetorical education, and philosophy. Greek learning entered Roman households in the persons of enslaved and freed tutors and doctors as well as in the plunder of statues.

Conquest also moved people. Thousands of soldiers were mobilized in most years and spent extended time overseas. Italian communities supplied contingents alongside Roman citizens. Traders and businessmen from Italy followed the armies and settled in provincial ports. Enslaved captives were moved to Italy and Sicily. Free people moved toward Rome itself in search of work and opportunity, so that the city grew and its social fabric became more heterogeneous. These movements meant that Roman society was increasingly a Mediterranean society, connected through commerce and coercion to a wide geographic frame.

It would be a mistake, however, to read these developments as producing an automatic, single line of change. The consequences varied by region, class, and circumstance. Some elite families grew richer, others lost ground, and new men occasionally rose through office or finance. Some Italian communities prospered from military supply and commerce while others remained relatively secluded. Some free workers found employment in construction and trade, while others faced competition from enslaved labor. What follows in this chapter therefore emphasizes variety and interaction. In particular, the chapter does not assume that conquest necessarily caused the later republican crisis. Conquest created conditions and questions. What Romans made of them depended on politics, and Chapter 19 will take up that story.

Section 18.2

Status, Rank, and Social Hierarchy

Roman society was a hierarchical one and Romans themselves spoke openly of rank and dignity. But the hierarchy was not a single ladder. It was a set of overlapping distinctions, and understanding a person's position means asking several questions at once. Was the person free or enslaved? A citizen or not? Freeborn or formerly enslaved? How much property did the person hold, and of what kind? Male or female, adult or child? Head of a household or subordinate within one? Bound by relations of patronage, and to whom? Residing in Rome, in a colony, in an allied community, or in a province, and under what local legal arrangement?

At the top of the citizen body stood the senatorial elite. Membership in the Senate was normally acquired through election to magistracies and enrolment by the censors, and the Senate's leading voices were former consuls and praetors. Within this group, the term nobiles generally referred to the established office-holding nobility, especially families able to claim consular ancestry, although its precise boundaries are debated. Such standing gave a family reputation, networks, and electoral advantage. A man without consular ancestors who reached the consulship was a novus homo, a new man. Cato was one, and his career shows both that such advancement was possible and that it required exceptional support and effort. Modern historians emphasize that the Roman governing class was open in principle to talent but in practice heavily dominated by a limited number of families, and that the appearance of a new man was noteworthy for exactly that reason.

Among the wealthy citizen elite were men of equestrian standing, who met a property qualification and had originally served as cavalry. The senatorial and equestrian elites overlapped substantially in census standing and landed wealth in this period, and the separate political identity of the equestrian order became more pronounced only gradually, especially later in the second century BCE. Many men of equestrian standing took public contracts, engaged in banking and long distance trade, or held large landed estates. Polybius observed that a great many Romans were involved in one way or another in state contracts, a comment that shows how widely public finance had penetrated Roman economic life. Many senators also had commercial interests, though they were formally discouraged from certain kinds of trade and often acted through agents, freedmen, or relatives.

The great mass of citizens ranged from prosperous landowners through modest farmers and artisans to the urban poor. The Roman census classified citizens by wealth, and military and political obligations were historically tied to those classes. Those with sufficient property to serve as legionaries were counted as assidui, while the very poor, the proletarii or capite censi, were counted by head and were not normally enrolled in the legions. Citizens of all classes voted, but the organization of assemblies, examined in Chapter 15, gave greater weight to the wealthy in some, and access to office depended heavily on money and connections. Political influence therefore differed sharply among citizens who were legally equal in status.

Outside the citizen body were the free noncitizens of Italy, the Latins and Italian allies, and the peregrini of the provinces, together with the freedpeople, who were citizens but with a lesser status than the freeborn, and the enslaved, who were legally owned and lacked independent civil-law capacity, even though Roman law recognized some acts performed by them on an owner's behalf. These groups are discussed in later sections. What should be clear here is that the old patrician and plebeian distinction had lost most of its social force. Patricians remained a small hereditary group with particular priesthoods, and plebeian families had entered the highest offices for generations. A wealthy plebeian consular family stood far above a poor patrician, so that wealth, ancestry in office, and legal status had replaced the older order as the most useful categories.

Legal status and wealth overlapped but were not identical. A wealthy freedman might have property exceeding that of many freeborn citizens, yet he could not hold magistracies and was socially stigmatized. A poor citizen could vote and expect certain legal protections that a wealthy provincial did not enjoy. This mismatch between status and wealth was a persistent source of tension and social comment in Roman literature, and it also helps explain why Romans cared so much about signs of rank: names, dress, seating at public events, and forms of address.

Section 18.3

The Roman Household: Familia, Domus, and Paterfamilias

The household was the fundamental building block of Roman society. It was a legal unit that held property and defined relations of authority, an economic unit that produced and consumed, a religious community that maintained cult, and a social unit that raised children, sheltered dependents, and represented its head in public life. To understand Roman society one must understand it as a society of households, and one must be careful not to assume that the Roman household resembled the modern nuclear family.

Historical Vocabulary
Familia, Domus, Paterfamilias, and Patria Potestas

Familia did not simply mean "family" in the modern sense. Depending on context, it could refer to all persons and property under the authority of a household head, including wife or children in certain legal arrangements, dependents, and enslaved people, and it could also refer to a wider kin group. Domus denoted the house in both physical and social senses: the dwelling, the household within it, and the family as a lineage with a reputation. The paterfamilias was the senior male in a household who was not himself under another's paternal authority. He was the legal owner of household property, and Roman law attributed to him patria potestas, extensive authority over his children and their descendants through the male line.

The term matters because it structures the Roman legal and social world. It also invites misunderstanding. A paterfamilias could be a young man whose father had died, and many adults remained under paternal power while their fathers lived. Modern students should avoid the shorthand that the oldest man could legally do anything to anyone in the household. Legal tradition described very wide powers, including in early law the right to punish and, in theory, to kill, but social expectation, kinship consultation, moral censure, economic interest, and practice limited its use, and evidence of ordinary practice is scarcer than evidence of legal statement.

A typical elite domus contained a married couple, their children, perhaps the paterfamilias's widowed mother or other relatives, and a considerable number of enslaved and freed dependents who performed domestic and administrative work. A modest citizen household might include a couple, children, and perhaps one or two enslaved people, or none at all. The rural poor and many urban residents lived in dwellings shared with more distant kin or lodgers. Household size, composition, and property varied enormously, so that generalizations from elite sources must be cautious.

Roman household authority was also property law. Under strict rules a person in paternal power (filiusfamilias) could not own property in his own right in the way a paterfamilias could, though households developed practical arrangements. A father could grant a son a fund of assets, the peculium, to manage, and the same device was widely used with enslaved people, who might hold and operate a peculium and in some cases save toward buying their freedom. Because the peculium reveals how flexible household economics could be, it links the sections on family and slavery. Inheritance was a central concern: property normally passed within the family, wills were widely used among propertied Romans, and the design of an heir's share, guardianship of minors, and provision for daughters were significant decisions with political and social consequences.

Religion also organized the household. The household cult honored the Lares, spirits associated with the household and its land, the Penates, protectors of the household's stores, and the household's guardian spirit, and the paterfamilias led rituals on behalf of the whole familia, including enslaved members. Household religious practice was not confined to the elite, and its details varied by region and family tradition. Later chapters, especially Chapter 23, return to Roman religion in a broader setting.

Finally, households were nodes in networks. Marriage alliances linked families. Friends and clients called at the house in the morning. Enslaved and freed members carried messages, managed accounts, and represented the master's interests. A household was therefore also a political and commercial actor, and its size and behavior signaled status. The fundamental point of this section is that familia should be read as a structure of authority and property in which people occupied different positions, and that the lived reality of those positions varied greatly from one household to another.

Section 18.4

Marriage, Women, Property, and Gender

Roman marriage was a social and legal arrangement rather than a religious sacrament. It rested on the consent and intention of the parties to live as husband and wife, together with the consent of their fathers if they were still under paternal power, and it was made visible through ceremony, cohabitation, and the reputation of the couple. Older forms of marriage, called cum manu, transferred a woman into her husband's legal authority. By the middle and later republic, marriage sine manu, in which the wife remained legally connected to her father's family and retained rights of inheritance from it, had become the prevailing form, though the precise chronology of the change is debated. This shift mattered for property. A woman who remained under her father's authority or, after his death, free of it, kept a much stronger connection to her natal family's property than one entirely absorbed into her husband's household.

The dowry (dos) was the property that a woman or her family transferred to the husband at marriage to help support the new household. It was the husband's to administer during the marriage but was normally to be returned in some circumstances, such as divorce or the husband's death, according to customary and later legal rules. The dowry connected marriage to family strategy. A wealthy father gave a dowry commensurate with his status and the alliance he sought, and the return provisions protected a woman's property to some extent. Divorce required no formal court proceeding in the republic and could be initiated by either spouse, though social opinion and practical financial arrangements constrained it. Elite marriages were often politically motivated, and divorce and remarriage were common among senatorial families in the late republic, though evidence for the second century is thinner.

Women's legal position must be described with care. Roman women could be Roman citizens, but citizenship did not give them the political rights exercised by adult male citizens: they could not vote or hold magistracies, and they were excluded from most public roles. Under early rules many adult women were subject to guardianship (tutela), which required the authorization of a guardian for certain transactions such as alienating major property. By the late republic, however, guardianship had in practice become weaker for many women, and women of means owned and managed land, lent money, made wills, and appeared in business affairs through agents. The lex Voconia of 169 BCE restricted the ability of the wealthiest citizens to name women as heirs, a reminder that legislation could tighten as well as loosen women's property position. It is a mistake either to say that Roman women had no rights or to imagine that elite women were nearly emancipated. Their position varied enormously by class, marital arrangement, family circumstances, and status.

Public discussion of women's roles appears in the sources, particularly in elite men's accounts of the repeal of the lex Oppia in 195 BCE, a measure passed during the Second Punic War restricting women's display of gold and finery and use of carriages. Livy's later narrative, whose speeches are literary compositions rather than transcripts, describes large groups of women demonstrating in the streets and Cato speaking against repeal. The episode is difficult to reconstruct in detail, but it indicates that women's visibility and display were matters of elite political concern and that women could take collective public action. Polybius, meanwhile, describes the elegant procession of Aemilia, the widow of Scipio Africanus, with its costly equipment, an example of the wealth and public presentation of an elite woman.

Women's religious roles were also significant. The Vestal Virgins served the state cult of Vesta, a special priesthood held by women. Matrons participated in festivals and cults reserved to them, and in 204 BCE the Romans, following an oracular instruction during the Hannibalic War, brought the cult of the Magna Mater, the Great Mother, to Rome, its ceremonies attended by women and men and administered under state supervision. The Bacchanalian affair of 186 BCE, discussed in Section 18.9, shows both the participation of women and men in unsanctioned religious societies and elite anxiety about them.

Women's work is harder to document, but it was substantial. Elite ideals honored the woman who managed the household, spun wool, and bore children. A republican epitaph often cited by historians, generally dated to the later second century BCE and known as the epitaph of Claudia, praises a wife for her devotion, her two sons, her pleasant speech, and her attention to the house and to wool. It should be read as a conventional statement of the ideal, not a description of the individual's private life. Nonelite women worked in agriculture, retail, food preparation, textile production, nursing, midwifery, and entertainment. Enslaved women worked in every household and workshop, often under conditions of sexual vulnerability discussed in Section 18.6. Freedwomen ran businesses and, like freedmen, are visible in commemorative inscriptions, though mainly from later periods.

The analytical distinction between legal rule, social expectation, and actual practice is especially useful here. Law shaped the framework, elite ideals prescribed conduct, and practice was worked out in households under pressures of property, family strategy, and personality. The sources rarely allow us to see practice clearly, and students should avoid both idealization and caricature.

Section 18.5

Childhood, Education, and the Roman Life Course

Roman life was short and uncertain by modern standards. Estimates of life expectancy at birth range in the twenties or low thirties, and though such averages are dominated by infant and child deaths, they reflect a world in which many children did not reach adulthood. Modern scholars estimate that roughly one in three infants died in the first year, and that a large share of children died before adolescence, though ancient evidence does not permit precision. Death in childbirth was also a significant risk for women. Disease, malnutrition, and the hazards of urban crowding shaped the life course of all social classes, though wealthier families had advantages in food and housing.

The newborn's status was not automatic. Roman sources refer to paternal acknowledgement of a newborn with the expression tollere liberos; scholars debate whether this involved a literal lifting ritual or more generally symbolized the decision to rear the child. Either way, the decision to raise or expose an infant lay with the family. Exposure, the abandonment of newborns, is documented in ancient sources, and the motives included poverty, illegitimacy, disability, and household strategy, though its scale is debated. Some exposed children were raised by others, occasionally as enslaved people, which links family practice to the supply of enslaved labor. Freeborn Roman children received a name, and by custom a purification and naming ceremony took place on a day near the eighth or ninth after birth. Boys wore a protective pendant, the bulla, and the child's clothing marked their status until formal coming of age.

Education varied sharply with wealth and sex. Elite boys learned reading, writing, and arithmetic, often from a household tutor or in small schools, and, as Greek culture became prestigious, increasingly studied Greek language and literature under a grammaticus. Later, advanced youths studied rhetoric, a skill essential for public and legal careers. Greek teachers were often enslaved or freed. Livius Andronicus, an early translator of Greek works into Latin, is traditionally described as an enslaved man from Tarentum, and the scholar Crates of Mallus, visiting Rome in the 160s BCE, is credited with stimulating grammatical study. Elite girls could also receive instruction in reading and literature, especially within the household, though their education was oriented toward household management and social roles, and their opportunities for public rhetorical training were far more limited.

For most Roman children, education meant learning work. Farm children helped with herding, weeding, and harvest, and urban children entered family workshops or shops, were apprenticed to craftsmen, or worked as errand runners and laborers. Enslaved children worked in households and workshops, and those born in the household, called vernae, might be raised alongside free children and sometimes enjoyed a degree of favor, though they remained property. Little of this experience was recorded by those who lived it. Coming of age for elite boys was marked by assuming the adult toga, at a date generally set in the mid teens, and often by public and family celebration. Military service, marriage, and political office followed on distinct age expectations, with minimum ages for office regulated by law in the second century. Girls could legally marry at a young age, though many elite girls married in their mid to late teens, and evidence suggests that ages varied by status and region. Marriage and childbearing structured women's adult lives, while men's lives were structured by service, office, and property management.

Age, like status, was thus a set of expectations rather than a single rule. The Roman life course connected the household to the state, since men's household position determined when they could vote, serve, and hold office, and women's roles in continuing the family and its property shaped the family's future position.

Section 18.6

Slavery and the Expansion of Enslaved Labor

Slavery was not new to Rome in the third century BCE. Enslaved people worked in Roman households and farms from the earliest periods for which evidence exists, and the Greek city-states examined in Chapters 10 and 13 also depended on enslaved labor. Roman law defined enslaved people as property, subject to the owner's ownership and control, and lacking many of the legal protections available to free persons. What changed after the Punic Wars was the scale, the geographic distribution, and the economic significance of slavery in Italy, together with its connection to a Mediterranean network of capture and trade. Historians therefore speak of a shift toward a society in which slavery was more central, sometimes called a "slave society" in contrast with a "society with slaves," though these labels are debated and the transition was gradual and regionally uneven.

Sources of enslavement

Enslaved people came to Roman households by several routes. War was the most visible. Captives from Roman campaigns were sold, often at the site of conquest, to dealers who followed the armies. Ancient authors give numbers for particular episodes, such as the sale of large numbers of Epirote captives in 167 BCE or the many inhabitants of Carthage enslaved in 146 BCE, but these figures, transmitted by later writers, are rhetorical or uncertain and should not be added together as demographic statistics. The general point remains sound: conquest produced captives in quantity, and the Roman market for enslaved people expanded to absorb them. Piracy and kidnapping also supplied enslaved people, and commercial trade brought them from regions beyond Roman control. The island of Delos in the Aegean became a major trading center after 167 BCE, and Strabo, writing much later, offers a famous claim about the numbers sold in a day there. It should be read as a rhetorical impression of large-scale commerce rather than a statistic.

Birth was another route. Children born to enslaved mothers were themselves enslaved, and Rome's slave population was therefore partly self-reproducing, though household and rural conditions affected fertility and survival. Roman citizens were normally no longer enslaved for debt after the reforms associated with the lex Poetelia Papiria, traditionally placed around 326 BCE, though the details and date are debated and other forms of dependency persisted. Abandoned infants who were raised as enslaved people added to the total. The overall picture is of a system supplied by war, commerce, and reproduction, with the relative weight of these sources varying across time and space.

Scale, with caution

Modern estimates of the enslaved population of Italy for the late first century BCE vary widely, with some scholars suggesting that enslaved people made up something like a fifth to a third of the peninsula's inhabitants, and others proposing lower figures. For the second century BCE, the evidence is even less secure. The important points are directional: the enslaved population grew substantially, it was concentrated in central and southern Italy and in Sicily, and it became economically important to elite households and estates. A historian who states a single precise figure for the enslaved population at a particular date claims more than the evidence supports.

Kinds of work and conditions of life

Roman slavery was not one occupation or one living condition. Enslaved people worked in households as cooks, cleaners, attendants, secretaries, doctors, tutors, and entertainers. They worked in agriculture, from the labor gangs of estates to the shepherds who moved flocks over long distances. They worked in workshops, shops, and transport, sometimes running businesses on behalf of their owners. They worked in mines, where conditions were notoriously harsh, and in construction and public service. Skilled and literate enslaved people, especially those from Greek-speaking regions, might occupy positions of responsibility and receive privileges, and some accumulated a peculium with the prospect of eventual manumission. Those in mines, chain gangs, and heavy agricultural labor had far less prospect of any improvement in their situation. The common defining fact across all of these conditions was legal unfreedom and the owner's coercive power.

That power was real and it was frequently violent. Owners could beat, sell, separate families, and punish enslaved people, and the Roman legal tradition gave little protection until much later, when imperial legislation began to limit certain extreme abuses. Enslaved women and men were subject to sexual exploitation, since law did not recognize an enslaved person's refusal as a legal bar to an owner's demands. Enslaved families were not legally recognized, though unions and relationships formed and were sometimes recorded, especially in commemorations, and they were always vulnerable to sale and separation. Historians should describe these facts plainly and avoid both euphemism and sensationalism.

Resistance and the problem of voices

Enslaved people resisted in many ways: by working slowly, sabotaging tools, feigning illness, running away, appealing to sympathetic owners, forming networks, and, on occasion, rising in armed revolt. Livy reports unrest among enslaved people in Latium in 198 BCE and among pastoral slaves in Apulia in 185 BCE, in the latter case with harsh repression and many condemned, though his figures are uncertain. The large enslaved revolt that began in Sicily in 135 BCE demonstrated that concentrations of enslaved labor could pose serious challenges to Roman authority, and it belongs to the years examined in Chapter 19. Fear of revolt shaped elite discourse and legal practice, and it fed a persistent Roman anxiety about the loyalty of those on whom the economy depended.

The difficulty for historians is that almost nothing in the surviving literature was written by enslaved people about their own experience in this period. Sources are owner's manuals, comedies, legal texts, and historians, and those that mention enslaved people generally treat them as objects or as characters in an owner's drama. A source that describes the rations of enslaved workers on an estate, as Cato does, tells us how an owner calculated costs and expectations, and offers glimpses of what conditions were like, but it cannot tell us what the workers thought, felt, or did. Later epitaphs and inscriptions provide more direct evidence of some enslaved and freed people, but mostly from the imperial period. The historian must therefore work with what the sources reveal about systems and practices while stating clearly what remains unknown.

Section 18.7

Manumission, Freedpeople, and Social Mobility

Roman slavery was unusual among ancient slave systems in the extent to which formerly enslaved people could become citizens. Owners freed enslaved people, a practice called manumission, for many reasons: as a reward for loyalty or long service, as a result of a purchase of freedom from the enslaved person's accumulated peculium, in a will, or as part of a calculated strategy to secure a lifetime of obedient service in expectation of eventual liberty. The prospect of manumission was itself an instrument of control, since hope of freedom encouraged compliance. It was not, however, universally available. The likelihood of freedom depended on occupation, closeness to the owner, and fortune, and those in mines and heavy rural labor had little chance.

Historical Vocabulary
Manumission, Libertus, and Patronus

Manumission was the act by which an owner freed an enslaved person. Formal methods in the republic included a ceremony before a magistrate, enrolment in the census with the owner's consent, and provision in a will. A libertus (feminine liberta) was a freed person, and the former owner became that person's patronus, or patron. A freed man of a Roman citizen owner usually became a Roman citizen, took the owner's family name as part of his own, and could vote but could not hold magistracies.

The concept matters because it shows how Roman society mixed legal exclusion with real social mobility. Freedpeople were not free from the past. They owed the patron respect and, often, defined services, and they carried a social stigma that freeborn Romans did not. Their children, born free to citizen parents, were freeborn citizens. The distinction between freedperson and freeborn citizen therefore shaped the social geography of Rome even when wealth was equal.

The freed person's new status brought advantages and constraints. Freedpeople gained a legal personality, could marry and have children who would be citizens by birth, could own property in their own names, and could engage in trade and craft. They typically remained connected to former owners by obligation, respect (obsequium), and in some cases specified services (operae). The patron had claims on the freedperson's loyalty and could expect assistance, and freed people often continued to work in the patron's business or household or managed enterprises on the patron's behalf. Patrons also gained by this arrangement, since freedpeople extended the reach of a household into commerce and public life.

Freedpeople could be economically successful. Many worked as artisans, traders, moneylenders, and managers, and some became wealthy, though evidence for spectacular wealth is more abundant for later periods. Political rights were limited. Freedmen were enrolled in the citizen body but historically in a restricted set of tribes, and they could not stand for magistracies or serve as senators. Roman writers frequently mocked the freedman who displayed wealth without inherited standing, and elite anxiety about upstarts reflects the fact that the system produced people who could be rich, legally citizens, and socially stigmatized at once. The stigma did not usually extend to their children, and it was therefore a mechanism by which some families rose across generations.

Manumission also affected the composition of the citizen body. As more enslaved people were freed, especially in urban households and in commercial occupations, the citizens of Rome came to include an increasing number of people of foreign origin, including Greeks, Syrians, and others brought to Italy as captives or through trade. Roman writers occasionally complained about the changing population of the city. Modern historians see this process as one of the ways in which Rome became a Mediterranean society, and a contrast with many ancient Greek cities, which were far more restrictive about admitting former slaves to citizenship. It should not be idealized: manumission was an act of the owner's will, it left formerly enslaved people in dependent positions, and it coexisted with a system built on violence and exploitation.

Section 18.8

Rural Italy: Farms, Estates, Land, and Labor

Most people in Roman Italy lived in the countryside, and most wealth was still measured in land. The rural landscape, however, was not uniform. Latium, Campania, Etruria, the Po Valley, Apulia, the Apennine highlands, and southern Italy each had distinct patterns of settlement and production. In some areas, especially in the coastal regions of Etruria, Latium, and Campania, market-oriented estates using enslaved labor became prominent. In others, small and medium farms predominated. Upland areas supported pastoralism and transhumance, with flocks moving seasonally between winter lowlands and summer mountain pastures. Some regions were heavily affected by the Hannibalic War, and others hardly at all.

Cato's manual describes one type of enterprise: a villa, a rural estate managed for profit, with an overseer (vilicus) and enslaved workers, producing wine, olive oil, and other goods for sale, and drawing on hired labor at peak seasons. He gives model estates, one for an oliveyard of about two hundred forty iugera (roughly 60 hectares) and one for a vineyard of about a hundred (roughly 25 hectares), with staffs measured in a dozen or so workers. His estates are supplied by markets and are near roads and a city. This is a valuable description of a particular kind of enterprise, but it is a model advocated by a wealthy owner and cannot be taken as an inventory of typical Italian farms.

Alongside such estates were the farms of citizen smallholders, families cultivating a few iugera with household labor and, where they could afford it, an enslaved person or two. These households supplied the legionaries who made up the Roman army, and their prosperity was a matter of public concern. Tenancy and sharecropping also existed, along with hired labor, and free workers and enslaved workers often labored side by side. Rural society included the rich owner, the modest landholder, the tenant, the hired day laborer, and the enslaved worker, and a given individual might occupy more than one of these positions over a lifetime.

A great deal of land was public land (ager publicus), acquired by conquest and held by the Roman state. It was assigned to colonists, distributed to individual citizens, or leased and occupied by others, including wealthy families who used it for large-scale agriculture and pasture. Disputes over its use are an important feature of the later republic, but this chapter's focus is on how rural society functioned. The term latifundium is often used for large estates, but historians now note that it is a later, loosely used, and sometimes polemical term, and that its application to second century BCE Italy requires caution. Large landholdings certainly existed and grew, but they were often composed of scattered parcels rather than single vast blocks, and their organization varied.

Military service affected rural households, and it affected them unevenly. The Second Punic War demanded intense and prolonged mobilization, while campaigns in the second century required different patterns of service, including long periods overseas in Spain and the east. Absence removed labor from the household farm, and a farmer's family faced the risk of loss if he was killed or disabled. But households adapted, using family members, neighbors, kin, and hired or enslaved labor, and the fact that many soldiers returned home after service suggests that adaptation was often successful. Regional variations were large, as were differences in the availability of land colonies for veterans and their families.

Primary Source Window

Cato the Elder, De Agricultura, on Managing an Estate, c. 160 BCE

Context: Cato's treatise is the oldest surviving Latin prose work. It gives advice on purchasing a farm, planting vines and olives, making wine and oil, feeding animals, and managing labor, along with recipes, religious formulas, and contract templates. It is addressed to an owner who expects to profit from an estate near markets and who will delegate day-to-day work to an overseer. The text as we have it may include later additions and appears to be loosely organized, and its precise composition history is debated.

Paraphrase of themes in Cato, De Agricultura Cato tells the owner to inspect the property promptly on arrival and to check the work and the accounts. He directs the overseer to keep the enslaved workers occupied, to see that they are supplied with set amounts of food, wine, and clothing, and to give a smaller ration to those who do less demanding work. Among the advice on selling surplus goods, he lists items to be sold off, including worn-out oxen and, in the same list, aged or sick enslaved workers. This is a paraphrase of passages across the work, not a continuous quotation, and readers should consult a reliable translation for exact wording.

Analysis: This source reveals how a wealthy owner of the period thought about enslaved labor as a factor of production to be measured, supplied, and discarded when no longer useful. It shows that rations, clothing, and supervision were calculated as costs, that enslaved people were managed through an intermediary, and that a market-oriented enterprise on this model was recognized and advocated. It also shows a mixture of labor, since Cato refers to hired workers at peak times and to contracts. Its limits are equally important. The author was an owner writing to owners, and the text says nothing directly about what enslaved people experienced, how they responded, or what solidarities or resistance existed. It describes a normative model, not a survey of Italian farming, and it reflects the region and scale of its author's interests. The sources for the same period that do describe other farms and other labor arrangements are far fewer.

Historical Debate

Did Mediterranean Conquest Destroy the Roman Small Farmer?

The question: A traditional narrative, drawing heavily on ancient accounts of the later reformers examined in Chapter 19, runs as follows: prolonged military service ruined small farms; wealthy Romans bought or seized the land; large estates worked by enslaved laborers replaced peasant holdings; displaced farmers drifted to Rome; and the resulting poverty and land shortage precipitated political crisis. How much of this sequence do the sources support?

Interpretation A: Substantial peasant displacement occurred: Some historians, influential from the mid twentieth century onward, argued that the second century BCE saw large-scale displacement of smallholders, expansion of slave-worked estates, and a decline in the number of citizens who met the property qualification for military service. They relied on ancient narratives, on census figures, and on interpretations of recruitment problems in Spain in the second century. Scholars such as Peter Brunt and Keith Hopkins, in different ways, argued for major demographic and economic consequences from war and enslaved labor.
Interpretation B: The picture was more varied and the traditional chain overstated: Other historians, drawing on regional studies, archaeological surveys, and reassessments of the census and of military service, have argued that small and medium farms persisted widely, that estates using enslaved labor were concentrated in particular regions rather than everywhere, that many soldiers served in ways compatible with agricultural life, that Italian population may have grown rather than declined, and that ancient accounts of the peasantry's ruin were shaped by later political agendas. Nathan Rosenstein, among others, has argued that the demands of military service were more compatible with household farming than the traditional model assumed.

Evidence: The debate depends on several kinds of evidence. Transmitted census totals of adult male citizens are roughly stable in the mid second century and then higher in the 120s BCE, but scholars disagree about what the census counted, how complete it was, and how to interpret changes in registration and rating. Archaeological field surveys in regions such as Etruria and Campania show changing patterns of rural settlement, including growth of villa sites in some districts and continued small farms in others, but dating and interpretation are difficult. Ancient authors writing generations later, especially about the reformers, narrated the story of ruined farmers to explain political conflict, so their testimony is not neutral. Cato's manual documents one form of estate but does not measure its prevalence. Finally, the definition of latifundium and the scale of enslaved agricultural labor remain contested.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes the interpretation of the political crises of the later second century. If the traditional model is largely correct, agrarian distress becomes a powerful structural cause of political conflict. If it is heavily qualified, historians must give more weight to political decisions, elite competition, questions of Italian status, and the way ancient writers framed land and recruitment. The debate also warns against reading later events backward into earlier decades.

Student task: Identify which elements of the traditional chain are supported by evidence discussed in this chapter and which require qualification. Then explain what additional evidence, such as more precisely dated survey data or independent information about recruitment, would help resolve the disagreement.

The balanced conclusion is that the traditional story of conquest destroying all small farmers and creating vast slave-worked latifundia is too simple. Estates using enslaved labor grew, and elite landholding expanded, especially in favored regions, and these developments had real consequences for many rural families. But small farms did not disappear, rural society remained varied, and the relationship between military service, demography, and land was complex. These qualifications prepare for Chapter 19 without pre-empting it.

Section 18.9

Urban Rome: Work, Housing, Food, and Neighborhood Life

Rome in the second century BCE was the largest city in Italy and one of the largest in the Mediterranean. Its population is uncertain, and modern estimates for the late republic range widely, from several hundred thousand to perhaps a million at the extreme, with lower figures for the earlier second century. The city's growth depended heavily on migration from Italy and farther afield, the arrival of enslaved people, and manumission; the balance between urban births and deaths is uncertain, and high urban mortality may have meant that the city could not sustain itself without newcomers. The concentration of wealth from conquest, the presence of the Senate and magistrates, and the demands of government and trade all drew people toward it.

Work in the city was varied. Artisans made textiles, pottery, metalwork, and leather goods. Builders and laborers worked on temples, roads, basilicas, and aqueducts. In the second century, the Aqua Marcia, completed in the 140s BCE, added a major water source to the earlier Aqua Appia and Anio Vetus. Commerce concentrated around the Tiber port, where goods, foods, and people arrived, and around the markets and shops in the center. Bakers, butchers, tavern keepers, and cooks fed the city. Bankers and moneylenders operated near the Forum, and new public buildings, including the basilicas built in the early second century, provided space for courts and trade. Enslaved and freed workers, foreigners, and citizens all shared these occupations, and boundaries between them were not always sharp.

Housing separated the rich from the poor. Elite families lived in a domus with an atrium and rooms arranged around courtyards, with space to receive visitors and to display family images and trophies. The poor and even the moderately well-off lived in rented rooms in multistory buildings called insulae, often cheaply built, crowded, and vulnerable to fire and collapse. The dense, uneven character of the urban environment made sanitation and public health difficult. Water supply improved with aqueducts, but ordinary residents drew from fountains and wells. Streets were crowded and noisy, and the boundary between commercial and residential space was porous.

Food supply was a constant concern. Rome depended on grain from Sicily, Sardinia, and later Africa, transported by sea and river and provisioned through tax grain and purchase, and shortages produced political tension. Ordinary diet centered on grain products, especially bread and porridge, supplemented by legumes, vegetables, olive oil, wine, and occasional meat and fish. Elite dining, by contrast, could be elaborate, and the arrival of eastern foodstuffs and cooking styles was seized on by moralists as evidence of moral decay. A state distribution of subsidized grain to citizens was a later development, and it belongs to the political story of Chapter 19 and afterward, while the larger infrastructure of the imperial capital belongs to Chapter 22.

Public life included festivals and games. The annual festivals honoring gods, such as the Ludi Romani, provided chariot races, dramatic performances, and processions, and comedies by Plautus and Terence were performed at such festivals. Gladiatorial contests, first recorded at Rome in the middle of the third century BCE as part of aristocratic funerals, became more common as elite families and magistrates sponsored them for prestige. Crowds attending these events, mixing citizens of varying rank with freed and enslaved people, formed part of the public texture of the city and were also a means by which the elite communicated status and sought support.

Associations and cults organized neighborhood life. Neighborhood groups, occupational associations, and religious societies provided social connection, mutual support, and ritual. The Senate treated some of these with suspicion. In 186 BCE the Senate responded to reports of Bacchanalian societies by issuing a decree, which survives in a bronze inscription found in southern Italy, restricting these groups and instructing magistrates to investigate. The episode, described also by Livy in a long and dramatized narrative, shows how quickly a perceived threat to public order and morals could lead to state action, and that the Senate's authority extended into allied Italy. It also shows that ordinary people, women and men, free and freed, formed religious communities beyond the household and civic cults.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Roman Household and Neighborhood: Rich and Poor

Consider two households in mid second century Rome, drawn from patterns in the evidence rather than from particular named individuals. In the first, a senatorial family occupies a domus in a fashionable district. The household includes the paterfamilias, his wife, children, perhaps an elderly relative, and a large staff of enslaved and freed members: a doorkeeper, cooks, cleaners, secretaries, tutors, litter bearers, and attendants. Water is supplied by household arrangements, food is purchased in quantity and prepared in a kitchen by enslaved cooks, and the day begins with clients arriving to greet the head of the house. The household shrine receives offerings, and the family keeps portrait masks of ancestors for display at funerals. Clothing is made in part in the household, and its quality signals status.

In the second, a freed craftsman, his wife, and their two children rent rooms on an upper floor of an insula. The rooms are small, poorly lit, and heated only by braziers, and cooking often takes place at cook shops and taverns rather than at home. Water is carried from a public fountain. The family works in the craftsman's workshop on the street below, and the children help. They eat mainly bread, porridge, legumes, oil, and cheap wine, with meat on festival days. They keep a small household shrine, belong to a neighborhood association, and attend public games. Illness, fire, rent increases, or a failed contract could ruin them, and their status as freed persons and as tenants gives them little cushion. The difference in scale, comfort, and security between the two households was enormous, yet they lived within the same city and shared some of its festivals and streets.

Historians reconstruct such lives from archaeological remains, comedies, inscriptions, and later sources, and since the direct evidence for the second century is limited, they often supplement it with material from the late republic and early empire. That practice is useful but risky, and students should treat such composite pictures as reasoned approximations.

Section 18.10

Wealth, Patronage, and Public Display

Wealth in the Roman elite came from several sources. Land remained the most respectable, but the profits of office, provincial administration, war, and commerce increased with expansion. A commander could gain substantial booty, part of which went to the treasury and part to his soldiers and himself. Provincial governors and their staffs had opportunities for enrichment, and public contractors (publicani) built roads, supplied armies, and collected taxes under contracts let by the censors and later by other authorities. Senators were formally discouraged from certain commercial activities, and they typically operated through relatives, freedmen, and business partners. Wealth was then converted into prestige through public display.

Display took recognized forms. Elite families staged elaborate funerals, with processions of ancestral portrait masks, funeral orations praising the deceased, and, increasingly, games. Successful commanders celebrated triumphs and used spoils to build temples and porticoes, so that the city's landscape recorded aristocratic achievement. Magistrates sponsored public games, and families built or restored monuments. Houses were designed for reception and display, with atria where visitors waited and ancestral images were shown. Such expenditure was expected and competitive, and it was also regulated: sumptuary laws, such as the lex Fannia of 161 BCE limiting banquet expenditure, and the censors' authority to punish luxury, reflected elite worries that competitive display was getting out of hand. When Cato was censor in 184 BCE, according to Livy, he taxed luxuries, including enslaved people purchased above a high threshold, a measure that reveals both the prevalence of expensive purchases and the political appeal of denouncing them.

Patronage tied the social order together. A patron (patronus) offered protection, legal help, money, and influence to dependents, called clients (clientes), who offered attendance, public support, recommendations, respect, and services in return, and sometimes electoral support. The relation could involve poor citizens and rich ones, freedpeople and their former owners, communities and their advocates, and even provincial cities and Roman senators. A regular ritual was the morning greeting, the salutatio, in which clients visited their patron. Patronage should not be understood as a rigid pyramid in which each Roman had exactly one patron. Individuals could have several patrons and be patrons themselves, and relationships varied in intensity, formality, and duration. Collective patronage, in which communities or provincial peoples cultivated relations with influential Romans, became increasingly important as Rome's influence grew.

Patronage mattered politically, since a patron's following of visible supporters enhanced reputation and could bring some electoral support, though modern scholarship rejects the old model in which aristocrats simply controlled blocs of client votes and holds that patronage alone cannot explain Roman voting behavior. It mattered socially too, since it provided a mechanism for mutual support and dependence. It also offered ordinary people a channel for seeking assistance in a society without a comprehensive public welfare system. But it was an unequal relationship, and it made the poor dependent on the favor of the powerful. The combination of wealth, patronage, and public display gave aristocratic families enormous advantages in competition for office, which is one reason that the composition of the governing elite changed slowly even in an age of expansion.

Section 18.11

Citizenship and Belonging in an Expanding Republic

Roman citizenship was one of the most consequential features of the Roman system. Chapter 16 examined how Rome incorporated Italian communities through differentiated arrangements, and this section views the result from the perspective of those living under it. In the second century BCE, the population of Italy and of the Roman sphere was divided into several legal categories, each with its own benefits and burdens.

Historical Vocabulary
Civitas, Latin Status, and Socii

Civitas means citizenship, the status of belonging to the Roman citizen body, and also the community itself. A Roman citizen (civis Romanus) had legal standing, protection under Roman law, rights to marry and hold property under Roman rules, and, if male and adult, the right to vote in Roman assemblies and, subject to age, property, and levy requirements, the obligation and privilege of service in the legions. Latin status (often called Latin rights) was held by certain communities, including many colonies. It gave their citizens a bundle of rights shared with Romans, such as commerce and, in specified circumstances, marriage under Roman law (conubium), along with a route to Roman citizenship in defined circumstances, but without full Roman political rights. The exact package varied with context. The socii, or allies, were Italian communities bound to Rome by treaties that specified obligations, chiefly military service, while retaining local government and identity.

These distinctions mattered because they structured Rome's relation to Italy. Rome was not simply a state ruling subjects but a system in which membership was graded, and the terms of that grading carried both advantages and resentments. They also complicate the map: Roman power extended over much of the Mediterranean, but Roman citizenship remained a much smaller area.

Roman citizens. Citizens included the residents of Rome and its tribal territory, of citizen colonies, and of communities to which citizenship had been granted, sometimes without the right to vote. Citizenship conferred access to Roman courts and legal protections against arbitrary punishment by magistrates, particularly the right of appeal (provocatio) in cases affecting life, which developed over time. Male citizens voted in assemblies, could stand for office if they met qualifications, and served in the legions, which was both an obligation and a source of status and potential reward. Citizens participated in the census and were classified by property, which determined their military and voting position. Citizenship carried social prestige, especially in contrast to those outside the community, and it conferred rights in marriage and inheritance under Roman civil law in ways that were valuable.

Latins. The Latin communities, including many colonies founded by Rome, were legally distinct. Their inhabitants could conduct commerce and, in specified circumstances, marry under Roman law, and in some periods a Latin who migrated to Rome and met conditions could acquire Roman citizenship. This route generated tension. In 177 BCE, Livy reports, representatives of Latin communities complained to the Senate that many of their citizens had moved to Rome, leaving their towns depleted and their military contingents reduced, and the Senate ordered those registered improperly to return. The episode illustrates the attractiveness of Rome and the pressure on allied communities, and it is evidence for migration and for the way citizenship rules could be adapted to serve Roman and allied interests.

Italian allies. The socii supplied large numbers of troops, roughly comparable to those of Roman citizens in infantry and greater in cavalry, according to Polybius's account of the Roman army. They fought in Roman wars, contributed to Roman victories, and were subject to Roman command, but they lacked the vote at Rome, and their local institutions and status depended on their treaties. They could be rewarded with a share of booty, though sources suggest allies were sometimes given smaller shares than Roman citizens, and they might be subject to harsh treatment by Roman magistrates. Their situation illustrates a growing tension: Rome relied on people who bore heavy military obligations but did not share full political membership. Some allied elites benefited from close connections with Roman senators and prospered, and many communities were content with the arrangement. But over time the question of recognition became more pressing.

Provincials and others. Many provincial inhabitants were peregrini, free people who were not Roman citizens, living under the provincial framework discussed in Chapter 17. Provinces also contained Roman citizens living abroad and communities with differing privileged arrangements. Peregrini paid taxes or tribute, and were subject to the authority of governors, though many communities retained local law and institutions. Provincials could seek redress through Roman channels in some circumstances, but the system offered limited protection against abuse, and Chapter 17 noted the rise of extortion trials. Individuals could gain citizenship through service or through the favor of Roman leaders, but such cases were exceptions. Freedpeople of citizen owners, as Section 18.7 showed, became citizens with limited rights. Enslaved people were outside the citizen body altogether.

Citizenship also became increasingly desirable. It offered legal protection, social prestige, access to opportunities in Rome, and a share, however uneven, in the benefits of empire, including public land, colonies, and booty. It was also politically consequential, since the vote and the ability to hold office gave citizens influence over the decisions of a state that ruled the Mediterranean. Whether and how to extend citizenship became an increasingly contested question, but this chapter does not narrate the conflict that eventually resulted. The point for now is that citizenship was a valued and unevenly distributed status, and that those who bore the burdens of Roman power without its full membership had grounds for grievance.

Section 18.12

A Society under Strain, but Not Yet the Crisis

By the middle of the second century BCE, Roman society had changed substantially since the days of the Punic Wars. It was wealthier, and the wealth was unevenly distributed, with an elite that could finance lavish display and a large population of poor and precariously placed people. It was more dependent on enslaved labor, in households, workshops, mines, and on many estates. It was more mobile, since soldiers, traders, captives, freedpeople, and migrants moved across Italy and the Mediterranean. And it was more politically complicated, since Rome ruled a large empire, relied on allies who lacked full political membership, and faced questions about the use of public land and the burdens of military service.

These developments did not mechanically cause political collapse. Many of the same conditions coexisted with decades of stability, and the institutions that governed Rome retained considerable capacity for adjustment. Some of the changes benefited large numbers of people, and much rural society continued to function. The pressures were not uniform across regions or groups. And historians remain divided on the magnitude of demographic and economic change, as the debate in Section 18.8 showed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Roman Society before and after Mediterranean Conquest

What Changed

  • The scale and geographic reach of enslavement expanded, and enslaved labor became economically central in parts of Italy and Sicily
  • Elite wealth grew and public display became more lavish and competitive
  • Rome's population and heterogeneity increased through migration, enslavement, and manumission
  • Greek culture entered Roman elite life more deeply, provoking both adoption and moralizing resistance
  • The importance of the questions of Italian status, land, and military service grew as Rome's overseas commitments deepened

What Remained Continuous

  • The household remained the basic unit of property, authority, religion, and inheritance
  • Land remained the most prestigious form of wealth, and citizen farmers still supplied much of the army
  • Patronage and reputation structured relations among elites and between elites and dependents
  • Republican institutions, including magistracies, Senate, and assemblies, continued to operate, and family connection still shaped office holding
  • Older forms of slavery, dependency, and family authority persisted, though their context altered

Who experienced change most directly? Captives and enslaved people, Italian allies who bore military burdens, migrants and urban workers, and families in regions of estate expansion faced the most direct changes. Who experienced greater continuity? Many rural households in regions of small farms, and members of established elite families whose position was reinforced, experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that conquest transformed some parts of society profoundly without replacing the whole, and warns against a single narrative of decline or collapse.

Cause and Effect

Conquest and Social Change in Second Century Italy

Long-term causes

A society already organized around households, land, citizen military service, patronage, and slavery, and a political system that rewarded military success and elite competition.

Short-term causes

The Punic Wars and the campaigns in Spain, Macedonia, Greece, and North Africa, which produced captives, booty, tribute, provincial revenue, and new commercial opportunities.

Event or transformation

The expansion of enslaved labor, the growth of elite wealth and estates in some regions, the growth of Rome, and increased migration and manumission.

Immediate effects

Greater inequality in wealth, new patterns of rural labor, urban crowding and food-supply concern, moral debates over luxury and gender, and increased reliance on allied contingents.

Long-term consequences

Questions about land, military service, citizenship, and elite competition that later political conflicts would bring to the surface, examined in Chapter 19.

Historical complexity

This was not a single deterministic chain leading inevitably to republican collapse. Regional variation, political decisions, institutional resilience, contingency, and the way ancient sources framed events all shaped outcomes.

Historical complexity: Social pressure becomes political crisis only through decisions made by particular people, in particular institutions, in specific circumstances. The same social conditions could have been managed differently.

The proper handoff to what follows is therefore a set of questions, not a story of decline. Roman expansion created a society richer, more unequal, more dependent on enslaved labor, more geographically mobile, and more politically complicated than the Republic of earlier centuries. Those changes raised questions about land, military service, citizenship, wealth, and elite competition that Roman politics increasingly struggled to resolve. Chapter 19 examines what happened when those questions became political conflicts.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the society of the Roman Republic after Mediterranean expansion, asking what determined a person's place in that society and how conquest reshaped households, labor, wealth, mobility, and citizenship without erasing older institutions. Its central argument is that Roman status was multidimensional. It depended on combinations of free or enslaved condition, citizenship, birth or manumission, wealth, sex, age, household position, patronage, and local legal community, and no single opposition, whether patrician and plebeian or rich and poor, captures it.

The chapter began by placing Roman society after the conquests described in Chapter 17. Conquest brought wealth, captives, tribute, commercial opportunity, and contact with Greek and Hellenistic culture, and it moved people, whether soldiers, traders, migrants, or enslaved captives. But its effects were uneven across regions and groups, and the chapter avoided treating them as a single line leading to later crisis. It also emphasized the problem of evidence, noting that legal texts, inscriptions, archaeology, comedy, and prescriptive writing all reflect particular perspectives and that later evidence, including much from Pompeii, must not be projected backward uncritically.

The chapter then examined social hierarchy. The senatorial elite, and within it the nobiles, dominated politics and enjoyed the greatest prestige, while equestrians grew in importance as contractors and financiers. Citizens ranged from prosperous landowners to the urban poor, and beneath and beside them stood freedpeople, free noncitizens, and enslaved people. The old patrician and plebeian distinction no longer explained social position, and legal status and wealth overlapped without coinciding.

The household was presented as the fundamental building block. Familia referred to persons and property under household authority, including enslaved people, while domus designated the house and family as social entity. The paterfamilias held extensive legal authority, but the chapter distinguished the legal formulation from social expectation and actual practice, and it emphasized the flexibility of devices such as the peculium. Household religion, inheritance, and networks of kin and dependents made the household an economic, religious, and political actor.

Marriage, women, and gender were examined with attention to both legal rule and lived variation. Marriage sine manu became prevalent, dowries connected marriage to family strategy, and divorce did not require formal court action. Women could own and manage property, though they lacked political rights and faced guardianship and restrictions such as the lex Voconia. Evidence for women's lives comes mainly from elite men's writings and commemorative texts, and the chapter stressed differences by class and status. Childhood, education, and the life course likewise varied with wealth and sex, with high mortality shaping family life and most children learning through work.

Slavery received major attention. Slavery was not new, but conquest and commerce increased its scale, geographic reach, and economic importance in Italy and Sicily. Enslaved people came from war, piracy, trade, and birth, and they worked in households, agriculture, workshops, mines, and services under widely varying conditions, all defined by legal unfreedom and coercive ownership. Ancient numbers of captives cannot be added into precise statistics, and modern estimates of the enslaved population vary widely. Enslaved people resisted in many ways, including armed revolt, but their own voices are almost entirely absent from the sources, a limitation that the chapter acknowledged plainly.

Manumission distinguished Roman slavery from many ancient systems, since formerly enslaved people could become citizens. Freedpeople remained bound to patrons by obligation and faced political and social limits, but they could marry, own property, and prosper, and their children were freeborn. The chapter used the case of rural Italy to show the danger of oversimplification. The traditional narrative in which conquest destroyed the small farmer and created vast slave-worked latifundia has been substantially complicated by archaeology, reassessments of the census, and studies of military service, and the Historical Debate showed which parts of the older account remain defensible and which need qualification. Cato's manual documented one model of estate management, while also demonstrating the limits of an owner's perspective.

Urban Rome was described as a growing, diverse city with sharp contrasts between elite domus and crowded insulae, dependence on imported grain, varied trades, festivals, games, and neighborhood associations. The Bacchanalian affair of 186 BCE illustrated the state's concern with unsanctioned associations and its reach into Italy. Wealth, patronage, and public display were shown to structure elite competition, and patronage was described as a network of relationships, not a rigid pyramid.

Finally, the chapter treated citizenship from the perspective of those living under it. Roman citizens, Latins, Italian allies, provincials, freedpeople, and enslaved people occupied different positions, with different rights and burdens. Roman power extended much further than Roman citizenship, and Rome depended increasingly on Italians who fought alongside citizens without sharing full political membership. The chapter closed by noting that expansion created a richer, more unequal, more mobile, and more complicated society, raising questions about land, service, citizenship, wealth, and elite competition that Chapter 19 examines when they became political conflicts. Nothing in this chapter makes that outcome inevitable.

This project asks you to reconstruct, cautiously, the composition and daily life of a Roman household of a chosen status, using different types of evidence and being explicit about the limits of each.

  1. Choose a household type. Select one: a senatorial family in Rome, a prosperous farmer with a small estate in Latium or Campania, a freed craftsman's household in the city, or an estate managed by a vilicus.
  2. Gather evidence. Using library resources, identify at least four kinds of evidence relevant to your household type, such as translated passages from Cato or Polybius, archaeological plans of houses or farms, inscriptions, comedy, and modern scholarly studies.
  3. Date your evidence. For each item, note whether it dates from the second century BCE or from a later period, and explain how that affects your confidence in using it.
  4. Reconstruct the household. Describe its likely members, legal statuses, work, property, religious practice, and relationships to patrons, clients, or neighbors, distinguishing what the evidence establishes from what you infer.
  5. Identify silences. Explain whose experience your sources do not represent, especially that of women, children, and enslaved people, and suggest what further evidence might help.
  6. Write your findings. In a 4 to 6 page essay, or an equivalent multimedia presentation with a labeled plan or diagram, present your reconstruction, using cautious language such as "the evidence suggests" or "historians debate" where appropriate.

Library resources to use: translated ancient sources, archaeological reports and atlases, reference works on Roman law and society, and the MGU History Library's materials on Roman social history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that empire is not only a matter of armies, treaties, and provinces but also of households, farms, workshops, and streets. The conquests examined in Chapters 16 and 17 were experienced by people as soldiers, captives, contractors, farmers, migrants, mothers, and neighbors, and the society in which they lived was transformed unevenly by the resources and pressures conquest created. Understanding Roman society in this period requires holding together the stability of household, land, and patronage with the growth of slavery, wealth, urban life, and demands connected to citizenship.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs throughout the textbook: political power extending faster than political membership. Rome ruled and mobilized far more people than it admitted to citizenship, and the distinctions among citizens, Latins, allies, provincials, freedpeople, and enslaved people were not marginal details but central features of the system. Later European empires would also face the problem of governing and drawing on populations whose legal standing differed from that of the ruling community, and the comparison should be made with care, since each context differed. Chapter 142 returns to the long history of empire, migration, and belonging.

The chapter shaped what follows in several ways. The questions of land, military service, citizenship, wealth, and elite competition that it identifies form the background to the political conflicts of Chapter 19, and the enlarged role of enslaved labor and Mediterranean commerce continued into the imperial period examined in Chapter 22. The household and patronage structures described here persisted into the empire, as did the practice of manumission and the social category of the freedperson. For the argument of Volume I, the chapter shows that the Roman world was not simply a set of institutions but a society of unequal, interdependent people, and that its later transformations grew from conditions that Romans of the second century could only partly see.

Above all, the chapter models a method. It asks students to distinguish legal rule from social expectation and practice, to notice whose voices survive and whose do not, to treat later evidence and later narratives with caution, and to resist explanations that turn a complex social order into a single chain of cause and effect. Those habits will be needed in Chapter 19, where the political crisis of the Republic must be explained without reducing it either to the ruin of the peasantry or to the ambition of a few politicians.
