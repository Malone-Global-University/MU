Norman England and the Growth of Medieval Monarchies

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 1085 to 1086, royal commissioners travelled county by county through England asking who held each piece of land, who had held it in the days of King Edward, what it was worth, and what it could pay. The scale of the inquiry astonished contemporaries. Its results show a kingdom conquered by a foreign aristocracy, yet governed through institutions that the conquerors had inherited as much as imposed.

According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, William the Conqueror held his Christmas court at Gloucester in 1085 and, after deep discussion with his counsellors, ordered a survey of the whole kingdom. The record that eventually emerged, known since the twelfth century as Domesday Book, listed landholders, estates, ploughs, tenants, mills, meadows, and values across most of England. The Chronicle's account of the survey complained that the inquiry was so thorough that not an ox or cow or pig escaped being written down. Whatever William's precise motives, the survey presupposes a kingdom in which the king could expect shire courts to assemble, local juries to answer under oath, clerks to write, and a body of tenants to be held accountable for what they held. Such a government did not appear in 1066. It was a conquered kingdom that already possessed an unusually developed administrative inheritance.

This chapter examines two linked developments. The first is the Norman Conquest of England, which rapidly displaced most of the old secular aristocracy and, over William's reign, progressively replaced much of the higher ecclesiastical leadership with continental newcomers, bound the kingdom to Normandy, and transformed landholding, castle building, church leadership, and the languages of power. The second is the growth of royal administration, finance, and justice across the twelfth century, especially under Henry I and Henry II, and the political limits that this growth encountered, which reached their most famous expression in Magna Carta in 1215. The chapter's central argument is that Norman kings built on Anglo-Saxon institutions of taxation, local government, written command, and royal justice, combined them with conquest, castles, and aristocratic lordship, and then extended them to a point where stronger monarchy provoked bargaining, resistance, and constitutional definition as well as obedience.

The plural in the chapter title matters. England is the best documented case of medieval governmental development, but it was not the template for Europe. The Capetian kings of France, who began with a modest royal domain around Paris and Orléans, built stronger royal authority by a different route, expanding territory and then administration. The chapter therefore closes by comparing England and France, and prepares Chapter 46, where the Holy Roman Empire provides a third and sharply contrasting pattern. Stronger monarchy in the High Middle Ages was uneven, negotiated, and reversible, and it was never the inevitable ancestor of the modern state, a theme developed in later volumes and revisited in Chapter 141.

The chapter also avoids two familiar simplifications. The first treats 1066 as the birth of England, as if an unformed society were organized for the first time by Norman efficiency. The second treats the Conquest as a transfer of crown from one dynasty to another with little social consequence. The evidence supports neither. Late Anglo-Saxon England had an effective land tax, shires, hundreds, sheriffs, royal coinage, and a vernacular written administration. Yet the Conquest also destroyed an English landed aristocracy within a generation, imposed castles and new lords on a resentful population, devastated parts of the north, and tied England's elite for generations to estates and loyalties across the Channel.

Historians reconstruct this period from an unusual combination of evidence. Narrative sources include the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, written in English by monastic annalists, the Norman writers William of Jumièges and William of Poitiers, who wrote partly to justify the Conquest, and the Anglo-Norman monk Orderic Vitalis, who had an English mother and a Norman father and wrote a generation later. The Bayeux Tapestry, an embroidery most likely made in England soon after 1066, offers a visual account from the victors' circle that is not wholly uncritical. Documentary evidence is far richer than for most of contemporary Europe: Domesday Book, royal writs and charters, the Pipe Rolls recording annual accounts, and treatises such as the Dialogue of the Exchequer. These records were produced by government for government, so they illuminate what rulers wished to know and collect far better than they illuminate the experiences of those they governed.

The chapter belongs to Part VII, which examines the expansion of Europe in the eleventh to thirteenth centuries. It follows Chapter 44's treatment of church reform and the Investiture Conflict, and it rests on Chapter 34's account of lordship and the debates over "feudalism," which are applied here to a particular conquest rather than retaught. It also stops short of Chapter 46's Empire, Chapter 47's crusading movement, and Chapter 51's Hundred Years' War. Within Volume II, its significance lies in showing how Christendom's kingdoms, as they grew wealthier and more populous, found new ways to tax, judge, record, and bargain.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the institutions of late Anglo-Saxon England that Norman kings inherited, and explain why they complicate the idea that the Conquest created English government.
  2. Analyze the competing claims to the English crown in 1066 without assuming that either William or Harold was straightforwardly the legitimate heir.
  3. Trace the campaigns of 1066 and explain why victory at Hastings did not immediately secure the kingdom.
  4. Explain how conquest, confiscation, castle building, and church appointments transformed the English aristocracy and landholding.
  5. Evaluate Domesday Book as both an instrument of government and a historical source with strengths and limits.
  6. Compare the governments of William I, Henry I, Stephen, and Henry II, and explain how civil war in the Anarchy revealed the limits of royal power.
  7. Assess the development of royal justice and the emergence of the common law without attributing it to a single founder.
  8. Explain how the Exchequer, the Pipe Rolls, sheriffs, scutage, and other revenues connected taxation to warfare and administration.
  9. Interpret the conflicts among kings, bishops, and barons, including the Becket affair and the revolt of 1173 to 1174, as evidence of both royal strength and its limits.
  10. Contextualize Magna Carta in the crisis of John's reign and distinguish its medieval purposes from its later constitutional reputation.
  11. Compare the English and Capetian paths toward stronger monarchy and explain why medieval state formation was neither uniform nor inevitable.
  12. Identify the Jewish communities, English speakers, and borderland populations whose experiences were shaped by Norman and Angevin rule.

Historical Context

By the middle of the eleventh century, western Europe was entering the period of growth examined in this Part. Chapter 42 traced the clearing of land and rising population, and Chapter 43 the revival of towns and trade. Kingdoms and principalities in the former Carolingian world had fragmented into territorial lordships, as Chapters 33 and 34 explained, and rulers who wished to command resources often did so by dealing with local lords rather than by governing directly. England and Normandy illustrate two different outcomes of that fragmentation. Normandy was a duchy created by Scandinavian settlement in the early tenth century, as Chapter 32 described, whose rulers had by the 1060s become formidable regional princes with a loyal aristocracy, a reformed church, and a capacity for aggressive military enterprise. England was a kingdom formed through the West Saxon conquest of the Danelaw and the unification of English peoples in the tenth century, as Chapter 33 discussed, and it had then endured further Danish conquest and a Danish king, Cnut, from 1016 to 1035.

The two societies were more closely connected than the later conflict suggests. Emma of Normandy, wife of both Æthelred II and Cnut, tied the English royal house to Normandy; Edward the Confessor, her son by Æthelred, spent his exile in Normandy before returning to rule England from 1042. Edward's court included Norman clerics and favorites, and by his death some Normans held lands and offices in England. Both societies were Christian, Latin in the language of the church, and part of the wider reform movement examined in Chapter 44. Their differences lay less in religion than in the nature of lordship, the structure of the aristocracy, and the machinery of rule.

Geography shaped the story, as Chapter 2 would lead one to expect. The English Channel, narrow enough to cross in a day with a favorable wind, was a highway as much as a barrier, binding the English coast to Normandy, Flanders, and Scandinavia. It also meant that an invading force needed to cross water, gather ships, and wait for winds, a vulnerability that gave Harold's navy and the weather real importance in 1066. England's rivers and Roman roads supported rapid movement of armies and royal officers, while its lowland south and east were comparatively wealthy and easy to administer. The north, upland and thinly governed, remained harder to command, a fact that explains much about the events of 1069 and 1070.

The kingdoms of Britain were not a single political space. Wales consisted of several native principalities. Scotland, ruled by its own dynasty of kings, was an independent kingdom whose rulers often had close dealings with England. Ireland was a patchwork of kingdoms and lordships. Norman and later Angevin expansion would touch all three, producing marcher lordships in Wales, a Normanized Scottish aristocracy under David I (reigned 1124 to 1153), and the first English intervention in Ireland in 1169 to 1172, though this chapter treats these only where they illuminate English monarchy. "England" and "Britain" are not interchangeable, and the term "English" in 1066 referred to a people with several regional identities, not a modern nation.

Finally, students should note the problem of terminology. Historians speak of "Anglo-Saxon" England, though contemporaries more often used "English," and the label can imply a more uniform society than existed. "Norman" is a regional label, though many conquerors came from Brittany, Flanders, Maine, and elsewhere in northern France. "Angevin," from Anjou, refers to the dynasty of Geoffrey of Anjou and Henry II. "Angevin Empire" is a modern term for Henry II's scattered dominions, not a name contemporaries used for a single state, and it should be handled with caution. "Feudalism," discussed in Chapter 34, is similarly a retrospective construct, and this chapter uses more specific terms such as tenant-in-chief, knight service, and honor wherever possible.

Section 45.1

England before 1066: An Unusually Strong Kingdom

The kingdom that William conquered was one of the most governed societies in Latin Christendom. Its territory was divided into shires, each with a royal official, the sheriff, who collected revenues, presided over the shire court, and led local forces. Shires were subdivided into hundreds, with courts that met regularly to settle disputes and to enforce royal commands. These units had been consolidated through the tenth century as West Saxon kings extended their authority over Mercia, East Anglia, and the north, and the system, though it varied by region, gave the crown a means of reaching ordinary landholders. In the Danelaw, the area of heavy Scandinavian settlement, the equivalent districts were called wapentakes and a different terminology persisted, a reminder that uniformity was limited.

Royal power also relied on written command. Anglo-Saxon kings issued sealed writs in English, short written orders addressed to shire courts, bishops, and local officials, which conveyed royal decisions about land, privilege, and justice. The existence of a royal chancery-like writing office, though small, was unusual in the eleventh-century West, and it is one reason the Norman kings could rule at a distance. Coinage was a second instrument. The English crown controlled a currency that was regularly renewed and minted at numerous towns under royal supervision, which gave the king both revenue and a measure of the kingdom's wealth.

The third pillar was taxation. The geld, a land tax assessed on hides, the units by which land was rated for taxation, was raised first to buy off Scandinavian attackers from the 990s and then, under Cnut, as a regular payment to support a standing fleet. Edward the Confessor abolished the heregeld, the army tax, in 1051, but the capacity to assess and collect land tax survived, and the Normans made use of it. Few other western kingdoms could tax their landholders so broadly or keep records of assessments across so many districts. That fiscal capacity was bound up with defensive needs: the late Anglo-Saxon state was shaped by warfare against Vikings and by the burghal system of fortified towns, and it learned to mobilize resources on a large scale.

Yet English kingship was far from absolute. The king governed with the advice of a council of leading men, the witan, and his rule depended on earls who controlled vast regions. In the 1060s the greatest of them, the Godwin family, held earldoms covering most of England, and Harold, as earl of Wessex, was in practice the king's chief minister. The aristocracy was powerful, regional loyalties were strong, and succession to the crown was decided by a combination of royal designation, kinship, election by leading men, and consecration by the church. This mixture meant that a childless king left a political vacuum with no fixed rule to fill it. England's strength and its vulnerability were thus the same fact: a rich, taxable, administered kingdom whose crown could be taken by whoever won the support of its elites.

Students should be careful not to romanticize this system. The evidence for Anglo-Saxon government is uneven, drawn largely from monastic archives, charters, law codes, and a few surveys. Royal power was exercised differently in Wessex and in Northumbria, and in many districts local lords held jurisdiction that the crown only partially controlled. What can be said confidently is that the Normans did not need to invent the idea that the king might send a writ to a shire court, that landholders owed the king a tax, or that the sheriff was a royal agent. They needed to capture these tools and turn them to their own ends.

Section 45.2

The Succession Crisis of 1066

Edward the Confessor died on 5 January 1066 without a child. The next day Harold Godwinson was crowned in Westminster Abbey. Harold's claim rested on several grounds: the support of the leading men, the likely, though not certain, deathbed designation reported by English sources, and his position as the greatest nobleman in the kingdom. Harold's kingship, however, was resisted from the start in the north, where the earldom of Northumbria had been disrupted in 1065 by a revolt against Harold's brother Tostig, who was exiled and nursed a grievance against his brother.

William of Normandy's claim is known chiefly from Norman writers, who had an interest in justifying it. They state that Edward had promised him the throne, perhaps during William's contact with the English court, and that Harold, shipwrecked on the Norman coast around 1064 to 1065, swore an oath to support William. The Bayeux Tapestry presents this oath as a central episode. Historians treat these stories with care. The oath may have occurred, but its circumstances, content, and binding force are contested, and Edward's alleged promise rests on sources written after the event by authors who had already decided the outcome was divine judgment. William, who was related to Edward only distantly, through the king's mother Emma, had no strong hereditary claim, and Norman writers laid weight on designation and oath rather than blood.

A third claimant, Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, based his claim on an alleged agreement between earlier kings, Magnus of Norway and Harthacnut, about mutual succession. Hardrada had the support of Tostig and a large fleet. A fourth, Edgar Ætheling, a grandson of Edmund Ironside and the last male of the West Saxon line, was a teenager living in England whose kinship was strong but whose political backing was weak. Each claim reflected a different idea of legitimacy: blood, designation, election, oath, and conquest. The question of who was the "rightful" king is therefore not something the evidence permits historians to answer simply. Contemporaries answered according to their loyalties, and ultimately victory in arms and consecration decided the question.

The papacy's role also deserves notice. William of Poitiers, the sole contemporary authority for the claim, states that Pope Alexander II supported William's cause and sent a banner, which fits the Norman duke's reformist alliance with the church, as Chapter 44 described. Recent scholarship has challenged how confidently this report should be accepted, so it is best treated as disputed. In any case William's cause was certainly presented as righteous, and Harold's earlier dealings with Archbishop Stigand, who held his office irregularly, gave the Normans propaganda material. Medieval conquest needed legitimating language, and William used the idiom of lawful succession, divine judgment, and church reform.

Section 45.3

Stamford Bridge, Hastings, and the Norman Conquest

Harold spent the spring and summer of 1066 guarding the south coast against an expected Norman landing, but in early September he disbanded his forces as the harvest and the season of campaigning ended. Almost immediately Hardrada and Tostig landed in the north, defeated the earls Edwin and Morcar at Fulford near York on 20 September, and received York's submission. Harold marched north with remarkable speed, surprised the invaders, and destroyed them at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, where both Hardrada and Tostig died. It was a major victory. It also pulled Harold's best troops far from the south, just as the winds shifted and William landed at Pevensey on 28 September.

William's invasion was a large and complicated operation. He had gathered ships and a mixed army of Normans, Bretons, Flemings, and others over the summer, relying on promises of land and plunder to hold the force together. He established a base at Hastings and began ravaging the surrounding countryside, a deliberate effort to provoke Harold into battle. Harold hurried south, gathered what forces he could, and met William near Hastings on 14 October at a site later called Battle. The English fought on foot in a shield wall on high ground. The Normans used cavalry, archers, and infantry. The battle lasted most of the day, and sources disagree on its details, including whether feigned retreats by Norman cavalry broke the line, but Harold was killed, along with two of his brothers, and the English army collapsed.

Hastings won a battle but not a kingdom. London and the English leadership did not immediately submit; Edgar Ætheling was briefly proclaimed king. William advanced through Kent, burned Southwark, circled around London, and received submissions from leading figures including Archbishop Ealdred and the earls Edwin and Morcar. He was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066 by Ealdred, in a ceremony that deliberately imitated English royal consecration. He retained the apparatus of English kingship, confirmed some English laws and customs, and issued writs in the traditional form, presenting himself as Edward's lawful successor and not merely as a conqueror.

The conquest itself took years. William returned to Normandy in early 1067 and left Odo of Bayeux and William fitz Osbern as regents, who built castles and suppressed disorder. Revolts followed: at Exeter in 1068, in Mercia and the north, and in 1069 when a large Danish fleet joined northern rebels and York's new Norman garrison was destroyed. William responded with fierce force. In the winter of 1069 to 1070 he devastated much of Yorkshire and the surrounding region, an episode traditionally called the Harrying of the North. Domesday Book recorded many northern estates as waste, and later chroniclers, notably Orderic Vitalis, condemned the slaughter and famine. Scholars debate how far "waste" in Domesday reflects deliberate destruction, though most accept that the campaign caused serious and lasting damage. Resistance continued, notably under Hereward at Ely in 1070 to 1071, but by about 1071 William's control was secure.

The speed of the conquest owed much to circumstance. The death of Harold and his brothers removed a generation of English leadership, the earls were divided, and the English had no obvious replacement for a king who could unite regional elites. The Normans had advantages in cavalry and castles, and they fought a war of conquest that included terror as policy. The fate of English society was shaped as much by the long process of occupation as by the single battle that gave the Conquest its name.

Section 45.4

Conquest, Resistance, and the Transformation of the Aristocracy

The most direct consequence of the Conquest was the replacement of the English landed elite. William treated revolt as forfeiture. Lands of those who had died at Hastings or rebelled were confiscated and redistributed to his followers, who held them as tenants-in-chief, directly from the crown, in return for providing a stated number of knights for the king's army. By the time of Domesday Book in 1086, very few Englishmen held land directly from the king in substantial amounts, and the great estates of the former English aristocracy had passed to a few hundred Norman, Breton, and Flemish lords, some of whom received compact blocks of territory, such as the great lordships on the Welsh and Scottish borders, and others scattered holdings across many counties. Modern estimates suggest that lay tenants-in-chief held roughly half the kingdom by value, with the crown and church holding most of the rest, though precise percentages are uncertain.

These tenants-in-chief in turn granted land to subtenants, many of whom were their own followers from the continent. The result was a layered structure of lordship, but one with notable features. Because the crown granted lands by conquest and confiscation, it retained a strong claim to supervise succession, marriage, and service. Because estates were often scattered, great lords found it hard to build compact regional powers of the sort that the Godwins had held. William also avoided creating earldoms of the earlier scale, except on dangerous frontiers like Chester and Shrewsbury. The king's control of the distribution of lands gave him unusual power over the aristocracy, and the Domesday survey would later confirm who held what, and from whom. Chapter 34 described lordship as a general system; here its specific consequences were a conquering king's capacity to design the landholding structure of an entire kingdom.

Castles were the visible instrument of this change. Before 1066 England had few castles in the continental sense, and the Normans built them rapidly: earth and timber mottes with baileys in the first generation, soon followed by stone keeps such as the White Tower in London. Castles protected garrisons, housed local lords, controlled towns and roads, and symbolized lordship. Many were built in towns, where houses were sometimes cleared for them. In places like Lincoln and Norwich, urban populations paid a price in demolished homes and new obligations. Castle building also required forced labor from the surrounding population, an everyday burden that the sources record unevenly.

The church was transformed alongside the lay elite. William's relationship with the papacy supported church reform, and under Archbishop Lanfranc (archbishop from 1070) English bishoprics and abbacies increasingly passed to Normans and other continental clerics. Stigand, the archbishop of Canterbury, was deposed in 1070, and by William's death only one English bishop, Wulfstan of Worcester, remained. Cathedral sees were moved from villages to towns, and monasteries rebuilt in the Norman Romanesque style on a grand scale. William also ordered that ecclesiastical pleas formerly heard in hundred courts be heard under episcopal jurisdiction, an important step toward a more distinct system of church courts, with long-term importance for the jurisdictional boundary between church and king. Bishops and abbots also owed knight service for their lands, so the church became a pillar of the king's military and administrative system, a point that connects directly with the tensions of Chapter 44.

Language and culture changed in less uniform ways. Anglo-Norman French became the language of the new elite, and Latin gradually replaced English as the language of royal writs in the generation after the Conquest, while English survived as the speech of most of the population and persisted in some monastic chronicles, such as the Peterborough continuation of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle into the 1150s. English, though marginal in written government, was not extinguished, and by the thirteenth century a recognizably Middle English was reappearing in literature. Names also shifted: William, Robert, and Henry replaced older English names among the elite. Intermarriage between conquerors and conquered occurred, especially after the first generation, and distinctions between "English" and "Norman" among the free population weakened over the twelfth century.

The Conquest also changed the position of Jewish communities. According to later tradition, William invited Jewish merchants and financiers from Rouen to settle in England, and substantial Jewish communities are evidenced in English towns from the early twelfth century. They were under direct royal protection and, over time, royal exploitation, as Section 45.10 explains. Jews appear in English sources as moneylenders, merchants, and scholars, and their presence reminds students that Norman England was not simply a Christian society divided between two ethnic groups.

Historical Vocabulary
Writ

A writ was a short sealed letter from the king, addressed to an official or court, ordering something to be done. Anglo-Saxon kings issued writs in English; Norman kings continued the practice in Latin. Early writs conveyed decisions about land, church privilege, or local justice. By the reign of Henry II, standardized writs such as the writ of right and writs initiating possessory assizes formed the entry point into royal courts, and the forms of writ available defined what kinds of legal claims could be heard. The writ matters in this chapter because it was the basic medium by which royal will reached the localities and, later, the origin of the "forms of action" in the common law. Students should not confuse it with a modern court order, since many early writs were instructions to local officials and not judicial decisions.

Historical Methods
Reading Administrative Records

Norman and Angevin England produced an unusual quantity of administrative documents: writs and charters, Domesday Book, the Pipe Rolls (annual accounts of sheriffs and other officials, with a surviving roll of 1130 and a nearly continuous series from the 1150s), law treatises such as the Dialogue of the Exchequer and the book attributed to Glanvill, and the plea rolls that begin around 1194. These records are invaluable because they reveal sums paid, lands held, cases heard, and procedures followed, often for ordinary people who appear in no chronicle.

They require careful reading. Each was made by the government for its own use and therefore records what officials wished to know: taxable value, not total wealth; legal claims that reached royal courts, not all disputes; debts owed to the crown, not the many arrangements outside it. Survival is uneven, since records were kept by institutions that survived and lost where they did not. Entries are in Latin formulae and abbreviations that conceal local speech and custom. The historian's task is to use the records as evidence of administrative practice and as indirect evidence of social life, while recognizing that the world they depict was shaped by the questions the government chose to ask.

Section 45.5

Governing a Conquered Kingdom: Domesday and Royal Information

The Domesday survey, conducted in 1086 after the Gloucester council of Christmas 1085, was the culmination of William's effort to master his kingdom. Commissioners, called legati, traveled in circuits, and in each shire a sworn jury of local men, both English and Norman, answered questions about every manor: its holder in the time of King Edward, its present holder, the number of hides it was rated at, ploughs, tenants of different statuses, mills, meadow, woodland, and its value then and now. A summary of the results appears in two volumes. "Great Domesday" covers most of England in a condensed, regular form, while "Little Domesday" preserves a fuller version for Essex, Norfolk, and Suffolk. Northern counties such as Northumberland and Durham, along with London and Winchester, are largely missing. At Salisbury in August 1086 William also received oaths of loyalty from landholders, including the subtenants of his tenants-in-chief. The oath underscored direct loyalty to the king even among men who held through other lords, although historians debate whether Salisbury represented a constitutional innovation or primarily a practical assurance of loyalty during the crisis of 1086.

Historians debate the purpose of the survey. It plainly served fiscal ends: it clarified who should pay the geld and what the royal estates were worth, and it aided a king who in 1085 faced a threatened Danish invasion and needed to feed and pay troops. It also functioned as a record of tenure and a tool for resolving disputes, since the Conquest had produced thousands of conflicting claims and many lords had seized more than they were entitled to. Possibly it was both a political demonstration and a practical inquiry. The comparison of "then" and "now" values reveals William's interest in how his kingdom had been affected by conquest and settlement, and the inquiry into tenure implied that every acre was held ultimately of the crown.

What Domesday reveals about the new England is striking. Roughly one fifth of the land by value was held by the crown, a quarter or so by the church, and the remainder by lay tenants-in-chief, all figures that depend on how historians treat ambiguous entries. It records a society of villagers of different statuses, with a large proportion of unfree or partly free peasants and a smaller group of slaves, a category that was declining and would soon vanish from the records. It documents about a hundred boroughs and many mills, and it depicts a landscape of fields, woodland, and meadow more densely settled in the south and east than the north and west. Population estimates drawn from it vary, with many scholars suggesting a total near two million, but such figures rest on assumptions about household size and omitted groups.

Domesday's limitations are as important as its uses. It omits the north, parts of the west, and many towns; it counts only some classes of people, generally heads of households; its formulae vary by circuit, and its measurements of "value" reflect assessments that may not equal market worth. It records what the crown wished to know and sheds little light on women, children, the very poor, or, indirectly, the life of English speakers whose names appear in Latinized forms. The primary source window that follows treats it as evidence of royal government rather than as a transparent snapshot of society. Its survival, and its later use as an authority on landholding, is part of its importance: after 1086, "the Domesday of the king" became a final reference for tenure, a role that gave it a long afterlife.

Section 45.6

Norman Kingship after William the Conqueror

William died in September 1087 near Rouen. In accordance with Norman custom, which treated inherited family land and newly won land as separable, he left Normandy to his eldest son Robert Curthose, England to his second surviving son William Rufus, and money to the youngest, Henry. The division split a cross-Channel aristocracy whose members held lands in both territories and had to choose between brothers. Rufus (reigned 1087 to 1100) survived an early rebellion, extracted heavy financial exactions, and quarreled with Archbishop Anselm, a conflict that Chapter 44 examined within the wider Investiture Conflict. Chroniclers, mostly clergymen, depict Rufus harshly, so their testimony needs caution, but his government was effective in raising money, partly through the ministerial talents of Ranulf Flambard.

Rufus died in a hunting accident in August 1100, and Henry, who was nearby, seized the treasury at Winchester and was crowned within days. In his coronation charter, the Charter of Liberties, he promised to end the abuses of his brother's reign, to limit the reliefs charged to heirs, to protect widows and daughters in marriage, and to restore the laws of King Edward with the improvements made by William. The charter is an early example of a king binding himself by written promise and a precedent to which the barons of 1215 would later refer. Henry then married Edith, renamed Matilda, whose mother was of the Old English royal line, a politically calculated union that gave his dynasty a link to the English past. In 1106 at Tinchebrai in Normandy he defeated and captured Robert Curthose, reuniting the duchy and the kingdom.

Henry I's reign was an era of steady institutional development. He spent much of his time in Normandy and so needed trusted officials to run England. He relied heavily on Roger, bishop of Salisbury, as chief minister, and on "new men" promoted from modest origins, who owed their positions to the king. The royal household, including its chamber, chancery, and treasury, evolved into a more regular system. Sheriffs were more closely supervised, itinerant royal justices began to travel, and the earliest surviving Pipe Roll, of 1130, shows that royal finance was already audited on a regular schedule. Henry also advanced the interests of the crown against local lords, supervised marriages and inheritances, and kept firm control of the castles. His church policy, a compromise settled with Anselm in 1107, preserved royal influence over episcopal appointments in return for the formal renunciation of lay investiture, an outcome parallel to the Concordat of Worms that Chapter 44 and Chapter 46 will place in its imperial setting.

The scale of this governmental growth should not be exaggerated. Henry's government was still itinerant, centered on the king's person and household, and dependent on his presence, his favor, and his ability to reward followers. Some of the evidence for the period comes from hostile or flattering chroniclers, and the administrative machinery developed unevenly. Nevertheless, by 1135 the English crown was governing a population of perhaps two million with a regularity that few contemporary rulers could match.

Section 45.7

The White Ship, Succession, and the Anarchy

On 25 November 1120 the White Ship, carrying Henry's only legitimate son William Ætheling and many young nobles from Barfleur, struck a rock and sank, with almost no survivors. Henry's remaining legitimate child was his daughter Matilda, widow of the Holy Roman Emperor Henry V, whom he forced his barons to acknowledge as heir in 1127 and again later. She married Geoffrey, count of Anjou, in 1128, a match that served Norman security against Anjou but alarmed barons reluctant to be ruled by a woman and by an Angevin. When Henry died in December 1135, his nephew Stephen of Blois, who had substantial lands in England and Normandy and the backing of his brother, the bishop of Winchester, crossed from Boulogne, was welcomed in London, and was crowned.

The resulting civil war, traditionally called the Anarchy, lasted from 1139, when Matilda landed in England, until 1153. The label itself dates from the nineteenth century, and historians dispute how far the realm descended into chaos. Matilda held much of the west, supported by her half-brother Robert of Gloucester, while Stephen held the southeast. Stephen was captured at Lincoln in 1141, and Matilda was never crowned because Londoners drove her out. The war was fought through sieges and raids, and barons exploited the conflict to extend their own power, strengthen castles, and bargain with both sides, sometimes obtaining earldoms from each. Some local districts suffered severe disruption, as an English chronicler lamented, and in other areas royal government continued with little change. Meanwhile, Geoffrey of Anjou conquered Normandy by 1144.

The conflict ended in 1153 when Matilda's son Henry, by then duke of Normandy and, through marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine in 1152, ruler of much of western France, invaded England. Stephen's son Eustace died, and under the Treaty of Winchester Stephen kept the crown for his life while Henry was recognized as heir. Stephen died in 1154. The Anarchy matters for the argument of this chapter because it demonstrates the limits of the inherited machinery. A well-organized administration did not on its own maintain the monarchy: when the succession was contested and the barons could choose sides, royal government depended on aristocratic loyalty, and strong kingship could dissolve into negotiation among regional power brokers. It also shaped Henry II's agenda. Later chroniclers and Henry's own officials treated restoration of order, the demolition of unlicensed castles, and the recovery of crown lands as the central tasks of the new reign.

Continuity and Change Over Time

England in 1065 and 1086

What Changed?

  • Almost the entire secular and ecclesiastical elite was replaced by continental newcomers, and the king claimed direct oaths from subtenants.
  • Castles, previously rare, became the dominant instruments of local lordship and the symbols of a new military order.
  • Anglo-Norman French became the language of the elite and Latin replaced English in royal writs.
  • Parts of the north lay devastated after 1069 and 1070, and a new Jewish presence appeared in English towns.

What Remained Continuous?

  • Shires, hundreds, and sheriffs, with their courts, remained the basic units of local government.
  • The geld continued as a land tax assessed on hides, and the coinage remained under royal control.
  • Writs and royal assemblies continued as methods of governing, along with English law customs, confirmed in principle by William.
  • Most of the population were English-speaking cultivators whose daily lives changed less than their lords did.

Who experienced change most directly? The English landed elite, the higher clergy, and the inhabitants of the devastated north felt the Conquest as rupture. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers and townspeople often found their obligations transferred to new lords with familiar demands. Why the pattern matters: The combination of elite replacement and institutional continuity explains why historians describe the Conquest neither as a mere change of dynasty nor as a complete reconstruction of society.

Historical Debate

How Revolutionary Was the Norman Conquest?

The question: Did the Norman Conquest fundamentally transform English society and government, or did it largely replace the elite of a kingdom whose institutions continued to function?

Interpretation A: Rupture. Some historians emphasize the scale of change: the near total replacement of the aristocracy, the imposition of castles, the devastation in the north, the reorganization of the church, the arrival of a new language of power, and the extension of tenures held of the king by military service. On this view, the Conquest established a new social and political order, and older English structures survived only in modified form.
Interpretation B: Continuity. Others stress that William took over a kingdom with developed taxation, local government, coinage, and written administration, and that his government relied on the shire and hundred courts and the geld. On this view, the late Anglo-Saxon state was the foundation of later English government, and the Conquest redirected it more than replaced it. Many scholars, accordingly, describe an aristocratic revolution within an inherited state.

Evidence: Debate depends on Domesday Book, which shows both the elite transfer and the continuity of assessment and local jurisdiction, on charters and writs, on the evidence for language and personal names, and on interpretations of the Chronicle and the Norman writers. The weight given to the rapidity of the transformation in the north versus the stability in the shires is crucial.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the origins of English government and the character of medieval monarchy. It also bears on memory: in later centuries, notably during the seventeenth century, writers invoked an English freedom lost to the "Norman yoke," a political myth that used the Conquest as an argument about contemporary rights and not as historical reconstruction. Current scholarship tends toward combined interpretations that distinguish elite, institutions, and local society.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under Norman and Angevin Rule

Most inhabitants of England were villagers who worked the land of a lord and owed him labor, rents, and customary dues. For many, the Conquest changed the name and language of their lord but not the rhythm of ploughing, harvest, and church feast days. The structure of obligations, however, shifted over time. Domesday records many peasants whose status was defined as villani, bordars, or slaves, while later twelfth-century records show a growing distinction between free tenants who could use royal courts and unfree villeins whose disputes with their lords were handled in the lord's court. Important royal land remedies, as Section 45.9 explains, protected free tenements, while villeins generally could not use those assizes against their lords, which made legal status increasingly important.

Royal forests, set aside by the Norman kings for hunting, imposed special laws that restricted clearing, grazing, and hunting across large areas, with penalties enforced by royal foresters, and local communities regularly resented them. Ordinary households faced overlapping burdens: royal geld and court fines, ecclesiastical tithes, and rents and customary dues owed to lords. Women's lives depended on rank and marriage: widows and heiresses in the king's gift were sometimes married off for royal profit, a practice that the Charter of Liberties and Magna Carta both sought to limit. Language mattered, too: a villager who sought justice in a royal court encountered a legal and administrative culture in which records were Latin and French was increasingly prominent, while remaining English-speaking at home. Jewish families lived in towns under the king's protection, engaged in trade and moneylending, and were vulnerable to hostility, as shown by the massacre of the Jewish community at York in March 1190.

Section 45.8

Henry II and the Angevin Monarchy

Henry II, crowned in December 1154, inherited England from his mother's line, Normandy and Anjou from his father, and, by marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine, one of the largest duchies of France. Eleanor had been queen of France until her marriage to Louis VII was annulled in 1152. Henry also exercised influence over Brittany and later claimed lordship in Ireland after his intervention of 1171 to 1172. His dominions stretched from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees. For Henry these territories were separate inheritances, each governed according to local custom and each held, in its continental parts, as a fief of the French crown. This is why historians speak of a composite monarchy and use "Angevin Empire" only as a convenient modern label. No single institutional structure governed the whole, and Henry's authority rested on a personal presence that he exercised by almost constant travel.

His first priority was to restore English royal authority after the Anarchy. He recovered crown lands, demolished many adulterine castles, reasserted control over sheriffs through an Inquest of Sheriffs in 1170 that investigated corruption, and gave his officials, many of them men of modest origins promoted by the king, wide powers. His household and chief officers, notably the justiciar who governed in his absence, kept the machinery functioning while the king traveled. Because Henry spent more than half his reign outside England, administration had to be regular enough to operate without him, and this need encouraged writing, accounting, and delegation. The growth of royal government was thus a consequence of the geography of Angevin power as well as of the king's own energy.

Henry's marriage and family were also political structures. Eleanor remained duchess of Aquitaine in her own right and was an active political figure, though her role was constrained by Henry's dominance. Their sons, Henry the Young King, Richard, Geoffrey, and John, were promised portions of the dominions, a form of succession planning that left them restless and open to the intrigues of Louis VII and later Philip II of France. The French kings, as lords of Henry's continental fiefs, had a legal basis for interfering and a clear interest in dividing the family. The English and French crowns thus became entangled in a dynastic and feudal rivalry that would outlive Henry, though this rivalry should be kept distinct from the much later Hundred Years' War, a conflict of different origins examined in Chapter 51.

Henry's government was also formidable in its demands. It claimed rights over land, over justice, and over the church, and it invested heavily in warfare to protect the Angevin lands. These claims generated the achievements of Henry's reign, examined in the next two sections, and also the conflicts of Section 45.11.

Section 45.9

Royal Justice and the Growth of the Common Law

Before Henry II, justice in England was delivered mainly by local courts: shire, hundred, and the courts of lords. Royal justice existed in the king's court and through occasional commissions, but access was limited. Henry's reign saw a rapid expansion of royal jurisdiction, through a combination of administrative procedures and statutory measures called assizes, which were decisions of the king in council, rather than statutes in the modern sense. The principal mechanism was the writ, which a litigant could purchase from the royal chancery to start a case, often directing the sheriff to summon a jury of neighbors to give a verdict.

The Assize of Clarendon (1166) required juries of presentment in each hundred to report to royal justices those suspected of serious crimes such as murder, robbery, and theft, and established sheriffs' duties in arresting and holding them. Ordeal by water was used when evidence was lacking, a practice that persisted until the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 withdrew clerical participation in ordeals, after which trial by jury gradually took its place. The assizes of novel disseisin (c. 1166), which protected recent possession of land against unjust dispossession, and mort d'ancestor (1176), which protected inheritance, allowed free tenants to bring land disputes before royal justices through quick procedures that used recognitions by local juries. The "grand assize," introduced around 1179, offered an alternative to trial by battle in disputes over right to land. Justices on circuit, called itinerant justices or eyres, brought royal law to the counties, and the central court that eventually sat at Westminster developed from the king's court. A treatise from about 1187 to 1189, associated with Ranulf de Glanvill, described these procedures in detail.

The consequences were profound, though gradual. Royal justice offered speedier remedies and more predictable procedures than baronial courts, and free men flocked to use it, paying fees that enriched the crown. It drew litigation away from lordly courts and thereby reduced aristocratic control of jurisdiction, which is one reason barons both used the king's courts and resented their growth. It also created, over time, a growing body of standardized remedies, procedures, and judicial practice, and a class of professional justices and clerks, who developed rules and forms, the beginnings of what later came to be called the common law, meaning the law common to the whole kingdom as distinct from local custom. The inclusion of juries as fact-finders, drawing on both Anglo-Saxon traditions of sworn local testimony and Frankish inquest practices that Normans had brought, was a distinctive feature.

Historians take care not to say that Henry "invented" the common law. Earlier kings had exercised royal justice, Anglo-Saxon law codes and local courts supplied much substance, and later judges, lawyers, and legislators continued to shape the system throughout the thirteenth century and beyond. The reforms of the 1160s and 1170s mattered chiefly because they created procedures and institutions that gave royal courts permanent importance. Those benefits were distributed unequally: unfree peasants could not use royal land assizes against their lords, and women and the poor faced practical obstacles. Royal justice was also a source of revenue and political leverage, as litigants paid for writs and the crown could withhold or sell favor.

Historical Vocabulary
Common Law

The common law is the body of law that developed from the decisions and procedures of the royal courts, applicable throughout England in contrast to local and lordly custom. It was not a code enacted at a single moment but an accumulation of writs, procedures, and judicial reasoning, beginning in the twelfth century and continuing for centuries. The term should not be confused with other uses, such as law as opposed to equity, or the law of Anglophone countries today, though those later meanings descend from it. Its importance here lies in showing how royal government and legal development reinforced each other.

Section 45.10

Taxation, the Exchequer, and Administrative Government

The twelfth-century English crown lived on a mixture of ordinary and extraordinary revenues. Its ordinary income came from royal estates, the profits of justice such as fines and amercements, the sheriffs' farms (fixed annual sums for the county revenues they administered), borough farms, revenue from forests, feudal incidents such as reliefs paid by heirs and the profits of wardship and marriage, and from Jewish communities, whose property and debts the king could claim. Extraordinary revenues came from the geld, which declined as a regular tax, and from new levies including aids, scutage, tallage on royal towns and estates, and, in 1188, the Saladin tithe, a tax on income and movables raised for a crusade, an episode that Chapter 47 situates within crusading history.

Scutage, "shield money," was a payment made by a tenant-in-chief in place of providing knights for the king's army. It became common under Henry II, who used it to hire mercenaries, who were more flexible than feudal levies. The shift from service to money shows the monetizing of royal government and the importance of warfare in driving administrative change. Wars against Louis VII, rebellion within Henry's family, and, under Richard, the ransom of a king captured returning from crusade, a sum reported by contemporaries as 150,000 marks and clearly enormous though not precisely known, placed heavy burdens on the kingdom. Under John, fiscal demands rose further, and the way scutage and aids were levied became a central grievance.

The institution that managed this system was the Exchequer, which by Henry I's reign met twice a year at Westminster, at Easter and Michaelmas, to audit sheriffs' accounts. It took its name from the checkered cloth on which counters were moved to calculate sums, an arrangement described in the Dialogue of the Exchequer, written about 1177 by Richard fitzNeal, treasurer of England. Sheriffs came to account for the farm of their shire and other receipts, and clerks recorded the results on rolls of parchment sewn end to end and stored rolled up, the Pipe Rolls. The earliest surviving roll dates from 1130, and a nearly continuous series survives from the reign of Henry II. The Exchequer was both a court and an accounting office, and its records meant that the king's debtors could not easily evade liability. Debts to the crown might be carried forward for years, giving the king a means of political pressure, since a baron in arrears depended on royal favor.

The Jewish communities were a particularly important part of this system. From Henry II's reign onward, English Jewish communities came increasingly under special royal protection, jurisdiction, and heavy fiscal exploitation, and by the end of John's reign the crown's claim to exceptional lordship over them was substantially developed. The crown profited from their lending by claiming a share of debts and, on a Jew's death, his estate. This arrangement made Jews valuable to the crown but dependent and vulnerable, and anti-Jewish violence, such as the York massacre of 1190 during Richard's departure on crusade, often involved debtors seeking to destroy records of their debts. The massacre, in which Jews who had taken refuge in the royal castle were killed or took their own lives, reveals the dangers of a society where religious hostility, royal finance, and aristocratic indebtedness intersected.

Administration was also personal. Royal officials, including the justiciar, treasurer, chancellor, and sheriffs, were often clerics or men dependent on royal favor. The crown rewarded them with church offices and lands. Their power could generate resentment among established barons, and their financial pressure could be intense. The system worked because it combined bureaucratic routines with royal discretion, and it provoked opposition when discretion seemed arbitrary.

Historical Vocabulary
Exchequer

The Exchequer was the royal financial department and court that audited the accounts of sheriffs and other officials, named for the checkered cloth used as a counting board. Its twice-yearly sessions and written records, the Pipe Rolls, made the crown's finances unusually visible to the government itself. It matters because it linked taxation, justice, and administration: those who owed money to the king could be pursued through procedures that blended accounting and law. It should not be understood as a modern treasury or ministry of finance, since it functioned alongside the king's household chamber and depended on royal authority.

Section 45.11

Kings, Bishops, and Barons: The Limits of Angevin Power

Henry II's relationship with the church produced the most famous conflict of his reign. In 1162 he appointed his chancellor and friend Thomas Becket archbishop of Canterbury, expecting cooperation. Becket instead defended ecclesiastical independence. The central issue, which should not be confused with the investiture dispute of Chapter 44, was how far the king's justice extended over clergy accused of crimes and how far appeals could go to Rome. In 1164 Henry issued the Constitutions of Clarendon, which claimed that clerics convicted in church courts could be degraded and then handed to royal justice and restricted appeals to the pope. Becket first accepted, then repudiated them, and fled abroad. In December 1170 four knights, interpreting an outburst by the king as a command, murdered Becket in Canterbury Cathedral. Henry denied responsibility but did public penance in 1174, and Becket, canonized in 1173, became the object of one of medieval Europe's most popular pilgrimage cults. The affair shows both the king's determination to extend jurisdiction and the strength of an international church whose claims limited royal power.

Henry also faced resistance within his family and aristocracy. In 1173 his eldest son, Henry the Young King, who had been crowned in 1170 but given no real power, rebelled with the support of his mother Eleanor, his brothers, Louis VII of France, the count of Flanders, and the king of Scots, William the Lion. Many barons in England and Normandy joined, motivated by local grievances and the new demands of royal officials. Henry crushed the revolt by 1174, capturing William the Lion at Alnwick and imprisoning Eleanor for most of the rest of his reign. The revolt shows that administrative strength did not guarantee loyalty. Barons who found themselves under tighter royal control, family members who lacked autonomy, and a French king with an interest in weakening Henry could ally readily.

The end of Henry's reign repeated the pattern. His sons rebelled again in the 1180s, Henry the Young King died in 1183, and in 1189 Richard, allied with Philip II, forced his father to terms shortly before Henry's death. Richard I (reigned 1189 to 1199) spent about a decade of his reign abroad: on crusade in 1190 to 1192 (a campaign examined within Chapter 47, not here), in captivity in the Holy Roman Empire in 1192 to 1194, and defending Normandy against Philip. England was governed in his absence by officials like Hubert Walter, archbishop and justiciar, who developed the machinery of taxation and justice further, partly to raise the huge sums the king required. The system proved resilient, and Richard's reign is often cited as evidence that the Angevin administrative apparatus could function without a king's constant supervision. The cost, however, was rising fiscal pressure, which fell heavily on landholders and towns and prepared the ground for John's difficulties.

Section 45.12

King John, Lost Lands, and the Crisis of Royal Authority

When Richard died in 1199, John succeeded, setting aside the claim of his nephew Arthur of Brittany, the son of John's elder brother Geoffrey. John's accession was contested in Anjou, Maine, and Touraine, where local lords favored Arthur and found support from Philip II. In 1200 John made a politically damaging marriage to Isabella of Angoulême, who was already betrothed to a Poitevin lord, giving Philip a legal pretext. In 1202 Philip, as John's overlord, declared John's continental fiefs forfeit for failing to answer a summons to his court. Arthur's death in John's custody in 1203 destroyed his reputation among many Norman and Angevin barons. Philip conquered Normandy in 1203 and 1204, taking Château Gaillard in March 1204 and Rouen in June, and also overran Anjou, Maine, Touraine, and much of Poitou.

The loss of Normandy was a watershed. It ended a connection between England and Normandy that had lasted since 1066 and compelled aristocratic families with lands on both sides to choose between the English and French kings, a process that began to separate the English aristocracy from the continent, though many retained claims and ambitions in France. For John, recovering the lost lands became the principal aim of his reign, and it required money, which he raised on a scale that exceeded his predecessors: heavy scutage, tallages, additional taxation on movables, large fines for royal favor, and ruthless use of royal debt as a political instrument. John's exploitation of feudal incidents, such as high reliefs, control of marriages and wardships, and arbitrary demands for payment, spurred outrage among barons. His government also became more centralized and more personal, with the king traveling constantly and intervening in justice, so that many felt the system of law lay at the king's whim.

John's quarrel with the church further weakened him. In 1205 Archbishop Hubert Walter died, and a dispute over the choice of his successor led Pope Innocent III to appoint Stephen Langton. John refused to accept him, and in 1208 England was placed under an interdict, suspending most church services, with John excommunicated in 1209. John seized church revenues, which enriched his treasury but intensified conflict. In 1213 he submitted, accepted Langton, and made England a papal fief, paying an annual tribute, to secure papal backing against his enemies. The submission was both a political stroke and a humiliation.

John then built a coalition for the reconquest of Normandy, allying with his nephew Emperor Otto IV and the count of Flanders. The plan collapsed on 27 July 1214 at Bouvines, near Lille, where Philip II defeated Otto and his allies. John's southern campaign in Poitou had also failed, and he returned to England in October 1214, financially exhausted and facing barons who refused further service and payment. Several of them, particularly in the north and east, had grievances over debts to the crown and royal exactions. In the spring of 1215, with demands unmet, a group of barons renounced their allegiance, took London in May, and compelled John to negotiate. Their cause was not purely baronial self-interest, as some churchmen and townsmen, including Langton, shared concerns about arbitrary rule, but the rebels' own motives mixed principle and grievance.

Section 45.13

Magna Carta, 1215: Bargaining with a Medieval King

At Runnymede, on the Thames between Windsor and Staines, John sealed a charter on 15 June 1215 that later became known as Magna Carta, the "great charter." It was not called that at first; the name came after the shorter Charter of the Forest was issued in 1217, to distinguish the larger document. Its origins lay in the barons' written demands, the Articles of the Barons, and in earlier models such as Henry I's Charter of Liberties. The text, divided by modern editors into sixty-three clauses, was written in Latin and mostly concerned feudal matters. It limited reliefs for heirs, regulated wardship and the marriage of heiresses and widows, and required that scutage and aids, except for the three customary occasions, be levied only with the "common counsel" of the realm, to be obtained under a procedure in which the greater barons and prelates were summoned individually and other tenants-in-chief by a general summons through sheriffs and bailiffs. It addressed royal justice, promising that royal courts would sit in a fixed place, that justice would not be sold, denied, or delayed, and that no free man should be seized, imprisoned, or deprived of rights except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land. It guaranteed the freedom of the English church, confirmed the liberties of towns, including London, and controlled royal forest abuses.

Its final clauses show its political character. Clause 61, the security clause, established a committee of twenty-five barons empowered to compel John to observe the charter, by seizing royal castles and lands if necessary. This was a plan for baronial control of the king, one no medieval ruler could accept for long. The settlement was fragile and mutual distrust remained deep: John appealed to Innocent III, who in August 1215 annulled the charter as an illegitimate coercion of a papal vassal, and civil war began. The rebels invited Louis, son of Philip II, who landed in England in 1216 and received considerable support. John died in October 1216, and the regents of his young son Henry III, led by William Marshal, reissued a revised charter in November 1216 and again in 1217, shedding the security clause and some controversial provisions, which helped end the war and reconcile many rebels. The reissue of 1225 became the definitive form, and Edward I's confirmation of 1297 was the first to be entered on the official Statute Roll. The clauses on common counsel for taxation (12 and 14) were dropped from the later reissues.

In its own time, then, Magna Carta was a failed peace treaty and a baronial list of grievances, many of which concerned the rights of tenants-in-chief and not the rights of all subjects. Many of its clauses had no relevance to unfree peasants, who made up a large proportion of the population, and its protections for "free men" covered a minority. Yet the charter also stated principles that outlasted its immediate context: the king was bound by law and custom, certain extraordinary levies required common counsel, and legal process protected against arbitrary punishment. These ideas drew on earlier charters, on canon law, and on feudal custom, and they were part of the broader medieval idea that kings ruled under law.

Later centuries read the charter differently. Seventeenth-century opponents of the Stuart kings, notably in the era examined in Chapter 77, invoked it as evidence of ancient English liberty, and later writers, including American colonists and nineteenth-century liberals, saw it as a foundation of constitutional government and individual rights. Historians reject the claim that it created democracy or parliamentary government: those institutions developed through different processes over subsequent centuries, and the charter's relevance in 1215 was limited. The distinction between what the document meant in 1215 and what later generations made of it is a clear example of the difference between history and memory. Taking the charter seriously in context allows its significance, as a record of bargaining between king and barons within a society that expected kings to rule justly, to be understood without anachronism.

Primary Source Window

Magna Carta, 1215, on Lawful Judgment and Common Counsel

Creator: The royal chancery, at the demand of baronial rebels and with the participation of Archbishop Stephen Langton and others · Date: 15 June 1215 · Type: A royal charter, in Latin, later numbered into clauses by modern editors

Context: The charter was issued during the crisis described in Section 45.12 and annulled within weeks. Its text survives in several contemporary copies and in later reissues.

Paraphrase of selected provisions Clause 39 provides that no free man is to be arrested, imprisoned, dispossessed, outlawed, or otherwise harmed except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land. Clause 40 adds that the king will not sell, deny, or delay right or justice. Clause 12 provides that no scutage or aid is to be imposed in the kingdom without common counsel, except for the king's ransom, the knighting of his eldest son, and the first marriage of his eldest daughter, and Clause 14 sets out how that counsel is to be summoned. These are paraphrases, not translations, and the numbering of clauses is a modern convention.

Analysis: These provisions show baronial concern with arbitrary punishment and exaction, and they treat law and consent as limits on kingship. The meaning of "free man," "peers," and "common counsel" in 1215 was narrower than modern readers may assume, since "peers" suggested equals in status, and "common counsel" meant the greater barons and prelates summoned individually and other tenants-in-chief summoned generally, not an elected body. The source reveals the grievances of the politically powerful and says little directly about peasants, women outside the clauses on widows, or Jews, who are the subject of clauses on debts owed to Jewish lenders.

Section 45.14

Capetian France and the Uneven Growth of Medieval Monarchy

England's path toward stronger monarchy was only one among several. In France, the Capetian dynasty, which took the crown in 987 with Hugh Capet, began from a very different position. The early Capetian kings directly controlled only a modest domain around Paris and Orléans, and great princes, the dukes of Normandy and Aquitaine, the counts of Flanders, Champagne, Anjou, and others, were often more powerful than the king. The Capetian monarchy survived because of dynastic continuity, the sacral aura of coronation at Reims, the support of the church, and the king's theoretical position as lord of all the great fiefs. Louis VI (reigned 1108 to 1137) suppressed unruly lords within the royal domain and strengthened its core, and Louis VII (reigned 1137 to 1180) cultivated church alliances and presided over a growing Paris, but even after the annulment of his marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine, the royal domain was small compared with Henry II's dominions.

Philip II, called Augustus, transformed this position. Over his long reign (1180 to 1223) he enlarged the royal domain several times over, by inheritance, purchase, and above all conquest of Normandy, Anjou, Maine, Touraine, and part of Poitou from John in 1202 to 1206, and by acquiring Artois, Vermandois, and other territories. His victory at Bouvines in 1214 over a coalition of John, Otto IV, and Flanders strengthened both the dynasty's prestige and its practical power. Philip also built administrative structures to govern an expanded domain. He appointed baillis, royal agents who supervised justice, finance, and military affairs in districts of the domain, and in conquered lands, sénéchaux. These officials were paid, removable, and accountable, and they limited the independence of local lords and local officers. The king also kept records, including registers of his rights and revenues, fortified Paris, and developed a financial administration that, though less visible in the sources than England's Exchequer, supported his campaigns.

A comparison with England is instructive. England inherited unusually centralized machinery, a conquering dynasty, and a small territory in which the king could travel easily, but it suffered recurrent aristocratic crises in the Anarchy, the revolt of 1173, and the rebellion of 1215. France began with a smaller and weaker royal domain and a decentralized structure of princely power, but expanded royal authority gradually through territorial acquisition, feudal law, and new officials. In both kingdoms, stronger monarchy depended on bargaining with elites and on royal success in war, and in both it was reversible. The fortunes of the kings also intertwined: the loss of Normandy strengthened the Capetians and weakened the Angevins, and shaped the fiscal and political pressures that produced Magna Carta.

These two paths were not the only ones. Chapter 46 turns to the Holy Roman Empire, where emperors, territorial princes, ecclesiastical lordships, and Italian cities produced a decentralized order that followed neither the English nor the French model. Elsewhere, the kingdoms of Iberia, Hungary, Poland, and Scandinavia built monarchy through frontier expansion, conversion, and negotiation with nobles, as Chapters 35, 47, and 54 discuss. Students should therefore resist reading the English and French cases as stages in a single march toward the modern state. Medieval monarchy was a family of political forms, shaped by dynastic accident, warfare, geography, law, and the resources of church and aristocracy, and the stronger kingdoms of 1215 were not destined to become the nation-states of later centuries.

A baron in 1215: Royal justice and finance offered opportunities, but demands for scutage, heavy reliefs, and arbitrary fines threatened family estates. Magna Carta was an attempt to fix limits on what the king might take.

Cause and Effect

From Conquest to Administrative Monarchy

Long-term causes

Late Anglo-Saxon shires, hundreds, sheriffs, geld, coinage, and writs, together with the long Scandinavian wars that taught English kings to raise money at scale.

Short-term causes

The Conquest created a need to control a hostile population, reward followers, and resolve land disputes, and the king's continental commitments required reliable government in his absence.

Event or transformation

Conquest and settlement combined with inherited institutions to produce Domesday, a service-based landholding structure, and, in the twelfth century, the Exchequer and common-law procedures.

Immediate effects

Greater royal revenue and a more accessible royal justice, along with new burdens on landholders, and the suppression of local autonomy.

Long-term consequences

A durable tradition of record keeping, professional justices, and royal courts, but also a tradition of charters and consent that limited the crown.

Historical complexity

The process was not a straight line. The Anarchy interrupted it, John's financial demands provoked reaction, and the interplay of English and continental politics shaped each stage. No single cause, ruler, or conquest explains the outcome.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Norman Conquest transformed England's ruling elite and landholding but did not create royal government from nothing. Late Anglo-Saxon England already possessed shires, hundreds, sheriffs, royal writs, coinage, and a land tax assessed on hides, and these institutions made it one of the most administered kingdoms in Latin Christendom. That inheritance, which was a product of the tenth-century consolidation of the English kingdom and of the long wars with Scandinavian invaders, is the first of the chapter's major themes.

The succession crisis of 1066 revealed competing medieval ideas of legitimacy. Harold Godwinson's election and coronation, William's claims of designation and oath, Harald Hardrada's claim through an earlier agreement, and Edgar Ætheling's blood right each drew on a different conception of kingship. The campaigns of that year, Stamford Bridge and Hastings, show the importance of timing, geography, and chance, and of the fact that Hastings secured a crown but not a kingdom. The years from 1066 to about 1071, marked by revolts, castle building, and the Harrying of the North, saw conquest become a durable occupation.

The Conquest rapidly displaced most of the old English landed aristocracy and, over William's reign, progressively replaced much of the higher church leadership with continental newcomers, who held land from the king in return for military service and who built castles to control the countryside. This was a social and political transformation, accompanied by changes in language and culture, but it was combined with the continued operation of shire and hundred courts and the geld. Domesday Book, the survey of 1086, embodies both sides of the process: it records the conquest's redistribution of land, and it shows a king able to command local juries and royal officers across most of the kingdom. As a source it is invaluable, and as a source it has important limits.

The twelfth century brought both expansion and crisis. Henry I developed household and financial administration, and the Pipe Roll of 1130 shows a functioning audit system. The loss of the White Ship in 1120 and the disputed succession of 1135 led to the civil war known as the Anarchy, which demonstrated that royal government depended on aristocratic loyalty. Henry II restored authority and expanded it dramatically, ruling a composite dominion across England and much of western France, which required regular administration capable of functioning in his absence.

Royal justice grew through writs, juries, assizes, and itinerant justices, creating procedures that became the common law. Henry II did not invent the common law, but his reign established the mechanisms and institutions that gave royal courts permanent importance. Taxation and administration developed in parallel, with the Exchequer, the Pipe Rolls, scutage, and other levies connecting royal finance to warfare. Jewish communities, increasingly subject to special royal protection and exploitation, were an important and vulnerable part of this system, and their history reminds us that Norman and Angevin England was religiously diverse.

Stronger monarchy provoked resistance. The Becket conflict showed the limits of royal jurisdiction over the church, and the revolt of 1173 to 1174 showed that family, aristocratic, and French interests could combine against a powerful king. John's loss of Normandy in 1204, the interdict, the defeat at Bouvines, and the financial demands of reconquest created a crisis that produced Magna Carta in June 1215. The charter was a coerced peace settlement between John and rebel barons that articulated existing expectations that kings should rule within law and custom, but it was annulled within weeks and acquired greater durability through its later reissues and reinterpretations.

Finally, the chapter compared England with Capetian France. Where England inherited strong machinery but suffered recurrent baronial crises, France began with a small domain and expanded royal power gradually, particularly under Philip II, through territorial conquest and new royal officials. The rivalry between these two monarchies produced the loss of Normandy, and each kingdom's trajectory was contingent. Chapter 46 will show a third, strikingly different pattern in the Holy Roman Empire.

The chapter's main conclusion is that stronger monarchy in the High Middle Ages was uneven, negotiated, and reversible. It rested on inherited institutions, on conquest and dynastic fortune, on warfare and the money it demanded, and on continuing bargaining with aristocrats and churchmen. It was not an inevitable step toward the modern state, a point of method to which later volumes, and Chapter 141, return.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a medieval kingdom could become both more powerful and more constrained. The Norman and Angevin kings gathered revenue, extended justice, and recorded their rights with a thoroughness that few contemporaries matched, yet each stage of that growth stimulated resistance and bargaining. The result was not a simple triumph of central authority but a political order in which kings, barons, bishops, towns, and, increasingly, free landholders contested the terms of rule.

The chapter also reveals how conquest and inheritance interact. William did not build English government from nothing, and the strength of the English state owed as much to its Anglo-Saxon past as to Norman innovation. Yet the Conquest bound England to Normandy, replaced its elite, and set its political life within a cross-Channel world, until the events of 1204 reshaped that world. Students should carry forward an appreciation of how structures survive political upheaval and how upheaval redirects them.

For Volume II, the chapter contributes a case study of how Christendom's kingdoms grew in the High Middle Ages, alongside the church reform of Chapter 44 and before the imperial pattern of Chapter 46. Comparing England and France shows the variety of medieval monarchy, and the later history of Magna Carta, the common law, and royal finance was shaped, in ways examined in Chapters 77 and 141, by medieval foundations that were never inevitable.

Chapter 46 now turns to the Holy Roman Empire, where emperors, princes, ecclesiastical territories, and Italian politics produced a decentralized order that followed neither the English nor the French path, and which completes this Part's survey of how medieval Europeans governed themselves.
