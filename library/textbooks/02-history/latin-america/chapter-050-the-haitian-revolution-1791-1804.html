The Haitian Revolution, 1791–1804

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: The Richest Colony and the End of Its Slave System

In August 1791, enslaved people in the plain north of Cap-Français rose against the plantation system that made Saint-Domingue the richest colony in the Atlantic world. Thirteen years later, their successors proclaimed an independent state and gave it an Indigenous name, Ayiti. In between, a society built on slavery abolished it through its own armed action, defeated or outlasted the armies of Spain, Britain, and Napoleonic France, and changed what slaveholders and the enslaved everywhere in the Americas believed was possible.

Saint-Domingue occupied the western third of the island of Hispaniola, the island on which Columbus had founded the first Spanish settlements three centuries earlier (Chapter 27). Spain had formally ceded the western part to France in 1697, and over the following century French planters turned it into a vast machine for producing sugar and coffee. By the 1780s the colony is commonly estimated to have supplied a very large share of the sugar and about half of the coffee consumed in Europe. It did so with some 450,000 to 500,000 enslaved people, most of them born in Africa, who labored under conditions so lethal that the slave population could be maintained only by continuous importation. Chapter 44 examined the Atlantic slave trade and plantation slavery across Brazil and the Caribbean; this chapter follows what happened when one of those systems was broken.

The revolution that began in 1791 was not one event but a sequence of overlapping wars. It was a slave insurrection, a struggle over the rights of free people of color, a conflict among white colonists with different loyalties to the French Revolution, a civil war among rival Black and mixed-race commanders, an international war involving Spain and Britain, and finally a war of independence against a French expeditionary army sent to restore the old order. These strands interacted constantly. Emancipation was proclaimed in 1793 by French commissioners who were trying to save the colony for the Republic, ratified by the National Convention in 1794, and defended by formerly enslaved soldiers who had every reason to doubt that Paris would keep its word. When Napoleon Bonaparte's government moved to restore slavery in 1802, the same soldiers turned against France.

The chapter therefore resists two simplifications. One treats the revolution as a footnote to the French Revolution, an echo of metropolitan ideals in a distant colony. The other treats it as an act of pure rupture with Europe, uninfluenced by Atlantic politics. The evidence shows something more interesting: the enslaved and free people of Saint-Domingue acted within the political possibilities created by French revolutionary debates, but they also drew on African political and religious experience, on a long tradition of marronage, and on their own assessment of what freedom required. They took ideas of liberty and citizenship further than most metropolitan revolutionaries were prepared to go.

Haiti is also part of Latin American history in a full sense. The language of the colony was French, and its enslaved majority spoke Haitian Kreyòl, but it belonged to the same Caribbean and Atlantic system as Spanish Cuba, Santo Domingo, Puerto Rico, and Portuguese Brazil. Its revolution affected the plantation economies of those places, the fears and strategies of Spanish American creole elites on the eve of the independence wars (Chapters 51 through 57), and the subsequent politics of abolition throughout the hemisphere. It was the first independent state in Latin America and the Caribbean, and the first in the Americas after the United States, and it was founded by people who had been enslaved. That fact shaped how it was received, isolated, and remembered.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. Many of the surviving documents were produced by planters, colonial officials, French soldiers, and travelers who regarded the insurgents as criminals or as instruments of others. Toussaint Louverture, Jean-Jacques Dessalines, and other leaders left letters, proclamations, and a constitution, but many of their followers could not write, and the voices of ordinary insurgents, especially women, come to us largely through hostile or retrospective sources. The anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot argued that the revolution was long treated as "unthinkable" within the categories of Western historical writing, which helps explain why its sources were underread. Historians from C. L. R. James, whose The Black Jacobins appeared in 1938, to Carolyn Fick, David Geggus, and Laurent Dubois have worked to recover its internal dynamics. Haitian historians in the nineteenth century, notably Thomas Madiou and Beaubrun Ardouin, wrote the first national histories, and their accounts, shaped by the factional politics of their own time, remain essential and contested sources.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It opens with the colony as it stood in 1789 and closes with the proclamation of independence in 1804 and the first months of the new state. It treats the revolution's repercussions elsewhere in the Americas as consequences rather than as separate narratives. It does not narrate the rebellions of Túpac Amaru II and Túpac Katari (Chapter 49), the French invasion of Iberia in 1808 (Chapter 51), or the wars of independence in Spanish America that followed, though it shows how Haiti touched them. Haiti's later history, including the division of the country, the indemnity imposed by France in 1825, and the long struggle for recognition, belongs to the wider study of nineteenth-century state formation, and only its beginnings appear here.

Historical Context

Hispaniola was the first island of sustained European colonization (Chapters 27 and 29). The Taíno population, which had numbered in the hundreds of thousands or more, collapsed in the decades after 1492 through violence, forced labor, and epidemic disease. The Spanish colony of Santo Domingo, centered on the city of that name, declined once the mainland conquests drew settlers toward Mexico and Peru, and the western part of the island became a haunt of buccaneers and French settlers. France formalized its claim in the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697. The French colony took the name Saint-Domingue, which is not interchangeable with modern Haiti: before 1804 it was a French overseas possession, and the eastern two-thirds of the island remained Spanish. The name "Haiti," or Ayiti, comes from a Taíno name for the island, which the founders of the independent state revived in 1804.

The eighteenth century saw rapid expansion. French investment, a growing European taste for sugar and coffee, and the mass importation of Africans transformed the colony. Saint-Domingue's plantations lay in three broad regions, the North Province around Cap-Français, the West Province around Port-au-Prince, and the South Province around Les Cayes, with mountain interiors that were thinly settled and difficult for authorities to control. The Plaine du Nord, a fertile lowland behind Cap-Français, was the most densely planted zone and became the first center of the 1791 uprising. Slave imports in the late 1780s ran to tens of thousands of people a year, and by 1789 a majority of the enslaved population had been born in Africa, with large numbers from West-Central Africa (the Kongo and Angola regions) and from the Bight of Benin and other parts of West Africa. This meant that African languages, religious practice, and military experience were living resources, not distant memories.

Beyond Saint-Domingue the French Atlantic was in political crisis. The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) had cost France its North American empire in Canada and left it more dependent on Caribbean sugar. Spain's Bourbon reforms, treated in Chapter 48, were an expression of a common imperial anxiety about revenue and control. The American Revolution (1776–1783) demonstrated that colonists could throw off a metropolitan government, and several hundred free men of color from Saint-Domingue had fought on the American side at the siege of Savannah in 1779. The French Revolution of 1789 then raised, in its most basic documents, questions about who counted as a citizen that slaveholding colonies could not avoid.

Historians who study this period work with an unusual combination of sources. Colonial administrative records, notarial acts, plantation inventories, the writings of the colonial jurist Moreau de Saint-Méry, and the correspondence of French officials give a detailed picture of the colony's economy and social structure from the planters' standpoint. Legislative debates in Paris, newspapers, pamphlets, and the reports of the civil commissioners record the metropolitan side. Letters and proclamations by Toussaint Louverture, the 1801 Constitution, and Dessalines's proclamations record the leadership's political thought. Oral tradition, Vodou ritual memory, and the Haitian national histories of the nineteenth century preserve other forms of memory, which must be read as interpretations rather than as transparent records of 1791. The term "revolution" is itself a historical claim: contemporaries often spoke of "troubles" or "revolt," and the choice of words in the sources reveals perspective.

Section 50.1

Saint-Domingue: The Richest Plantation Colony

Saint-Domingue occupied the western third of Hispaniola, a rugged island of high mountain ranges separated by fertile lowlands. France's possession of the territory was confirmed by the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697, after decades in which French buccaneers and settlers had occupied the northern and western coasts. The colony's geography shaped its economy and later its war. Flat, irrigable plains, notably the Plaine du Nord behind Cap-Français, the Cul-de-Sac plain near Port-au-Prince, and the Artibonite valley, were suited to sugar. The surrounding mountains, with their cooler climate and steep slopes, became coffee country in the later eighteenth century. Between the plains and the mountains lay interior regions that colonial authorities could patrol only with difficulty, and these would shelter fugitives and insurgents.

Sugar was the great engine of wealth. By 1789 the colony contained on the order of eight hundred sugar plantations, together with several thousand smaller estates producing coffee, indigo, and cotton, though figures differ by source. Sugar plantations were industrial enterprises. Cane had to be cut and rushed to the mill within hours, crushed, boiled in a sequence of kettles, and cured, and during the harvest the mill and boiling house ran day and night. Planters invested heavily in irrigation, mills, and the purchase of enslaved laborers, and they expected large returns. Coffee spread rapidly in the final decades before the revolution and was cultivated on smaller properties, including many owned by free people of color. Estimates of the colony's share of European sugar and coffee consumption in the 1780s are commonly cited as very high, about half of the coffee and a large fraction of the sugar, and historians generally agree that Saint-Domingue was the most valuable overseas possession of any European power in this period.

That wealth was a creation of Atlantic commerce. Merchant houses in Bordeaux, Nantes, Le Havre, and Marseille financed slave voyages to West and West-Central Africa, sold captives in Saint-Domingue, advanced credit to planters, and carried the colony's produce back to Europe, where much of it was re-exported to northern markets. The mercantilist rules of the exclusif required the colony to trade only with France, and planters, who resented the prices and credit terms this imposed, engaged in smuggling with North American and British merchants. The colony thus bound together French port cities, African coastal and inland trading networks, North American suppliers, and the plantations. Royal officials, the governor and the intendant, supervised this system and collected taxes, but they were often at odds with local planters who wanted greater autonomy in commerce and law.

The human scale of the system is the key to understanding the revolution. In 1789 the colony's population was roughly half a million people. By one frequently cited reckoning there were about 452,000 enslaved people, perhaps 30,000 whites, and about 28,000 free people of color, though contemporary counts were imperfect and modern estimates vary. The enslaved were thus the overwhelming majority, and in the plantation districts of the North they outnumbered whites by margins of many to one. Cap-Français, the colonial capital of the North, and Port-au-Prince and Les Cayes in the West and South, were ports and administrative centers whose prosperity depended on the plantation hinterland. Chapter 44 traced the Atlantic slave trade and plantation slavery across the Caribbean and Brazil; Saint-Domingue was the most intensive example of that system in the French Atlantic.

Historical Vocabulary
Code Noir

The Code Noir ("Black Code") was a royal edict issued by Louis XIV in 1685 to regulate slavery in the French colonies. It defined enslaved people as movable property, prescribed punishments for flight and resistance, required baptism in the Catholic church, and set out rules on marriage, manumission, and treatment, including nominal limits on masters' power.

The edict was a mixture of control and obligation, and it was enforced unevenly, since planters regarded many of its provisions as interference. Students should avoid reading it as a description of daily life in Saint-Domingue, where violence often exceeded legal limits. It matters to this chapter because later laws, including the restrictions on free people of color and the revolutionary decrees of the 1790s, were debates about whether and how to revise the system the Code Noir had defined.

Section 50.2

Enslaved Society, African Worlds, and Resistance

The enslaved population of Saint-Domingue was not a single bloc. Because deaths exceeded births on the plantations, the colony relied on continuous importation, which ran to tens of thousands of captives a year in the late 1780s, and by 1789 a majority of the enslaved had been born in Africa. Many came from West-Central Africa, the Kongo and Angola regions, while others came from the Bight of Benin, the Bight of Biafra, the Gold Coast, and Senegambia. Colonial documents sorted them into "nations" such as Congo, Arada, Nago, Mina, Ibo, and Bambara, labels that reflected ports of embarkation and European categories as much as self-identification, but that also tracked real differences of language and culture. Colony-born people, or creoles, formed a minority, and they often held the skilled positions and domestic posts. The result was a population with fresh memories of African political and military life and with considerable linguistic diversity, held together by a developing creole language, Kreyòl.

Religious life drew on this variety. Planters were required by the Code Noir to baptize the enslaved as Catholics, and many were, but alongside the church there existed ritual societies and healing practices derived from African traditions, which scholars group under the name Vodou. Historians associate Vodou's development in Saint-Domingue with the blending of rites from the Bight of Benin and West-Central Africa, and some link the later emergence of the militant Petwo rites to Kongo influences, though the details of this development remain debated. These religious networks were sources of authority and mutual support that cut across plantation boundaries, and colonial authorities suspected, with good reason, that they could serve as vehicles for organization.

Work was differentiated. Field laborers, organized in gangs under drivers, bore the heaviest burdens, and mortality was highest in the sugar plantations. Skilled enslaved workers, including sugar-boilers, coopers, masons, carpenters, and blacksmiths, formed an elite within the plantation, and domestic servants, coachmen, and artisans in towns had access to information and mobility. Drivers, who supervised others, often held positions of influence that could be turned to organizational ends. Many enslaved people cultivated provision grounds, small plots on which they grew food, and sold surplus at Sunday markets in towns and crossroads. These markets were commercial, social, and informational spaces, and women were prominent among the traders. They also fostered an economic life in which the enslaved accumulated small property and developed networks that stretched across plantations.

Resistance took many forms, from daily acts such as work slowdowns, sabotage, and feigned illness, to flight and organized conspiracy. Marronage, flight from the plantation, ranged from short absences to permanent settlement in the mountains and forests. In the southern mountains and along the Spanish border, maroon communities persisted for decades, and colonial authorities at times negotiated with their leaders. The best-known early conspiracy is associated with Makandal, an escaped enslaved man and ritual specialist accused of organizing a network of poisonings in the 1750s. He was captured and burned at Cap-Français in 1758. Historians debate how far the poisoning plot was real and how far it reflected planter fears, but the case entered colonial memory and shaped planters' anxieties about African religion and secret networks. What mattered for the revolution was not the success of any single conspiracy but the existence of a large population with experience of withdrawal and of communication outside the planters' sight.

Section 50.3

Free People of Color and the Colonial Racial Order

The free population of color was almost as numerous as the white population, perhaps twenty-five to thirty thousand people. It included people of mixed African and European descent, many of them the children of unions between white men and women of color or enslaved women, as well as people of African descent who had been freed or had been born free. Free women of color were numerous, often owned property in towns, and were prominent as market traders and heads of households. The gens de couleur libres were a diverse group in wealth and occupation: some were artisans and small shopkeepers, while others, especially in the South and West, were planters, particularly in coffee and indigo. Estimates of their share of the colony's land and enslaved workforce are uncertain but commonly run from a quarter to a third, a substantial minority.

Because many free people of color owned enslaved workers, the group was not an obvious ally of the enslaved. Their central grievance was discrimination among the free, not slavery itself. From the 1760s and 1770s, colonial legislation increasingly restricted them, barring them from certain professions and from officer rank in the militia, regulating their dress and their use of titles, and requiring them to adopt names distinct from those of white families. These measures hardened a color line as the group's wealth and numbers grew, and they were backed by an elaborate colonial vocabulary of racial classification, systematized by the colonial jurist Moreau de Saint-Méry, which assigned names to supposed gradations of ancestry. Historians stress that such categories were instruments of social control, not a biological taxonomy, and that the boundary between categories could be tightened by law.

Free men of color also served in the colonial militia and in military expeditions. A unit of free men of color from Saint-Domingue, the Chasseurs-Volontaires, fought on the American side at the siege of Savannah in 1779, an experience that gave participants combat training and a vocabulary of rights. Some elite free people of color had been educated in France, and a few, such as the wealthy planter Julien Raimond, became effective advocates in Paris. Alexandre Pétion and André Rigaud, who would become major figures in the revolution, belonged to this milieu. Their situation was contradictory: they were property-holding members of a slaveholding society who were nevertheless excluded from political equality on racial grounds.

This contradiction explains why the revolution began as a struggle among the free. When the French Revolution proclaimed equal rights, free people of color demanded their application, while many white colonists defended racial privilege as the foundation of the colonial order. Poorer whites, the petits blancs, had little property but defended racial status because it was their principal advantage. Wealthy planters, the grands blancs, were divided between those who favored autonomy from the metropole and those who relied on royal authority. These divisions, which meant that the free population was fractured at the moment the metropole itself fell into crisis, created the political opening in which the enslaved majority acted.

Historical Vocabulary
Gens de couleur libres

The gens de couleur libres ("free people of color") were people of African or mixed African and European descent who were legally free. The related term affranchi ("freed person") referred strictly to those who had themselves been manumitted, though contemporaries and later writers often used it loosely. Colonial documents also used terms such as mulâtre and nègre libre, which reflected the racial hierarchy of the period and are explained here for historical reading, not endorsed.

The category cut across class: it included wealthy planters and poor artisans. Its history shows that race in Saint-Domingue was a legal and political hierarchy that could be tightened or relaxed by law, not a fixed biological taxonomy. The group's demand for equality in 1789 to 1792 set the first stage of the revolution, and its leaders, including Pétion and Rigaud, remained central actors throughout.

Section 50.4

The French Revolution and the Crisis of Colonial Authority

News of the events of 1789 reached Saint-Domingue within weeks and set off a scramble over what the new politics would mean. Planters in Paris organized a lobby, the Club Massiac, to defend colonial interests, and white colonists in Saint-Domingue sought representation in the National Assembly and formed local assemblies. In 1790 a colonial assembly met at Saint-Marc and claimed broad legislative authority over the colony, defying the royal governor, while royalist officials and soldiers disputed the claim. Behind these quarrels lay a fear that the egalitarian principles of the Revolution would be turned against the plantation system, and a desire to keep decisions about slavery and race in colonial hands. The metropolitan decree of 8 March 1790 on the colonies gave local assemblies significant powers, but it left the status of free people of color ambiguous, and colonists interpreted it as excluding them.

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, adopted in August 1789, proclaimed that men are born and remain free and equal in rights. The universal phrasing was a problem for a slaveholding empire. The Assembly, wary of the economic stakes and of the colonial lobby, repeatedly avoided any measure that would touch slavery, and many deputies who criticized slavery in principle accepted its continuation in the colonies as an economic necessity. A small abolitionist group, the Société des Amis des Noirs, which included figures such as Brissot, Condorcet, and the abbé Grégoire, campaigned against the slave trade and for the rights of free people of color, but it had limited influence in 1789 and 1790. Free men of color, led by lobbyists such as Julien Raimond and Vincent Ogé, pressed their claims in Paris. They were met with sympathy from some deputies and implacable resistance from the planters' lobby.

The weakening of royal authority in the colony compounded the problem. Soldiers sometimes sided with colonial assemblies against their officers, and the governor's authority eroded; in the West, units clashed over loyalty, and in March 1791 soldiers murdered a royalist colonel, Mauduit, at Port-au-Prince. The colonial elite, never united, divided further between supporters of the assemblies and supporters of the crown, and the political language of the colony filled with words such as nation, law, and king whose meaning no one could fix. The enslaved were not outside this conversation. Authorities reported rumors that the king had granted freedom or a reduction of labor and that planters were concealing the decree. Whether or not such rumors were widely believed, they show that the enslaved understood that authority had split and that a political situation had opened which they could exploit.

It is worth stressing how much this early phase was about rights within the existing order. Almost no one in the colonial assemblies, and few in Paris, proposed ending slavery in 1789 or 1790. Abolition was not a program of the French Revolution at its outset. It emerged from events in the colony and from the exigencies of war, and understanding that sequence is a central task of the sections that follow.

Section 50.5

Vincent Ogé and the Struggle for Free Citizenship

Vincent Ogé was a prosperous free man of color from the North Province, a merchant and coffee-estate owner who had gone to Paris and associated with the Amis des Noirs. Frustrated by the Assembly's evasions, he returned to Saint-Domingue in the autumn of 1790 and wrote to the colonial authorities invoking the decree of 8 March 1790 and demanding that free men of color be recognized as active citizens. When he was rebuffed, he gathered a small armed force in the North, a few hundred men, with another free man of color, Jean-Baptiste Chavannes, a coffee planter and veteran of Savannah, who led a related movement in the Grande-Rivière area. Their objective, as best the sources allow historians to judge, was equal political rights for free men of color. Evidence suggests that Ogé did not call for the end of slavery and avoided enlisting enslaved people, which reflects the limits of the free coloreds' program in 1790.

The rising was quickly defeated by colonial militias. Ogé and his companions fled across the border into Spanish Santo Domingo, but the Spanish authorities handed them back to the French. After a trial, Ogé and Chavannes were executed at Cap-Français in February 1791 by being broken on the wheel, a punishment normally reserved for the gravest crimes and carried out publicly as a warning. Their executions were grisly and drew attention in France, where opinion was shocked by the savagery of the sentence. For the colonial authorities and white colonists, the aim was to make clear that claims to equality would be met with force.

The consequences were large. The repression radicalized free men of color, many of whom concluded that rights would have to be won by arms, and it hardened white colonists who now felt that any concession invited disaster. In May 1791 the National Assembly, moved by the news, granted political rights to a small group of free men of color, those born to free parents on both sides, but colonial whites refused to implement the decree, and in late September 1791 the Assembly effectively reversed it by deferring to colonial assemblies the question of the status of free people of color. The episode shows the Assembly's inability to hold a position in the face of colonial pressure, and it left the free coloreds with no legal remedy.

Ogé's story also reveals the limits of an interpretation that frames the revolution as the inevitable extension of Enlightenment principles. His movement was a demand for citizenship by a propertied group that accepted slavery. It was the enslaved who, in August 1791, redefined the stakes. The relationship between the two movements, the free coloreds' campaign for equality and the enslaved majority's insurrection, was therefore neither identical nor oppositional but intertwined, sometimes allied and sometimes in conflict, as later sections show.

Section 50.6

The August 1791 Insurrection

The insurrection that began on the night of 21 to 22 August 1791 in the Plaine du Nord was planned. In the preceding weeks, delegates from many plantations, among them drivers, artisans, and coachmen who had freedom of movement and standing among their fellows, met in the northern parishes to coordinate a rising that would strike estates simultaneously. Surviving evidence about these meetings is fragmentary and comes largely from colonial investigators and from later narratives, but it shows that the plot was not a spontaneous outbreak. Its organizers exploited the political confusion of 1791 and the rumors of royal favor, and they drew on religious and social networks that crossed plantation lines.

The rising spread with astonishing speed. Within days dozens of plantations in the parishes of the Plaine du Nord had been burned, and white overseers, owners, and their families were killed, though some were spared and some were held as prisoners. A prisoner who spent weeks with the insurgents, Gros, later published a narrative that offers a rare, though hostile and partial, view of the insurgent camps. By early September, thousands of the enslaved had joined, and colonial writers estimated that hundreds of plantations had been destroyed. These figures vary and were inflated by fear, but historians agree that the destruction in the North was enormous and that tens of thousands of people eventually took part in or were swept up in the insurrection. Cap-Français, defended by troops and militia, held, and refugees crowded into it.

Violence ran in all directions. The insurgents killed many whites and burned plantations, and colonial forces and militias responded with executions of captured insurgents and reprisals against enslaved people suspected of sympathizing. Colonial narratives dwell on the insurgents' atrocities and are silent about their own, while insurgents and later Haitian narratives emphasize the cruelty of slavery that provoked the rising. Historians reject the idea that either side held a monopoly on brutality, and they note that the extreme violence of the plantation system, which the enslaved had endured for generations, was part of the background that made the insurrection violent. The initial rising aimed to destroy the plantation order in the North and to secure the freedom of its participants, but its leaders did not yet have a program for governing a state.

The effects were immediate and structural. The wealth of the richest part of the colony was in ruins, white colonists were thrown into panic, and the political quarrels of 1789 and 1790 were overtaken by a war. The insurgents controlled much of the northern countryside and mountains while the colonial government held the ports. In Paris, the news arrived in the autumn and transformed debates about the colonies, since it was now clear that the colony's survival required a settlement with either the enslaved or the free coloreds. The rising of August 1791 thus began a process that would last more than a decade and would end with the creation of a new state.

Section 50.7

Bois Caïman, Revolutionary Organization, and Historical Memory

A ceremony at a place called Bois Caïman ("Caiman Wood"), traditionally dated to the night of 14 August 1791, occupies a central place in Haitian national memory. In the tradition, the enslaved leader Boukman and a priestess presided over a Vodou ritual in which participants swore to rise against slavery, and the gathering is remembered as the religious and political launch of the revolution. The story has inspired generations of Haitian writers, artists, and political activists, and it is invoked as a sign of the revolution's African spiritual roots.

The documentary evidence is thin and later. Contemporary sources from 1791 record that the insurgents gathered and planned, but they do not describe the Bois Caïman ceremony in the form later tradition gives it. The earliest detailed published account is that of the French colonist Antoine Dalmas, whose history of the revolution appeared in 1814 and which reported a nighttime assembly with ritual elements before the rising. The Haitian historian Thomas Madiou, writing in the 1840s, gave a fuller version, and further details, including the date of 14 August, the identification of a priestess, and the substance of the oath, accrued in later tellings and in twentieth-century Haitian historiography and oral tradition. The name of Cécile Fatiman as the priestess appears in later sources rather than in anything contemporary with the event.

Historians such as David Geggus have therefore cautioned that the ceremony as traditionally narrated cannot be verified, while recognizing that some form of nocturnal gathering with religious dimensions is plausible given the need for organization and the importance of ritual networks. Others, including scholars who emphasize Vodou's role in Haitian history, stress that the tradition's persistence is itself a historical fact worth understanding. A careful account must therefore distinguish three things: what happened in 1791, about which the evidence permits the conclusion that a coordinated insurrection was planned and that religious and social networks supported it; what the surviving documents record, which are partial and mostly later; and what national memory has made of the episode, which is a powerful interpretation of the revolution's meaning. These are different kinds of knowledge, and it is a mistake to treat the third as evidence for the first.

What can be said with confidence about revolutionary organization is more modest and more significant. The insurrection required communication among plantations, leadership with authority over diverse groups, and a shared sense of purpose, and the enslaved had resources for all three: markets, kinship, drivers' networks, religious societies, and the political awareness that the events of 1789 to 1791 produced. Dutty Boukman, described in sources as a driver and religious figure, was killed in battle in November 1791, and his head was displayed at Cap-Français as a warning. His death did not end the insurrection, which had by then developed other leaders and its own momentum.

Section 50.8

From Slave Revolt to Revolutionary War, 1791–1792

In the months after August 1791 the northern insurgency acquired commanders and a military organization. Jean-François, Georges Biassou, and Jeannot were the best-known leaders, and they took on titles and ranks borrowed from royal and military usage. Jeannot, known for ferocity, was executed on Biassou's orders late in 1791. The insurgent camps in the northern hills organized supply, discipline, and intelligence, and they included people with skills and experience from African wars and from the colonial militia. Toussaint, who had been a free man and a plantation employee before the rising, joined Biassou's forces in the autumn of 1791 and served as a secretary and in a medical capacity, according to later accounts, a first step in a rapid rise.

The leaders spoke in the language of royal loyalty, using the name of the king of France and sometimes claiming to fight on his behalf against the colonial assemblies and the planters who opposed royal authority. Historians interpret this language as a political tactic. It positioned the insurgents within a recognizable political tradition, allowed them to claim legitimacy against the colonists, and created room for negotiation with royal officials. In late 1791 Jean-François and Biassou communicated with a delegation of commissioners and with the colonial assembly, reportedly seeking freedom for a limited group of leaders and the return of the remaining insurgents to work. The assembly rejected these overtures. The episode is often cited to show that the earliest leaders' demands were limited, but it is also possible that the offers were tactical, and the evidence does not allow certainty. What is clear is that insurgents in 1791 did not possess a single fixed program of independence.

Elsewhere the revolution took different forms. In the West and South, the principal fighting in 1791 involved white militias and free men of color, and the enslaved were drawn in unevenly. In November 1791 much of Port-au-Prince burned in fighting between whites and free coloreds, and a series of local alliances and betrayals followed. In the South, free men of color under leaders including André Rigaud began to build armed forces and to hold territory. Alliances shifted, with free coloreds at times allying with royal troops, at times with white colonists, and at times with insurgent bands. The colony was thus divided into zones of competing authority, and no side had the strength to impose order.

Spain, which held the eastern part of the island, began to play a role. Spanish authorities in Santo Domingo gave refuge and supplies to some insurgents and encouraged them to attack French positions, and by 1793 the connection had become formal. For the Spanish crown, the aim was not to endorse emancipation but to weaken a rival empire and to expand its influence, and arming the insurgents served that purpose. For the insurgent leaders, Spanish support provided arms, a refuge across the border, and legitimacy through royal commissions. The war of 1791 and 1792 was therefore a complex struggle with at least four active parties, white colonists, free coloreds, insurgent leaders, and royal officials, and with Spain waiting at the border.

Section 50.9

Commissioners, Galbaud, and the Crisis of 1793

In Paris, the news of the insurrection and the continuing deadlock led to the law of 4 April 1792, which granted political equality to all free people of color and free Black people in the colonies. It was a victory for the free coloreds' campaign and for the abolitionist minority, passed in part because the Assembly feared that denying rights would drive the free coloreds into alliance with the enslaved. The Assembly sent three civil commissioners with several thousand troops to enforce the law. Two of them, the radical lawyer Léger-Félicité Sonthonax and Étienne Polverel, dominated the mission after the third, Ailhaud, left. They arrived in September 1792 with orders to restore order and implement racial equality among the free, not to end slavery.

The commissioners met resistance from white colonists and royalist officers, who regarded them as agents of a revolution they distrusted. Sonthonax dissolved the colonial assembly, dismissed the governor, and moved against opponents, while seeking allies among the free people of color, who now held legal equality and were recruited into the new regime's forces. The commissioners' position was weak: they controlled few reliable troops, the northern countryside was in insurgent hands, and the white population was hostile. The execution of Louis XVI in January 1793 and the outbreak of war between France and both Spain and Britain in February and March 1793 threatened the colony with invasion and cut off support from France.

The crisis came with the arrival of a new governor, François-Thomas Galbaud, a white colonial proprietor who reached Cap-Français in May 1793. The commissioners, who distrusted him, dismissed him, but he had the support of many white colonists and of sailors in the naval squadron in the harbor. In June 1793, naval forces landed, and street fighting erupted in Cap-Français over several days. The commissioners, outnumbered, released and armed Black and mixed-race prisoners and called on the insurgents camped in the hills to enter the city, promising freedom to those who fought for the Republic. Large parts of Cap-Français burned, and thousands of white colonists fled by ship, many to ports in the United States.

The fire at Cap-Français was a turning point. The commissioners now depended on formerly enslaved fighters, who had demonstrated their strength and who expected something in return. In the countryside, northern insurgent leaders, including those allied with Spain, were courting the same people with their own offers. The commissioners had come to enforce racial equality among free people and found themselves bidding for the allegiance of the enslaved. The next step, emancipation, was therefore both an act of conviction by Sonthonax and a response to a military and political emergency in which the colony might be lost to Spain, Britain, or its own planters.

Section 50.10

Emancipation in Saint-Domingue, 1793–1794

On 29 August 1793, Sonthonax proclaimed the abolition of slavery in the North Province. The proclamation came after the burning of Cap-Français and in the face of Spanish and British pressure, and it followed efforts by insurgent leaders allied with Spain to win followers with their own appeals. On the same day a proclamation issued from a camp in the north in the name of Toussaint called on Black people to join him, an appeal historians read as evidence that Toussaint was by then a leader in his own right and was shaping his followers' expectations. Sonthonax acted on his own authority, without prior instructions from Paris, in a war emergency. His motives combined real conviction, since he was a committed opponent of slavery, with pragmatism, since freedom was the price of loyalty.

Polverel, who was in the West and South, followed in a series of measures in September and October 1793 that culminated in general emancipation in the South on 31 October. Polverel was more cautious than Sonthonax and had doubts about the colony's economic future without plantation labor. In late October 1793 he issued regulations that freed the enslaved but required them to remain on their estates as paid workers, receiving a share of the crop, and that restricted movement without passes and prohibited vagrancy. The regulations show that the commissioners understood emancipation as an end to ownership of people but not to compulsory plantation labor, a distinction that would shape the politics of the following decade.

The distinctions among legal emancipation, citizenship, plantation labor, and freedom of movement are central. Emancipation meant that no person could be owned. Citizenship, which the Convention would declare in 1794, meant that the formerly enslaved held the rights of French citizens. Plantation labor was the continuing obligation to work on estates that the commissioners and later leaders thought essential for revenue, supplies, and military support. Freedom of movement, the ability to leave the estate and farm one's own land, was what many of the freed expected and what the regulations withheld. For the formerly enslaved the gap between what was proclaimed and what was practiced was a source of disappointment and resistance, and it would recur under Toussaint and his successors.

The proclamations did not end the war. Spain and Britain continued to attack, white planters in some areas welcomed the British, and many insurgent leaders in Spanish service did not accept the commissioners' authority. Emancipation by local officials was also insecure: it lacked the force of metropolitan law, and it depended on the commissioners' ability to hold power. That uncertainty explains why the formerly enslaved watched closely for the verdict of Paris, and why the Convention's decree of February 1794 mattered even though it ratified what had already happened.

Section 50.11

The Abolition Decree and the International War

In Paris, a delegation of three deputies from Saint-Domingue, Jean-Baptiste Belley, a formerly enslaved man, Jean-Baptiste Mills, a man of mixed descent, and Louis-Pierre Dufay, a white colonist, arrived in early 1794. On 4 February 1794 (16 Pluviôse of Year II), the National Convention decreed that slavery was abolished in all the French colonies and that all men living there, without distinction of color, were French citizens with full constitutional rights. It was the first general abolition of slavery by a major colonial power. Historians read the decree as a response to the situation in Saint-Domingue rather than a gift from the metropole: it ratified emancipation already proclaimed by the commissioners, it was shaped by the lobbying of the deputies and the commissioners' reports, and it was intended to keep the insurgents' armies loyal to the Republic against Spain and Britain.

The decree mattered differently from the colonial proclamations. It gave emancipation the force of metropolitan law and a claim on the Republic's authority, which made reversal more difficult for French governments, at least until 1802. It linked the insurgents' freedom to the Republic's survival in a way that colonial proclamations could not, and it gave Black and mixed-race officers a basis for loyalty to France. Its geographic effects were uneven. In Guadeloupe, the Republican officer Victor Hugues retook the island from Britain in 1794 and enforced abolition, while Martinique, under British occupation, remained a slave society. Slavery continued elsewhere in the French empire, including the Indian Ocean colonies, where local assemblies resisted the decree.

Meanwhile the international war reshaped Saint-Domingue. Britain, which in February 1793 had accepted an agreement with white planters promising to preserve slavery, landed troops at Jérémie in September 1793 and took the Môle-Saint-Nicolas and other points. In June 1794 British forces occupied Port-au-Prince. Spain, in control of much of the northern interior through its insurgent allies, advanced as well. For a time it seemed that Saint-Domingue would be partitioned between Spain, Britain, and a shrinking Republican zone. British planters and ministers saw an opportunity to seize the world's richest colony and to remove a revolutionary example from the Caribbean, where Jamaica's slaveholders watched events anxiously.

The war was also a struggle over the meaning of emancipation. British occupation meant the preservation or restoration of slavery in the territories it held, while the Republic offered freedom. Spain offered ranks and titles to its Black allies but not general emancipation. Formerly enslaved people had every reason to prefer the Republic, provided its promise could be believed, and that calculation shaped the choices of Toussaint and others in 1794. By mid-1794, a combination of the Convention's decree, the military collapse of Republican forces in some regions, and political maneuvering had set the stage for a decisive realignment.

Section 50.12

Toussaint Louverture and the French Republic

Toussaint, born around 1743 on the Bréda plantation near Haut-du-Cap, held positions of some trust as an enslaved man, was freed some years before the revolution, probably in the 1770s, and was literate and a devout Catholic. By 1793 he had risen from secretary and physician to Biassou to the commander of an independent force, trained and disciplined, serving the Spanish crown with the title of a general. Later he adopted the name Louverture ("the opening"), whose origin is debated. He was an exceptionally capable organizer and diplomat, and he assembled around himself a cadre of officers, among them his nephew Moïse, Charles Bélair, and, from 1794, Jean-Jacques Dessalines. Historians caution against presenting him as the sole protagonist: the revolution was made by thousands of insurgents, by other commanders such as Jean-François, Biassou, Macaya, and Sans-Souci, by free men of color such as Rigaud and Pétion, and by the commissioners and deputies in Paris and the colony.

In May 1794, Toussaint broke with Spain and joined the French Republic. Most accounts link the decision to news of the Convention's decree, which offered the freedom that Spain could not. Some scholars add that he had already been in contact with the Republican governor Étienne Laveaux and had reason to distrust his Spanish employers. The defection was militarily significant: Toussaint brought a trained force and seized positions held by Spain's allies in the north, and in the following months, working with Laveaux, he drove Spanish-allied forces from much of the territory they had held. Jean-François and Biassou, who remained in Spanish service, lost ground and later left the island.

His relationship with the Republic was one of cooperation and growing independence. In 1796, when the mixed-race officer Villatte imprisoned Laveaux at Cap-Français, Toussaint marched on the city and freed him, and Laveaux, in gratitude, named him lieutenant governor. The commissioner Sonthonax returned to the colony in 1796 and cooperated with Toussaint at first, then clashed with him, and in 1797 Toussaint arranged his departure under the pretext of sending him to France as a deputy. By then Toussaint had become the dominant Black commander and, increasingly, the colony's de facto ruler. He used the language of loyalty to the Republic and the defense of freedom, while building an army and administration that depended on his personal authority.

Toussaint's rise was a political achievement as much as a military one. He cultivated allies among white planters and French officials, created a network of officers loyal to him, and presented himself as the guarantor of order and emancipation. His position rested on the army and on the plausibility of his claim to defend freedom against restoration of slavery. The tensions in that position, between loyalty to France and autonomy, between emancipation and the demands of a plantation economy, and between the aspirations of the formerly enslaved and the priorities of a military state, run through the rest of his career.

Section 50.13

Defeating Britain and Building Autonomous Power, 1795–1798

The British occupation cost far more than its planners expected. Britain committed tens of thousands of soldiers to the Caribbean over the course of the 1790s, and in Saint-Domingue yellow fever, malaria, and dysentery killed large numbers of them, in some years more than died in combat. European soldiers arriving from temperate climates had no acquired immunity to yellow fever, whereas many people born in the colony or in Africa had some resistance, though no population was immune from every illness. The British held coastal enclaves, notably Môle-Saint-Nicolas, Port-au-Prince, and Jérémie, but they could not control the interior, and the formerly enslaved fighters who opposed them, under Toussaint in the West and Artibonite and under Rigaud in the South, proved effective in guerrilla and conventional operations. The campaign became one of the costliest failures of the British army in the French Revolutionary Wars.

Toussaint's armies grew in size and discipline. His officer corps combined former insurgents and veterans of the colonial militia, and he insisted on drill, supply, and administration. In 1797 and 1798 he campaigned in the West and against positions in the Artibonite and the Mirebalais, while Rigaud pressed the British in the South. Peace with Spain at Basel in 1795, by which Spain ceded Santo Domingo to France, removed one enemy, and the British, facing mounting losses and wider strategic demands, began to look for a way out. The commander Thomas Maitland concluded that the occupation was unsustainable, and in 1798 he opened negotiations with Toussaint.

The Maitland agreement, concluded in the late summer of 1798, provided for the withdrawal of British forces from their remaining positions, including Port-au-Prince, and included assurances that Toussaint would not export revolution to Jamaica. A commercial understanding opened trade between Saint-Domingue and British-controlled shipping. The French agent Gabriel Hédouville, who had arrived in 1798 with instructions to rein in Toussaint, resented the independent diplomacy and tried to undermine him by encouraging Rigaud and by stirring disputes among officers. A revolt at Fort-Liberté led by Moïse in October 1798 gave Toussaint the pretext to force Hédouville out, and Hédouville left the colony. The episode is a measure of how far metropolitan authority had receded.

Toussaint also dealt directly with the United States. Amid the Quasi-War between the United States and France, the Adams administration sent an agent, Edward Stevens, to Saint-Domingue, and a trade arrangement in 1799 allowed American merchants to supply the colony and, in effect, to treat Toussaint as the effective ruler. The colony thus conducted an independent foreign policy while remaining nominally French. By the end of 1798 Toussaint controlled most of the colony's territory except the South, commanded a large army, administered justice and finance through appointed officials, and negotiated with Britain and the United States. He had created autonomous power inside a formally French framework, a position that Paris would eventually find intolerable.

Section 50.14

The War of the Knives, 1799–1800

The South Province remained under the control of André Rigaud, a free man of color and a capable military commander who had fought the British and who resisted Toussaint's growing authority. The rivalry was partly personal and partly institutional: both men claimed to represent the Republic, both commanded armies, and the ambiguity of Hédouville's departure left the question of supreme command unresolved. Rigaud's followers included many free men of color, among them Alexandre Pétion and Jean-Pierre Boyer, and Toussaint's included both Black officers and some mixed-race officers. The conflict is often described as a war between Black and mixed-race forces, but the evidence shows a more complicated picture, with leaders and soldiers of all backgrounds on both sides and with the colony's political divisions, rather than color alone, driving the fighting.

Fighting began in mid-1799 and became known as the War of the Knives, from the savagery with which it was fought. Toussaint's forces advanced into the western and southern peninsulas, while Rigaud's held key towns, and sieges, including the defense of Jacmel under Pétion, dragged on into 1800. The war involved massacres of prisoners and civilians by both sides. Dessalines, entrusted with the campaign in the south, gained a reputation for ruthlessness that would mark his later career. The war also drew in outside powers: the United States navy, as part of its quasi-war with France, supported Toussaint by blockading Rigaud's ports, a sign that the colony's internal conflict had international dimensions.

Toussaint's victory in the summer of 1800 left Rigaud exiled in France and the South under Toussaint's control. The consequences were serious. The war killed many thousands, left bitterness among the free coloreds of the South, many of whom were persecuted afterwards, and showed that the revolution's internal conflicts could be as bloody as its external ones. It also removed the last significant rival to Toussaint within the colony and made him unquestioned master of Saint-Domingue. At the same time it fractured the coalition of Black and mixed-race officers that had defended emancipation, a division that would reappear in the struggle for independence and in later Haitian politics.

Section 50.15

Freedom, Labor, Land, and the Reconstruction of Saint-Domingue

With peace in sight, Toussaint turned to the reconstruction of an economy that years of war had devastated. He believed that the colony's survival, and with it the security of freedom, depended on restoring export agriculture, which supplied revenue to pay soldiers, buy arms and supplies, and sustain trade with Britain and the United States. In October 1800 he issued a regulation on cultivation, extending the principles of Polverel's earlier rules. Agricultural workers, the cultivateurs, were free people but were required to remain on the estates where they worked, to labor under supervision, and to receive a portion of the crop, commonly set at a quarter. Movement without authorization was prohibited, vagrancy was punished, and the army was charged with enforcing the system. Dessalines was appointed to oversee the policy in the field and became associated with its severity.

The formerly enslaved understood freedom differently from the generals. For many field workers, freedom meant control over their own time and labor, the ability to leave the estate where they had been enslaved, to farm their own plots, and to sell food in local markets, as they had done on provision grounds before emancipation. Some had already occupied abandoned land during the war. They regarded forced labor on sugar estates, supervised by officers, as an extension of slavery under another name. The tension between the two views, between a state that needed export revenue and workers who wanted independence, was among the revolution's central unresolved conflicts, and it would persist for decades.

The reconstruction also reshaped landholding. Toussaint encouraged white planters who had fled to return and manage estates, and he leased or assigned abandoned properties to his officers, many of whom became planters themselves. A new elite of military proprietors emerged, and the plantation sector revived to a considerable degree, though at lower levels than before 1791. Women played significant roles as laborers, market traders, and household managers, and their work in food production was essential to the colony's survival. Resistance to the labor regime took the form of desertion, absenteeism, and occasional revolt, and in October 1801 an uprising in the North led by Toussaint's nephew Moïse, who sympathized with the cultivators, was suppressed. Toussaint had Moïse executed in November 1801, a decision that alienated many in the army and that shows the costs of the policy.

Historians disagree about how to judge this regime. Some emphasize that Toussaint and his officers were acting under severe constraints, facing external threats and a ruined economy, and that their labor policy was a continuation of what the commissioners and the Republic had established. Others, in the tradition of Carolyn Fick's study of the revolution from below, stress that the regime betrayed the aspirations of the formerly enslaved and anticipated the coercive character of later Haitian governments. Both views capture part of the truth. What is clear is that emancipation transformed legal status without resolving the question of who would control land and labor, a question that Haiti, and later other post-emancipation societies treated in this textbook, would confront for generations.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultivateur

A cultivateur ("cultivator") was the legal status of agricultural laborers in revolutionary Saint-Domingue after emancipation. Cultivateurs were free people, entitled to a share of the crop or wages, but obliged to remain on the plantation and work under supervision. The term shows how emancipation was written into law while compulsion continued.

Students should distinguish the legal status from the experience. Cultivateurs often resisted the system by leaving estates, working their own plots, or negotiating conditions, and the tension between their view of freedom and the leaders' view of economic necessity recurs through Haitian history.

Everyday Life and Social History

Provision Grounds, Markets, and Households in a Society at War

Before and during the revolution, much of everyday life for the enslaved turned on the provision ground and the Sunday market. On these small plots, women and men grew food, raised animals, and sold surplus, and the markets linked plantations and towns. These routines continued during the war, when armies required supplies and cultivators sold food to both sides. Women were central to this economy, as marketers, cooks, and household managers. After emancipation, many cultivators sought to expand work on their own plots and resisted the demand to spend all their time on plantation crops, a pattern that anticipated the peasant economy of nineteenth-century Haiti.

War also disrupted households. Families were separated by sales, flight, military service, and death, and children grew up in camps, on the road, or in towns swollen with refugees. Disease, particularly yellow fever, malaria, and dysentery, killed soldiers and civilians alike, and shortages of food and medicine followed the burning of fields. Religious life continued through Vodou societies and Catholic observance, sometimes overlapping, and religious networks provided community support. The evidence for these experiences is thin and scattered across plantation records, military reports, travelers' accounts, and later oral memory, and historians must reconstruct them with care.

Section 50.16

Santo Domingo and the Constitution of 1801

Toussaint's next step was to unify the island. Under the Treaty of Basel of 1795, Spain had ceded Santo Domingo to France, but the cession had never been implemented. The Spanish colony was thinly populated, with a smaller proportion of enslaved people than Saint-Domingue and an economy based on cattle ranching, and its Spanish officials and elite distrusted the revolutionary regime next door. Toussaint feared that the eastern colony could serve as a base for a French or British attack, and that its continued slavery undermined the principle of general emancipation. In early 1801, after obtaining reluctant authorization from the French agent Roume, he sent his army into Santo Domingo, and the Spanish governor surrendered in January. Toussaint proclaimed the abolition of slavery in the east, though its practical effect was limited by the short duration of his control and the small size of the enslaved population there.

The occupation made Toussaint ruler of all Hispaniola. He then convened a central assembly of colonial notables, most of them white and mixed-race planters and officials, to draft a constitution. The text was approved and promulgated on 7 July 1801. It named Toussaint governor for life, with the right to choose his successor, declared that slavery was abolished forever, established Catholicism as the only publicly practiced religion, required the continuation of agricultural labor, and described the colony as a French possession governed by its own special laws. It was sent to Paris after promulgation, not before, a sequence that Napoleon Bonaparte regarded as an affront.

The constitution displayed a deliberate ambiguity. By retaining French sovereignty in form, it avoided an open declaration of independence, which would have invited immediate attack and which Toussaint, for various reasons, may not have wanted. By assigning legislative power to the colony and executive power to Toussaint for life, it created autonomous government in practice. Historians debate how to read this ambiguity, as sincere loyalty, as strategic camouflage, or as a transitional arrangement whose logic led toward independence, and the debate recurs later in the chapter. Whatever Toussaint's intentions, the document convinced Napoleon that the colony had escaped French control.

It was then sent to Paris, where it reached Napoleon Bonaparte.

It establishes the Catholic religion as the only publicly practiced faith. It makes Toussaint governor for life with the right to name his successor. It requires that agricultural labor be maintained and declares that the colony is governed by its own laws while remaining a French possession.

Analysis: The text is simultaneously radical and conservative. Its permanent abolition of slavery and its guarantee of freedom regardless of color go further than the legislation of the Convention in ensuring continuity of rights, and its assertion of colonial lawmaking power challenges metropolitan sovereignty. Yet the same document concentrates power in one man, privileges one religion, and reinforces the plantation labor system. It was drafted in the name of the colony by an assembly that did not represent the cultivators, and it says little about their demands for land. The document also shows ambiguity about independence: it retains French sovereignty in form while making the colony autonomous in practice, which fueled later debates about Toussaint's aims. Napoleon, reading it, saw a challenge to his authority and to French control of a major colony.

Limitations: The text was drafted by a body of notables convened under Toussaint's authority, and the cultivators whose labor it regulated had no voice in it. It records what the regime wished to establish, not how its provisions were experienced or enforced, and it was written for several audiences at once, including French officials and foreign merchants. Toussaint's own intentions about independence cannot be read from its wording alone, since a document drafted under threat of French retaliation would have had reasons to stress loyalty.

Section 50.17

Napoleon and the Leclerc Expedition

Napoleon Bonaparte, who seized power in France in 1799 and became First Consul, wanted to reassert metropolitan control over the colonies and recover their economic value. His interest in Saint-Domingue was bound up with a wider vision of an American empire. In 1800 Spain secretly agreed to return Louisiana to France, and Saint-Domingue was to be the productive core of a Caribbean and Mississippi system. Toussaint's constitution, with its assertion of autonomy and its governor-for-life clause, made the colony's loss of French control appear complete. Peace with Britain, in preliminaries in October 1801 and the Treaty of Amiens in March 1802, freed French ships and soldiers for an American campaign.

Napoleon appointed his brother-in-law, General Charles Victor Emmanuel Leclerc, to command the expedition. The force that left French ports in December 1801 and reached Saint-Domingue in early 1802 numbered roughly twenty thousand soldiers, and reinforcements followed, so that the cumulative total committed over the next two years was considerably higher. Estimates vary, with many scholarly accounts placing the total on the order of forty thousand or more, and the number that died, mostly from disease, was very large. Leclerc's wife Pauline Bonaparte, Napoleon's sister, accompanied him. Officers of Black and mixed-race backgrounds, including Rigaud, who had been exiled in France, traveled with the fleet, to be used in the effort to gain support.

Public proclamations told the population that France would respect freedom, and Leclerc initially presented himself as a restorer of order, not of slavery. The government's private instructions, and its parallel decisions, tell a different story. Documents show that Napoleon planned to disarm the Black army and remove its leaders, and most historians conclude that the expedition's leaders intended eventually to restore slavery in Saint-Domingue, once the army had been broken. The question of how explicitly and how soon Napoleon intended this, and how much he was deterred by fear of renewed resistance, remains debated. The legal context is important. A law of 20 May 1802 maintained slavery in colonies, such as Martinique, that were returned to France under the Treaty of Amiens and where abolition had not been enforced, and did not by itself restore slavery in places where it had been abolished. In Guadeloupe, where abolition had been enforced, French forces under General Antoine Richepanse landed in May 1802, and a separate arrêté of July 1802 restored slavery there. Treating these as distinct measures is necessary for accuracy, but together they made French intentions plain to observers in Saint-Domingue.

Section 50.18

Crête-à-Pierrot, Surrender, and the Arrest of Toussaint

The fleet arrived off Saint-Domingue in late January and early February 1802, and French troops landed at several points. Leclerc demanded that the commanders submit. Henry Christophe, commanding at Cap-Français, replied that he could not surrender the city without orders from Toussaint, and when the French landed anyway, he burned much of it and withdrew. Dessalines and other commanders likewise destroyed towns, including Léogâne, rather than leave them to the French. Toussaint, who had expected negotiation, found himself in a full-scale war. He issued orders to resist and to withdraw to the mountains, while his officers fought to defend the colony's ports and interior.

The most celebrated engagement of the spring was at Crête-à-Pierrot, a fort in the Artibonite valley defended in March 1802 by a garrison of roughly one to two thousand under Dessalines and the officer Louis Lamartinière, a force that included women and children. French forces attacked for about three weeks, and the garrison inflicted heavy casualties before breaking out at night when the fort could no longer hold. Tradition associates Lamartinière's wife, Marie-Jeanne, with the defense, and historians treat the details with caution while accepting that women took part. The siege showed the Haitian army's capacity for disciplined defense and cost the French many men, though the French took the position.

Disease was changing the war. Yellow fever began to take hold among the French troops in the spring, and the death toll rose steeply through the summer. Leclerc, who had hoped for a quick campaign, found his army depleted. In April and May 1802, the Haitian commanders, under pressure and uncertain of the future, began to negotiate. Christophe came to terms in April, Dessalines followed, and Toussaint agreed in May to lay down arms and retire to his plantations near Ennery, with the understanding that he would be left in peace. Historians debate why he accepted, whether from exhaustion, a belief in the French promises, a calculation that the fever would wear down the French, or a desire to avoid further destruction.

On 7 June 1802, General Jean-Baptiste Brunet invited Toussaint to a meeting at the Georges plantation in the Ennery area, inland from Gonaïves, and had him seized. He was taken to the coast and deported with members of his family on the frigate Créole to France. Imprisoned at the Fort de Joux in the Jura mountains, he wrote a memoir addressed to Napoleon defending his conduct, and he died there on 7 April 1803, in cold and isolation. A famous remark attributed to him at his departure, comparing his removal to the felling of a tree whose roots remain, is reported in later sources and cannot be verified as to its wording. The arrest of Toussaint did not end the war. Instead it removed the one leader who might have negotiated a settlement and deprived the French of the legitimacy they had claimed, while leaving his lieutenants, Dessalines, Christophe, and Pétion, nominally serving France.

Section 50.19

Dessalines, Rochambeau, and the War for Independence

News from Guadeloupe sealed the matter. Louis Delgrès, a mixed-race officer who led resistance to the French reconquest, issued a proclamation addressed to the world protesting the restoration of slavery and died with several hundred followers in an explosion at Matouba on 28 May 1802 rather than surrender. The news of Guadeloupe and the arrival of reports that slavery was being re-established spread through Saint-Domingue in the late summer. Officers and soldiers who had joined the French command began to fear for their freedom. Insurgent bands under leaders such as Sans-Souci, Macaya, and Lamour Derance had never stopped fighting, and in October 1802 Pétion and other officers who had served with the French defected, followed by Christophe and Dessalines. The French executed Charles Bélair and his wife Sanité, a Black officer remembered as a fighter, in October 1802.

Leclerc died of yellow fever on 2 November 1802 and was succeeded by General Donatien de Rochambeau. Rochambeau's tactics were harsher than his predecessor's. French forces used mass drownings and executions, and according to French and Haitian accounts imported dogs from Cuba to hunt captured prisoners. These methods, intended to terrorize, hardened resistance and ended any chance of compromise. Yellow fever, meanwhile, continued to devastate the French garrisons, killing far more soldiers than combat did. Reinforcements arrived but could not make up the losses, and by 1803 the French army was a shadow of its original strength.

The coalition that fought the French was fragile. It joined former rivals, including Black officers who had served under Toussaint, mixed-race officers who had supported Rigaud and been persecuted in the War of the Knives, and bands of insurgents who owed allegiance to no one. Dessalines emerged as its commander through a combination of military reputation and a willingness to forge alliances. At a gathering at Arcahaie in May 1803, the leaders reportedly agreed to recognize him as commander in chief. According to a national tradition, the white band was removed from the French tricolor to create a new flag, and Catherine Flon is said to have sewn it. The documentary basis for the flag story is disputed, and historians treat it as a national tradition. The gathering itself, in contrast, reflects a real political consolidation.

The nature of the war was changing. In 1802, Haitian leaders had fought to defend emancipation within the French Empire. By 1803, with slavery restored in Guadeloupe, Toussaint dead in a French prison, and French soldiers drowning prisoners, most concluded that freedom could not be secured under French rule. Independence became the explicit goal, though the date and process of that shift were gradual. The renewal of war between France and Britain in May 1803 meant that the British navy blockaded the Haitian coast and cut French supply lines, an external development that strengthened the Haitian position.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Emancipation within the Empire, or Independence?

The question: Did the leaders of the revolution, and especially Toussaint Louverture, seek independence from France, or did they seek freedom and citizenship within a French Empire that independence became necessary only when Napoleon threatened to restore slavery?

Interpretation A — Freedom within the empire: Many historians emphasize that Toussaint consistently professed loyalty to France, that the abolition of 1794 had given the Republic a claim on the insurgents' allegiance, and that the 1801 Constitution kept the colony French. On this view independence was a reluctant outcome, forced by Napoleon's policy of restoring slavery, and the revolution is best understood as a struggle to make the universal promises of French citizenship real.
Interpretation B — Independence as a long-term goal or logical trajectory: Other historians stress Toussaint's autonomous diplomacy, his occupation of Santo Domingo, his constitution, and his creation of a powerful army as steps toward de facto sovereignty, and point to the roots of the insurgency in an African-derived political and religious culture that was not simply French. On this view, independence was implicit in the revolution's logic, even if its leaders could not say so openly.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Toussaint's letters and proclamations, which are formal and often strategically loyal, on the 1801 Constitution, and on accounts by observers and by Haitian historians, who wrote with the benefit of knowing the outcome. Toussaint's own voice survives mainly in documents dictated to secretaries and shaped for French audiences, which limits what they reveal about his private intentions.

Why it matters: The question affects how the revolution is placed in the history of the Atlantic world. If its aim was citizenship, it was a radical extension of French revolutionary universalism. If its aim was independence, it belongs to the story of anti-colonial struggle. Many scholars now suggest that these were not mutually exclusive, and that aims changed under pressure: leaders could be sincerely loyal to French citizenship while building the capacity to defend freedom without France.

Section 50.20

Vertières and the Founding of Haiti, 1803–1804

By the autumn of 1803, the French had been driven back to a few coastal positions, and Rochambeau was besieged in Cap-Français with the remnant of his army. Napoleon, facing the renewed European war and the collapse of the expedition, had abandoned his American plans, and in April 1803 he agreed to sell Louisiana to the United States. The failure in Saint-Domingue was one factor in that decision, though historians disagree about how much weight to assign it among Napoleon's motives. The Caribbean disaster therefore shaped events well beyond the island.

The decisive battle took place on 18 November 1803 at Vertières, a fortified position just south of Cap-Français. Haitian forces under Dessalines attacked the French line, and a commander named François Capois, known as Capois-la-Mort, became famous for leading charges under heavy fire, so that, according to tradition, French drummers halted in admiration. The battle did not destroy the French army, but it convinced Rochambeau that resistance was futile. He negotiated an evacuation with Dessalines and, rather than surrender to the Haitians, capitulated to the British squadron offshore. The French left Cap-Français and the remaining positions in the following weeks. The French army had lost tens of thousands of soldiers and sailors, most of them to disease. Haitian losses, military and civilian, over the whole period from 1791 to 1804 are far less certain but were very large, with estimates running well over a hundred thousand.

On 1 January 1804, at Gonaïves, Dessalines and his generals proclaimed the independence of the country. The Act of Independence, drafted by Dessalines's secretary Louis Félix Boisrond-Tonnerre, renounced French rule, denounced the crimes committed by the French, and pledged the signers to live free or die and never to submit to a colonial master. Dessalines was acclaimed governor-general for life. The country was given the name Haiti, from Ayiti, an Indigenous name for the island, a deliberate choice that tied the new state to the island's earlier history and rejected the colonial name. This should not be read as a claim of descent from the Taíno, who had been virtually destroyed centuries before, but as an appropriation of Indigenous memory by people constructing a new national identity, an act of the kind later creole nationalists elsewhere in the Americas would also make (Chapter 58).

Independence did not end French presence on Hispaniola: Santo Domingo in the east, which Toussaint had occupied in 1801, remained under French control in the following years, and Haiti's borders and security were unsettled. But the declaration marked the moment at which the revolution's leaders chose sovereignty over incorporation in a French empire that had tried to restore slavery. It was the first independent state in Latin America and the Caribbean, and its founders, many of them formerly enslaved, understood that its survival depended on prevailing in a world of hostile empires.

Paraphrase of central provisions: The text declares that the signers renounce forever any allegiance to France, which it accuses of cruelty against the people of the island. It states a resolve to live free or die and to prevent any colonist or master from ever again establishing himself on the soil. It adopts the name Haiti, from the original name of the island, and it calls on the signers and the people to swear an oath to defend the independence of the country.

Analysis: The Act is simultaneously a legal declaration, a political oath, and an indictment of French colonialism. By renouncing France and adopting the Indigenous name, it breaks with the colonial past and asserts a new identity. Its language of oath and vengeance reflects the experience of the war, especially the atrocities of Rochambeau's campaign, and anticipates the harsh measures taken in 1804. The signers were the military leaders of the revolution, and the document speaks in the name of the people while being issued by the army.

Limitations: The text was written by a single author for a ceremonial occasion, and its rhetoric cannot be treated as a transparent record of what ordinary cultivators thought or wanted. Accounts of its composition, including a famous remark attributed to Boisrond-Tonnerre about the materials needed to write it, come from later tradition and cannot be verified. The document says little about land, labor, or the status of women, issues that mattered greatly to the formerly enslaved, and it does not describe the diplomatic strategy the new state would pursue.

Section 50.21

The New Haiti and the Revolution's Hemispheric Repercussions

The new state faced immense problems. Its economy and population had been devastated, its leaders feared a French return, and no foreign government recognized it. Dessalines held the title of governor-general for life, and in early October 1804 he was proclaimed emperor as Jacques I and crowned within days, adopting a form of rule modeled on Napoleonic France, a choice that reflected both his sense of the need for strong authority and the conventions of rank current in the period. The state's immediate priorities were security, fortification, supplies for the army, and the revival of export agriculture to pay for both.

Between February and April 1804, Dessalines ordered the killing of most of the remaining French colonists in Haiti. The massacres, which scholarly estimates place at several thousand victims, though exact numbers are uncertain, spared some groups, including priests, doctors, and others considered useful, and varied by region, with some local commanders refusing or limiting the orders. In a proclamation in April, Dessalines presented himself as having avenged the crimes of the French. Historians interpret the killings in different ways. Some stress the context of Rochambeau's atrocities and fear of a returning army, and see the massacres as a drastic measure of security and revenge. Others emphasize the horror of violence against civilians, many of whom had no part in French atrocities, and its effect on Haiti's international image. Both elements belong to an honest account, and the violence was used by slaveholding powers as proof that emancipation led to chaos.

The revolution's effects elsewhere were large. Tens of thousands of white planters, free people of color, and enslaved people who accompanied them left Saint-Domingue between 1791 and 1804 for Jamaica, Cuba, Louisiana, and the United States. In eastern Cuba, French planters and their enslaved workers helped found a coffee economy and brought expertise in sugar production. The collapse of Saint-Domingue's exports removed the world's largest sugar producer from the market, raised prices, and opened a space that Cuba, and later Brazil, exploited through the massive expansion of slave-based plantation agriculture. In this sense the revolution contributed to the growth of the "second slavery" in the nineteenth century, an irony that complicates any simple story of the revolution as the beginning of the end of slavery (Chapters 61 and 66).

The United States was affected in several ways. The failure of the French expedition contributed to Napoleon's decision to sell Louisiana in 1803, doubling the territory of the United States. Refugees arrived in American ports, bringing reports of the revolution, and slaveholders in the South feared contagion while Black communities and abolitionists drew inspiration. The Jefferson administration, sensitive to Southern opinion, refused recognition to the new state. Authorities in Cuba, Jamaica, Brazil, and elsewhere tightened controls on enslaved people and attributed conspiracies to Haitian influence, though the extent of direct influence in each case is debated. In Venezuela, a revolt in Coro in 1795 had already drawn on reports from the Caribbean.

For Spanish America, Haiti was a warning and a resource. Creole elites in Venezuela, New Granada, and the Río de la Plata, who feared both Spanish rule and the possibility of a social revolution among Black and Indigenous people, tended to seek independence in ways that preserved their social order, and the memory of Saint-Domingue shaped their rhetoric about "race war" and their caution about arming slaves (Chapters 51 through 57). Haiti would later provide material support to Latin American independence movements, most famously to Simón Bolívar in 1815 and 1816, a story treated in Chapter 53, and its later struggle for recognition, including the indemnity imposed by France in 1825, belongs to the history of nineteenth-century state formation. What this chapter establishes is that by 1804 the Haitian Revolution had altered the Atlantic world: it had demonstrated that the enslaved could end slavery and found a state, it had removed a major plantation economy from the French Empire, and it had made the question of abolition inescapable for every slaveholding society in the hemisphere.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Saint-Domingue to Haiti

What Changed

  • Slavery was abolished, and legal racial hierarchies were dismantled
  • Formerly enslaved people became soldiers, officers, administrators, and rulers
  • A French colony became a sovereign state, with a new name and a new constitutional order
  • Plantation owners largely disappeared as a class, and ownership of land shifted over time

What Stayed the Same

  • Export agriculture remained central to state revenue, and leaders enforced labor obligations
  • Military rule and centralized authority persisted, with generals dominating politics
  • Divisions of color, class, and region continued to shape politics
  • The country remained poor, devastated by war, and dependent on trade with hostile powers

Who experienced change most directly: The formerly enslaved, who gained freedom, and the white planter class, which lost property and, for many, life or homeland. Greater continuity: Field laborers, whose working conditions and relation to the export economy changed less than their legal status, and who continued to press for land. Why the pattern matters: It shows that emancipation transformed legal status without immediately resolving questions of land, labor, and power, which shaped Haitian history throughout the nineteenth century and mirrored debates that arose in other post-emancipation societies.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Haitian Revolution

Long-term causes

A plantation economy dependent on mass importation of Africans, extreme mortality, a large African-born population, a tradition of marronage and ritual organization, and a racial hierarchy that tightened against free people of color.

Short-term causes

The French Revolution's declarations of rights, the quarrel among white colonists and between whites and free people of color, the repression of Ogé, and the weakening of royal authority.

Event or transformation

The 1791 uprising and the following thirteen years of war ended slavery and produced an independent state.

Immediate effects

Emancipation in 1793–1794, the defeat of Britain and Spain in the colony, Toussaint's ascendancy, and the failure of the French expedition.

Long-term consequences

The founding of Haiti, the Louisiana Purchase, the growth of plantation slavery in Cuba and Brazil, fear and inspiration across the Americas, and the isolation and indebtedness of the new state.

Historical complexity

The revolution was not caused by a single idea or leader. Metropolitan politics, colonial divisions, African experience, international war, and disease all mattered, and its outcomes included both liberation and new forms of coercion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Haitian Revolution from the plantation society of Saint-Domingue in 1789 to the proclamation of an independent Haiti in 1804. Its central argument is that the revolution was a sequence of interacting struggles whose participants made decisions under severe constraint, and that the abolition of slavery and the founding of a new state were the outcome of the enslaved majority's armed action in an Atlantic world of revolution and war.

Saint-Domingue was the wealthiest colony of the French Empire, producing sugar and coffee through the labor of some 450,000 to 500,000 enslaved people, most of them African-born. The system was lethal: it depended on continuous importation of new captives, which kept African languages, religions, and military experience alive in the colony. It also generated a social structure divided among wealthy and poor whites, a substantial population of free people of color, many of whom were property owners and slaveholders, and an enslaved majority.

The French Revolution opened a political crisis in the colony. White colonists sought autonomy, free people of color demanded equal rights, and Paris vacillated. The repression of Vincent Ogé in 1790 and 1791 displayed the determination of colonial authorities to defend a racial hierarchy, and it exposed the divisions among the free that the enslaved would exploit.

The uprising of August 1791 in the Plaine du Nord was an organized insurrection that drew on networks among enslaved drivers and artisans, on African-derived religious practice, and on a tradition of marronage. The traditional story of the Bois Caïman ceremony is a powerful founding memory, but its details rest on later sources, and historians treat it with caution. The movement expanded rapidly and reshaped the colony.

Emancipation came through the interaction of colonial and metropolitan politics. The law of April 1792 granted equality to free people of color. War with Spain and Britain in 1793 made the commissioners dependent on the insurgents, and Sonthonax and Polverel proclaimed abolition in 1793. The Convention ratified it in February 1794. The decree was neither a simple gift from Paris nor a purely colonial act, and it left questions about land and labor unanswered.

Toussaint Louverture, who had served Spain, joined the French Republic in 1794 after abolition and became the dominant figure in the colony. He helped defeat Spain and Britain, defeated Rigaud in the War of the Knives, occupied Santo Domingo, and in 1801 promulgated a constitution that made him governor for life, abolished slavery forever, and kept the colony within the French Empire. His regime restored plantation production through compulsory labor, which many cultivators resented. Historians continue to debate whether his ultimate aim was independence.

Napoleon Bonaparte's decision to send the Leclerc expedition and to restore slavery in French colonies provoked the final stage of the revolution. Toussaint was arrested and deported in 1802, dying in a French prison in 1803. The restoration of slavery in Guadeloupe and the brutal methods of French commanders drove the remaining leaders, including Dessalines, Christophe, and Pétion, into a united resistance. Disease devastated the French army, Britain resumed war with France, and the Haitians won at Vertières on 18 November 1803.

On 1 January 1804, Dessalines proclaimed Haiti an independent state. The new regime was authoritarian and wary of a French return, and in October 1804 Dessalines took the title of emperor. Its leader ordered the massacre of many remaining French colonists, an episode that historians interpret as both a measure shaped by the war's brutality and a grave act of violence against civilians. The state's later history of division, debt, and isolation lies beyond this chapter.

The revolution's effects were hemispheric. It removed the world's largest sugar producer and opened opportunities for Cuba and Brazil, even as it inspired enslaved and free people of color and frightened slaveholders. It contributed to the sale of Louisiana, shaped the thinking of Spanish American creole elites, and, through Pétion's aid to Bolívar, linked Haiti to the independence wars of South America. It also posed a challenge to the racial assumptions of the Atlantic powers, which responded with nonrecognition and economic pressure.

Students should take away a method as well as a narrative. Sources written by planters, officials, and soldiers shaped the record of the revolution, and later memory, both French and Haitian, added interpretations. Reading these sources critically, and asking whose voices are missing, particularly those of enslaved women and ordinary cultivators, is necessary for understanding the revolution as a historical process and for placing Haiti within the history of Latin America.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Saint-Domingue
The French colony on western Hispaniola from 1697 to 1804, distinct from modern Haiti and from Spanish Santo Domingo.
Ayiti (Haiti)
A Taíno name for the island, adopted in 1804 as the name of the independent state.
Code Noir
The 1685 royal edict regulating slavery in French colonies, defining the enslaved as property while prescribing nominal obligations of masters.
Grands blancs
Wealthy white planters and officials of Saint-Domingue, divided over autonomy and loyalty.
Petits blancs
Poorer white colonists who defended racial privilege as their principal social asset.
Gens de couleur libres
Legally free people of African or mixed descent, a group that included planters, artisans, and soldiers.
Affranchi
A person who had been freed from slavery, a term often used loosely for free people of color.
Marronage
Flight from slavery, whether temporary or permanent, and the creation of fugitive communities.
Vodou
A religious tradition of Saint-Domingue and Haiti blending West and West-Central African practices with elements of Catholicism, providing community leadership and ritual authority.
Creole
A person born in the colony, as distinct from African-born; also the Kreyòl language of Haiti.
Metropole
The home country of a colonial empire; here, France.
Rights of Man and of the Citizen
The 1789 French declaration of universal rights, whose application to colonies and to the enslaved became a revolutionary dispute.
Civil commissioners
Officials sent by the French government in 1792 with broad powers, including Sonthonax and Polverel.
Emancipation
The act or process of freeing enslaved people, here proclaimed locally in 1793 and legislated in 1794.
Abolition
The legal ending of slavery as an institution, as in the Convention's decree of 4 February 1794.
Cultivateur
The legal status of free agricultural laborers after emancipation, bound to remain on plantations.
Bois Caïman
A ceremony traditionally dated 14 August 1791 and linked to the start of the uprising, whose details are known mainly from later sources.
War of the Knives
The 1799–1800 civil war between Toussaint Louverture and André Rigaud in the south.
Constitution of 1801
The colonial constitution naming Toussaint governor for life and declaring slavery abolished forever.
Leclerc expedition
The French military force sent in 1801–1802 to reassert control over Saint-Domingue.
Yellow fever
A mosquito-borne disease that devastated European armies in the Caribbean, shaping the outcomes of British and French campaigns.
Vertières
The 18 November 1803 battle near Cap-Français that led to the French evacuation.
Act of Independence
The proclamation of 1 January 1804 at Gonaïves founding Haiti.
Jacques I
The imperial title taken by Jean-Jacques Dessalines in October 1804, a form of rule modeled on Napoleonic France.
Sovereignty
Supreme political authority, contested in this chapter among the colony, the metropole, and foreign powers.
Indemnity
A payment imposed on Haiti by France in 1825 as the price of recognition, a later consequence of the revolution.
Second slavery
A term for the expansion of large-scale slave-based export production in nineteenth-century Cuba, Brazil, and the American South.
Atlantic Age of Revolution
The period of connected revolutions and crises of empire from the 1770s to the 1820s, of which the Haitian Revolution was a central part.

Comparing Colonial, Metropolitan, and Haitian Accounts of the Revolution

This project asks you to compare how different kinds of sources describe one episode of the revolution and to evaluate what each can and cannot reveal.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Haitian Revolution was among the most consequential events of the Atlantic Age of Revolution. A society built on the labor of enslaved Africans ended slavery by its own armed action and created a state that its neighbors found hard to accept. The revolution forced a question that metropolitan revolutionaries had preferred to avoid, whether the universal language of rights applied to everyone, and its answer, written in war, remained a challenge to every slaveholding order in the hemisphere.

The chapter also shows how freedom and compulsion could coexist in the aftermath of emancipation. Leaders who defended abolition faced the problem of rebuilding a ruined export economy in a hostile world, and they often responded with discipline that cultivators resisted. The tension between freedom as legal status and freedom as control of land and labor recurs in the post-emancipation histories of Brazil, Cuba, and other societies treated later in this volume.

Haiti's founding belongs to the larger story of Part XI, in which imperial reform, colonial rebellion, and Atlantic revolution together undermined the legitimacy of colonial rule. Chapter 51 turns to Europe, where Napoleon's invasion of Iberia in 1808 produced a crisis of sovereignty in the Spanish and Portuguese empires. Students should carry forward the awareness that Spanish American creoles who debated independence did so in a world that already contained Haiti, and that their fears, strategies, and occasional debts to it shaped the independence movements of the following decades.
