The U.S.–Mexican War, La Reforma, and the French Intervention

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Republic That Would Not Disappear

In 1845 Mexico was a republic of perhaps seven or eight million people with an unpaid army, a nearly empty treasury, and a northern frontier it could neither settle nor defend. Within twenty-two years it lost roughly half its national territory to a neighboring republic, fought a civil war over the legal privileges of the Church and the army, and defeated a European-backed emperor. That a Mexican republic still existed in 1872 is the problem this chapter sets out to explain.

The chapter's central question is how Mexico survived territorial defeat, internal constitutional conflict, and foreign imperial intervention while reconstructing its political institutions and defending its national sovereignty. The three crises examined here are usually taught separately: the war with the United States of 1846–1848, the civil conflict known as the War of Reform of 1858–1861, and the French Intervention and Second Mexican Empire of 1861–1867. Seen together, they form a single generation-long struggle over what the Mexican state was, who could govern it, and who had the right to decide its borders and laws.

Chapter 59 followed Mexico from the collapse of Iturbide's empire through the contests between federalists and centralists, the rise of Antonio López de Santa Anna, and the loss of Texas. This chapter begins where that story reached its limit. It does not retell Texas independence, but it asks how annexation in 1845 turned an unresolved boundary and an unrecognized separation into a war. It then follows the war's consequences: a treaty that transferred an enormous territory, a discredited political order, a revolution in Guerrero that overthrew Santa Anna, and a generation of liberal politicians who concluded that Mexico's survival required dismantling the legal privileges of the Church and army.

Those reforms, collectively called La Reforma, were not a single event. They were a sequence of laws, a constitution, a civil war, and a further set of laws issued by a government that for much of the conflict controlled little more than the port of Veracruz. Their meaning was contested at the time and remains so. Some Mexicans saw them as the foundation of equal citizenship and a modern state; others saw an attack on religion and on communities that depended on corporate property. The chapter distinguishes the individual statutes and treats their social effects as uneven and still debated.

The final crisis was international again. Mexico's suspension of foreign debt payments in 1861 gave Britain, France, and Spain a pretext for intervention, but only France stayed to build a political project, supported by Mexican Conservatives who had lost the civil war. Napoleon III's attempt to install the Austrian archduke Maximilian as emperor produced a regime that was never fully secure, depended on foreign troops, and alienated the very Conservatives who had invited it. Republican resistance, led by Benito Juárez and a group of regional commanders, outlasted it.

The evidence is rich and partisan. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, the Plan of Ayutla, the Ley Lerdo, the Constitution of 1857, and the Plan of Tacubaya survive as documents whose texts can be read closely, though each was written to persuade. Diplomatic correspondence, military reports, newspapers, pamphlets, congressional debates, private letters, and the memoirs of soldiers and politicians supplement them. Church sources, imperial records, and the testimony of ordinary people are less complete, and the experiences of Indigenous villagers, women, and conscripted soldiers must often be reconstructed from legal petitions, parish records, and official complaints.

The chapter covers 1845 to 1872 and is organized in five divisions: the war with the United States; territorial loss and the crisis of the republic; La Reforma and civil war; the French Intervention and empire; and the restored republic. It points forward to Chapter 64's account of the War of the Triple Alliance, to Chapter 67 on Cuba and Puerto Rico, to Chapter 69 on land and Indigenous dispossession, to Chapter 70 on the new hemispheric order, and to Chapter 71, where the Porfiriato builds on the consolidated state that Juárez's generation left behind. Events are dated as precisely as the sources allow, and where historians disagree, the chapter says so.

Historical Context

Mexico's independence in 1821 had left a country with enormous territory, a mining and agricultural economy damaged by a decade of war, a population divided by region, language, and legal status, and no agreed institutional form. Chapter 58 examined the constitutional experiments of the early republics. Chapter 59 traced the federal Constitution of 1824, the centralist constitutions of the 1830s and 1840s, the repeated rise and fall of governments through military pronouncement, and Santa Anna's recurring presence. Governments rarely collected enough revenue to pay an army, and they borrowed from merchants and moneylenders at ruinous terms, often with the Church as a lender of last resort.

Two institutions held privileges inherited from the colonial period. The Catholic Church was the country's largest institutional landholder and a major lender, and its clergy were tried in ecclesiastical courts. The army, with its own courts, was both the guarantor of order and the principal threat to civilian government. Those legal privileges, called fueros, were reinforced by Indigenous and municipal corporate landholding, which gave villages a collective claim on land and a legal personality. Liberals regarded corporate privilege as an obstacle to a nation of equal individuals. Conservatives regarded it as the social order that kept the country together.

The northern frontier was thinly governed. Comanche, Apache, Navajo, and other Indigenous powers controlled large areas and raided deep into northern Mexico, which weakened the northern states and left them skeptical of what Mexico City could offer. Texas had been settled by Anglo-American immigrants, many of them slaveholders, and its independence in 1836 had never been recognized by Mexico. To the north, the United States had been growing by purchase, settlement, and war, and its political culture increasingly spoke of continental destiny, a language that slavery's defenders and its opponents both used for their own purposes.

Europe's relationship to Mexico was shaped by debt and by ideology. British bondholders held loans from the 1820s, French and Spanish nationals pressed claims for property damage and unpaid contracts, and Mexican Conservative exiles in Europe courted monarchists who hoped to counter republican and Protestant influence in the Americas. Meanwhile the United States, which had proclaimed the Monroe Doctrine in 1823, had neither the means nor, after 1861, the attention to enforce it. These overlapping pressures meant that each of Mexico's crises compounded the last.

Division I — The U.S.–Mexican War, 1845–1847 · Section 63.1

Texas Annexation, Manifest Destiny, and the Border Crisis

Mexico never accepted the loss of Texas. When Texan forces captured Santa Anna in 1836, he signed agreements recognizing Texan independence that the Mexican government disavowed, and no Mexican government recognized the separation afterward. For most Mexicans the matter was a rebellion in a province, not a settled boundary. The Republic of Texas nevertheless survived for nearly a decade, sought recognition from Britain and France, and in 1845 chose annexation by the United States. The joint resolution authorizing annexation was signed by President John Tyler on 1 March 1845, Texas accepted its terms, and the state was admitted at the end of the year. Mexico broke diplomatic relations in response and had warned that annexation would mean war.

The boundary was a second unresolved issue. Texas claimed the Rio Grande as its southern and western boundary, a claim the Republic had asserted in 1836 but never enforced in the settled lower valley or in New Mexico. Mexico held that the historic boundary of the province of Coahuila y Tejas was the Nueces River, some 150 miles to the north, and the area between the rivers was inhabited by Tejanos and Mexican ranchers and patrolled by both sides. James K. Polk, elected in 1844 on an expansionist platform, wanted both the boundary and California, which he hoped to acquire through purchase if possible. The phrase "manifest destiny," coined by a U.S. journalist in 1845, captured an ideology of continental expansion that was both popular and bitterly contested, since many Americans feared that new territory would extend slavery, and the war itself would intensify that dispute.

Polk used both diplomacy and force. He sent the diplomat John Slidell to Mexico in late 1845 with instructions to settle the boundary and offer to purchase California and New Mexico, and he ordered General Zachary Taylor's army to the Nueces and, in early 1846, to the Rio Grande opposite Matamoros. Mexican politics ensured that Slidell would fail. President José Joaquín de Herrera's government was suspected of weakness for even considering a hearing, and it was overthrown by General Mariano Paredes at the end of 1845. Any Mexican leader who sold territory, or appeared to, risked being toppled as a traitor by rivals, and none could govern without the army. Neither side was acting irrationally, but each calculated from its own domestic politics, and the result was a confrontation that either side could convert into war.

Section 63.2

The Outbreak of War and the Northern Campaign, 1846–1847

The first shots came on the Rio Grande. On 25 April 1846 Mexican cavalry attacked a U.S. patrol in the disputed area, killing or wounding several soldiers. Polk told Congress on 11 May that Mexico had shed American blood on American soil, and Congress declared war on 13 May, though a minority, including Whigs and antislavery Democrats, believed the president had provoked the conflict. Mexico responded with a declaration of defensive war. Taylor won the battles of Palo Alto on 8 May and Resaca de la Palma on 9 May, in which U.S. mobile artillery proved decisive, and Mexican forces under Mariano Arista withdrew across the river.

The government in Mexico City meanwhile changed again. Paredes fell in the summer of 1846, and a coalition of federalists restored the federal Constitution of 1824 in August. Santa Anna, exiled in Cuba, was permitted by U.S. naval authorities to return, in the mistaken expectation that he would favor a negotiated peace. He instead took command of the army, assembled a force at San Luis Potosí, and was soon the dominant political and military figure again. Taylor, in the meantime, captured Monterrey in September after hard urban fighting and agreed to an armistice that Polk found too generous. In February 1847 Santa Anna marched north to meet Taylor at Buena Vista, where after two days of hard fighting on 22–23 February he withdrew, his army depleted by casualties, hunger, and desertion.

Mexico's difficulties were political and fiscal as much as military. The army was largely raised by forced levy, officers frequently quarreled, and the government could not feed, pay, or transport troops across the desert north. The war also deepened divisions over who should pay for it. In January 1847 the acting government under Valentín Gómez Farías authorized raising funds by mortgaging or selling Church property, provoking clerical opposition and a revolt in the capital in February by National Guard units, the "Revolution of the Polkos," which forced the measure's withdrawal and weakened the government at a critical moment. Several states were reluctant to contribute men and money. U.S. forces had a navy, better artillery, and a government that could borrow and raise volunteers, though they suffered heavily from disease, which killed far more of them than combat did.

Section 63.3

The Western Campaigns: New Mexico, California, and Indigenous Territories

The war in the West was fought over regions with weak ties to Mexico City and complicated internal politics. Stephen Kearny's Army of the West left Fort Leavenworth in the summer of 1846 and entered Santa Fe on 18 August, the governor, Manuel Armijo, having withdrawn without a battle. New Mexico's reaction was divided. Merchant elites with commercial ties to the Santa Fe trade, residents who resented the central government, and some Pueblo communities accepted the occupation or adapted to it, while others viewed it as a threat. In January 1847 a revolt at Taos, joined by Hispano and Pueblo participants, killed the U.S.-appointed governor, Charles Bent, and others before being suppressed by U.S. troops. The revolt's participants had distinct grievances, and the U.S. response, including trials and executions, made clear that occupation would be enforced.

California's story was equally layered. Settlers and the explorer John C. Frémont proclaimed the short-lived Bear Flag Republic in June 1846, and a U.S. naval force under John Sloat raised the flag at Monterey in July. Californio leaders were divided among themselves, with rivalries between the northern and southern elite and between officials appointed from Mexico and local landholders. A Californio uprising in Los Angeles in September 1846 won local victories and the engagement at San Pasqual in December, but by January 1847 resistance gave way to the Treaty of Cahuenga, which ended fighting in California. Native Californians, many of them laboring on ranchos and in former mission communities, were not parties to these arrangements, and gold discovered in January 1848, days before the peace treaty, brought a flood of migrants that overwhelmed all earlier arrangements.

Indigenous nations were neither bystanders nor beneficiaries. Comanche, Apache, and Navajo power had weakened northern Mexican states for years, and the war removed even their limited protection. U.S. officials promised that they would restrain raiding, a commitment embodied in the treaty that proved impossible to keep. For the Indigenous nations of the Southwest, the change of sovereignty did not end their own claims to land and authority. Instead it replaced one distant government with another that had greater military power and fewer reasons to negotiate. The consequences for frontier communities, Hispano, Indigenous, and Anglo, were violence and displacement for decades.

Section 63.4

Veracruz, the Campaign against Mexico City, and the Fall of the Capital

Polk, frustrated by the stalemate, authorized a second front. Winfield Scott landed roughly 8,600 troops near Veracruz on 9 March 1847 in a large amphibious operation, besieged and bombarded the city until terms of capitulation were agreed on 27 March; U.S. forces occupied it on 29 March, at a considerable cost to civilians. Scott then advanced inland. At Cerro Gordo on 18 April he outflanked Santa Anna's strong defensive position, and U.S. forces occupied Puebla in May. The advance was slowed by supply difficulties and by guerrillas, including priests and rural bands who harassed convoys, but Scott's army reached the Valley of Mexico in August.

The final battles were fought within sight of the capital. At Contreras (Padierna) on 19–20 August, a U.S. flanking attack defeated General Gabriel Valencia's force, whose commander had disobeyed Santa Anna's orders, and at Churubusco on 20 August Mexican defenders fought hard, among them the San Patricio Battalion, composed largely of Irish and other immigrant deserters from the U.S. Army whose members were later executed or branded. An armistice and unproductive negotiations with the U.S. envoy Nicholas Trist followed. Fighting resumed at Molino del Rey on 8 September and ended with the assault on Chapultepec on 13 September, where the military college was held by cadets and regulars. Mexican tradition remembers six young cadets as the "Niños Héroes," a patriotic memory whose details are debated but which reflects the war's central place in national memory.

U.S. troops entered the capital on 14 September, and the occupation provoked an uprising by poor residents, who attacked soldiers with stones and gunfire, and the occupiers answered with artillery and arrests. Santa Anna resigned the presidency and left; a government formed under Manuel de la Peña y Peña and then Pedro María Anaya moved to Querétaro and eventually negotiated. Mexicans remained divided. Some advocated continued guerrilla war, while others feared a prolonged occupation and, in the United States, an "All Mexico" faction called for annexation of the whole country. Peace advocates in Mexico argued that to refuse terms was to risk losing everything, a calculation that made the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo possible.

Division II — Territorial Loss and the Crisis of the Republic, 1848–1855 · Section 63.5

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo and Mexico's Territorial Losses, 1848

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed on 2 February 1848 by Mexican commissioners and by Nicholas Trist, whom Polk had recalled but who negotiated anyway. It established the Rio Grande as the boundary of Texas and ceded to the United States a vast territory that included present-day California, Nevada, and Utah and large parts of Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming, an area of roughly 525,000 square miles. The United States paid $15 million and assumed up to about $3.25 million in claims by its citizens against Mexico. Counting Texas, the loss amounted to roughly half of Mexico's territory. The U.S. Senate ratified the treaty on 10 March with amendments, the Mexican Congress accepted them in May, and ratifications were exchanged on 30 May, with an accompanying protocol at Querétaro that clarified some Mexican concerns but had uncertain legal force.

The treaty promised protections to the Mexican residents of the ceded lands, perhaps 75,000 to 100,000 people by common estimates. Those who remained could choose to retain Mexican nationality or acquire U.S. citizenship; residents who did not declare a choice within one year after the exchange of ratifications would be deemed to have chosen U.S. citizenship. The treaty also promised respect for their property. In practice the guarantees were weaker than they appeared. The U.S. Senate deleted Article X, which specifically addressed Mexican land grants, and revised Article IX on the rights of residents. More general property protections remained, but their subsequent application was contested. Land titles issued under Spanish and Mexican law were then adjudicated before U.S. commissions and courts, a process that was slow, expensive, and often unfavorable, especially in California and New Mexico. Citizenship was formally extended but political participation, language, and landholding were often constrained, and the experience of Tejanos, Californios, and Nuevomexicanos varied widely by class and region.

For Indigenous nations, the treaty was a transfer of a claim they did not recognize. Article XI committed the United States to restrain and punish cross-border Indigenous raids and to help return certain captives. Mexico later sought compensation for losses caused by raids, but the United States disputed that the treaty created a general monetary indemnity obligation; the disagreement became a continuing diplomatic grievance. The new boundary also cut through the Tohono O'odham, Apache, Kickapoo, and other homelands, and some groups, including Black Seminole and Kickapoo communities, later migrated into Mexico. In Yucatán a separate crisis, the Maya uprising known as the Caste War that began in 1847, deepened Mexico's vulnerability, and local leaders even sought outside aid in 1848. The war's end therefore did not close Mexico's frontier crises, and it left a population in the north that had to renegotiate its relationship with two states.

Section 63.6

Postwar Reconstruction, Santa Anna, and the Gadsden Purchase

Mexico emerged from the war with weak finances and a political class divided over its meaning. The governments of José Joaquín de Herrera (1848–1851) and Mariano Arista (1851–1853) tried to restore order and reduce military expenses, but the treasury was exhausted, the treaty payment was committed to pay debts, and rebellions continued. Filibusters, armed adventurers from the United States, threatened Sonora and Baja California in the early 1850s, among them the French-born Gaston de Raousset-Boulbon and the American William Walker, and the Caste War continued in Yucatán. Conservatives, who had long argued that Mexico needed a strong executive and a defense of the Church, gained ground as federalist politicians were blamed for the defeat.

In April 1853 Conservative leaders, led by the intellectual and politician Lucas Alamán, invited Santa Anna back to govern. Alamán died in June, but the government continued as a centralized personal dictatorship. Santa Anna took the title "Most Serene Highness," restricted the press, suppressed dissent, reorganized the army, and strengthened ties with the Church. His regime depended on revenue it could not raise, so it sought new sources, and in 1853 he agreed to negotiate with the United States over the boundary, which had been surveyed unsatisfactorily after 1848 and which U.S. interests wanted adjusted for a southern railroad route.

The Gadsden treaty, signed on 30 December 1853 by the U.S. minister James Gadsden, transferred the Mesilla Valley, about 30,000 square miles in southern Arizona and New Mexico, to the United States for $10 million after the U.S. Senate reduced the original sum, and it released the United States from the Indigenous-raid provisions of the earlier treaty. Ratifications were completed in mid-1854. In Mexico the transaction was widely condemned as another sale of national territory to fund a dictator, and it was a major cause of the revolt that followed. It is distinct from the cession of 1848, which followed a war, whereas the Mesilla sale was a negotiated transfer in time of peace made by a government of doubtful legitimacy.

Section 63.7

The Revolution of Ayutla and the Rise of Liberal Reform, 1854–1855

The revolt began in the south. On 1 March 1854 a plan proclaimed at Ayutla, in what is now Guerrero, declared that Santa Anna had forfeited authority and called for a provisional president and an extraordinary Congress to write a new constitution. Ignacio Comonfort, a customs officer in Acapulco, revised the plan to appeal to moderates, and the movement found its leader in Juan Álvarez, an aging general and regional chieftain with a base among the peasants, ranchers, and Afro-descended and Indigenous communities of the hot coastal lands. Álvarez's veterans fought a prolonged guerrilla campaign, and the revolution spread to other states as Santa Anna's regime lost popular and military support.

The coalition was broad and not united. It included radical Liberals, the so-called puros, who wanted fundamental changes in the legal order, and moderados, who wanted reform but feared social and religious conflict. It included exiles, among them Benito Juárez, who had been expelled by Santa Anna and who worked in New Orleans before joining Álvarez at Acapulco in 1855. It also included Melchor Ocampo, Ponciano Arriaga, Ignacio Ramírez, and Guillermo Prieto, men who would shape the reform. Santa Anna left the country in August 1855, and Álvarez became provisional president in October, forming a cabinet that included Juárez as minister of justice and ecclesiastical affairs.

Álvarez's regime was short, and its military base in the south made the capital's elite uneasy. He resigned in December 1855 and handed power to Comonfort, a moderate who sought to carry out reform gradually. The revolution established principles that would structure the next twelve years: that sovereignty derived from a constitution written by an elected Congress, that the army and Church should be subject to civil law, and that the republic's survival depended on a state strong enough to enforce its own laws. The political coalition was a reaction to defeat and dictatorship, and its members soon discovered that they disagreed about almost everything else.

Division III — La Reforma, Constitutional Government, and Civil War, 1855–1861 · Section 63.8

Benito Juárez, Liberalism, and the Early Reform Government

Benito Juárez was born in 1806 in the Zapotec village of San Pablo Guelatao in Oaxaca, was orphaned young, and was educated through the patronage of a lay Franciscan, Antonio Salanueva, and a seminary and the Institute of Sciences and Arts in Oaxaca City. He became a lawyer, a municipal and state official, and, from 1847 to 1852, governor of Oaxaca, a record of disciplined administration. He married Margarita Maza, who shared the burdens of his exile and public life. His early career illustrates both the opportunities and limits of liberalism: an Indigenous provincial could rise through law and politics, but only by entering institutions that were mostly governed by others. Juárez is a central figure in Mexican national memory, but his achievement should be understood through the collective effort of a generation of Liberals, not as a solitary one.

The first legislation of the Álvarez and Comonfort governments dealt with the legal privileges of the Church and army. The Ley Juárez, issued on 23 November 1855, abolished the special courts, or fueros, in civil cases and limited them in criminal ones, so that clergy and soldiers would be subject to ordinary courts for non-professional matters. Clergy retained jurisdiction over purely religious offenses, and the criminal fuero could be waived by the Church. The measure was modest in legal terms but large in symbolic ones, because it asserted that a single law governed all citizens. Bishops protested, one resigned in protest, and the law became a rallying point for opposition.

Comonfort, who became president in December 1855, tried to combine reform with conciliation and soon faced revolts. In Puebla, rebels led by Antonio de Haro y Tamariz took up arms in early 1856 in defense of the Church, and Comonfort suppressed them and then, in March, ordered the seizure of the property of the Puebla bishopric to pay for the campaign. This expedient showed how the government's fiscal needs and its anticlerical program reinforced one another. Liberals were not united about how fast to move, and the division between puros and moderados would shape the Constituent Congress that followed.

Historical Vocabulary
Fuero

A fuero was a legal privilege or jurisdiction attached to a corporate group. In Mexico the principal fueros were ecclesiastical, which allowed clergy to be tried by Church courts, and military, which gave soldiers their own courts. The word, from Spanish legal tradition, also referred to the charters of towns and regions.

Fueros were not a simple form of corruption. Defenders saw them as protections for institutions that served the community. Liberals saw them as inequality before the law. The abolition of fueros was the first major step of La Reforma and the clearest expression of its claim that citizens should have equal legal status.

Section 63.9

The Ley Lerdo, Corporate Property, and the Social Consequences of Reform

The law of 25 June 1856, known as the Ley Lerdo after Finance Minister Miguel Lerdo de Tejada, ordered civil and religious corporations to sell the urban and rural properties they owned, giving priority to the tenants who rented them at a price calculated by capitalizing the annual rent. Its target was the so-called "dead hands" (manos muertas), property held in perpetuity by institutions that, in the Liberal view, kept land out of circulation and prevented the creation of a class of small owners. The law also taxed each transfer, which produced revenue for a government short of money. It did not confiscate Church property; the Church was to be paid, and the Church could continue to hold buildings used for worship and the like.

The law's reach was wider than its authors' main target. Municipal and Indigenous village corporations also held land, and the statute applied to them, although lands in direct public use were exempt. Clarifying rules in October 1856 allowed small parcels to be adjudicated to occupants. Liberals expected village members to become smallholders. In practice the effects were uneven. Some villages and families bought land, some resisted, and some lost land to speculators, neighboring estates, and officials, and many village communities adapted by dividing property nominally while maintaining collective practices. Historians have long debated the size of the effect. The more dramatic losses of village land came later, under the Porfirian land laws, and recent scholarship emphasizes that rural communities often defended their rights through litigation and politics.

The law also had large consequences for the Church. Church leaders denounced it, and the clergy's role as lender and landlord was undermined, though many properties were bought by the Church's allies. The economic effects were mixed: some buyers were foreigners and speculators, and the intended class of small proprietors did not appear on the scale hoped. The political effects were decisive, because the law forced property holders and tenants to take sides, and it hardened clerical opposition. The Ley Lerdo should be distinguished from the nationalization of 1859, which confiscated Church property outright.

Historical Vocabulary
Desamortización

Desamortización, usually translated "disentailment," meant forcing the sale of property held by corporations that could not legally sell it. It differs from nationalization, in which the state takes property without compensating the owner.

Students should not treat the two as the same. The Ley Lerdo of 1856 was disentailment; the Reform Laws of 1859 nationalized Church property. Both were part of La Reforma, but they had different legal logics and different social consequences, and Indigenous village lands were affected mainly by the first.

Section 63.10

The Constitution of 1857 and Competing Visions of the Mexican Nation

The Constituent Congress met from February 1856, and its debates were among the most important in Mexican history. Its Liberal members, among them Arriaga, Ramírez, and Prieto, drew on the Constitution of 1824, on the U.S. and French models, and on Mexican experience. The constitution proclaimed on 5 February 1857 opened with a declaration of individual rights: it reaffirmed the existing abolition of slavery, protected freedom of work, education, speech, and press, and guaranteed due process. It constitutionally established the amparo, a judicial remedy with earlier Mexican precedents that allowed individuals to challenge unconstitutional acts and became one of Mexico's most distinctive contributions to constitutional law. It restored a federal system, vested legislative power in a single chamber, and restricted the executive, a deliberate response to Santa Anna's dictatorship.

The constitution did not declare religious toleration, though it did not establish the Church either, and it assigned the federal government authority over religious worship and discipline. It incorporated the principle that corporations could not own real property, which constitutionalized the Ley Lerdo. The Ley Iglesias of April 1857, authored by José María Iglesias, regulated clerical fees for baptisms, marriages, and burials, which burdened poor parishioners. Church leaders responded by threatening to deny sacraments to officials who swore allegiance to the constitution and by denouncing the document. For many Catholics this placed an unwelcome choice between their religion and their loyalty to the state.

The competing visions were real. For Liberals, the nation consisted of individual citizens equal before the law, with a state that regulated religion and promoted education and commerce. For Conservatives, the nation was a Catholic community with hierarchical institutions, historic corporate rights, and a strong executive able to maintain order, and some had come to believe that a monarchy might be necessary. These visions were not simply progressive and reactionary. Liberals relied on a state they distrusted and on a corporate-property reform that undermined communities, while Conservatives defended popular religious practice that many ordinary Mexicans valued. Many Mexicans fit neither camp and sought moderation.

Section 63.11

The Plan of Tacubaya and the Beginning of the War of Reform

Comonfort was elected president under the new constitution and took office on 1 December 1857, with Juárez as president of the Supreme Court and therefore next in line. Comonfort found that the constitution weakened the executive he had to run, and Conservative pressure mounted. On 17 December General Félix Zuloaga proclaimed the Plan of Tacubaya, which declared the constitution inadequate to the country's wishes and called for a new congress. Comonfort initially joined the plan and had Juárez arrested, a calculation that he could control the movement. He soon discovered that the Conservatives wanted much more, reversed himself in January 1858, and left the country, a decision that left the constitutional order in confusion.

Zuloaga was recognized as president in the capital, and the Church and many of the army supported him. Under the constitution, the president of the Supreme Court was to succeed, so Juárez, freed from custody, left the capital and on 19 January 1858 assumed the presidency in Guanajuato. For three years Mexico had two governments, each claiming legitimacy. The Conservatives held Mexico City and the central regions, and the Liberals drew strength from northern states, the Pacific south, and the Gulf ports, though lines were fluid and many regions changed hands. Juárez's government moved to Guadalajara, where he narrowly escaped execution during a mutiny in March, then traveled through Colima and by sea through Panama and Havana to Veracruz, arriving in May 1858.

The war was thus a conflict over constitutional legitimacy as much as over religion. The Plan of Tacubaya claimed to represent the nation's will, while Juárez's claim rested on the text of the constitution. The war's participants included professional officers, local militias and National Guard units, bandits, guerrillas, and priests, and the line between political and social conflict was often blurred. It lasted three years because neither side could destroy the other, because both lived off the countryside, and because the Church's money sustained the Conservatives while Veracruz's customs revenues sustained the Liberals.

Section 63.12

The War of Reform, the Reform Laws, and Liberal Victory, 1858–1861

The war was fought across the country with shifting fortunes. Conservative commanders, notably Miguel Miramón, who became president in early 1859 at the age of twenty-seven, won many early battles against Liberal armies led by Santos Degollado and others, and Conservative forces besieged Veracruz twice. Atrocities occurred on both sides, including the killing of doctors and students at Tacubaya in April 1859 by Conservative troops. Conservative guerrillas killed the Liberal leaders Melchor Ocampo and Santos Degollado in 1861 after the war's formal end. The Liberal government's survival depended on Veracruz's customs revenue and on the failure of Conservative offensives against the port.

From Veracruz the Juárez government issued the Reform Laws in July 1859, a program far more radical than the measures of 1855–1857. The laws nationalized Church property and suppressed monasteries, established civil marriage and a civil registry of births and deaths, secularized cemeteries, and reduced the number of religious holidays. A later decree in December 1860 declared freedom of worship. These laws completed the separation of Church and state and were more drastic than the Constitution of 1857 required. They were adopted partly out of political necessity: Church property financed the Conservatives, and the government hoped that nationalization would deprive them of resources and attract buyers to the Liberal cause.

International diplomacy mattered. The United States recognized the Juárez government in April 1859, and in December Juárez's government signed the McLane–Ocampo treaty, which would have granted the United States transit rights across Mexican territory in exchange for payment, but the U.S. Senate rejected it in 1860, and many Mexicans were relieved. European governments leaned toward the Conservative regime. The Liberals won decisively at Calpulalpan on 22 December 1860, when forces under Jesús González Ortega defeated Miramón, and Juárez entered Mexico City on 11 January 1861. The victory restored constitutional government but not peace: Conservative guerrillas continued, the treasury was empty, the Church property sales had not produced the revenue hoped for, and the republic faced a mounting foreign debt.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Rival Visions of National Reconstruction

The question: Did Liberal reform create the foundation of a modern Mexican nation, or did it destabilize the country and harm the communities it claimed to emancipate? And was the Conservative alternative a defense of social order or an obstacle to national integration?

Interpretation A — Liberalism as national foundation: Many historians, following a tradition that dominated Mexican national history, see the Reforma as establishing equal citizenship, civil authority, constitutional government, and the secular state that made later modernization possible. On this view, Conservative resistance to the Reform and the invitation of foreign intervention discredited the Conservatives and legitimated the Liberal republic.
Interpretation B — Costs and continuities: Other scholars, working from regional and social history, emphasize that Liberal reform harmed Indigenous communities, burdened the poor, and left Church wealth largely in the hands of a small number of buyers. They also stress that Conservatives defended popular religion and local order, and that Liberals and Conservatives shared more than their rhetoric suggested. Studies of villages, in work by scholars such as Florencia Mallon and Guy Thomson, show that rural communities adopted and reshaped Liberal ideas to defend their own interests, which complicates the view that Liberalism was simply imposed from above.

Evidence: Both views draw on legislation, debates, pamphlets, notarial and land records, and local archives. The evidence is richest for elites and thinnest for the people who experienced the reforms most directly.

Why it matters: The question affects how Mexican liberalism, the Church's role, and the origins of later land concentration are understood. It also shapes how historians interpret the Porfiriato, which inherited both the Reform state and its social effects.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation and identify the kinds of local evidence that could test them.

Suggested scholarship (not direct quotations): Jan Bazant, Los bienes de la iglesia en México (1856–1875) (1971); Florencia E. Mallon, Peasant and Nation (1995); Guy P. C. Thomson with David G. LaFrance, Patriotism, Politics, and Popular Liberalism in Nineteenth-Century Mexico (1999). These works examine different aspects of property, rural political agency, and national formation; the debate's interpretations are teaching syntheses, not claims that the authors fall into two exclusive schools.

Division IV — The French Intervention and the Second Mexican Empire, 1861–1867 · Section 63.13

Foreign Debt, European Intervention, and Napoleon III's Mexican Project

Mexico had borrowed abroad since the 1820s and had repeatedly defaulted. After the civil war the Juárez government faced empty coffers and chronic revenue problems, and on 17 July 1861 the Congress decreed a two-year suspension of payments on the foreign debt. Britain, France, and Spain, whose citizens held much of it, signed the Convention of London on 31 October 1861, agreeing to a joint naval demonstration to press their claims. Their aims differed. Britain wanted payment, Spain had its own ambitions in the Caribbean, and France under Napoleon III, whose government held claims that included disputed bonds issued by the Swiss banker Jecker, was thinking much larger.

Napoleon III hoped to establish a friendly monarchy in Mexico that would check U.S. power, secure French commercial and financial interests, and fit his idea of a "Latin" civilization under French leadership. His advisers, including Mexican exiles such as José María Gutiérrez de Estrada and the Conservative general Juan Almonte, assured him that Mexico would welcome a monarch. The U.S. Civil War, which began in April 1861, reduced American capacity to resist, and Secretary of State William Seward could offer only diplomatic protests. Historians debate how much each factor mattered, but the combination of debt pretext, Conservative invitation, and U.S. distraction made the intervention possible.

Spanish troops landed at Veracruz in December 1861, followed by British and French contingents. Juárez's foreign minister negotiated the preliminary Convention of La Soledad in February 1862, which recognized the Mexican government and allowed the allied troops to move inland for health reasons. But when it became clear that France meant to remain and support Almonte, Britain and Spain withdrew in April 1862, and French reinforcements arrived. The republic now faced an enemy with a modern army, a powerful navy, and a political plan, while Mexican Conservatives who had lost the civil war saw an opportunity to regain power.

Historical Vocabulary
Intervention and Sovereignty

Intervention refers to one state's use of force or coercion to affect another's internal affairs. The term is often used for the French action of 1861–1867, and Mexicans commonly use it, while the French spoke of an "expedition." Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory and independence from external control.

Students should notice that the word had several meanings in this chapter: territorial control, as in the loss of land; constitutional authority, as in the question of who legitimately governed; and national independence, as in the defense against a European-backed monarchy. Each form was threatened in different ways in the three crises.

Section 63.14

The Battle of Puebla and the French Occupation of Mexico City, 1862–1863

The French advance from Veracruz toward Mexico City was checked at Puebla. On 5 May 1862 General Ignacio Zaragoza, born in what is now Goliad, Texas, and commanding a smaller and less well-equipped Mexican force that included Porfirio Díaz, defeated the French under Charles de Lorencez, who attacked fortified positions at Loreto and Guadalupe and withdrew with several hundred casualties. The victory was celebrated across Mexico and remains commemorated as Cinco de Mayo, today more prominent in Mexican American communities than in most of Mexico. It was a political and moral triumph that showed the French could be beaten, but it did not change the underlying military balance.

Napoleon III answered by sending reinforcements under General Élie-Frédéric Forey, and Zaragoza died of typhoid in September 1862. The French besieged Puebla from March 1863 and compelled it to surrender in May after a hard defense under Jesús González Ortega. The capital could not be held, and Juárez's government left Mexico City on 31 May 1863, beginning the itinerant republic that would last four years. French troops entered the capital on 10 June. That spring is remembered also for the action at Camarón on 30 April 1863, in which a French Foreign Legion company fought to the end against far larger Mexican forces, a story central to the Legion's tradition.

The French and their Mexican allies then built a political structure. A council of Mexican notables, convened in July 1863, voted to establish a monarchy and offer the crown to Maximilian of Austria, and a regency under Almonte and others governed in the interim. Historians question how representative the notables were and how well the later claims of popular endorsement were founded, since those votes were organized under French occupation. The occupation extended French control across much of the central plateau but did not subdue the rest of the country, and it produced a state that depended on foreign troops it could not afford to dismiss.

Section 63.15

Maximilian, Carlota, and the Establishment of the Second Mexican Empire

Ferdinand Maximilian, archduke of Austria and younger brother of Emperor Franz Joseph, was a former naval officer and governor of Lombardy–Venetia with liberal inclinations and an ambitious wife, Charlotte (Carlota), the daughter of the king of the Belgians. He accepted the Mexican crown at his castle of Miramar in April 1864 after a delegation presented the claim of popular endorsement, signing a treaty with Napoleon III that committed Mexico to heavy financial obligations to France in exchange for continued French military support, and he renounced his rights in the Austrian succession. The couple arrived at Veracruz in late May and entered Mexico City on 12 June 1864.

The empire was a project of European monarchism, Mexican Conservatism, and French power. Its Mexican supporters included Almonte, Miguel Miramón, Leonardo Márquez, Tomás Mejía, many clergy and landowners, and some moderate Liberals who thought the republic had failed, and the court at Chapultepec and Mexico City developed elaborate ceremony. Maximilian also appointed Liberal-minded ministers, and he built roads, patronized science, and reorganized territorial administration. Carlota served as regent in his absence. Yet the regime's tie to foreign troops was its defining feature, since the French army under François Bazaine was its principal instrument, and it could not collect enough revenue to replace it.

The empire's dilemma was that Maximilian was not the monarch his Conservative supporters expected. He kept the Reform Laws, including the nationalization of Church property, because reversing them would have meant reopening the property settlement that had created a new class of owners, and he sought to rule above parties. The papal nuncio arrived in late 1864 expecting a restoration of Church property and privileges, and Maximilian refused. The Church and Conservatives felt betrayed, while Liberals saw an imposter. The regime thus alienated those who invited it and failed to win those who opposed it.

Section 63.16

Maximilian's Government and the Republican Resistance

Maximilian's policies were reformist and paternalistic. The empire issued laws protecting freedom of the press, with qualifications, and guaranteeing religious toleration in practice. A labor decree of November 1865 limited the debts that bound workers to haciendas and restricted corporal punishment, and the government also sought to restore lands to villages. Historians debate how far these measures reflected genuine concern, how much they were enforced, and how much they were intended to win support against the republic. Little of the program could be carried out without a functioning administration, and the empire controlled few places firmly beyond the garrisons.

Against it, Juárez's government survived as a mobile republic. It moved from San Luis Potosí to Saltillo, Monterrey, and Chihuahua, reaching Paso del Norte, now Ciudad Juárez, in 1865, close to the U.S. border. Its legitimacy was challenged when Juárez's term expired in November 1865 and he extended it by decree on grounds of wartime necessity, a measure that prompted complaints from General González Ortega and others. Resistance was organized by regional commanders: Porfirio Díaz in Oaxaca until his capture in early 1865 and subsequent escape, Mariano Escobedo in the north, Ramón Corona in the west, Vicente Riva Palacio in Michoacán, and many guerrilla leaders. The republic's strength lay in its dispersion, in local militias, and in communities that supplied food and recruits.

The war became harsher. On 3 October 1865 Maximilian, under pressure from Bazaine, signed the so-called Black Decree, which declared that captured republican guerrillas would be treated as bandits and executed within a day, and many were. Republican forces responded in kind in some places. Civilians suffered requisitions, forced levies, and reprisals from all sides. Mexican participation was substantial on both sides: Indigenous and peasant communities from the Sierra of Puebla and Oaxaca joined the republican cause, while others, including some Indigenous towns, accepted imperial promises. Women ran households and farms, followed armies as camp followers and nurses, and in some cases acted as couriers and informants. Margarita Maza raised funds and sympathy for the republic in the United States while the family lived in New York.

Section 63.17

French Withdrawal, the Siege of Querétaro, and the Fall of the Empire, 1866–1867

The empire's fate was decided by Mexican and international forces together. The U.S. Civil War ended in April 1865, and the United States, which had never recognized the empire, turned its attention to Mexico. Seward pressed France diplomatically, and U.S. commanders allowed republican agents to buy weapons and permitted some arms to cross the border. At the same time, Napoleon III's position weakened in Europe, especially after Prussia's victory over Austria in 1866, and French public opinion grew critical of the cost. In January 1866 Napoleon announced a phased withdrawal, to be completed by early 1867, and the last French troops left in March 1867.

Carlota traveled to Europe in mid-1866 to plead for support from Napoleon and the pope, failed, and suffered a mental collapse from which she never recovered, living in Europe until 1927. Maximilian considered abdication but chose to remain, partly from a sense of honor and partly from the hope that Conservative generals could defend the empire. Republican forces meanwhile gained ground. Díaz won victories in Oaxaca in 1866, Escobedo and Corona advanced in the north and west, and imperial forces were pushed back. The empire's Mexican army was not strong enough to hold the country without French support, and many imperial garrisons collapsed or defected.

Maximilian made his last stand at Querétaro, where he and his generals, including Miramón and Mejía, were besieged by Escobedo from March 1867. The siege lasted until 15 May, when the city fell after a breach, the circumstances of which, including the role of Colonel Miguel López, remain disputed. Maximilian was captured, tried by court martial, and sentenced to death. Appeals for clemency came from European figures, but Juárez refused, arguing that the sentence upheld the republic's right to defend itself and deterred future interventions. Maximilian, Miramón, and Mejía were shot on 19 June 1867 on the Cerro de las Campanas. Díaz took Mexico City on 21 June, and Juárez entered the capital on 15 July, issuing a manifesto that defended the victory in terms of respect for rights and law.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Defeat and Reform to Intervention

Long-term causes

Fiscal weakness since independence, unresolved debts, political instability, privileged corporations, and European monarchism and ambition.

Short-term causes

The Reform civil war, the suspension of debt payments in 1861, Conservative exile lobbying, and U.S. preoccupation with its Civil War.

Event or transformation

France, having stayed after Britain and Spain withdrew, occupied the capital and established the Second Empire.

Immediate effects

Four years of war, an itinerant republican government, a disappointed Conservative camp, and a regime dependent on foreign troops.

Long-term consequences

The restoration of the republic with enhanced legitimacy, the discrediting of Conservatism and monarchism, and a state strengthened by war.

Historical complexity

The empire fell through the combination of Mexican resistance, French withdrawal, U.S. diplomacy, and the regime's own contradictions, and no single factor accounts for it.
Division V — The Restored Republic and National Consolidation, 1867–1872 · Section 63.18

Benito Juárez and the Restored Republic, 1867–1872

The restored republic faced the tasks of reconstruction. Juárez called elections in 1867 and proposed constitutional amendments, including the restoration of a Senate and a presidential veto, which he put before the voters in a referendum, a procedure that critics said exceeded the constitution. He won the presidency in October against Díaz, and Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada became a dominant minister. The government reduced the army, which had grown during the war, and discharged many officers, a policy that created discontent. It resumed diplomatic relations with some states, but relations with France and Britain were slow to recover, and the foreign debt remained a burden.

Education was a major priority. The law of 2 December 1867 established free and secular primary education in the capital and federal territories, and the National Preparatory School, founded under Gabino Barreda, who promoted positivist philosophy, trained a generation of professionals. Roads, telegraph lines, and railways expanded, and the line from Veracruz to Mexico City, built by a British company with government support, opened at the beginning of 1873. Economic recovery was slow, and budget deficits persisted. Regional rebellions and banditry continued, including an agrarian uprising in the Chalco region led by Julio Chávez López that was suppressed in 1869.

Politics remained contentious. Juárez's decision to seek reelection in 1871 provoked opposition from Díaz and Lerdo. When no candidate won a majority, Congress chose Juárez, and Díaz proclaimed the Plan de la Noria in November 1871, denouncing reelection and calling for revolt. The rebellion failed, and Díaz was later pardoned. Juárez died of a heart attack on 18 July 1872, and as president of the Supreme Court, Lerdo succeeded him and was elected in October. The republic had survived, but its unity depended on a leader whose personal prestige was immense and whose methods, including decrees and the use of force against rivals, were open to criticism.

Section 63.19

War, Reform, Land, Religion, and Everyday Mexican Society

The wars exacted a heavy social toll. Armies were recruited by levy and seldom paid, and soldiers and their families, including soldaderas who cooked, nursed, and carried supplies, followed the troops. Disease, hunger, and violence killed many, though reliable totals do not exist. Haciendas, mines, and trade were disrupted, and requisitions fell on villages and towns. The National Guard, a citizen militia that became important during the Reform and intervention, gave many men a political role and a sense of participation, and it was one root of the patriotic politics of the period.

Religion remained central to daily life even as the state became more secular. The Reform Laws closed many convents and monasteries, which affected nuns and the people dependent on convent charity and education, and civil marriage and the civil registry shifted authority over family status to the state, though most Mexicans continued to marry and baptize in church. Women's property rights, the dowries and inheritances that supported many households, were affected by changes in Church credit and property. Indigenous villages continued to defend communal landholding through litigation and negotiation, and some, like the Zapotecs of Oaxaca, supported Juárez, while others resisted Liberal land policy or fought for their own interests, as in Yucatán and the Chalco region.

The social effects of the period were uneven and contradictory. Liberal legislation promoted equal citizenship but also created conditions for land concentration, and the republic's victory strengthened a national identity based on resistance to foreign invasion and a civil religion of heroes and anniversaries. African-descended communities on the Pacific coast, who had supported Álvarez, benefited from the end of the dictatorship but continued to experience racial inequality. Writers such as Ignacio Manuel Altamirano, born in a Nahua community in Guerrero, helped build a national literature. The restored republic left a state that was stronger than before and a society still marked by profound regional and social divisions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Levies, Villages, and Households in an Age of War

For most Mexicans the three conflicts arrived as requisitions, recruiters, and rumors. Press gangs seized men for the army, and families lost labor and income. Villages supplied maize, mules, and shelter to passing armies, whether Mexican, American, French, Liberal, or Conservative, and sometimes more than one of them in a single season. Women kept farms and shops running, nursed the sick and wounded, and organized religious and charitable activities that the state increasingly regulated.

The sources record these experiences unevenly. Petitions to local officials, parish and civil registries, court cases about land and inheritance, and complaints about requisitions allow historians to glimpse them, but the voices of illiterate villagers, enslaved or formerly enslaved people, and women are usually mediated by scribes and officials. The silences are themselves evidence of whose experience the surviving records preserved.

Section 63.20

Mexico's Struggle for Sovereignty: Defeat, Reform, and Republican Survival

The three crises of 1845–1867 tested sovereignty in different ways. The U.S.–Mexican War was a conflict between states that ended in territorial loss, and it exposed the weakness of a government that could not raise revenue, command its armies, or unite its regions. The War of Reform was a civil conflict over who had constitutional authority and what the legal order should be, in which both sides claimed to speak for the nation. The French Intervention challenged national independence and the republican form of government at the same time, and it was defeated by a combination of domestic resistance and international circumstances. In each case sovereignty meant something different: control of territory, legitimacy of government, and independence from foreign rule.

Several factors explain why the republic survived. State capacity, though limited, grew through the wartime reliance on customs revenue, the National Guard, and regional commanders. Constitutional legitimacy gave the Juárez government a claim that its rivals could not match, since its legal succession could be demonstrated and the empire's could not. Political coercion also mattered: the republic used levies, forced loans, executions, and decrees, and it suspended parts of the constitutional order in wartime. Social divisions both weakened and strengthened the republic. Conservative and imperial coalitions had substantial but regionally uneven support, while the republic's supporters included many Indigenous, mestizo, and Afro-Mexican communities with their own reasons for opposing a foreign emperor.

The consolidation was incomplete. Mexico's territorial losses were permanent, and the borderlands remained zones of conflict, migration, and negotiation. The Church lost its property and legal privileges but not its social influence. The land reforms harmed some communities and enriched others, and the problem of land would reappear in the Mexican Revolution. The restored republic was more stable than its predecessors but depended on personal leadership and, soon, on the military power of Porfirio Díaz, who would rule for most of the next four decades. Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Mexico in 1845 and 1872

What Changed

  • Mexico's territory shrank by about half, and its northern border was fixed
  • Ecclesiastical and military fueros were abolished, Church property nationalized, and the state secularized
  • A federal constitution with an individual-rights framework and amparo became the national standard
  • The monarchist alternative was discredited and republicanism became the national consensus

What Stayed the Same

  • Fiscal weakness, foreign debt, and dependence on a few revenue sources
  • Military pronouncements and regional rebellions as tools of politics
  • Rural inequality, haciendas, and the contested place of Indigenous communities
  • The popular importance of Catholicism

Who experienced change most directly: borderland residents, the clergy, and village communities facing new land rules. Greater continuity: rural workers and many urban poor. Why the pattern matters: Mexico gained a durable legal and political framework but did not resolve the social tensions that would shape the Porfiriato and the Revolution.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Mexico survived territorial defeat, internal constitutional conflict, and foreign intervention between 1845 and 1872. The three crises were distinct but connected, and together they transformed the Mexican state. The chapter's central argument is that Mexico's survival depended on a combination of regional resistance, constitutional legitimacy, Liberal reform, and the failures of its opponents, not on military victory alone or on the personal qualities of any leader.

The war with the United States grew out of Texas's annexation, an unresolved boundary, Polk's expansionism, and Mexico's political instability. Neither government was unified, and each acted under domestic constraints. U.S. forces won in the north, took New Mexico and California, and defeated Mexican armies in the campaign from Veracruz to the capital, while Mexican resistance, though often brave, was weakened by fiscal collapse, factional conflict, and forced recruitment.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ceded roughly 525,000 square miles and fixed the Rio Grande boundary. Its protections for Mexican residents were weakened in practice, and the new border divided Indigenous and Mexican-descended communities. The Gadsden Purchase of 1853–1854 added the Mesilla Valley under Santa Anna's dictatorship, which helped provoke the Revolution of Ayutla.

The revolution brought a Liberal coalition to power. La Reforma began with the Ley Juárez, which curtailed ecclesiastical and military fueros, and the Ley Lerdo, which required corporations to sell their property. The Constitution of 1857 established individual rights, amparo, and a federal republic. These measures had different purposes and consequences, and their effects on the Church, Indigenous communities, and the rural poor were uneven and remain debated.

The Plan of Tacubaya and Comonfort's abandonment of the constitution produced the War of Reform. Juárez asserted constitutional succession and governed from Veracruz, where in 1859 he issued the Reform Laws that nationalized Church property and established civil marriage and registry. The Liberals won at Calpulalpan in 1860, but the victory left the treasury empty and the Conservatives defeated but not eliminated.

The suspension of debt payments in 1861 led to the Convention of London and the intervention of Britain, France, and Spain. Only France stayed, pursuing Napoleon III's plan for a friendly monarchy supported by Mexican Conservatives. Mexican victory at Puebla in 1862 was symbolic, but French forces captured Puebla and the capital in 1863, and Maximilian arrived in 1864.

The Second Empire was a fragile regime. It depended on French troops, alienated the Church and Conservatives by keeping the Reform Laws, and failed to win Liberals. The republic, led by Juárez and regional commanders, survived through an itinerant government and widespread resistance. The end of the U.S. Civil War, French difficulties in Europe, and Napoleon III's withdrawal contributed, but republican resistance was essential. Querétaro fell in May 1867, and Maximilian was executed in June.

The restored republic under Juárez and then Lerdo faced reconstruction, rebellions, and contested elections. It expanded education, built railways and telegraphs, and consolidated constitutional government, but it depended on personal leadership and left land and social tensions unresolved. The chapter has shown that national survival was neither inevitable nor simply the result of a heroic figure. It was the outcome of political choices, regional resistance, and favorable international circumstances, and it laid the foundations for the Porfiriato.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that national survival can be a process of improvisation as much as design. Mexico lost a war, divided over its constitution, and defeated a foreign-backed emperor, and the republic that emerged was shaped by all three experiences. The Liberal state that Juárez's generation built was stronger in law and legitimacy than its predecessors, but it was built at a cost that fell unevenly on different regions and communities.

The period also shows how domestic and international histories were intertwined. The war with the United States cannot be understood without Mexican politics, and the intervention cannot be understood without the Reform. The border created in 1848 and the land policies of the Reforma set conditions for later conflicts over property, citizenship, and migration that continue into the twenty-first century, and the memory of the intervention shaped Mexican nationalism for generations.

Chapter 64 takes up the War of the Triple Alliance, another conflict that transformed a South American state, and Chapter 67 turns to Cuba and Puerto Rico, where Spanish rule persisted. Chapter 71 returns to Mexico under Porfirio Díaz, whose government inherited the Reform state and used it in ways that its authors did not foresee. Students should carry forward the view that the survival of a republic is a historical achievement requiring explanation, and that its limits are as revealing as its successes.
