The Treaty of Tordesillas, Brazil, and Competing Atlantic Empires

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Drawing a Line on an Ocean

In 1494 two Iberian monarchies agreed on a north–south line running through an ocean neither could accurately measure, across lands neither had seen, inhabited by peoples who had never been consulted. The agreement is one of the most famous in Atlantic history, yet its real significance lies less in the line itself than in the gap between what kings claimed on paper and what anyone could actually control.

In March 1493, Christopher Columbus, returning from his first voyage, was forced by storms to put into the Tagus estuary at Lisbon. There he was received by King João II of Portugal, the monarch whose mariners had spent most of the preceding seventy years pushing Portuguese ships down the African coast. According to the accounts that survive, João II was unsettled by what Columbus reported and quickly asserted that the lands Columbus had reached fell within the sphere Portugal already held under earlier treaty and papal grants. Within weeks, both crowns were competing to secure diplomatic advantage in Rome and to prepare ships. What had seemed to Columbus a triumphant return was, for the two Iberian courts, the opening of a legal and strategic crisis.

This chapter studies how that crisis was managed, and how it failed to produce the clean partition of the world that its authors imagined. It examines the papal bulls of 1493, the negotiations between the Catholic Monarchs, acting principally on behalf of the Crown of Castile in the western claims, and Portugal, and the treaty signed at the Castilian town of Tordesillas on 7 June 1494. It asks what authority the papacy claimed to exercise, why Portugal refused to accept the first papal arrangement, why the dividing line was moved westward, and what the treaty actually tried to regulate. It also takes seriously a practical problem that undermined the whole exercise: in the 1490s no navigator could reliably determine longitude at sea, so no one could say with confidence where the line fell.

The chapter then turns to Portuguese Brazil, which this textbook treats as a subject in its own right rather than as an appendix to Spanish America. Pedro Álvares Cabral's fleet reached the coast of what is now Brazil in April 1500, and over the following decades Portuguese crews, merchants, and exiles established a thin presence on the Atlantic shore, centered on the extraction of brazilwood. That presence depended on exchange with, and coercion of, Tupi-speaking peoples who were neither conquered nor passive. A claim inscribed in a treaty was not the same as occupation, and the gap between the two defines the early history of Portuguese America.

Finally, the chapter considers the practical limits of the Iberian partition. Spanish and Portuguese captains crossed the nominal line in both directions; other European powers, beginning with France, rejected the idea that two crowns could divide the overseas world between themselves; and Indigenous peoples on both sides of the line neither recognized papal or royal sovereignty nor had reason to. The chapter therefore introduces a problem that runs through the next three centuries of Latin American history: the distinction between claim and control.

The evidence for this period is textual and cartographic rather than archaeological. It includes the treaty and the papal bulls themselves, the letter written to King Manuel I from the Brazilian coast in 1500, early Portuguese and Spanish maps such as the Cantino planisphere of 1502, royal contracts for the brazilwood trade, and later chronicles. Almost all of it was produced by Europeans. Tupi-speaking peoples left no comparable written record of these first encounters, and historians must read European sources carefully, and alongside later ethnographic and archaeological research, to recover their perspective.

Chapter 28 follows Chapter 27's account of Columbus and the Caribbean encounter without retelling it, and precedes Chapter 29's treatment of epidemic disease, demographic collapse, and the Columbian Exchange. This chapter's subject is the legal, diplomatic, and commercial architecture within which Iberian expansion proceeded, and the first stages of Portuguese presence in Brazil. Fuller treatment of Portuguese colonial governance, plantation society, and slavery in Brazil belongs to later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Columbus's 1492–1493 voyage created an immediate diplomatic conflict between Castile and Portugal.
  2. Trace the earlier papal grants and the Treaty of Alcáçovas (1479) that shaped Portuguese and Castilian expectations before 1492.
  3. Describe the papal bulls of 1493 and analyze the authority the papacy claimed in issuing them.
  4. Explain why Portugal negotiated directly with the Catholic Monarchs (for the Crown of Castile), and why the dividing line was moved westward in 1494.
  5. Assess why the location of the Tordesillas line could not be determined with confidence, with reference to the problem of longitude.
  6. Analyze why Indigenous peoples were excluded from the legal framework of Iberian partition and how that exclusion differed from practical reality.
  7. Describe Cabral's 1500 arrival in Brazil and interpret the evidence provided by Pero Vaz de Caminha's letter.
  8. Explain the brazilwood trade and the role of Tupi-speaking peoples within it.
  9. Distinguish diplomatic claim, reconnaissance, commercial contact, and sustained settlement as different stages of imperial presence.
  10. Evaluate why Tordesillas did not in practice divide the Atlantic, including the roles of navigation, private mariners, and rival European states.
  11. Compare Spanish and Portuguese approaches to their early American claims without treating Brazil as a footnote.
  12. Connect the treaty and early Brazil to the larger history of competing Atlantic empires.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Columbus's voyage create a legal problem for Portugal and Castile when neither crown had previously known that these lands existed?
  2. What did the papacy claim the authority to do in 1493, and why did both Iberian monarchies seek papal approval for their claims?
  3. Why did João II choose to negotiate rather than accept the papal line, and what does his decision suggest about Portuguese priorities?
  4. What is the difference between a claim to sovereignty, the right to trade, and effective control of territory?
  5. How did technological limits, especially the inability to measure longitude, shape the practical meaning of the treaty?
  6. How should historians interpret the fact that Indigenous peoples had no role in agreements that purported to allocate their lands?
  7. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct the first Portuguese–Tupi encounters, and what perspectives do those sources omit?
  8. How did the brazilwood trade work, and why did it create a Portuguese presence without creating a Portuguese colony?
  9. Why did France and, later, other northern European powers reject the premise of Iberian partition?

Historical Context

Two developments covered in earlier chapters are essential background here. Chapter 25 examined Castile, Aragon, and Portugal on the eve of Atlantic expansion, and Chapter 26 examined the Portuguese movement down the West African coast and the early Atlantic slave trade. Chapter 27 followed Columbus's voyages and the first Spanish settlements in the Caribbean. This chapter assumes those narratives and summarizes only what is necessary.

Portuguese expansion in the fifteenth century was a sustained state-and-merchant enterprise. Portuguese ships occupied or colonized the Madeira and Azores archipelagos, established trading positions along the West African coast, developed commercial access to gold and enslaved people, and gradually extended their knowledge of Atlantic winds and currents. In 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa, demonstrating that an oceanic route to the Indian Ocean was possible. Portugal's long investment in this African route explains why João II viewed any rival Castilian activity in the Atlantic with alarm, and why he was more interested in securing the sea route around Africa than in the western lands Columbus had reached.

Castile's Atlantic ambitions were of more recent date. The Castilian crown, unified in a dynastic sense with Aragon through the marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand, had acquired the Canary Islands and completed the conquest of the Muslim kingdom of Granada in January 1492. Columbus's voyage had been sponsored in that setting, and it gave the Castilian monarchs a potential route to Asian wealth that did not pass through waters Portugal had long dominated. That same fact made the voyage diplomatically dangerous.

The legal culture of the period also mattered. Late medieval Christian Europe had developed ideas, contested even at the time, about the pope's authority to grant rights over lands not ruled by Christian princes. Papal bulls had by then authorized Portuguese expansion and enslavement in Africa, and both Iberian crowns understood papal endorsement as a valuable form of legitimacy vis-à-vis other Christian rulers. It is essential to understand, however, that papal donations were recognized as binding, if at all, among European Christian states. They had no standing whatsoever among the Indigenous peoples of the Americas, who lived under their own political and religious systems and who were examined in Volume I.

Finally, students should be aware of the evidence problem. The surviving documents were produced by European courts, churchmen, and mariners. They reveal a great deal about Iberian diplomatic reasoning but very little directly about how Indigenous communities understood the arrival of Europeans or the claims being made in their name. Sections 28.6 and 28.9 address this limitation directly.

Section 28.1

After 1492: Spain, Portugal, and the Problem of Atlantic Claims

When Columbus returned to Europe in March 1493, he carried news of islands and peoples unknown to European geography, along with small quantities of gold and several Indigenous captives. Columbus believed, and later insisted, that he had reached the outer edge of Asia. Whether or not that belief was correct, its practical effect was to place Castile in an area of the Atlantic that Portuguese officials regarded as their own preserve. The issue was not primarily the lands themselves, whose extent and value were unknown, but the legal and commercial architecture of the Atlantic as a whole.

Portugal's position rested on the Treaty of Alcáçovas of 1479, which had, as part of the settlement of the war over the Castilian succession, allocated Atlantic zones between the two crowns. Castile had been confirmed in possession of the Canary Islands. Portugal had been confirmed in its rights over Madeira, the Azores, the Cape Verde Islands, and the African coast, and in a right, read broadly by Portuguese officials, to navigation and exploration south of the latitude of the Canaries. From the Portuguese viewpoint, Columbus had reached lands by sailing west from the Canaries and then south-west, which, on one reading, brought his discoveries into the region allocated to Portugal.

Castile's counterargument was that Columbus had sailed a westward route that Alcáçovas had not addressed, and that the treaty's provisions concerning Africa did not govern new lands across the ocean. João II appears to have taken a practical rather than purely legal approach. According to the chronicle tradition, he contemplated sending a fleet to the western lands and, at the same time, opened negotiations. The Castilian monarchs responded by sending an embassy to João and, simultaneously, by appealing to Pope Alexander VI, a fellow subject of the Crown of Aragon by birth and therefore, from the Castilian perspective, a promising advocate.

The dispute illustrates a pattern that will recur throughout this volume: European states treated overseas expansion as a matter of competing legal claims among themselves, long before they had the capacity to occupy what they claimed. That the lands in question were already inhabited, and that their inhabitants had complete political and cultural systems of their own, was acknowledged in practice by the very need to negotiate with, trade with, and fight Indigenous peoples, but it played no role in the legal discussions between the two crowns.

Section 28.2

Earlier Papal Grants and the Portuguese Atlantic

The papacy had been involved in Portuguese expansion for decades before 1493. Papal bulls issued in the middle of the fifteenth century, including those associated with Nicholas V (Dum diversas, 1452, and Romanus Pontifex, 1455) and Calixtus III (Inter caetera, 1456), recognized Portuguese rights to explore and trade in the African Atlantic, sanctioned the conquest of non-Christian peoples there, and gave Portugal a claim to priority over Christian rivals. A later bull of Sixtus IV, Aeterni regis (1481), confirmed the provisions of Alcáçovas. Dum diversas and Romanus Pontifex explicitly authorized the Portuguese crown to conquer specified non-Christian peoples and to reduce captives to perpetual slavery. Historians debate the relationship between this legal-religious authorization and the developing Portuguese slave trade, a subject addressed in Chapters 26 and 44, but the authorization itself is explicit, and Portuguese officials used the texts to support monopoly claims.

What did these documents actually do? They did not bind Indigenous or African rulers, who did not recognize papal authority, and they did not automatically bind other European monarchs, several of whom, including the kings of France and England, were prepared to contest Iberian claims. Their practical value was diplomatic. A papal grant gave a crown a formal, publicly recognized statement of legitimacy that could be invoked in negotiations with other Christian states and that could be used to justify, to Christian subjects and clergy, campaigns that might otherwise have been difficult to explain in religious terms.

The Portuguese Atlantic at the end of the fifteenth century was, therefore, a zone in which Portugal held legal positions built on papal grants and on treaty, commercial positions built on forts, islands, and trading arrangements, and navigational knowledge built on decades of experience. Castile, by contrast, had the Canaries, the Columbus voyage, and a developing royal interest in western expansion. Each crown wanted to avoid giving the other the initiative in Rome, and each had reason to fear that an open contest would invite intervention by other powers. This combination of competitive and cooperative incentives explains why both sides were prepared to accept a compromise that, on its face, looked like a division of the world.

Section 28.3

The Bulls of 1493 and Papal Diplomacy

In May 1493, Pope Alexander VI issued a series of documents in response to Castilian petitions. The most famous, Inter caetera, granted to the Castilian crown rights over lands discovered, or to be discovered, in the western Atlantic that were not already held by a Christian prince, and tied those rights to the obligation to spread Christianity. Other documents of the same period, including Eximiae devotionis, extended to Castile privileges comparable to those the papacy had previously granted to Portugal. A later bull, Dudum siquidem, issued in September 1493, broadened the Castilian grant in ways that Portuguese officials found especially alarming, since it appeared to reach toward Asia.

The bulls contained a line of demarcation, running north to south and placed 100 leagues to the west of the Azores and Cape Verde Islands. Lands west of that line not already held by a Christian ruler were to belong to Castile. The line was new in concept. Earlier Iberian arrangements had divided the Atlantic largely by latitude, not by a meridian, and the idea of a north–south boundary in an open ocean reflected the novel situation created by westward voyages.

Students should be careful about the claim made by the papacy. The bulls asserted that the pope, as vicar of Christ, could grant rights of conquest and evangelization over non-Christian lands. This was a theory with deep roots in medieval canon law and was contested by jurists even at the time. Some theologians and lawyers, in Spain and elsewhere, would later argue that non-Christian peoples possessed legitimate political authority and property rights that papal grants could not extinguish, a debate that became prominent in Spain in the sixteenth century and is treated in later chapters of this volume. The bulls themselves, however, reflect the prevailing assumption of the Iberian courts in 1493.

From Lisbon's point of view, the arrangement was unsatisfactory. The 100-league line, measured from the Atlantic island groups, left Portuguese ships with limited room to sail westward into the open ocean, which was a practical necessity for any vessel heading toward southern Africa. Portuguese navigators had learned that the most efficient route southward involved a long arc west and south through the Atlantic, taking advantage of prevailing winds, a technique sometimes called the volta do mar. Portuguese officials had reason to worry that so narrow a western margin could constrain or complicate this maneuvering, and protection of the route toward southern Africa is one important explanation for João II's insistence on a more westerly boundary. João II therefore declined to accept the bulls as settling the question and pressed for direct negotiation with Castile. Castile, for its part, had little interest in risking open conflict.

Section 28.4

Negotiating the Treaty of Tordesillas

Negotiations took place in the months following the bulls, culminating in the treaty signed at Tordesillas on 7 June 1494. The Castilian side ratified it in July and the Portuguese crown in September 1494. The agreement was a bilateral treaty between monarchies, not a papal act, although both sides viewed papal confirmation as desirable; Pope Julius II provided it in 1506.

The treaty's central provision moved the line of demarcation far to the west. Instead of 100 leagues from the Azores and Cape Verde Islands, the line was to run 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Islands and mainlands discovered or to be discovered east of the line were assigned to the Portuguese sphere, while those west of it were assigned to Castile; the treaty also regulated navigation across the respective zones. The treaty permitted Castilian ships to cross the seas on Portugal's side of the line while traveling to Castile's western zone, provided they did not seize or claim discoveries there. It also stipulated that discoveries made by either crown within the other's assigned zone belonged to the crown to which that zone had been allocated. The treaty further provided for a joint expedition of pilots, sailors, and other specialists to determine and mark the line, a plan that was never effectively carried out.

Why did Portugal press so hard for a westward shift? A major and widely cited explanation is that João II wanted secure sea room for the southern Atlantic route, which would run toward the Cape of Good Hope and the Indian Ocean. A line 370 leagues from Cape Verde gave Portuguese ships ample space to execute the volta do mar without straying into Castile's zone. A second explanation, more controversial, is that Portuguese officials already suspected or knew that land lay to the southwest, and sought to keep it within their sphere. This question is examined in the Historical Debate later in this chapter. Whichever explanation is preferred, it is important to note that the treaty did not name Brazil, since no European document of the time could have named it in any settled sense.

Castile's acceptance of the westward shift is usually explained by its interest in a stable, legally secure arrangement and its recognition that it could not immediately exploit the Atlantic space in question. The Castilian monarchs also preserved what mattered most to them: a large western sector in which Columbus and others could operate. The treaty was, in short, a pragmatic compromise between two monarchies with overlapping interests, not a statement of geographical knowledge. It also transferred what had been a papal prerogative into the hands of the crowns themselves, a development that mattered later when other states rejected the premise that any pope could allocate the world.

Section 28.5

Where Was the Line? Geography, Longitude, and Uncertainty

A line defined as 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands raises immediate practical questions. First, which island in the archipelago was the starting point? Second, how long was a league? Different leagues were in use, and the treaty did not specify which one was meant. Third, and most fundamentally, how could anyone determine, at sea or on land, whether a given place lay east or west of a north–south meridian?

Latitude, the distance north or south of the equator, could be estimated with reasonable accuracy in this period by measuring the height of the sun or the pole star with an astrolabe or quadrant. Longitude, the distance east or west along a line of latitude, could not. Determining longitude requires knowing the difference between local time and the time at a reference point, and in the 1490s no shipboard clock was reliable enough for the task. Navigators relied on dead reckoning, estimating distance from speed, heading, and elapsed time, a method subject to large cumulative errors. A dependable chronometer for use at sea would not be developed until the eighteenth century.

The consequences were significant. Modern reconstructions, which vary depending on the league assumed and the point of origin in the Cape Verde Islands, place the line at very roughly 46° to 50° west of Greenwich. Such a line would pass through the eastern part of South America, cutting across what is now Brazil, and places some reconstructions near enough to the Amazon region that the allocation of particular northern-coast landfalls depends on the assumed position of the line. One commonly cited reconstruction falls near 46°37′ W. But contemporaries had no means of verifying the position, and cartographers, pilots, and courtiers frequently drew the line in different places, often in ways that favored their own crown. Some Portuguese maps placed it further west, enlarging the Portuguese zone; some Castilian ones shifted it east.

This uncertainty was not a minor technicality. It meant that, in practice, the treaty's boundary was a matter of negotiation, interpretation, and power rather than measurement. The issue became acute when the question shifted from the Atlantic to the Pacific. After Magellan's voyage reached the Philippines and the Moluccas (the Spice Islands) in the early 1520s, Castile and Portugal each claimed that the Moluccas fell on their side of the line's extension around the globe. The dispute was settled not by measurement but by money and diplomacy in the Treaty of Zaragoza (1529), in which Castile renounced its claim to the Moluccas in return for a payment. Geography, in short, was subordinate to negotiation.

Historical Vocabulary
Line of Demarcation

A line of demarcation is a boundary, here a north–south meridian, intended to allocate zones of overseas exploration and sovereignty between two powers. The term is used both for the 100-league line in the papal bulls of 1493 and for the 370-league line of the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494.

Modern students often picture a line drawn on a finished world map. In 1494 no such map existed. The line was a verbal formula applied to a poorly known ocean, and its practical location was uncertain. It is best understood as a diplomatic instrument for managing competition between two crowns, not as a precise territorial division. It also should not be confused with later colonial boundaries within the Americas, which were shaped by settlement, warfare, and later treaties.

Section 28.6

Sovereignty on Paper and Indigenous Worlds in Practice

The bulls and the treaty shared an assumption that European rulers could allocate rights over lands that they had not seen, occupied, or governed. For the peoples who lived in those lands, the assumption had no force. Volume I examined the political systems of the Americas as they existed before sustained European contact: the Mexica and their tributary and rival states, the Inca realm of Tawantinsuyu, the Taíno chiefdoms of the Caribbean, and the many autonomous peoples of the Amazon basin and the Atlantic coast. None of these societies recognized papal authority, none was party to the treaty, and most had not yet encountered Europeans at all.

It is therefore important to distinguish three different meanings of the word "claim." A claim in the diplomatic sense was a position asserted against other European powers. A claim in the commercial sense was a right to trade, often exercised through monopolies. A claim in the territorial sense, effective control over land and people, required settlement, military capacity, and the cooperation or submission of Indigenous communities. Tordesillas dealt with the first and, to some degree, the second. It said nothing that could automatically produce the third.

In the decades after 1494 this gap shaped everything. In the Caribbean, Castilian settlers used coercion, violence, and labor systems described in Chapter 27 to establish control, and Indigenous resistance and demographic catastrophe (examined in Chapter 29) produced outcomes that neither crown had planned. In Brazil, as the following sections show, the Portuguese crown claimed a large territory but for decades possessed only a few coastal trading positions, and the terms on which its agents operated were set as much by Tupi-speaking communities as by Lisbon.

Later jurists would return to the question of whether papal donations or discovery could confer legitimate sovereignty over inhabited lands. Some Spanish theologians, notably in the sixteenth century, argued that Indigenous peoples had legitimate rulers and property, and that the crown's rights, if any, rested on other grounds. These debates belong to later chapters. For this chapter, the point is that the legal structure created in 1493–1494 was, from the start, an agreement among Europeans about how to manage competition, not an account of the political realities of the places it described.

Section 28.7

Cabral and the Portuguese Arrival in Brazil, 1500

Portugal's first priority after Tordesillas was the route to India. Vasco da Gama's fleet sailed in 1497, reached Calicut on the Malabar coast in 1498, and returned with spices and proof that the eastern route was viable. In March 1500, King Manuel I dispatched a much larger fleet, commanded by Pedro Álvares Cabral, with about thirteen ships and more than a thousand men, to establish commercial relations in India and to build on Gama's success.

On 22 April 1500, after a wide swing into the Atlantic consistent with the volta do mar, Cabral's fleet sighted land on the coast of what is now the state of Bahia. The first landmark was a hill the Portuguese named Monte Pascoal, after Easter. The fleet anchored and, a few days later, moved to a harbor the sources call Porto Seguro. There the Portuguese met people usually identified as Tupiniquim, a Tupi-speaking people, exchanged goods with them, celebrated Mass, and raised a cross. The land was initially named after the Holy Cross, and the name later gave way to "Brazil," derived from the dyewood that became its first export.

Whether Cabral's landfall was accidental or intended has been debated for centuries. The wide westward swing was standard practice for ships sailing south, and a deviation of the kind that would bring a fleet to the Brazilian coast was plausible. Some historians, however, have argued that the crown had prior knowledge of land in the southwest and intended the fleet to confirm it. This is addressed in the Historical Debate below. What is certain is that Cabral sent a ship back to Lisbon with news, and that the fleet continued toward India after leaving at least two convicts, degredados, on the coast to learn about the land and its people. Convicts were a common instrument of Portuguese reconnaissance, as they were expendable and could be left to acquire language and local knowledge.

The most important surviving account of the landing is a letter written by Pero Vaz de Caminha, the fleet's scribe, to King Manuel I. It is examined in the Primary Source Window below. Other sources include later chronicles and the testimony of pilots. It is also necessary to note that Cabral was not the first European to reach the Brazilian coast in 1500. Spanish captains, including Vicente Yáñez Pinzón, who had sailed with Columbus, reached the northern coast in early 1500, and other Castilian expeditions followed. These voyages were, under the treaty's terms, problematic, because some of the landfalls may have lain on the Portuguese side of the line. Cabral's voyage is famous because it was documented, because it was tied to royal policy, and because it was followed by sustained Portuguese activity.

Section 28.8

Brazilwood, Coastal Exchange, and the First Portuguese Presence

The Portuguese crown's initial reaction to Cabral's discovery was measured. The new land had no evident gold or silver and lay far from the India route on which Portugal's resources were concentrated. The first organized Portuguese response was a reconnaissance expedition of 1501–1502, in which crews followed the coast southward, named many features after saints' days, and gathered information. A second major reconnaissance expedition followed in 1503–1504. Florentine navigator Amerigo Vespucci took part in at least one of these voyages, although the details of his participation are contested. The resulting knowledge appeared on maps such as the Cantino planisphere of 1502, which shows the line of demarcation and the newly explored coast.

The commodity that drew Portuguese merchants was brazilwood (pau-brasil), a tree whose heartwood yields a red dye prized by European textile makers. Brazilwood was abundant along parts of the Atlantic coast. The crown asserted a monopoly over the trade but, rather than operating it directly, granted concessions to merchant partnerships, including a consortium associated with the Lisbon merchant Fernão de Loronha (Noronha), in exchange for payments and obligations to explore the coast and build trading posts. The arrangement reflected a standard Portuguese approach: use private capital to develop commerce while the crown collected revenue and retained nominal sovereignty.

The trade depended on Indigenous labor. Tupi-speaking communities cut the trees, prepared the logs, and carried them to the shore, where they were loaded onto Portuguese ships. In return they received iron tools, cloth, mirrors, and other goods. Iron axes and knives, in particular, were valuable for clearing land and working wood. The exchange took the form of barter and was organized around relationships with particular communities and their leaders, sometimes reinforced by the intermarriage or cohabitation of Europeans with Indigenous women, and often by the work of interpreters, some of them degredados or shipwrecked sailors who had lived among coastal peoples. European merchants built small fortified trading stations, feitorias, at favorable points, in which goods were stored pending shipment. These were modest installations, not colonies.

For roughly three decades, this coastal commerce was the main form of Portuguese presence. It created a pattern of contact, exchange, and dependence, and it introduced European goods and diseases to the Atlantic coast, a subject taken up in Chapter 29. It also brought competition. French ships, particularly from Normandy and Brittany, traded for brazilwood and cultivated their own relationships with coastal peoples. The Portuguese crown periodically sent armed patrols against such "interlopers," with limited success. The territorial and administrative system that later characterized Portuguese Brazil, including hereditary captaincies introduced in the 1530s, a governor-general after 1549, and plantation agriculture, belongs to later chapters, especially Chapters 39 and 41. What matters here is that the claim made at Tordesillas and in Cabral's landing was turned into a thin but real commercial presence before it was turned into a colony.

Historical Vocabulary
Brazilwood (pau-brasil)

Brazilwood is the common name for a dyewood tree of the Atlantic forest, whose heartwood yields a red pigment. Europeans already knew a similar dyewood from Asia, and the name "brasil" predates the American discovery. The Portuguese applied it to the American tree, and the land eventually took its name from the commodity.

The term matters because it shows how a commercial product shaped a colonial identity, and because the trade depended on Indigenous labor organized through exchange rather than on European settlement. It should not be confused with the sugar plantation economy that later dominated colonial Brazil and relied on very different labor systems, including large-scale African slavery.

Section 28.9

Indigenous Peoples and the Limits of Portuguese Control

The peoples of the Atlantic coast of Brazil in 1500 were largely Tupi-speaking communities, among them those later called Tupinambá, and they were by no means a single political entity. They lived in villages of large communal houses, practiced cultivation of manioc and other crops supplemented by fishing, hunting, and gathering, and were linked by kinship, alliance, and frequently by warfare. Conflicts among communities involved captive-taking and ritual practices that European observers found striking and that they interpreted through their own assumptions. Volume I chapters on Amazonia and on southern South America introduced the broader Indigenous world of which these communities were part.

For the Portuguese, the first relationships were built on exchange, and the terms of exchange were negotiated. Coastal communities chose when and with whom to trade, and they integrated European goods into existing systems of prestige and alliance. Leaders who controlled access to Europeans could gain advantage over rivals, and alliances with Europeans were sometimes pursued as means of strengthening a community against its Indigenous enemies. It would be mistaken to picture these communities as simply receiving Europeans or as resisting them uniformly. The record shows variation: cooperation, avoidance, hostility, and opportunism, depending on circumstances, leadership, and past experience.

Portuguese control in this period was limited in several ways. The number of Europeans on the coast was small. The crown lacked the resources for large-scale occupation, given its commitments in Africa and Asia. Coastal trade depended on the goodwill and cooperation of Indigenous communities, and outposts could be vulnerable to attack when relations soured. The vast interior was unexplored. There were episodes of violence, including attacks on Europeans and punitive expeditions by them, and there were early instances of enslavement and coercion of Indigenous people, which would grow in scale after the 1530s as Portuguese settlement expanded. But in the first decades the principal dynamic was commercial exchange under conditions in which Indigenous communities retained substantial autonomy.

Historians face an evidentiary challenge in reconstructing this encounter. The sources were written by Europeans, who interpreted Tupi society through their own categories, often emphasizing features that seemed most strange and omitting what they did not understand. Tupi-speaking communities left no written records. Scholars therefore draw on later ethnographic accounts (which must be used with care because of the demographic and political changes that intervened), linguistic evidence, archaeological research, and careful reading against the grain of European texts. This work also reminds students that Indigenous history in Brazil does not end with Portuguese arrival. It continues through the colonial period and into the present, as later chapters show.

Section 28.10

Spain, Portugal, and Rival Exploration beyond the Treaty Line

The treaty produced a pattern of exploration in which each crown's navigators crossed the line, sometimes deliberately and often by accident. Spanish captains such as Vicente Yáñez Pinzón and Diego de Lepe, who sailed along the northern coast of South America in 1499–1500, reached areas that later became part of Portuguese Brazil by any reasonable reading of the line. Castilian expeditions along the Atlantic coast of South America continued in the following decade. In 1516 Juan Díaz de Solís entered the Río de la Plata estuary, which lies in a zone of uncertain or contested status on the Castilian side of most readings, and was killed by Indigenous inhabitants. The expedition of Ferdinand Magellan, which sailed under Castilian auspices in 1519 and passed the Río de la Plata and the strait later named for him, illustrates how the treaty's logic extended around the globe.

Portuguese ships likewise operated in ways that the treaty's provisions did not cleanly govern. The Brazilian coast, at least the portion east of the line, was Portuguese by the terms of the treaty, but the treaty said nothing about how the limits of that zone should be policed, and Portuguese traders pushed southward toward the Río de la Plata region, where Castilian and Portuguese interests would collide for centuries. In later chapters, the border between Portuguese and Spanish America will emerge as a product of settlement, warfare, missions, and later treaties rather than of the 1494 line.

Magellan's circumnavigation and the dispute over the Moluccas made plain that the treaty could not be applied uniformly to a globe. The line had to have an antimeridian on the other side of the world, and the location of spice-producing islands relative to that antimeridian could not be established with the technology of the day. The Treaty of Zaragoza of 1529 resolved the dispute by payment rather than measurement. That both crowns were willing to negotiate in this way shows that they treated the partition as a flexible instrument of diplomacy more than as a fixed geographical truth.

Section 28.11

Why Tordesillas Did Not Divide the Atlantic in Practice

Several factors combined to prevent the treaty from dividing the Atlantic as its authors intended. The first was geographic ignorance. The line's location could not be determined, and so its application to new discoveries was a matter of dispute. The second was the power of private and semi-private actors. Mariners, merchants, and pilots pursued profit with only loose supervision, and their commercial and personal networks crossed royal boundaries, with Italians, Flemings, Germans, and others providing capital and expertise to both crowns. The third was Indigenous agency, discussed above: neither Iberian crown could impose its claims without the cooperation, acquiescence, or defeat of the peoples who lived in the lands concerned.

The fourth and most consequential factor was the rejection of the premise by other European powers. Kings of France, England, and later the Dutch Republic had not been parties to the agreement and did not accept that a papal donation or a bilateral treaty could exclude them from oceanic trade and settlement. The French king Francis I is reported to have asked, with sarcasm, to see the clause in Adam's will that divided the world between Spain and Portugal. Whatever the authenticity of the remark, the sentiment reflects a real position. French ships traded for brazilwood within the Portuguese zone, and French corsairs preyed on Iberian shipping. Over the following century, legal thinkers such as the Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius would articulate arguments for freedom of the seas, challenging the concept of exclusive claims to ocean space, although that debate lies largely beyond this chapter.

A fifth factor was the Protestant Reformation, which from the 1520s undermined the papal authority on which the 1493 bulls had rested, at least for northern European states that broke with Rome. For those states, papal grants had no legitimacy at all. By the late sixteenth century, English, French, and Dutch ventures in the Atlantic and Caribbean openly ignored the Iberian partition. The contest thereafter was less about competing readings of the treaty than about the actual ability of each power to occupy, defend, and exploit territory.

This leads to the central problem the chapter has sought to develop: the distinction between claim and control. The Iberian crowns claimed enormous territories. They controlled, at first, very little: islands, coastal stations, and some strategic points. Over time, in different regions and by different means, they built systems of governance, taxation, and labor extraction that converted some claims into control. Even then, control was often partial and contested, particularly on frontiers, as later chapters on Chile, the Río de la Plata, and the Amazonian and northern borderlands will show.

Section 28.12

From Diplomatic Partition to Competing Atlantic Empires

The Treaty of Tordesillas had long-term consequences that its authors did not foresee. One was the linguistic and administrative division between Spanish America and Portuguese Brazil. Because the treaty placed the easternmost part of South America in the Portuguese zone, and because Portugal followed up with trade and, eventually, settlement, the region became the core of a Portuguese-speaking colony. Yet this outcome cannot be attributed to the treaty alone. The eventual shape of Brazil owed as much to later Portuguese expansion, including movement well beyond any plausible reading of the line, as to the diplomatic arrangement of 1494.

Another consequence was the establishment of a model in which European rivals competed over transoceanic claims by legal argument as well as force. The arguments used at Tordesillas, about discovery, papal authority, and prior possession, would be revived and transformed in later disputes, including those among Spain, Portugal, France, England, and the Dutch Republic. Much of the history of the Caribbean, the Guianas, and the Atlantic coast of North America in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries can be read as a long contest over whether the Iberian claims would be honored, ignored, or overturned.

A third consequence was a division of imperial energy. Portugal, committed to the Indian Ocean, gave comparatively little attention to Brazil in the first decades, and only after the 1530s did it begin to invest in systematic colonization, in part because of French competition. Castile, by contrast, turned its attention westward and, within a generation, would be conquering the Mexica and Inca realms, examined in Volume II's later chapters. The same treaty that placed Brazil in Portugal's sphere thus helped give the two empires distinct strategic priorities and distinct colonial trajectories, which students should compare without assuming that either was more "natural" or inevitable.

Finally, the treaty's history underscores a point important for the remainder of this textbook: the people who lived in the lands concerned were not absent from the story. Their decisions to trade, resist, ally, or avoid shaped the real outcome at least as much as the decisions of kings, popes, and navigators. Chapter 29 turns to a process that altered Indigenous societies more profoundly than any diplomatic agreement: the epidemics, demographic collapse, and ecological exchanges that followed Atlantic contact.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Pero Vaz de Caminha's Letter to King Manuel I, 1500

Creator: Pero Vaz de Caminha, scribe of Cabral's fleet · Date: Written at Porto Seguro in early May 1500 · Type: Official letter to the king

Context: Caminha wrote to inform King Manuel I of the fleet's landfall and of the people encountered there. The letter was sent back to Portugal with a returning ship. It remained in Portuguese archives and was not widely known until rediscovered and published in the nineteenth century.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): Caminha describes the coast and its vegetation, reports that the Portuguese saw people on the shore and exchanged gifts with them, remarks at length on their appearance, lack of clothing, and apparent ease, records that Mass was celebrated and a cross erected, notes the apparent absence of gold and metals and the potential for the land to support settlement, and reports that two convicts were to be left behind. He also suggests that the inhabitants might readily be converted to Christianity.

Analysis: The letter is invaluable as the earliest detailed written description of a Tupi-speaking community (commonly identified as Tupiniquim) by a European observer. It also shows how Portuguese officials framed the encounter: through religious purpose, the search for wealth, and assumptions about the receptivity of the people. Caminha's descriptions are filtered through his own expectations and through a very limited ability to communicate. He did not speak the language, and he could not know how the Indigenous people understood the events. The letter also reveals what Portuguese officials valued: its attention to gold, to the prospects for settlement, and to conversion. Missing entirely are Indigenous perspectives, and the letter tells us nothing of the diplomatic disputes with Castile that shaped the context of the voyage.

Questions for students:

  1. What does Caminha report about the landing and the people the Portuguese met?
  2. Who was the audience, and how might that have shaped the letter's content?
  3. What can historians learn about early Portuguese priorities from the letter?
  4. What can historians not establish about Tupi-speaking communities from this source alone?
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Portugal Know about Brazil before 1494, or before 1500?

The question: Did João II press for a westward shift of the line in 1494 because he knew or suspected that land lay to the southwest, and was Cabral's landfall in 1500 intentional?

Interpretation A — Prior knowledge or strong suspicion: Some historians argue that Portuguese pilots, after decades of Atlantic exploration, had probably encountered signs of land, such as drifting vegetation and bird movements, or even the coast itself, and that João II's insistence on the 370-league line was designed to secure that territory. They also point to the apparent ease of Cabral's landfall and to the speed with which the crown responded.
Interpretation B — Navigational rationale, and accident or opportunism: Other historians emphasize that the westward shift is fully explained by the need for sea room for the volta do mar, and that the Atlantic route passes close enough to Brazil that a fleet standing well to the west could reach it without foreknowledge. They also note that surviving documents provide no direct proof of pre-1494 discovery, and that the crown's modest early interest in Brazil seems at odds with deliberate planning.

Evidence: The debate rests on treaty text, navigational practice, later chronicles that may reflect hindsight, the silence of the surviving records, and arguments about Portuguese royal secrecy. Because the crown had reasons to conceal navigational information, an absence of documents is not conclusive either way.

Why it matters: If Portugal knew, the treaty becomes an instrument of deliberate imperial planning. If not, it becomes a flexible compromise whose outcome for Brazil was largely contingent. The debate illustrates the difficulty of inferring intention from outcome.

Student task: Identify what kinds of new evidence would most decisively support one interpretation, and explain why silence in the sources is difficult to interpret.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Line of 1494

These are reconstructed analytical perspectives, not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not hold equal power.

Portuguese crown: The treaty secured sea room for the route to Asia and preserved the long-standing Portuguese position in the Atlantic. Brazil, when found, was a secondary prize within a strategy centered on India.
Catholic Monarchs / Crown of Castile: For the Crown of Castile, the treaty converted Columbus's voyage into a recognized legal position and avoided a war with Portugal, at the price of ceding part of the western Atlantic to Portuguese claims.
Tupi-speaking communities of the Brazilian coast: The agreement was unknown and irrelevant. What mattered was the arrival of strangers with valuable goods, the opportunities and dangers this created for alliance and rivalry, and, in time, the demands for labor and the new threats that came with sustained contact.
French and other northern European merchants and rulers: The treaty was a bilateral arrangement that did not bind them. They traded where profit lay and, in time, challenged the Iberian claims directly.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Claims and Control in the Atlantic

What changed?

  • The Atlantic went from a zone divided by latitude (Alcáçovas) to one with a meridian line (1493–1494).
  • Portugal extended its reach from Africa to the coast of South America.
  • A region previously unknown to Europeans entered European commercial and legal systems.

What remained continuous?

  • Indigenous societies continued to govern themselves and to negotiate the terms of contact.
  • Royal monopoly claims coexisted with dependence on private capital and local cooperation.
  • Navigation depended on wind systems and experience, not on precise measurement.

Who experienced change most directly? Coastal Indigenous communities, who faced new trade, new rivalries, and eventually new demands. Who experienced greater continuity? Interior peoples, for whom daily life changed more slowly at first. Why the pattern matters: It shows that legal and diplomatic change can precede and diverge from change on the ground.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Treaty of Tordesillas

Long-term causes

Decades of Portuguese Atlantic expansion, papal grants, the Treaty of Alcáçovas, and the legal tradition of papal authority over non-Christian lands.

Short-term causes

Columbus's 1492–1493 voyage, João II's claim under Alcáçovas, Castilian appeal to Alexander VI, and Portugal's need for sea room on the southern route.

Event or transformation

The 1493 bulls and the 1494 treaty, placing a line 370 leagues west of Cape Verde.

Immediate effects

Avoidance of open Iberian conflict; Portuguese security for the African route; later basis for Portuguese presence in Brazil.

Long-term consequences

Spanish–Portuguese division of South America in broad outline; a model for claims-based imperial competition; rejection by northern European powers.

Historical complexity

The treaty did not determine Brazil's later borders or colonial form; those resulted from settlement, warfare, and later treaties.
Everyday Life and Social History

Life on Ship and Shore

The people who carried out Iberian expansion were not only kings and navigators. Cabral's fleet carried sailors, soldiers, artisans, priests, and scribes, in crowded ships on voyages of many months, with limited food, frequent sickness, and high risk of shipwreck. Among them were convicts, who could be sentenced to exile in distant lands as an alternative to punishment at home, and who served as expendable scouts. Those left on the Brazilian coast had to survive by relying on Indigenous hospitality, and some became intermediaries who learned Tupi languages.

On the Brazilian coast, Tupi-speaking villages were organized around extended households, agricultural work, fishing, and ceremonial life. Women played central roles in cultivation and food preparation; men hunted, fished, and fought. The brazilwood trade drew men into heavy labor cutting and carrying timber, and introduced iron tools that altered work patterns. Sources on women's roles in these exchanges, including relationships with European men, are sparse and were written by men with their own agendas, and must be read with caution. Sources are equally thin on how children and elders experienced the new contact.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Iberian monarchies, with papal involvement, attempted to convert Atlantic exploration into legally defined spheres of sovereignty, and how those attempts interacted with geography, Indigenous societies, private enterprise, and rival European powers. It began with the crisis created by Columbus's return in 1493 and with the legal and commercial background supplied by Portuguese expansion, earlier papal grants, and the Treaty of Alcáçovas.

The papal bulls of 1493 represented a first attempt at resolution, favoring Castile and setting a line 100 leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde. The bulls rested on a medieval conception of papal authority over non-Christian lands that was contested even among contemporaries and that had no standing among Indigenous peoples. Portugal, whose strategic interest lay in securing sea room for the southern route to Africa and India, declined to accept the papal line and negotiated directly.

The Treaty of Tordesillas, signed on 7 June 1494, placed the line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. It was a bilateral agreement between monarchies, later confirmed by the papacy in 1506. Its authors could not determine the line's exact location, because longitude could not be measured at sea and because the treaty did not specify which league or island was to be used. In practice, the line was a matter of interpretation and negotiation, as the later dispute over the Moluccas and the Treaty of Zaragoza of 1529 demonstrated.

The chapter gave Portuguese Brazil independent treatment. Cabral's fleet, bound for India, reached the Brazilian coast on 22 April 1500, and Pero Vaz de Caminha's letter provides the most detailed surviving account of the first encounter. Spanish captains had reached the northern coast earlier in the same year, illustrating that the line was crossed almost immediately. Whether Portugal had prior knowledge of the land remains debated.

Portuguese presence in Brazil in the first decades was primarily commercial. The brazilwood trade, organized under royal concession and private partnerships, depended on the labor and cooperation of Tupi-speaking peoples, who exchanged timber for iron tools and other goods. Portuguese control was limited to a few coastal stations, and Indigenous communities retained substantial autonomy. The systematic colonization that characterized later Portuguese America, treated in later chapters, came after the 1530s, partly in response to French competition.

Indigenous peoples were excluded from the legal framework of partition but shaped its practical outcomes. Tupi-speaking communities negotiated trade, used alliances with Europeans in their own rivalries, and sometimes resisted. The sources, overwhelmingly European, require careful reading to recover their perspectives, and Indigenous history in Brazil continues long after 1500.

Tordesillas did not divide the Atlantic in practice. Geographic uncertainty, private mariners, Indigenous agency, and, above all, the refusal of France, England, and later the Dutch to accept the Iberian premise undermined exclusive claims. The Reformation further eroded the authority of papal grants for northern European states. The chapter's central problem, the difference between claim and control, runs through the colonial history that follows.

The treaty nonetheless had lasting effects. It helped give Portugal and Castile different strategic priorities and colonial trajectories, contributing to the eventual division between Portuguese and Spanish America. It also established a model of claim-based imperial competition. Yet the later shape of Brazil owed more to settlement, warfare, and subsequent treaties than to the line itself.

Chapter 28 has asked how two crowns tried to make an ocean into a legal map. Chapter 29 turns to processes that neither crown planned or controlled: the epidemics, demographic catastrophe, and biological and ecological exchanges that transformed the Americas, Europe, and Africa after 1492.

Chapter Reflection

The Treaty of Tordesillas is often remembered as a bold act of global partition. A closer look shows something more modest and more revealing. It was a compromise between two monarchies that could not measure the space they were dividing, could not occupy most of what they claimed, and had not consulted anyone who lived there. Its importance lies in what it reveals about how early modern empires worked: through claims, negotiation, and improvisation rather than plans carried out as written.

The chapter also shows why Brazil deserves independent study. Portuguese America began not as a settler colony but as a commercial periphery of an empire oriented toward Asia, sustained by exchanges with Indigenous communities who retained considerable power. Its later history, including the plantation economy and the enormous importance of African slavery, grew out of decisions made after this first phase, and cannot be understood without it.

The problem of claim versus control will return in every later volume, from frontier zones under Spanish and Portuguese rule to the disputed borders and contested sovereignty of the republican era. The next chapter turns to a transformation that no treaty addressed and no monarch controlled: the epidemics, demographic collapse, and ecological exchanges that remade the Americas after 1492, and that shaped the world in which every colonial claim would be tested.
