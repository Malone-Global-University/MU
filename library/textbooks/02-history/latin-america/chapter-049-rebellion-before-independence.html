Rebellion before Independence

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Rebellions That Were Not Yet Independence

Between 1780 and 1800 people across Iberian America rose against, conspired against, or petitioned against colonial rule, and almost none of them was fighting for the nations that later claimed them.

In November 1780 a kuraka and muleteer in the province of Tinta seized a provincial governor and began the largest insurrection in the history of colonial Spanish America. Within months Aymara communities were besieging La Paz, farmers and artisans in New Granada were marching on the capital, and similar discontent surfaced in Minas Gerais, Bahia, and the coast of Venezuela. This chapter examines those movements as separate responses to colonial conditions.

The central argument is that the rebellions exposed deep tensions in Iberian government but did not form a single march to independence. Participants sought an end to abusive taxes and forced labor, the restoration of Indigenous authority, the defense of local privilege, the abolition of slavery, or republican government, and their goals shifted over time. Many invoked the king.

Three distinctions organize the chapter. The Andean uprisings were several movements, led by Túpac Amaru II, Tomás Katari, and Túpac Katari, among others. The Comunero revolt in New Granada began with fiscal grievances and exposed divisions between elite negotiators and popular radicals. The Inconfidência Mineira was a conspiracy discovered before it acted, and the Bahian movement of 1798 drew on a different social base.

Women appear as leaders: Micaela Bastidas, Bartolina Sisa, Gregoria Apaza, and Tomasa Tito Condemayta in the Andes, with Manuela Beltrán commemorated in New Granada. Enslaved and free people of African descent appear in the Andes, New Granada, Bahia, and Coro. Indigenous communities fought on both sides.

Evidence demands care. Trial records, interrogations, official correspondence, and seized rebel letters were produced largely by the side that won. Later nationalist memory transformed several rebels into heroes. Historians read all of it against the grain.

This chapter follows Chapter 48 on reform and precedes Chapter 50 on the Haitian Revolution. It closes around 1800, and the crisis of 1808 belongs to Chapter 51.

Historical Context

By the later eighteenth century, the Spanish and Portuguese empires governed vast, varied societies. Indigenous communities paid tribute and, in the highlands, supplied mine labor. Enslaved Africans and their descendants worked plantations, mines, and cities. Creoles, mestizos, and free people of color occupied intermediate and shifting positions.

Chapter 48 described the Bourbon and Pombaline reforms: administrative centralization, higher taxes, expanded monopolies, new customs houses, and new militias. The reforms were driven by imperial rivalry, especially after the Seven Years' War, and by fiscal demands of war with Britain. In many places they broke informal arrangements between officials and local elites and communities.

The Atlantic world was in motion. The American Revolution (1775-1783) and the French Revolution (from 1789) circulated ideas of rights and republics, though their reach in Iberian America was limited and uneven. Spanish authorities feared contagion, censored texts, and monitored foreign visitors.

Slavery was central to Brazil, the Caribbean, and parts of Spanish America, and fear of slave revolt shaped elite politics. The Haitian Revolution, treated in Chapter 50, began in 1791, and its effects on thinking about slavery and race overlap with the later part of this chapter.

Section 49.1

Imperial Reform and the Crisis of Colonial Authority

Chapter 48 described the Bourbon and Pombaline reform programs from the standpoint of the governments that designed them. This chapter reverses the angle and asks how colonial subjects, officials, and local elites responded when those programs reached them. Between about 1780 and 1800 a sequence of insurrections, protests, and conspiracies challenged Iberian authority in the southern Andes, in the highlands of New Granada, in Minas Gerais and Bahia, and on the Venezuelan coast. The largest, the Andean rebellions of 1780–1783, was among the most destructive upheavals in the history of Spanish America. The smallest, a conspiracy of a few dozen men that was betrayed before it acted, left little trace beyond the investigators’ files. Historians group them together because they overlapped in time and shared a context, not because they formed a single movement.

The shared context was a change in the way the crown extracted resources and exercised control. After the Seven Years’ War, which exposed imperial weakness, Madrid and Lisbon sought more revenue and more direct administration. In Spanish America this meant higher sales taxes (alcabalas), customs houses in interior towns, tighter state monopolies on tobacco and spirits, military reorganization, and the dispatch of royal visitors with sweeping powers. In the Andes the older machinery of tribute, the mit'a labor draft, and forced sales (the reparto) continued alongside these innovations. In Portuguese Minas Gerais the crown pressed for the gold revenue it expected despite falling output. Reformers saw rationalization and defense. Many subjects saw the breaking of customary arrangements in which tax collection had always been negotiated locally.

Grievances were not identical, and neither were the people who held them. An Indigenous household in Tinta paid tribute, owed labor, and was forced to buy goods it had not chosen. A muleteer or small trader in the same province felt the customs post and the sales tax. A tobacco farmer or cotton weaver in the Socorro district of New Granada faced monopolies and levies on products that sustained the household. A mining contractor in Vila Rica worried about the derrama, a forced collection of arrears. An artisan or soldier in Salvador faced rising food prices and a color line that excluded him from advancement. An enslaved or free Black worker on the Coro coast confronted a different system of coercion altogether. What looked from the capital like one imperial problem was in practice many local ones.

Two further conditions shaped what followed. First, colonial coercive capacity was thin. Garrisons were small, militias were drawn from the very people who might rebel, and orders took weeks to travel, so officials depended on negotiation, local intermediaries, and the symbolic authority of the crown. That dependence helps explain how rebellion could spread quickly through a region and also why, once reinforcements arrived, repression was often exemplary and theatrical. Second, there was no common leadership or program. The leaders of Cuzco, Chayanta, La Paz, Socorro, Vila Rica, Salvador, and Coro did not coordinate, and in several cases did not know of each other’s aims. Treating these events as a prelude to the independence wars of Chapters 52–57 would impose a later outcome on people who acted within different horizons. This chapter therefore proceeds region by region and closes around 1800, leaving the Haitian Revolution to Chapter 50 and the crisis of 1808 to Chapter 51.

Section 49.2

Kings, Communities, Rights, and Competing Political Visions

The political language of late-colonial rebellion was overwhelmingly royalist. In the Iberian monarchies the king was understood as the fount of justice, and petition, litigation, and appeal to royal protection were normal and often effective ways of resisting abuse. A common formula across Spanish America, heard in the Andes, in New Granada, and elsewhere, combined loyalty to the monarch with rejection of ‘bad government’: the king was just, his local officials and new exactions were not. This was not simply a mask. It expressed a conception of legitimate order within which grievances could be voiced, and it permitted people to take extraordinary actions while claiming to defend the lawful order against corrupt agents.

The principle had a practical history. Indigenous communities in the Andes had litigated against corregidores, priests, and neighboring landholders for generations, and kurakas such as José Gabriel Condorcanqui knew how to cite the Laws of the Indies and cultivate lawyers and sympathetic clergy. Rebels often framed violence as enforcement: the seizure and execution of an abusive corregidor was presented as the delivery of justice that the courts had refused. Historians such as Sergio Serulnikov have shown how deeply the Chayanta movement in particular depended on legal argument and on the expectation that higher royal authority would respond to a well-founded claim.

Other political languages competed with royalism and sometimes combined with it. In the Andes, memory of the Inca past, carried in oral tradition, in portraits of Inca lords, and in the standing of families claiming descent, could be converted into a claim to rule. Whether Túpac Amaru II intended a restored Indigenous kingdom or used Inca symbolism to mobilize a coalition while continuing to speak of reform remains debated. Aymara insurgents around La Paz invoked their own authority, drawing on Andean ideas of order and renewal. In Portuguese America and Venezuela, a small number of educated men read about the American and French revolutions and discussed republican government, while plebeian participants in Salvador and Coro emphasized equality, wages, and the end of racial discrimination or slavery.

These visions also reflected social position. Creoles resented peninsular officials but feared that mass mobilization could turn on them, and so mostly declined to follow leaders who invoked Indigenous sovereignty. Indigenous nobles could be defenders of their communities or beneficiaries of the colonial order, and some fought for the crown. Mestizos and traders occupied an uneasy middle. Enslaved and free people of African descent had interests of their own that rebel proclamations addressed only intermittently. The movements studied here were alliances of unlike groups, and their instability followed from the fact that victory would have meant different things to different members.

Historical Vocabulary
Royal Legitimacy and Vassalage

Royal legitimacy was the principle that the monarch's authority rested on divine sanction and a duty to deliver justice. Vassalage described the reciprocal tie of subjects to the crown: loyalty and service in return for protection and justice. Rebels who invoked these ideas often claimed that officials, not the king, had broken the bargain. The language is easy to misread as either naive or merely tactical; historians generally treat it as a real framework within which grievances became politically speakable.

Section 49.3

Organizing Rebellion: Networks, Leadership, Communication, and Evidence

Collective action required infrastructure that colonial society already supplied. Muleteers and traders carried letters, rumors, and orders along the roads connecting Cuzco, Potosí, Lima, and Buenos Aires. Markets, fairs, and religious festivals gathered people from many communities. Kinship, godparenthood, and community office created trust, and leaders of intermediate status, such as kurakas, traders, and local officers, could mediate between villages and outsiders. Insurgent armies in the Andes were not separate from society. They moved with households, relied on communities to supply food and fodder, and depended on collective decisions to join. A village that chose not to rise could deprive a column of its grain and its guides, so leaders constantly negotiated, persuaded, and compelled.

Leaders also needed to construct authority. They issued edicts, passes, and appointments, named captains and colonels, held trials, and used seals and signatures in imitation of colonial government. Letters were the connective tissue of these movements, and because many leaders and nearly all followers could not write, letters were dictated, copied, and carried by trusted messengers. Money, powder, mules, and intelligence were scarce. A column that could not feed its followers dissolved, and in the Andes the harvest calendar and the cold season affected campaigns as much as battles did. Later sections show repeatedly that logistics, not only courage or numbers, determined outcomes.

The evidence for these events comes largely from the side that suppressed them. Trial records, interrogations, and confessions were produced under coercion by officials who wished to demonstrate conspiracy or to assign blame. Officers’ reports exaggerated rebel numbers to magnify danger or excuse defeat, and vocabulary such as ‘bandit,’ ‘traitor,’ and ‘barbarian’ framed what was recorded. Rebel proclamations and letters survive largely because authorities seized them, and they have passed through copyists, translators, and modern editors. Later memory added further layers: republican states celebrated some rebels as precursors and rewrote their aims. Historians such as Charles Walker, Sergio Serulnikov, Ward Stavig, and David Cahill read these sources against one another, test claimed numbers against logistics, and ask whose voices are heard only through hostile transcription. Students should do the same.

Section 49.4

The Southern Andes before 1780: Tribute, Forced Sales, and Indigenous Politics

The southern Andes, running from the Cuzco region through the basin of Lake Titicaca to Upper Peru (the Audiencia de Charcas), were densely Indigenous. Communities (ayllus) held land collectively, paid tribute in silver, and in much of the highlands owed mit'a service to the mines of Potosí. Provincial governors called corregidores purchased or were granted their posts with the expectation of profiting, and the reparto mercantil, the compulsory distribution of goods to Indigenous households at fixed prices, became their main source of income. Priests added fees and labor demands. In 1776 the Audiencia de Charcas, with Potosí, was transferred from the Viceroyalty of Peru to the new Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata, creating different administrative channels for the two halves of the region and complicating the later royalist response.

The Cuzco region combined haciendas, textile workshops (obrajes), and a commercial economy built on muleteering along the royal road between Lima and the Altiplano. Many Indigenous households engaged in trade, and some kurakas were wealthy. The alcabala rate rose in the 1770s, and a customs house was established at Cuzco in 1780, directly affecting the Andean and mestizo traders who moved goods through the region. Visitor José Antonio de Areche, dispatched in 1777 with extraordinary powers, intensified fiscal scrutiny. The arrangement combined older exactions with newer ones, and communities that had survived one regime of extraction found themselves facing another.

Conditions varied sharply. In some provinces kurakas mediated effectively between communities and officials and retained legitimacy; in others, kurakas were outsiders imposed by corregidores, or were accused of abusing tribute collection and mit'a rotation, so that disputes over office and obligation became local crises. Boundary and land conflicts among communities, litigation against haciendas, and conflicts with parish priests produced regular petitions and occasional violence. Protest had a history: the movement of Juan Santos Atahualpa in the central jungle (from 1742), the Lima conspiracy of 1750, and local risings of the 1770s, including one in Urubamba in 1777, show that Andean society was accustomed to resisting. Historians therefore explain the great rebellion as an interaction between widely shared fiscal burdens and the particular politics of each province, not as the automatic result of oppression.

Historical Vocabulary
Reparto Mercantil, Corregidor, and Kuraka

The corregidor was a provincial magistrate and tax collector. The reparto mercantil was the compulsory distribution of goods to Indigenous households at assigned prices, a source of official income and a notorious grievance. The kuraka (Spanish cacique) was the hereditary or appointed Indigenous authority who mediated between his community and colonial government. Understanding the three together shows why disputes over tribute, goods, and office were intertwined.

Section 49.5

José Gabriel Condorcanqui and the Beginning of the Túpac Amaru Rebellion

José Gabriel Condorcanqui was a kuraka of Surimana, Pampamarca, and Tungasuca in the province of Tinta, a muleteer and trader on the Cuzco–Altiplano road, and a man literate in Spanish and at ease with colonial law. His birth year is uncertain in the published literature: Sara Beatriz Guardia, drawing on documents, gives 19 March 1741, while other reference works give 1738 and 1742, and this chapter therefore says only that he was born around 1740. Educated at the Jesuit Colegio de Caciques in Cuzco, he married Micaela Bastidas in 1760 and inherited the cacicazgo after the death of his elder brother. He claimed descent from the Inca line, and from the 1760s pursued genealogical and inheritance suits, including a claim bound up with the Marquesado de Oropesa. David Cahill’s research shows that these claims were contested by Cuzco elites, so his descent, his legal efforts, and the recognition he actually obtained must be kept distinct. Inca ancestry became politically powerful during the rebellion, but it was asserted and disputed long before it was accepted by many followers.

For years Condorcanqui’s politics was legal. He petitioned about the mit'a, about the abuses of corregidores, and about the burdens of his own community, and in 1777 he traveled to Lima, where, according to Micaela, his eyes were opened. The failure of petition mattered. By 1780 the provincial corregidor of Tinta, Antonio de Arriaga, had become the principal target of resentment: he was accused of exploiting the reparto, and the visitor Areche’s fiscal program offered no relief. Condorcanqui’s decision to act reflected a calculation that legal channels had closed.

On 4 November 1780, the saint’s day of Charles III, Condorcanqui seized Arriaga, forced him to write instructions to his agent to send funds and arms to Tungasuca, and held him until he was executed in the plaza of Tungasuca on 9 or 10 November, sources differing by a day. Early proclamations ordered the abolition of corregidores, the reparto, and unjust taxes, and he adopted the name Túpac Amaru II, evoking the last Neo-Inca ruler executed in 1572. In the first weeks he also ordered obrajes closed in some places and, on 16 November, issued a decree freeing enslaved people who joined him; how far the decree was carried out is uncertain. He spoke in the king’s name and insisted that his enemy was corruption, and historians continue to debate how much of what came later, including claims to sovereignty, was already envisioned in November.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why the Túpac Amaru Rebellion Erupted in 1780

Long-term causes

Mit'a and tribute burdens, the reparto, kuraka disputes, and a long tradition of petition and local protest, combined with Inca memory.

Short-term causes

Higher alcabalas and new customs houses in the 1770s, the visita of José Antonio de Areche, and failed petitions by Condorcanqui.

Event or transformation

The seizure and execution of Arriaga in November 1780 opened armed insurrection.

Immediate effects

Thousands joined; the victory at Sangarará spread the movement across the southern highlands.

Long-term consequences

Repression, the suppression of Inca symbols, and altered relations between crown, Creoles, and Indigenous elites.

Historical complexity

No single cause suffices. The same grievances existed elsewhere without producing comparable war, so leadership, networks, and timing mattered.
Section 49.6

From Sangarará to the Siege of Cuzco

News of Arriaga’s execution brought royalist forces from Cuzco’s province, composed largely of militia, many of them Indigenous and mestizo, into the field. At Sangarará on 18 November 1780, Túpac Amaru’s followers defeated this force. Royalist troops had taken shelter in the church, which burned during the battle, killing many inside, and the Bishop of Cuzco excommunicated Túpac Amaru for the burning. Túpac Amaru answered that his quarrel was with oppression, not the Church. Contemporary reports gave the rebel force as 60,000, an exaggeration typical of official counts, but the victory drew large numbers of recruits from the highlands, and it terrified the Creole and mestizo population of Cuzco. Many Indigenous nobles feared a caste war and chose the crown.

What should have followed was a march on Cuzco, and both then and since, historians have debated why it did not come at once. Micaela Bastidas pressed for immediate attack. Túpac Amaru, according to the most common reading, judged that Cuzco, home of the Spanish elite and a population of Creoles and wealthy mestizos hostile to the rising, would be difficult to take, and he turned south to widen the rebellion in the Collao and Upper Peru, win adherents, and cut the city’s supplies. Tomasa Tito Condemayta, cacica of Acos, reportedly warned that the situation in the city was not favorable. In early December he crossed the Vilcanota range; his cousin Diego Cristóbal moved on provinces across the river, and Micaela administered Tungasuca. This strategy had logic, but it also gave Cuzco time to organize, and in December local bands under minor leaders carried out massacres of Creoles, mestizos, and others in Calca and nearby towns, which hardened royalist opinion.

By the time the rebel columns approached Cuzco, in late December 1780 and early January 1781, the balance had shifted. Reinforcements under Field Marshal José del Valle had arrived from Lima, Areche was organizing the city’s defense, and Indigenous lords such as Mateo Pumacahua and Nicolás Rosas of Anta brought thousands of Indigenous troops to the royalist side. On 3 January Túpac Amaru sent an official letter to the city council asking that his emissaries be admitted, promising that the surrendered would not be harmed; there was no negotiation. The viceroy ordered militias prepared to crush the rebellion and its leaders. The rebels did not take Cuzco. Over the following weeks royalist columns drove them back toward Tinta, defections and betrayals multiplied, and Túpac Amaru’s force diminished as supplies and money ran short.

Several points should be held together. Indigenous soldiers and officers fought on both sides; Pumacahua’s later career, when he joined the Cuzco revolt of 1814 against the crown, shows how alliances shifted with context. Terrain mattered: Cuzco sat in a valley ringed by hills that gave defenders advantages, and the rebels’ largely infantry columns could not easily storm fortified positions defended by cannon. Logistics mattered: paid and unpaid followers drifted away when food and silver ran out. And the decision not to attack immediately, whether wise or mistaken, cannot be judged simply by its result.

Section 49.7

Micaela Bastidas and Women's Political and Military Leadership

Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua was a political and military leader in her own right. Born in 1744, according to Guardia, to a family of no rank, she had no formal schooling, and unlike her husband she did not read or write, and signed only her name. Guardia reports that she understood Spanish but did not speak it. Her education came from managing land, animals, accounts, and wages during her husband’s long absences on commercial business and legal suits, and from the cacicazgo’s own politics. When Túpac Amaru marched south after Sangarará she remained at Tungasuca as the rebellion’s administrative and political head, and the surviving record shows her giving orders, issuing safe-conducts and edicts, directing recruiting expeditions, writing to kurakas, and receiving and acting on intelligence.

Her authority is documented in specific acts. A safe-conduct of 27 November 1780 ordered guards on the roads to let bearers pass. On 7 December she warned the governors of several communities that her husband was about to march on Cuzco and threatened to destroy those who failed to send people. On 10 December she ordered the arrest of a man who had opposed her husband’s commands. On 13 December she appointed a colonel and a captain by edict. She corresponded with captains, priests, and several women, and was addressed as ‘señora gobernadora.’ She also monitored treason: she warned of priests writing to the bishop and of Juan Antonio de Figueroa, an artilleryman who, according to the bishop, aimed shots to spare royal troops. These are not the tasks of an assistant.

She also argued strategy. After Sangarará she urged an immediate attack on Cuzco and, as the delay lengthened, wrote with growing urgency about money, morale, and the danger of defections. The letter of 6 December 1780, examined in the Primary Source Window below, rebuked her husband sharply, and in her next letters she announced that she would gather people and begin encircling Cuzco herself. Scholars interpret the disagreement differently. Some see the rebel leaders as split between her wish for a quick, risky assault and his plan to isolate Cuzco and win allies inside it; others stress that both were reasoning from imperfect information and that neither can be shown to have been clearly right. What the correspondence demonstrates beyond dispute is that she exercised independent judgment and held real command.

She was not the only woman with authority. Tomasa Tito Condemayta, cacica of Acos, commanded followers and, according to Guardia, replied to Micaela’s request for support that she took orders only from Túpac Amaru; the exchange shows both her independence and the limits of Micaela’s authority. Other women are named in the sources as correspondents and as provisioners, among them Ángela Pacuri and Margarita Condori. Women also fought, carried supplies, tended the wounded, and led households through the war. Royal officials tended to treat women leaders as exceptional or as monstrous, and the sources’ language shapes what historians can reconstruct.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Micaela Bastidas to Túpac Amaru, Tungasuca, 6 December 1780

Creator: Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua, dictating; addressed to her husband, then campaigning south of Tungasuca · Date and place: 6 December 1780, Tungasuca · Type: Rebel letter, preserved because royal authorities seized rebel papers, later transcribed and published in the Colección Documental de la Independencia del Perú (CDIP, vol. 2, 1971) and earlier collections

Context: After Sangarará (18 November) many rebels wanted to march on Cuzco, and Micaela agreed. Túpac Amaru instead moved south to extend the rising and cut Cuzco's supplies. Left to run Tungasuca, Micaela was administering the rebel base, issuing passes and orders, and receiving reports of defections. Sara Beatriz Guardia, in a volume published by Peru's National Library, counts nineteen letters from her to him between 23 November 1780 and 23 March 1781. The letters survive in colonial investigative files and are known today through documentary publications and later scholarly discussion, so what students read is a transcribed and edited text, not the original manuscript. This letter is among the sharpest.

Paraphrase of the letter, with one short phrase translated from the Spanish: she rebukes him for lingering in towns where there was nothing to accomplish, warns that paid soldiers will drift away once money runs out, and that the troops "lack provisions." She argues that delay has given Cuzco's defenders time to place cannon on the Picchu hill, and that her repeated advice to attack at once has been treated cheaply.

Related documents: In a letter of 7 December she says she is gathering people to march on 11 December toward Paruro and encircle Cuzco gradually. On 10 December she reports a local betrayal and says she is nearly overwhelmed, with few people. Her letters of this period also show orders: a safe-conduct of 27 November, an order of 10 December to arrest a man who disobeyed her husband's commands, and an edict of 13 December naming officers. A letter of 24 January 1781, cited by Guardia to CDIP vol. 2, p. 458, shows her still writing to him as royalist forces closed in.

Analysis: The letter shows a leader assessing logistics, morale, money, and timing, and challenging a commander who was also her husband. Scholars debate the strategy: Túpac Amaru may have wished to isolate Cuzco and win allies within it before assaulting a city defended by Creoles and by Indigenous nobles such as Mateo Pumacahua, while Micaela feared the cost of delay. Limits: Guardia reports that Micaela could not read or write and understood but did not speak Spanish, and infers, without citing a document, that she dictated in Quechua. Whatever the language, secretaries and later copyists wrote, translated, and transcribed, and editors selected. The passages Guardia reproduces are partial, with omissions marked, and the letter reaches us through the hands of those who prosecuted the rebellion. It tells us little about how ordinary soldiers or Indigenous communities saw her.

Section 49.8

Coalitions, Social Conflict, Religion, and the Radicalization of the Uprising

The coalition that followed Túpac Amaru included Indigenous commoners and some kurakas, mestizos and muleteers, artisans and small traders, and a handful of Creoles, as well as enslaved and free people of African descent. His proclamations ended the reparto, corregidores, and the more burdensome taxes and, in the decree of 16 November, offered freedom to enslaved people who joined him. Appeals to Creoles to unite as ‘Americans’ against peninsular misgovernment mostly failed, because Creoles who disliked Spanish officials liked rebel armies less. Many Indigenous nobles likewise chose the crown, fearing the loss of their own status in a movement that invoked an Inca restoration. The Church was divided: bishops and many priests condemned the movement, while some priests, among them clergy who had been close to the family, helped it.

The conflict became more polarized as it lengthened. Some rebel bands, acting without central control, killed Creoles, mestizos, and peninsulares indiscriminately, as at Calca in December, and the targeting of church property in some episodes outraged Catholic opinion. Royalist leaders portrayed the war as one of racial extermination, which helped them mobilize Indigenous auxiliaries and to justify atrocities of their own. Local feuds, such as old boundary disputes and rivalries over kuraka office, were carried into the war, and Indigenous communities sometimes fought one another under the banners of the two sides. Radicalization thus reflected military pressure, social fear, and local politics, not an unchanging program. It also left a long legacy of mistrust between Andean communities and the Creole and mestizo populations that surrounded them.

Everyday Life and Social History

Feeding, Moving, and Surviving an Andean War

Armies moved with families. Women prepared food, tended animals, and carried goods; children and elders traveled or were left to manage fields. Mule trains, llama caravans, and community storehouses supplied columns, and a failed harvest or a destroyed village could end a campaign. Letters and rumors traveled with muleteers and market traders, and populations learned of events by word of mouth.

The burdens fell on the households that sustained fighters, whether insurgent or royalist: requisitioned grain, lost herds, abandoned fields, and exposure to epidemic and cold. Sources record these costs unevenly, usually when officials counted losses.

Section 49.9

Royalist Counteroffensive, Capture, and the Executions of 1781

As the rebel forces retreated from Cuzco, royalist columns commanded by Field Marshal del Valle and aided by Indigenous contingents pursued them through the valleys of Tinta. Battles at Quiquijana and Checacupe in early 1781 turned the war. Micaela’s letters of February 1781 record the deterioration in blunt terms: enemy troops in Quiquijana, rebel forces retreating to Checacupe, and former friends turning against them. The royalist offer of pardons, and the suffering of communities in the war zone, encouraged defections. In April 1781, betrayal by former followers brought the capture of Túpac Amaru and Micaela Bastidas, together with several members of their family.

The trials were run by Areche and judges in Cuzco and were designed to produce a confession of conspiracy and a full list of accomplices. On 18 May 1781, in the main plaza of Cuzco, Túpac Amaru, Micaela Bastidas, relatives, and several other leaders, including Tomasa Tito Condemayta, were executed. The sentences prescribed extreme punishments: garroting, hanging, mutilation, quartering, the display of body parts in distant towns, and the burning of remains. The sentence against Micaela included cutting out her tongue before she was killed. Accounts say the executioners had difficulty carrying out the sentence on Túpac Amaru. These punishments were not simply cruelty; they were theater meant to demonstrate that the crown could destroy the rebel family’s body, memory, and claim to Inca descent. Their youngest son, Fernando, was spared and exiled.

Executing the principal leaders did not end the war. Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru and other relatives took command and continued fighting around Lake Titicaca. The movement in Chayanta, which had begun earlier, and the Aymara insurgency around La Paz were already independent and continued under their own leaders. Royal authorities sought to remove the symbolic basis of rebellion by suppressing Inca portraits, dress, and ceremonies, a theme taken up in section 49.20, and the decapitation strategy failed in part because the rebellion was not a single hierarchy. The war entered its most dispersed and, for many communities, most brutal phase after the executions.

Section 49.10

Tomás Katari and the Chayanta Rebellion

Tomás Katari was an Indigenous tributary from the community of Macha in the province of Chayanta, in the Audiencia de Charcas. His struggle began in the late 1770s as a legal contest over authority, and it was, in its origins, a dispute about tribute and the legitimacy of local officials. The corregidor of Chayanta, Joaquín Alós, and the Indigenous cacique Blas Bernal, whom Katari and many communities regarded as illegitimate, were accused of taking more than the lawful tribute and of distorting the rotation of the mit'a. Katari argued that the proper customary tribute authority had been displaced and that the royal tribute system, as written, protected communities against such exactions.

His campaign was therefore both political and legal. He brought petitions to the Audiencia of Charcas and, in 1778, traveled to Buenos Aires to appeal to the new viceroy of the Río de la Plata, Juan José de Vértiz; he pursued his claims through 1779. The Audiencia sometimes ruled in his favor in principle, but local officials, with Alós and Bernal at their head, imprisoned him more than once, and legal victories on paper were not enforced. Serulnikov’s work stresses that this experience taught communities something important: royal justice existed, but it could be reached only through the pressure of collective action. In 1780 the dispute widened into open revolt, and, according to the standard account, crowds forced the release of Katari from custody, and in the summer he returned to Macha with royal papers affirming his claims. His movement therefore began before the Cuzco rising and drew its strength from different sources.

Katari’s rebellion differed from Túpac Amaru’s in aims and organization. It was centered on community autonomy, the legitimacy of local authorities, and the enforcement of the crown’s own rules, and it relied on the language of royal justice more than on Inca restoration. Its leaders were commoners and kurakas of modest rank, not a claimant to the Inca line. Contact with Túpac Amaru’s movement existed, and news of the Cuzco rising raised the stakes and the fears of officials in Charcas, but the Chayanta insurgents acted on their own agenda.

In January 1781, while being escorted under guard toward the Audiencia seat at La Plata (Chuquisaca), Katari was killed, pushed from a cliff by his guards, according to the usual account. The death did not end the movement. His brothers Dámaso and Nicolás took command, and Dámaso in particular led forces against La Plata in the early months of 1781, while others rose in the region. Royalist forces defeated them in turn, and the brothers were killed. The movement’s centrality to the war in Upper Peru, and its distinctness, is why historians insist that Tomás Katari be studied separately from both Túpac Amaru II and Túpac Katari, the Aymara leader near La Paz, with whom he is often confused despite the similarity of names. Katari’s name, in fact, was one that Julián Apaza would later adopt.

Section 49.11

Túpac Katari, Bartolina Sisa, and the Sieges of La Paz

Julián Apaza, an Aymara man born in Ayo Ayo, took the name Túpac Katari, combining the names of Túpac Amaru and Tomás Katari, and led a large insurgency in the province of La Paz. In March 1781 his forces began a siege of the city that lasted several months; a second siege began in August. Aymara communities in the surrounding countryside cut the roads, seized the city’s food supply, and gathered at El Alto, the high plain overlooking La Paz, where an insurgent camp developed administrative and ritual structures. Inside the city, famine and disease killed many of the inhabitants, and cannon fire and attempts to break the blockade failed until relief columns reached La Paz.

Bartolina Sisa, who was Katari’s companion and, according to sources, a woman of commercial experience, and his sister Gregoria Apaza were leaders in their own right. Sisa exercised military and political authority within the insurgent camp, helped direct the siege, and was cited by royalist officials as a principal commander. She was captured in 1781 and executed in La Paz in September 1782. Gregoria Apaza also exercised command and was condemned. The interrogations that survive show officials struggling to understand women as leaders and often attributing their actions to Katari. The record also shows that the insurgents’ organization included women’s labor in provisioning, communication, and ritual.

The royalist response combined columns from Charcas and from Buenos Aires, led by commanders such as Ignacio Flores and José de Reseguín, with large Indigenous auxiliary forces. The first siege was lifted in late June or early July 1781 when Flores’s expedition reached the city; after it withdrew, the siege resumed in August, and it was finally broken in October 1781 when Reseguín’s forces arrived, reaching the area on 17 October. Katari was captured in November 1781 after betrayal by an associate and executed shortly thereafter, his body quartered and displayed. His movement overlapped with the Cuzco rising and drew on its prestige, but its organization, base, and aims were Aymara. Relations among insurgent groups were uneven: cooperation, rivalry, and mutual suspicion all appear in the sources.

Section 49.12

The Wider Andean War and the Suppression of Rebellion, 1781–1783

By mid-1781 the Andean war had become a set of overlapping regional conflicts. Besides Cuzco and La Paz, rebel and local forces were active in Oruro, where in February a coup by Creoles joined by Indigenous groups killed Spaniards but quickly became a social conflict and ended in repression; in Chayanta and Charcas, where Katari’s brothers fought on; in the Titicaca basin, Puno, and Azángaro, where Diego Cristóbal and relatives led the main Túpac Amaru forces; and in places such as Sorata, where in August rebels reportedly dammed a river and flooded the town. Royalist commanders coordinated columns from Lima, Cuzco, and Buenos Aires. They used pardons, destruction of villages, and Indigenous auxiliaries. Both sides committed massacres.

Negotiation followed the shift in the balance. On 11 December 1781, Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru signed a peace agreement at Lampa with royal commanders offering a general pardon. His formal surrender and oath followed on 27 January 1782 at Sicuani, and these are separate events that should not be confused. The agreement ended much organized fighting, but it did not restore confidence. Authorities soon violated its terms, arrested former rebel leaders, and carried out executions: Bartolina Sisa and other Katarista leaders died in 1782, and Diego Cristóbal himself was executed in 1783 after accusations of renewed conspiracy. Royal commanders treated pardon as a tactic and punishment as policy.

The human cost was very large and cannot be stated precisely. An estimate of about 100,000 deaths, associated with Charles Walker and repeated in Peruvian institutional accounts, circulates widely, but it is an attributed approximation, not a census. It is unclear what territory it covers, how many deaths came from combat as opposed to massacre, hunger, and epidemic, and how reliable the surviving evidence is. Beyond casualties, fields and herds were destroyed, trade networks were disrupted, and communities were divided by betrayals and reprisals. The war left Andean society wary and the colonial state determined to prevent a repetition, consequences examined in section 49.20.

Section 49.13

Socorro and the Fiscal Origins of the Comunero Revolt, 1781

The Socorro and San Gil district in the highlands of the Viceroyalty of New Granada (roughly the modern Colombian department of Santander) was a region of small and middling farmers, cotton spinners and weavers, and tobacco growers, whose households depended on goods that the new fiscal policies targeted. The crown’s need for defense revenue after the war with Britain led the regent-visitor Juan Francisco Gutiérrez de Piñeres to raise the alcabala, impose a levy to support the naval Armada de Barlovento, and tighten the royal monopolies on tobacco and aguardiente. He also tightened enforcement, using guards and informers. Women’s spinning and weaving were integral to household income, and the new measures struck at the household economy directly.

On 16 March 1781, in the town of Socorro, a crowd tore down the edict announcing the new taxes and shouted against the regent. Tradition credits a woman, Manuela Beltrán, with tearing the edict. Contemporary documents say little about her, and her later fame as a heroine owes much to commemorative memory, so historians distinguish what is documented, an angry crowd destroying the edict, from the heroic figure constructed afterward. Protest then spread through neighboring towns, and local Creole leaders, wealthier than most participants, moved to direct the movement.

The term comunero, from común, the commons, referred to the community of subjects claiming to defend their common interests against abusive government. The movement should be distinguished from the earlier Comunero revolt in Paraguay in the 1720s and 1730s, which centered on disputes with governors and Jesuits and had different participants and aims. As in the Andes, the New Granada comuneros often cried ‘long live the king and death to bad government,’ and they framed protest as a defense of the lawful order.

Historical Vocabulary
Comunero, Capitulaciones, and Alcabala

Comunero meant a member of the commons, here those who joined the protest in 1781. Capitulaciones were negotiated agreements; the Capitulations of Zipaquirá recorded the rebels' terms. The alcabala was a sales tax, a frequent grievance across the empire. These terms show how rebels used legal and fiscal language to frame demands.

Section 49.14

The Comunero March and the Capitulations of Zipaquirá

As protest spread, the comuneros organized. Juan Francisco Berbeo, a Creole of Socorro, was chosen captain general of the commons and became the movement’s principal commander and negotiator. A large crowd, whose size is variously given in the sources and certainly exaggerated in some, marched toward Santafé de Bogotá, the viceregal capital, and drew in people from many towns and from Indigenous communities of the Muisca highlands, whose grievances over tribute and land were different from those of the Socorro farmers. Gutiérrez de Piñeres left the capital. The Audiencia, with few troops and a population alarmed by the march, sought negotiation, and the Archbishop of Bogotá, Antonio Caballero y Góngora, who would later become viceroy, played the central mediating role.

The Capitulations were approved in Santafé on 7 June 1781 and ceremonially sworn at Zipaquirá on 8 June. They abolished or reduced the new taxes and the Armada de Barlovento levy, lowered burdens connected to the tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, gave Americans preference for local offices, and addressed some Indigenous demands about tribute and lands. They represented a compromise. They also revealed divisions. Berbeo and the Creole leaders sought fiscal relief and a share in government; artisans and small farmers wanted lower burdens and protection from officials; Indigenous communities sought protection for their communal lands (resguardos) and relief from tribute, and found their demands included only partly. The royal officials, who signed under duress, regarded the agreement as unbinding.

The coalition at Zipaquirá was temporary because its members disagreed about what victory meant. Social groups cooperated against a fiscal program they all disliked, but Creole elites feared the popular mobilization they led, and as soon as the concessions were won, many returned home. This created the opening for the crown to disavow the agreement, as the next section shows.

Section 49.15

José Antonio Galán, Popular Radicalism, and the Failure of Compromise

Soon after Zipaquirá the authorities began to undo it. Caballero y Góngora, having secured the dispersal of the march, obtained from the Audiencia and, afterward, from the viceroy, a declaration that the Capitulations had been extracted by force and were void, and he arranged a general pardon for most participants. The new viceroy, Manuel Antonio Flórez, was prepared to use troops, and the arrival of reinforcements helped. The elite comuneros, who had agreed to disband, found they had little power to enforce the terms, and many accepted pardons and some official positions.

José Antonio Galán, a man of modest origins from the Socorro region, did not accept the settlement. He led a more radical group in the Magdalena valley and the towns around Mariquita, calling for relief for the poor, restoration of Indigenous lands, and, according to reports, freeing enslaved people. Other Indigenous leaders in the Muisca highlands, such as Ambrosio Pisco according to some accounts, tried to use the rising for their own ends. Historians differ on how far Galán’s program went beyond local redress. What is clear is that his group represented the popular side of the comunero coalition, whose interests the Creole negotiators did not share.

Royal forces captured Galán and several associates in late 1781. He was sentenced on 30 January 1782 and executed on 1 February 1782 with companions including Isidro Molina, Lorenzo Alcantuz, and Manuel Ortiz. The sentence prescribed hanging, dismemberment, display of the remains, and the razing of houses, and was intended as a public lesson. Berbeo and other elite leaders were pardoned. The contrast shows how punishment tracked social rank. The comunero episode demonstrated that the alliance of Creoles, plebeians, and Indigenous communities could force concessions but could not hold together once those concessions were secured, and that the crown could exploit the division.

Section 49.16

Minas Gerais: Fiscal Grievances and the Inconfidência Mineira of 1789

Minas Gerais in the later eighteenth century was Portuguese America’s richest and most urbanized captaincy, built on gold and diamonds, enslaved labor, and a society of mining contractors, merchants, officials, clergy, and a large population of free people of color. Gold output peaked around mid-century and then declined. The crown, however, continued to expect a fixed revenue: the royal fifth (quinto) on gold and a minimum annual quota, commonly stated as 100 arrobas. When receipts fell short, arrears accumulated, and the governor could order a derrama, a forced collection of the shortfall from the population. By the late 1780s, arrears had grown so large that rumors of a derrama alarmed a mining elite that included debtors to the crown.

Such conditions made the captaincy a setting for conspiracy. Among those involved were army officers, lawyers, clergy, poets, and wealthy contractors, many with ties to Coimbra or to libraries of Enlightenment books, including works on the American Revolution. José Joaquim da Maia, a Brazilian student in France, is known to have met Thomas Jefferson in Nîmes in 1787 to ask about possible American help, which was not forthcoming. Poets such as Tomás Antônio Gonzaga and Cláudio Manuel da Costa moved in the same circles. They talked about a republic in Minas, a university, factories, and the means to launch a revolt, probably timed to the proclamation of the derrama.

This was a conspiracy, not a mass movement. The conspirators did not have an army, a unified program, or a plan that involved the general population, and the historian must be careful not to retroject the later national story onto the planning. The sources, particularly the investigative records preserved in Portuguese and Brazilian archives, show a group that discussed possibilities and disagreed about them, which is how the next section proceeds.

Section 49.17

Tiradentes, Republican Plans, Repression, and the Limits of the Minas Conspiracy

Joaquim José da Silva Xavier, called Tiradentes (‘tooth-puller’), was an army ensign of modest background who worked also as a dentist and prospector, and was among the most talkative of the plotters. He traveled to Rio de Janeiro and spoke openly in taverns and houses about revolt. Others, including the poets Gonzaga, Cláudio Manuel da Costa, and Inácio José de Alvarenga Peixoto, and the priest Carlos Correia de Toledo, took part in meetings at which the conspirators debated whether to establish a republic, what to do about the governor, and who would lead. Their disagreements were real. Some wanted a republic and others hesitated; attitudes to slavery ranged from proposals of limited emancipation to retaining the institution, and several of the leading men were themselves slaveholders. To describe them as uniformly abolitionist or democratic is mistaken.

The conspiracy collapsed before any rising. In March 1789 the governor, the Visconde de Barbacena, suspended the derrama, which removed the immediate trigger, and the plot was denounced by Joaquim Silvério dos Reis, a conspirator and large debtor to the crown seeking relief. Arrests followed. Cláudio Manuel da Costa died in custody in July 1789, and the case in the devassa (judicial inquiry) in Vila Rica and Rio lasted until 1792. Under questioning, some participants blamed others, and Tiradentes, after first denying involvement, accepted responsibility, which helped make him the principal defendant.

The sentence handed down in April 1792 condemned a number of men to death, but the queen, Maria I, commuted most to exile in Africa. Tiradentes was hanged in Rio on 21 April 1792, and his body was quartered and displayed along the road to Minas. Gonzaga went into exile in Mozambique. The investigative files in Portuguese archives provide the best contemporary evidence, and they also show how prosecutors constructed a conspiracy from testimony given under pressure. The image of Tiradentes as a national martyr was largely constructed in the nineteenth century and especially under the republic proclaimed in 1889, and the contrast between that image and the documentary record is one of the clearest examples in the chapter of memory shaping history.

Section 49.18

The Bahian Conspiracy of 1798: Artisans, Soldiers, Race, and Equality

Salvador da Bahia, Brazil’s former colonial capital (the seat of colonial government had moved to Rio de Janeiro in 1763) and still a major sugar and slave-trading port, had a large population of free and enslaved Black and mixed-ancestry people. In the 1790s high food prices and shortages, military discipline and low pay, and a color line that restricted advancement fed discontent among artisans and soldiers. Educated men, such as the physician Cipriano Barata and members of local intellectual circles, read works circulating from Europe, and some discussed French revolutionary principles. In August 1798 handwritten notices posted in public places in Salvador called for revolt, attacked colonial rule and its officials, and invoked liberty and equality.

The investigation identified participants among tailors, other artisans, soldiers, and a few enslaved people, and a smaller circle of better-educated men. Among those most prominent in the record were the tailors Manuel Faustino dos Santos Lira and João de Deus do Nascimento and the soldiers Lucas Dantas and Luís Gonzaga das Virgens. The programs attributed to them included a republic, free trade, improved pay for soldiers, and an end to racial discrimination in appointments, and some documents refer to the abolition of slavery. What the accused actually proposed is difficult to separate from what prosecutors alleged, and individuals’ positions varied. Not every person accused supported the same program, and some had been drawn in by acquaintance or rumor.

The authorities arrested dozens of suspects. In November 1799, four men of humble origins, the two tailors and the two soldiers, were hanged and quartered, and others were whipped or exiled. The more prominent and better-off suspects generally received lighter treatment or were acquitted. Compared with Minas Gerais, Bahia involved a more plebeian and racially mixed group, a more explicit language of equality, and a stronger focus on race. Compared with the Andean rebellions, it remained a conspiracy that was discovered before it could act. Historians continue to debate how much of the movement’s program was widely shared and how much was the product of a few leaders and of the investigation.

Section 49.19

Coro, Venezuela, and Atlantic Revolutionary Conspiracies

Two events in the Captaincy General of Venezuela illustrate different kinds of challenge. In May 1795 an uprising broke out in the Sierra de Coro on the northwestern coast, led by José Leonardo Chirino, a free man of African descent. José Caridad González, who had escaped enslavement in Curaçao and settled in Spanish territory, is also associated with the rising, but contemporary testimony about his direct part in planning or leading it is contradictory, and his leadership cannot be treated as established. The rebels included free zambos, enslaved workers, and Indigenous people, and they attacked haciendas and officials. Witnesses reported that they invoked a ‘law of the French,’ understood to mean an end to slavery and taxes. The movement was suppressed within days by planters’ militias and troops. Chirino was captured, taken to Caracas, and executed in December 1796. This was an armed social rebellion with antislavery content, rooted in local conditions of plantation labor, taxation, and race, and informed by news carried through Caribbean ports.

The Gual and España conspiracy was a different kind of project. Manuel Gual, a military officer, and José María España, a local official, planned to overthrow Spanish rule in Venezuela with the help of Spanish republican prisoners sent to La Guaira after a failed plot in Spain in 1796, including Juan Bautista Picornell. The group drafted ordinances proposing a republic, equality among social groups, and the abolition of certain taxes and monopolies, and circulated a translation of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. The plot involved a mixture of officers, local landowners, and artisans. On 13 July 1797 authorities discovered it in La Guaira. España was executed in Caracas in 1799; Gual fled to Trinidad and died there in 1800.

The two events should not be conflated. Coro was an armed rising of mostly poor and Black participants against local conditions; Gual and España was a conspiratorial project of educated men with republican ambitions that was discovered before action. Together they show that Atlantic ideas circulated through ports, newspapers, exiles, and sailors, and that officials already feared their effects. The Haitian Revolution, which began in 1791 and which authorities cited in connection with these events, is the subject of Chapter 50.

Section 49.20

Colonial Repression, Administrative Changes, and the Consequences of Rebellion

The authorities answered rebellion with a combination of force, exemplary punishment, and calculated concession. Trials were designed not only to punish but to gather information and demonstrate sovereignty, and sentences were staged in public: the executions in the plaza of Cuzco, the display of Galán’s remains, the hanging and quartering of Tiradentes and the Bahian tailors. Exile, confiscation of property, and imprisonment fell on wider circles of relatives and followers. Pardons were used tactically, as at Lampa, and at times violated, and elite participants such as Berbeo and the better-off Bahian suspects regularly received lighter treatment than plebeians.

In the Andes, officials sought to eliminate the symbolic and institutional basis of a future rising. Authorities prohibited Inca portraits, certain forms of dress, and public ceremonies, and Cuzco’s Indigenous nobility was placed under suspicion; kurakas lost privileges and many saw their offices curtailed. Royal officials considered proposals to suppress the Quechua language, though their application was limited. The crown continued the program of intendancies, replacing the corregidores whom rebels had attacked with intendants and subdelegates in Peru in the 1780s, and created an audiencia at Cuzco in 1787. These changes had been in preparation and were accelerated, not originated, by the rebellion. The reparto was restricted, but tribute and the mit'a continued.

The consequences differed by region. In New Granada the concessions of 1781 were revoked, but the experience of mass protest influenced later administrative caution. In Brazil and Venezuela the repression was largely deterrent, and the colonial order held. For Creoles, the lesson of the Andes and of Galán’s radical phase was that mobilization of the poor and Indigenous majority was dangerous, which shaped the caution of many elites in later years. For Indigenous communities the lesson was more ambiguous: violence had failed, but the legal and political arguments of Katari’s movement remained usable, and litigation continued, as discussed in Chapter 47. Historians therefore distinguish consequences produced by rebellion from reforms already underway, and they stress that concession and repression coexisted.

What changed: Corregimientos gave way to intendancies; Inca symbols and some kuraka privileges were suppressed; military garrisons expanded.

Section 49.21

Rebellion before Independence: Political Visions, Contradictions, and Historical Memory

Compared side by side, the movements show how varied late-colonial resistance was. The Andean insurgencies were primarily Indigenous in their base. The movement of Tomás Katari began as a legal campaign for legitimate authority, that of Túpac Amaru combined Inca symbolism with petition for reform and, in time, broader claims, and that of Túpac Katari and Bartolina Sisa drew on Aymara communities besieging a Creole city. The comuneros of New Granada blended fiscal protest by farmers and artisans with Creole leadership that sought negotiation, and a popular radicalism under Galán that the elite would not follow. The Minas conspirators were educated men with republican ideas but divided views of slavery. The Bahian movement drew on artisans, soldiers, and free people of color, and spoke of equality. Coro was an antislavery rebellion, and Gual and España a republican plot.

Several patterns recur. Most movements began with fiscal or administrative grievances and sought redress through royal channels, speaking the language of loyal protest. Leaders usually came from intermediate positions, such as kurakas, traders, Creole landowners, officers, and artisans. Violence escalated when coalitions fractured along ethnic and class lines, and fear of caste war helped the crown rally Creoles and Indigenous nobles. Betrayal and the logistics of supply decided many outcomes. Repression targeted plebeians more severely than elites. None of these movements aimed at the creation of a nation-state in the nineteenth-century sense, and the names now used to locate them require care. Peru, Brazil, and other regions had colonial geographic and administrative identities, such as the Viceroyalty of Peru and the Estado do Brasil, but these did not necessarily correspond in territory or political meaning to the sovereign nation-states established after independence. Bolivia, Colombia, and Venezuela in their modern forms, for example, emerged from different colonial jurisdictions such as the Audiencia de Charcas, New Granada, and the Captaincy General of Venezuela.

Memory transformed these events. Túpac Amaru became a national symbol in Peru, above all in the twentieth century; Bartolina Sisa and Túpac Katari became emblems in Bolivian Indigenous politics; Galán and Manuela Beltrán entered Colombian civic commemoration; Tiradentes became Brazil’s principal republican martyr. Such memory belongs to the history of nation-building and often attributes to rebels purposes they did not hold. Royal loyalty remained widespread after 1800, and the crisis that destroyed Iberian authority came only with the events of 1808 treated in Chapter 51. What these movements did show was the fragility of imperial legitimacy and the readiness of many people to test it. The Haitian Revolution, treated next, would show what could happen when enslaved people themselves overturned a colonial order.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Precursors of Independence or Distinct Responses?

The question: Should the late-eighteenth-century rebellions be understood as early stages of the independence movement?

Interpretation A - Precursors: The movements revealed the fragility of imperial authority, spread ideas of resistance, and became symbols that later leaders and nations claimed.
Interpretation B - Distinct responses: Participants pursued local goals, often claimed loyalty to the crown, and acted in situations quite different from 1808-1825; connecting them risks teleology.

Evidence: Proclamations, trial records, and later commemorations; each is shaped by authors with purposes.

Why it matters: It affects whether independence appears inevitable or contingent. Many historians combine the views, treating the rebellions as evidence of colonial crisis without a straight line to independence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the rebellions, protests, and conspiracies of Iberian America between about 1780 and 1800 as distinct responses to the pressures of imperial reform, not as a single movement toward independence. Its central claim is that these events exposed deep tensions within colonial government while pursuing different objectives: relief from taxes and forced labor, the restoration of Indigenous authority, the defense of local privilege, the abolition of slavery, and, in a few cases, republican government.

The common background was the fiscal and administrative intensification described in Chapter 48. Higher sales taxes, new customs houses, monopolies, forced sales, labor drafts, and intrusive visitors fell on different groups in different ways. Colonial coercive capacity was thin, which made rebellion possible and repression theatrical. Rebels usually spoke the language of loyal protest, rejecting bad government in the king’s name, while other political languages, from Inca memory to republicanism, competed with it. Evidence for all of these movements comes mainly from investigators and prosecutors, which demands source criticism.

In the southern Andes, José Gabriel Condorcanqui, Túpac Amaru II, a kuraka and trader whose Inca ancestry was claimed, litigated, and contested by Cuzco elites, seized and executed the corregidor Arriaga in November 1780 and defeated a royalist force at Sangarará on 18 November. The rebels did not take Cuzco, where royalist reinforcements and Indigenous lords such as Pumacahua and Rosas defended the city. Historians debate whether the turn south reflected sound strategy or fatal delay. Betrayal and logistics undid the rebel army; Túpac Amaru and Micaela Bastidas were captured in April and executed at Cuzco on 18 May 1781.

Micaela Bastidas governed Tungasuca, issued passes, orders, and appointments, corresponded with captains, kurakas, and women, watched for treachery, and argued for strategy, as her letter of 6 December 1780 shows. Tomasa Tito Condemayta commanded her own followers. The sources that preserve their voices were produced and preserved by their enemies, and the language of transmission is a matter of inference.

The movements of Tomás Katari in Chayanta and Túpac Katari in La Paz were distinct from Túpac Amaru’s. Katari’s began in the late 1770s as a legal campaign, with his journey to Buenos Aires in 1778, and drew on the expectation of royal justice. After his killing in January 1781 his brothers fought on. Julián Apaza, Túpac Katari, with Bartolina Sisa and Gregoria Apaza, led Aymara communities in two sieges of La Paz in 1781, broken by columns from Charcas and Buenos Aires. Katari was captured and executed in November 1781, Sisa in 1782.

The war continued in Oruro, Charcas, and the Titicaca basin under Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru and others. The Peace of Lampa was signed on 11 December 1781, Diego Cristóbal’s surrender followed at Sicuani on 27 January 1782, and he was executed in 1783. An attributed estimate of about 100,000 deaths expresses scale but not precision.

In New Granada, fiscal protest in Socorro on 16 March 1781 grew into the comunero march, led by Berbeo, and the Capitulations approved at Santafé on 7 June and sworn at Zipaquirá on 8 June. The coalition fractured along lines of class and ethnicity, authorities repudiated the terms, and Galán, who represented popular radicalism, was sentenced on 30 January 1782 and executed on 1 February, while elite leaders were pardoned.

In Portuguese and Spanish America, conspiracies differed from insurrections. The Inconfidência Mineira of 1789 was a plot of educated and propertied men, divided on republicanism and slavery, discovered before action; Tiradentes was executed in 1792, and his hero’s image is largely a later creation. The Bahian conspiracy of 1798 involved artisans, soldiers, and free people of color and spoke of equality; four men were executed in 1799. The Coro rising of 1795 was an antislavery rebellion, while the Gual and España conspiracy, discovered on 13 July 1797, was a republican plot with Atlantic connections.

Authorities combined punishment with concession, and repression fell hardest on plebeians. In Peru they suppressed Inca symbols, restricted kurakas, and continued intendancies and an audiencia at Cuzco, but reforms already underway must be separated from changes caused by the rebellions. Later memory made many of these figures national symbols, which can obscure their aims. The events reveal the fragility of imperial legitimacy without constituting a unified or inevitable independence movement; the Haitian Revolution, treated in Chapter 50, would show the transformation of an entire colonial order.

Chapter Reflection

These rebellions matter because they show a colonial order under strain, compelled to negotiate with its subjects and, when negotiation failed, to answer with extraordinary violence. Their variety, from an Indigenous war with Inca symbolism to a legal campaign over tribute, a fiscal protest led by Creoles, a conspiracy of poets, and an artisans’ plot, warns against treating resistance as a single story. Each had its own causes, leaders, and limits.

They also reveal how fragile coalitions were. Creoles, Indigenous nobles, mestizos, free people of color, and the enslaved had interests that overlapped in opposition to particular policies but diverged on what should replace them. Fear of caste war and of social upheaval helped the crown keep many of these groups apart. That fear shaped Creole politics for decades, and it will recur in the independence wars and in the republics that followed.

The movements left lasting traces in law, administration, and memory. They accelerated intendancies, new courts, and surveillance of Indigenous nobility, and they taught communities which arguments could still be made through litigation. Later nations remembered Túpac Amaru, Bartolina Sisa, Túpac Katari, Galán, and Tiradentes as precursors, and that memory has been politically powerful. Historians must keep it separate from what these people proposed and could have imagined in their own time.

Chapter 50 turns to Saint-Domingue, where enslaved people overturned a plantation colony and created an independent state. The Haitian Revolution altered the way rulers, planters, free people of color, and the enslaved across the Atlantic world understood the possibilities and dangers of revolution, and it frames the crisis of Iberian legitimacy that Chapters 51 and after will trace.
