The Great Depression and Import-Substitution Industrialization

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: When the Market Abroad Stopped Calling

Between 1929 and the early 1950s, the countries of Latin America discovered how much of their prosperity had been set by decisions made in distant financial centers, and in response they built states and economies that looked inward as never before. The change was real but uneven, and it left some old problems intact.

In the late 1920s, a coffee planter in São Paulo, a sugar mill owner in Cuba, a nitrate miner in northern Chile, a tin miner in the Bolivian highlands, and an oil worker on Lake Maracaibo all depended, in different ways, on buyers in the United States and Europe. Their products crossed oceans and paid for railways, imported machinery, textiles, flour, and the debts their governments had contracted abroad. When foreign demand and foreign lending contracted after 1929, the consequences ran through ports, treasuries, plantations, mines, and households. This chapter asks how Latin Americans lived through that contraction, how governments and social groups responded, and how the response contributed to an economic strategy later called import-substitution industrialization, or ISI.

Two simplifications need to be set aside from the beginning. The first holds that Latin America was a purely agrarian region until 1929 and that industry then appeared as if by decree. Textile mills, breweries, flour mills, cement plants, and metalworking shops already operated in Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, Colombia, and elsewhere, and in São Paulo, Buenos Aires, Monterrey, and Medellín industrial communities had existed for decades. The second simplification holds that governments adopted a fully formed doctrine of ISI in response to the crash. In fact, many of the most important measures, such as exchange controls, devaluations, and debt suspensions, were emergency responses to a balance-of-payments crisis. Only gradually, during the 1930s and above all in the 1940s, did some of those measures become components of deliberate industrial policy, and a systematic theory of developmentalism did not appear until the end of the period.

The chapter also takes social history seriously. Aggregate figures for export value or manufacturing output do not tell us what it meant for a family to lose a wage when a nitrate field closed, for a smallholding coffee grower to see the price of a harvest fall, for a woman to enter a textile mill, or for a rural migrant to find a place in a new industrial neighborhood. Indigenous communities, Afro-Latin American workers, women, and rural laborers experienced the crisis and the recovery differently from exporters, industrialists, and bureaucrats, and often with fewer resources for bargaining. Industrial growth could offer opportunity, but it could also reproduce older patterns of racial and regional exclusion.

Evidence comes in several forms. Trade statistics, central-bank and treasury reports, and the records of international bodies such as the League of Nations allow historians to measure the shock, although national series differ in quality and many early figures are estimates. Legislation, decrees, and the founding charters of development agencies reveal what governments intended. Labor codes, union records, and strike reports document worker organization. Industrial censuses, factory photographs, newspapers, and oral histories give glimpses of working life. The economic ideas of the period, especially the structuralist argument associated with Raúl Prebisch, are themselves historical sources and must be placed in their own time rather than read back into the 1930s.

Within Volume IV and Part XVII, this chapter provides the regional economic and social framework for the chapters that follow. It is comparative by design. Mexico's postrevolutionary institutions were treated in Chapter 74, and the political careers of Getúlio Vargas, Juan and Eva Perón, the Bolivian revolutionaries of 1952, and the reformers of Guatemala's Democratic Spring belong to Chapters 76 through 79. Here those countries appear only as far as their economic and social circumstances illuminate the region-wide pattern. The chapter covers roughly 1929 to about 1950, with limited background before 1929 and a closing look at the early 1950s, and it is organized in five divisions: the collapse of the export-led model, social crisis and political change, the emergence of ISI, regional paths, and the consequences of war and developmentalist thought. The three Map Studies are text-based atlas exercises; readers need an external historical or economic atlas, since no cartographic plates are embedded in this edition.

The chapter's argument has four parts. The Depression exposed the vulnerability of export-dependent economies, but those economies were diverse and some industrial sectors predated 1929. Crisis accelerated state intervention and rearranged relations among exporters, industrialists, workers, and governments, but those changes were uneven and were neither automatically democratic nor redistributive. Manufacturing expanded through a combination of earlier foundations, market conditions, policy, and wartime disruption, and a deliberate ISI model took shape over time. Finally, industrialization transformed economies without ending dependence on export earnings, imported technology, foreign finance, or unequal social structures.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the structure of Latin American export economies on the eve of the Great Depression, including their dependence on particular commodities, foreign capital, and customs revenue.
  2. Explain how falling commodity prices, collapsing trade volumes, and the contraction of foreign lending transmitted the Depression to Latin America, and why national crises differed in timing and severity.
  3. Compare the effects of the crisis on Brazilian coffee, Cuban sugar, Chilean copper and nitrates, Bolivian tin, and other exports, and the policies adopted in response.
  4. Analyze debt suspension, devaluation, and exchange controls as emergency responses that later became building blocks of industrial policy.
  5. Assess the human consequences of the Depression for miners, plantation workers, industrial workers, urban households, and Indigenous communities.
  6. Evaluate why political regimes were destabilized and why governments intervened more actively in economies, without assuming that intervention was democratic.
  7. Distinguish independent labor activism from state-sponsored incorporation of unions, and explain the political weight of industrial workers.
  8. Define import-substitution industrialization and distinguish spontaneous substitution from deliberate policy.
  9. Trace the policy toolkit of ISI, including tariffs, licensing, exchange rates, development banks, and public enterprises, and weigh its benefits and costs.
  10. Compare regional paths among the large industrializing economies, smaller economies, mineral and petroleum exporters, and Caribbean and Central American economies.
  11. Interpret the contribution of World War II and of Prebisch and ECLA to developmentalism, while distinguishing ideas from policies.
  12. Connect the chapter to the political histories of Chapters 76 through 79.

Historical Context

Chapters 68 through 70 described the export boom that followed the mid-nineteenth century: coffee in Brazil and Colombia, sugar in Cuba and the Caribbean, nitrates and copper in Chile, tin in Bolivia, beef and wheat in Argentina and Uruguay, bananas in Central America, and petroleum in Mexico and Venezuela. That boom was financed by British and, increasingly after the First World War, United States capital, and it was tied together by railways and ports. By the 1920s, loans from New York banks flowed to Latin American governments and municipalities in large amounts, so much so that the period is sometimes remembered as a time of easy credit. Governments came to rely heavily on customs duties, which rose and fell with trade volume, and on borrowing abroad.

The export boom also had a social and political shape. Landowning and mining elites, merchants, and foreign companies tied to export sectors dominated many governments, although the degree varied. In some countries, such as Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, and Brazil's São Paulo, a sizable urban working class and middle class had emerged. In others, including much of Central America, the Andes, and the Caribbean, landholding was highly concentrated and Indigenous and rural populations were largely excluded from formal politics. The First World War had already interrupted European supplies and given some industries a boost, and the Mexican Revolution, described in Chapters 71 through 74, had by this time shown that mass social demands could remake a state. Even so, in most of the region the political order of the 1920s rested on an alliance between export interests and governing elites.

Manufacturing in 1929 was neither negligible nor dominant. Cotton textiles, food and beverage processing, flour milling, leather and footwear, cement, and light metalworking served domestic markets in several countries, often behind moderate tariffs that had been imposed for revenue as much as protection. Foreign firms operated meatpacking plants, mines, utilities, and sometimes factories. Industrial workers were concentrated in a few cities. This was the foundation on which later expansion would build, and historians such as Warren Dean for São Paulo and Stephen Haber for Mexico have shown how much industrial experience existed before the crash.

Interpretation of the period has changed. Older accounts, strongly influenced by structuralist and dependency writing of the 1950s and 1960s, tended to present the Depression as a sudden break that forced a turn inward. Later economic historians, including Carlos Díaz Alejandro, Victor Bulmer-Thomas, and Rosemary Thorp, emphasized variation among countries, the importance of policy choices, and the fact that some economies recovered relatively quickly. Others have stressed continuities in export dependence. This chapter follows that more differentiated view, and it treats the economic ideas of the later 1940s as part of the story rather than as a description of what policymakers in 1931 already believed.

Latin America's Export-Led Economies on the Eve of the Great Depression

By 1929, most Latin American economies were organized around a handful of primary commodities. Coffee dominated Brazil's foreign earnings, with São Paulo state the center of production, and it was also the largest export of Colombia and much of Central America. Sugar governed Cuba's economy and was central to the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico. Chile depended on nitrates, whose importance had been declining because of synthetic substitutes, and on copper, much of it mined by United States firms at Chuquicamata and El Teniente. Bolivia's exports were overwhelmingly tin, controlled by a few mining houses. Argentina and Uruguay sold beef, wheat, maize, wool, and hides, mostly to Britain and continental Europe. Peru's exports were more varied, including sugar, cotton, copper, and petroleum. Venezuela, which by the late 1920s had become one of the world's leading oil exporters, and Mexico, whose petroleum output had peaked earlier in the decade, tied their revenues to foreign oil companies. Central America exported coffee and bananas, with United Fruit and other firms holding enormous land and infrastructure.

Dependence on a single product meant that the fate of whole economies hung on the price in New York, London, or Hamburg. Coffee was central to Brazilian and Colombian export earnings; sugar dominated Cuban sales abroad; Bolivia depended heavily on tin and Venezuela increasingly on petroleum. Exact commodity shares vary with the year, export definition, and statistical source, and cannot responsibly be inferred from broad labels alone. The Statistical Study later in this chapter therefore uses identifiable trade-value and price-and-volume series rather than unsupported national percentage estimates. What matters is the pattern: the more concentrated the export base, the more severe the exposure, although other factors, including the structure of ownership and the capacity of the state, shaped outcomes.

Foreign capital and ownership mattered. British investment had built much of the railway and port infrastructure of Argentina, Uruguay, and Brazil, while United States investment grew rapidly after 1914 in Cuba, Chile, Peru, Central America, Colombia, and Venezuela. In enclave sectors such as copper, oil, bananas, and some sugar, foreign firms controlled production, shipping, and often company towns, leaving relatively small portions of the value in national hands beyond taxes and wages. Transport networks tended to run from interior producing areas to ports rather than to link domestic regions with one another, a pattern that shaped later industrial geography.

State finance reflected this structure. Customs duties on imports and export taxes supplied a large share of public revenue in many countries, and governments borrowed abroad to pay for infrastructure and to cover deficits. When trade and credit contracted, revenue fell just as debt service obligations stayed fixed. Inequality compounded the problem: land concentration, limited domestic markets, and low wages meant that the benefits of the export boom were unevenly distributed and that the population's purchasing power was too narrow to cushion the shock easily.

It would be wrong, however, to treat the region as a single case. Argentina had one of the highest per capita incomes in the world and a diversified urban society, while much of Central America and the Andes remained overwhelmingly rural and poor. Mexico's postrevolutionary state, Brazil's federal system with powerful states, and Cuba's formal sovereignty constrained by United States influence differed in the instruments they had at hand. The Depression therefore struck a family of related but distinct economies, and the responses that followed reflected those distinctions.

The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Transmission of the Global Depression

The New York stock market crash of October 1929 is the date most associated with the Depression, but historians distinguish the crash from the broader contraction that followed. The Latin American shock arrived through several channels: falling prices and volumes of exports, the drying up of foreign lending, the rising real burden of fixed debt service, and the protectionist measures adopted by industrial countries. The crash was more a signal than the cause. In fact, United States lending to Latin America had begun to decline in 1928 as New York's stock boom drew funds elsewhere, and some primary-commodity prices, including sugar and coffee, were already weak before the crash because of overproduction.

Export prices fell far faster than the prices of manufactured goods that Latin America imported. The decline in the terms of trade reduced the purchasing power of exports, so that a given volume of coffee or tin bought fewer imports than before. Export volumes also fell where demand collapsed, and in some products governments or cartels restricted output. Industrial countries raised tariffs, and the United States Smoot–Hawley Tariff of 1930 was one of several measures that cut off markets. Credit contraction removed the loans that had financed deficits and permitted debt service. For countries dependent on both exports and loans, the combination was severe.

Timing and intensity varied. Countries with highly concentrated exports and heavy foreign debt, such as Chile, Bolivia, Cuba, and Peru, were hit early and hard. Chile's export earnings collapsed to a small fraction of their 1929 level by 1932, and contemporary observers at the League of Nations described it as among the hardest-hit economies in the world. Argentina, with grain and beef exports and a more diversified economy, suffered a serious but less extreme decline. Colombia, whose coffee was produced by many smallholders and whose debt service was manageable, experienced a milder contraction. Brazil's coffee economy was in crisis, but its large domestic base and policy responses shaped a distinctive path. Venezuela's oil revenue held up better than most commodity exports, although it too felt the downturn. For the region as a whole, the historical ECLA compilation reports merchandise export value falling from US$2,714.0 million in 1929 to US$1,076.6 million in 1932, a decline of 60.3 percent in current dollars (exports f.o.b., excluding gold). This measures nominal export earnings, not the physical volume of goods exported. ECLA also calculated a decline of 48.1 percent when comparing annual average export values for 1925–1929 and 1930–1934, a different time interval and denominator. National outcomes varied widely, and recovery began in many countries between 1932 and 1935.

The speed of recovery is itself a historical finding. Economic historians, notably Díaz Alejandro, noted that several Latin American economies returned to their 1929 output levels earlier than the United States did, in part because abandoning orthodox policies, including the gold standard, gave governments room to maneuver. That recovery was uneven and did not undo the social damage of the first years. It also depended on domestic demand and import compression more than on a revival of exports, which is a central reason the Depression is connected with the later turn toward industry.

Historical Vocabulary
Terms of trade

The terms of trade are the ratio between the prices a country receives for its exports and the prices it pays for its imports. When the ratio falls, a given quantity of exports buys fewer imports, even if the physical volume sold has not changed.

The concept is essential to the chapter because the early 1930s saw a sharp deterioration for primary-commodity exporters, and because structuralist economists later argued that the problem was long-term rather than cyclical. Students should note that this later claim was contested, that terms of trade varied by commodity and period, and that the concept describes prices, not the overall health of an economy.

Division I · Section 75.3

Coffee, Sugar, Copper, Tin, and the Collapse of Export Earnings

Brazilian coffee. Brazil produced a majority of the world's coffee, and for decades the state of São Paulo had supported prices through valorization schemes that bought and stored surpluses. These schemes depended on foreign credit, which dried up after 1929, and surpluses grew. From 1931, the federal government and later the National Coffee Department, established in 1933, purchased coffee and destroyed it, with cumulative destruction through the early 1940s generally estimated in the tens of millions of bags, a figure that represented several years of normal output. The policy sustained planters' incomes at the cost of public finance and, indirectly, supported domestic demand, because payments to planters circulated in the economy. It also meant that coffee growers, rather than being ruined, were partly protected, a decision tied to the political weight of the export sector. The Revolution of 1930, treated in Chapter 76, was in part a political consequence of this crisis.

Cuban sugar. Cuba's economy was more thoroughly dependent on a single product than any other major economy. Sugar prices had weakened in the late 1920s, and the United States tariff of 1930 raised duties on Cuban sugar, reducing access to its main market. The international Chadbourne Plan, proposed by New York attorney Thomas L. Chadbourne and negotiated with Cuban producers and the Machado government alongside other sugar-exporting interests, sought to restrict supply and raise prices; the resulting international agreement was signed in Brussels on May 9, 1931. Cuban production fell to a fraction of its late-1920s level, mills closed or cut hours, and wages in cane fields collapsed. The political consequence was the fall of President Gerardo Machado in 1933 and a period of revolutionary upheaval, discussed in the political summaries of Section 75.6. Later United States quota legislation and a new trade agreement in 1934 gave Cuba guaranteed access but also tied it more closely to a managed United States market.

Chilean copper and nitrates. Chile's nitrate industry had been in decline since the 1920s because synthetic nitrogen made nitrate less necessary, and the crisis accelerated its collapse. Copper, produced largely by United States firms, fell with industrial demand. Because Chile's government relied on export taxes on these products, its revenue and its capacity to import collapsed together. Thousands of miners lost work in the northern desert and moved south in search of employment, an episode that fed urban poverty and political radicalization, including the short-lived Socialist Republic of June 1932. Copper production and employment recovered in the later 1930s, and the sector remained dominated by foreign firms until later decades.

Bolivian tin. Bolivian tin was mined in the high Andes by a workforce that was largely Indigenous, in conditions of altitude, dust, and low wages, and exported through firms owned by a small number of mining houses. The Depression cut prices and volumes, and an international tin control scheme from 1931 restricted production, throwing miners out of work. Bolivia suspended debt service in January 1931. The Chaco War with Paraguay from 1932 to 1935 further strained state finances and drew many highland men into conflict. The wider political consequences belong to Chapter 78, but the economic structure explains much of Bolivian political radicalization.

Other exports. Colombian coffee proved more resilient, helped by its smallholder base, a national federation of growers, and a currency that adjusted. Argentine and Uruguayan beef and grain faced declining prices and British imperial preference. Peruvian sugar, cotton, and oil showed varied performance, with some recovery as prices stabilized. Central American coffee and bananas fell sharply in price, with serious consequences in El Salvador and Guatemala, discussed below. In each case, the details of ownership, labor, and state policy shaped the outcome, and comparisons require attention to the measurement period and the unit, whether value, volume, or price.

Official Reports on Brazil's Coffee Purchase and Destruction Program

Creators: Brazilian federal agencies, including the National Coffee Council and its successor the National Coffee Department · Dates: 1931 onward · Type: Government administrative reports and statistical series · Verified record: Rosemary Thorp, economic history synthesis, chapter 4 (context; original administrative reports not collated)

Context: Brazilian federal authorities published periodic accounts of coffee purchased, stored, and destroyed, and of the revenue raised by a tax on coffee exports used to finance the program. Foreign observers, including United States and British consuls and the League of Nations, also reported on the policy.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: the reports present the destruction of coffee as a regulated measure to balance supply with demand, report quantities in sacks, and describe the financing as a charge on exports. They treat the program as a temporary expedient while markets recovered.

Analysis: These documents reveal how the state framed intervention in orderly administrative terms and how planters' interests were protected. They are strong evidence for quantities and instruments, although totals vary across compilations and some figures were reported in different periods and measures. They say little about the workers who handled the coffee, the pickers who lost seasonal work, or smallholders outside the main commercial structure, and they cannot show how destruction affected consumers or foreign buyers. Students should treat the official language of regulation with the same care they would apply to any government account of its own program.

Division I · Section 75.4

Debt, Currency Depreciation, Exchange Controls, and the Breakdown of International Trade

The collapse of export earnings produced a shortage of the foreign currency needed to pay for imports and service foreign debt. Governments had three broad options: maintain debt payments and gold-standard parities through deflation, devalue the currency, or suspend payments and impose controls. In the early 1930s, a large majority of Latin American governments chose some combination of the second and third. Bolivia suspended payments in January 1931, and over the next two years Peru, Chile, Brazil, Colombia, Uruguay, and many others followed in whole or in part. Argentina, with close ties to British creditors, maintained its service on the foreign debt through most of the decade, partly in exchange for trade concessions, while Haiti and the Dominican Republic, under United States financial supervision of customs revenue, followed a different pattern.

Currencies depreciated sharply as governments abandoned the gold standard, and the cost of imports rose in domestic terms. Exchange controls emerged as governments rationed scarce foreign currency, giving priority to debt service, essential imports, or favored sectors. Argentina introduced controls in 1931. Chile, Brazil, Colombia, Uruguay, and others adopted rationing systems of various strictness. Under these systems, importers needed permits to obtain foreign exchange, and the allocation of exchange became a powerful instrument of economic policy. Multiple exchange rates, in which different transactions received different rates, were another device used in several countries.

International trade itself fragmented. Britain's imperial preference arrangements of 1932, the United States tariffs, and the bilateral agreements pursued by Nazi Germany reshaped markets. Germany offered compensation trade arrangements in which goods were exchanged without hard currency, and Brazil, among others, sent increasing volumes of coffee, cotton, and other products to Germany in exchange for German manufactures. Argentina's agreement with Britain in 1933, discussed in Section 75.13, tied meat exports to preferential treatment of British goods. United States reciprocal trade agreements under the Good Neighbor Policy later reopened some channels. For Latin American governments, bilateralism was a pragmatic way to dispose of exports, and it shifted trade patterns away from earlier arrangements.

These measures were largely improvised emergency responses, not manifestations of a coherent industrial strategy. Officials like Argentina's central bankers or Brazil's finance ministers defended them as temporary and in many cases would have preferred a return to orthodoxy. Yet their effects were industrial. Devaluation and controls made imported manufactures expensive and scarce, which raised the profitability of domestic production, while the allocation of scarce exchange could favor raw materials and machinery over finished consumer goods. In this way, instruments introduced for balance-of-payments reasons became protective in practice and, later, became deliberate tools of policy.

Historical Vocabulary
Exchange controls

Exchange controls are government rules restricting access to foreign currency, typically by requiring permits for imports or foreign payments and by setting the exchange rate at which currency may be bought or sold.

In the early 1930s, such controls were emergency measures to conserve scarce foreign currency. Over time, they became a means of directing imports toward machinery and raw materials and away from consumer goods, and of favoring some producers over others. They matter to the chapter because they illustrate how a short-term crisis tool became a long-term industrial instrument. They should not be confused with tariffs, which tax imports, although the two often worked together.

Division II · Section 75.5

Unemployment, Rural Poverty, Wage Decline, and the Human Consequences of Depression

The human cost of the Depression was distributed unevenly. In the mines of Chile and Bolivia, layoffs and reduced shifts removed the only wage income from entire communities, and nitrate towns in the Atacama were emptied as workers and their families moved or were repatriated. In Cuba, cane cutters and mill workers, who already faced seasonal work, saw both employment and daily wages fall dramatically, and rural families sometimes turned to subsistence plots, if they had access to land. In Brazilian coffee districts, colonos, the tenant families who tended trees and picked in return for housing, wages, and small plots, faced reduced payments and uncertain work, although the destruction program and the continuing need for cultivation cushioned some of the shock.

Smallholders faced different pressures. Colombian coffee growers, many working small plots, experienced falling cash income but retained food crops and local ties. Indigenous communities in the Andes, Mexico, and Central America, which often provided seasonal labor to plantations and mines, saw wage opportunities shrink while land pressures persisted. In El Salvador, the collapse of coffee prices cut wages and credit for plantation labor and for Indigenous and peasant communities in the western highlands. In January 1932, a rebellion in that region, linked to the Communist leader Farabundo Martí and to deep grievances, was crushed by the military government of General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez in a killing known as La Matanza, with commonly cited estimates ranging from roughly ten thousand to thirty thousand dead. Indigenous identity was targeted in the repression, and the episode is a reminder that the Depression's consequences were political and violent as well as economic.

Urban households responded to unemployment and falling purchasing power by combining sources of income. Women took in washing or sewing, ran small shops, or worked as domestic servants, a sector that remained among the largest employers of women. Children left school to work. Families moved in with relatives, pawned goods, bought on credit, or relied on mutual aid societies. Meanwhile, wage declines were often accompanied by price changes that altered real incomes unevenly: imported goods became dearer, while food prices could fall with agricultural prices, so that employed workers did not always suffer equally with the unemployed. Historians stress that measuring real wages in this period is difficult because of limited and uneven data.

Public relief was limited. Most states lacked unemployment insurance or sizable welfare systems, and charity and local municipalities carried much of the burden. In Mexico, hundreds of thousands of Mexicans and Mexican Americans were repatriated from the United States in the early 1930s, often under pressure, adding to unemployment at home. Migration within countries, from mines and plantations to cities and from highlands to coasts, reshaped communities. Aggregate statistics on output cannot capture these experiences, and students should be wary of equating a recovery in national income with recovery in household welfare.

Division II · Section 75.6

Political Instability, Oligarchic Crisis, and the Expansion of Government Intervention

The Depression weakened governing coalitions that had rested on export prosperity. Between 1930 and 1933, governments fell or were overthrown in a large number of countries, including Argentina, Brazil, Peru, Chile, Cuba, Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, and several in Central America. The forms of change varied: military coups, revolts, constitutional transitions, and in some cases the consolidation of dictatorship. A comparative treatment must avoid reducing these events to a single type, and it must remember that the economy was one factor among many, interacting with earlier political divisions.

In Argentina, a military coup on 6 September 1930 ended the presidency of Hipólito Yrigoyen and began a period that Argentines call the Infamous Decade, in which elections were manipulated and conservative coalitions governed while the state expanded its economic role. In Brazil, the Revolution of 1930, treated in Chapter 76, ended the Old Republic's oligarchic arrangements. In Chile, the dictatorship of Carlos Ibáñez collapsed in 1931 amid economic distress, followed by a turbulent period that included a naval mutiny and a brief Socialist Republic, before the return of Arturo Alessandri in 1932 and a more orthodox recovery. In Cuba, Machado's government, increasingly repressive and discredited by economic collapse, fell in August 1933, followed by the revolt of sergeants in September and a short reformist government. The Platt Amendment, the instrument of formal United States oversight, was abrogated in 1934, although United States economic influence remained strong. In Peru, Bolivia, and Colombia, regimes changed under economic pressure. Colombia saw the end of a long period of Conservative rule in 1930 and the emergence under Alfonso López Pumarejo in 1934 of a Liberal reform program known as the Revolution on the March.

Authoritarian responses were common in Central America and the Caribbean. Jorge Ubico in Guatemala, Maximiliano Hernández Martínez in El Salvador, Tiburcio Carías in Honduras, Anastasio Somoza García in Nicaragua, and Rafael Trujillo in the Dominican Republic established personal or military regimes in the 1930s that combined repression with limited public works and control of labor. These were not reformist states, and they illustrate that economic crisis did not necessarily lead to democratic or redistributive outcomes. In Uruguay, President Gabriel Terra carried out a coup in 1933 that curtailed the country's democratic institutions.

Across these varied cases, governments expanded intervention. They created central banks and marketing boards, regulated prices and exchange, supported agricultural producers, and in some cases established labor ministries and social insurance. Reformist governments in Mexico, Colombia, Chile after 1938, and elsewhere linked intervention to land reform, labor protection, or social programs. Conservative and authoritarian governments used intervention to stabilize elite interests or to build state power. The common feature was a shift away from the liberal expectation that markets would adjust, and toward a political economy in which the state allocated foreign exchange, credit, and protection. The detailed political histories of Brazil, Argentina, Bolivia, and Guatemala are reserved for Chapters 76 through 79.

Division II · Section 75.7

Organized Labor, Trade Unions, Strikes, and the Emergence of Mass Politics

Labor organization predated the Depression. Anarchist, syndicalist, socialist, and mutualist organizations had operated in Argentina, Chile, Brazil, Mexico, Cuba, and elsewhere since the late nineteenth century, and strikes among railway, port, meatpacking, textile, and mining workers had been frequent. The crisis changed the context. Unemployment weakened workers' bargaining power in the short run, yet it also radicalized some workers, brought new communist and socialist organizing, and persuaded governments that labor relations required regulation. As industry grew in the later 1930s and 1940s, factory and service workers, with a concentrated presence in capital cities and ports, acquired significant leverage.

Scholars distinguish between several patterns of relationship between unions and states. In some cases, notably Mexico under the Confederation of Mexican Workers (CTM) formed in 1936, and in Chile, where a Popular Front government took office in 1938, unions allied with reformist coalitions and parties and gained legal recognition. In Brazil, labor legislation and a Ministry of Labor created from 1930 tied unions to the state through registration, official recognition, and contributions, a model described as corporatist. In Argentina, the General Confederation of Labor (CGT), founded in 1930, split over political strategy in the mid-1930s, and its later relationship to the state was transformed in the 1940s. Ruth and David Collier's influential comparative work distinguishes state-led incorporation from party-led incorporation, and it argues that the form of incorporation shaped later political regimes. Students should treat that framework as an interpretation, not a rule, and note that independent labor activism continued alongside state-sponsored structures.

Labor legislation expanded. Governments introduced or revised labor codes, setting rules on contracts, hours, minimum wages, union registration, and strike procedures, and some established social security funds. Such measures gave workers rights and protections, and they also gave governments tools to supervise and contain union activity. A recurrent tension was between protection and control: laws that legalized unions often required official recognition, limited strikes, or tied union leadership to state patronage. Communist unions in several countries were harassed, tolerated, or used in alliance depending on the political moment and international developments.

Industrial workers were a minority of the workforce in most countries, outnumbered by agricultural workers, domestic servants, and informal laborers. Nonetheless, they carried disproportionate political weight. They were concentrated in capital cities and strategic sectors such as transport, ports, mines, and energy, where strikes could paralyze economies. Their organizations were visible and easier to mobilize than dispersed rural populations. Political leaders recognized that urban workers could be a base of support, and parties and governments competed for their loyalty. This is the background to the populist and mass-political experiences of Chapters 76 and 77, which should be read as developments of conditions described here.

Division II · Section 75.8

Cities, Internal Migration, Women, and the Changing Social Structure

Urbanization accelerated in the 1930s and 1940s as migrants left rural districts for capital cities and ports. São Paulo, Buenos Aires, Mexico City, Santiago, Lima, Bogotá, Medellín, and Caracas grew rapidly, drawing workers from impoverished countryside, declining mining districts, and plantation zones. Migrants included European immigrants in the earlier decades, but increasingly they were internal migrants from the northeast of Brazil, the interior provinces of Argentina, rural Mexico, the Andean highlands, and the Colombian countryside. They settled in tenements, in rented rooms, and increasingly in informal settlements on the edges of cities, creating neighborhoods with limited services but strong community networks. Housing shortages and unregulated construction became enduring features of urban life.

Women's paid work took several forms. Domestic service remained the largest category of female employment in most cities, and many women worked in the informal economy as street sellers, laundresses, seamstresses, and food vendors. In textile mills, garment workshops, tobacco factories, and food processing, women formed a large share of early industrial workers, although in some places, such as São Paulo's textile industry, the share of women declined as men entered the industry and as employers and unions shaped hiring patterns. Women's wages were consistently lower than men's, and protective legislation sometimes restricted women's work while ostensibly shielding them. At the same time, women organized, participated in strikes, joined unions, and entered political debate. Suffrage campaigns achieved national voting rights for women in Brazil in 1932, Cuba in 1934, and later in Argentina in 1947, Chile at the national level in 1949, and Mexico in 1953, though in different political circumstances.

Middle-class growth accompanied industrialization and state expansion. Clerks, teachers, civil servants, technicians, and professionals multiplied as bureaucracies and firms grew. These groups depended on salaries, favored state employment and education, and often supported reformist or nationalist programs, though their politics varied. The social structure of cities became more complex, with an industrial working class, a salaried middle class, small shopkeepers, and a large informal sector beside the older elite.

Race and region structured access to the new opportunities. In São Paulo, employers had favored European immigrants in factory hiring, and Afro-Brazilians, many of them descendants of the formerly enslaved, found themselves concentrated in lower-paid and less stable work, a pattern that Afro-Brazilian organizations such as the Brazilian Black Front, founded in 1931, protested. In Andean and Mexican cities, Indigenous migrants entered urban labor markets as manual workers and domestic servants, often facing discrimination and being classified by language, dress, and origin. Regional inequality persisted, as industry concentrated in a few metropolitan areas while the interior, the Brazilian northeast, southern Mexico, and the Andean highlands lagged. Industrial growth therefore offered mobility to some and reinforced exclusion for others.

Everyday Life and Social History

Urban Industrial Households: Wages, Work, and Daily Survival

A household in an industrial district of São Paulo, Buenos Aires, Santiago, or Mexico City in the 1930s or 1940s typically depended on several incomes. A father or an adult son might work in a mill, a meatpacking plant, or a metal shop, while a mother or daughter did piecework, took in laundry, worked as a domestic servant, or sold food at a stall. Children contributed as messengers or helpers, and school attendance was often brief. Rent for a room or a place in a shared tenement consumed a large share of income, and in new peripheral settlements families built their own housing on land of uncertain legal status.

Factory discipline changed daily rhythm. Shifts were set by bells and whistles, and employers sometimes provided housing, schools, or sports clubs as paternalistic ties, particularly in company-run mill villages. Food consisted largely of staples such as bread, beans, rice, maize, potatoes, and cheap meat, and purchases on credit at neighborhood shops were common. Social life revolved around mutual aid societies, church organizations, unions, football clubs, dance halls, and the radio, which spread rapidly in the 1930s and 1940s and carried music, news, and political speech into working-class homes. Migrants brought regional foods, music, and religious practices that blended with urban cultures.

The surviving evidence is uneven. Factory records, industrial censuses, and union newspapers tell much about wages and organization, while household budget surveys, where they exist, tell more about consumption, and oral histories collected later from workers preserve experience that written sources neglect. Women's unpaid and informal work is especially hard to measure, and its absence from official statistics is itself a historical problem.

Division III · Section 75.9

What Was Import-Substitution Industrialization? Origins, Definitions, and Early Development

Import-substitution industrialization means the domestic production of goods that a country previously imported. As a description of a process, it is simple: when a country that once bought cotton cloth, shoes, or cement abroad begins to make them at home, it substitutes domestic output for imports. As a description of policy, it is more complex, because governments can encourage that process deliberately through protection, credit, and public investment, or it can occur without any such design. Economists have therefore distinguished spontaneous substitution, resulting from shortages, high import prices, or currency depreciation, from deliberate ISI, in which policy is directed toward fostering domestic manufacturing.

Substitution did not begin in 1929. Textile mills had operated in Mexico, Brazil, and Colombia since the nineteenth century, breweries and flour mills served urban markets, and the First World War had interrupted imports and spurred domestic production in several countries. Tariffs, often designed for revenue, gave incidental protection. In São Paulo, coffee wealth and immigrant entrepreneurship had financed a diversified industrial base by the 1920s, while Mexico's Monterrey had developed steel, glass, and brewing before the revolution's end. The Depression accelerated rather than created these trends.

The mechanisms of acceleration were straightforward. Currency depreciation and exchange controls raised the domestic price of imports, while the collapse of export earnings forced reductions in import volume. At the same time, owners of idle or underused capital, including planters, merchants, and importers, sometimes redirected resources into manufacturing, and existing factories used spare capacity to meet demand that imports could no longer satisfy. The result was a shift in relative prices and profitability that favored domestic manufacturing. Industrial output in several large economies recovered earlier and more strongly than agricultural export sectors, and manufacturing's share in output rose, though estimates differ by country and source.

The transition from spontaneous substitution to deliberate policy took place gradually. In the 1930s, most governments still thought of their emergency measures as temporary and were more preoccupied with exchange stability, debt, and export recovery. By the late 1930s and especially the 1940s, however, officials, industrialists, and military leaders increasingly argued that industrialization was a national goal, linked to economic security and independence. Institutions such as development banks, state corporations, and industrial ministries gave policy a more systematic form, and by the late 1940s intellectuals had formulated a justification, discussed in Section 75.18. Albert Hirschman's later analysis described the process as proceeding in stages, beginning with easy substitution of simple consumer goods and moving to more difficult substitution of intermediate and capital goods, a framework that this chapter uses cautiously.

Historical Vocabulary
Import-substitution industrialization (ISI)

Import-substitution industrialization refers to the expansion of domestic manufacturing to replace imported goods, and, in its policy sense, to a development strategy that uses protection, credit, public investment, and exchange policy to promote that expansion for the domestic market.

The term describes both a process and a strategy, and historians distinguish the two. Substitution occurred spontaneously in the 1930s in many places, while an explicit ISI strategy took shape more slowly. The label can mislead if it implies that ISI was uniform across the region or that it began everywhere at the same time, or that it excluded exports entirely, since export earnings still paid for imported machinery and inputs.

Division III · Section 75.10

Tariffs, Import Licensing, Currency Policy, and Protection of Domestic Manufacturing

The toolkit of protection included tariffs, quantitative restrictions, exchange controls, and preferential access to foreign currency. Tariffs raised the price of imports, protecting domestic producers and bringing revenue. Import licenses and quotas limited the quantity of particular goods that could enter. Exchange controls and multiple exchange rates allowed governments to give industrial firms cheaper foreign currency for importing machinery and raw materials, while charging more for luxury or competing consumer goods. Governments also used public purchasing to favor domestic suppliers and offered subsidies, tax exemptions, and cheap credit to selected industries.

The economic reasoning combined several arguments. One was infant-industry protection, the idea that new industries need temporary shelter until they can compete. A second was balance-of-payments reasoning: if export earnings were weak or unstable, reducing imports through domestic production eased pressure on foreign exchange. A third was a national security and autonomy rationale, strengthened by the experience of war, and a fourth was employment, since manufacturing provided urban jobs. The arguments were persuasive to many policymakers, and industrialists, labor unions, and nationalist officers formed coalitions in their favor.

The costs were also recognized, then and later. Protection raised prices for consumers, who paid more for domestically produced goods that were often inferior or more costly than imports. Exchange controls and overvalued currencies, often used to cheapen imported machinery, disadvantaged exporters by reducing the local-currency value of their earnings, which discouraged exports and further constrained the foreign exchange on which industry depended. Protection could shield inefficient firms and encourage the formation of concentrated, sometimes monopolistic, industries. Government finances could be strained by subsidies and by the loss of tariff revenue where import restrictions replaced duties. Allocation of licenses and exchange also opened opportunities for favoritism and corruption.

Policy varied. Mexico used tariffs and import licenses extensively, with a 1941 law offering tax benefits to new manufacturing industries. Brazil relied heavily on exchange allocation and, later, on a system of differential exchange rates. Argentina combined exchange controls with state credit. Smaller economies, with limited internal markets, faced narrower scope. The point for students is that ISI was never a single formula, and that its design reflected the political balance among exporters, industrialists, workers, and bureaucrats in each country.

Consider a manufacturer of cloth who needed imported looms and dyes but competed with finished cloth shipped from abroad. A government facing scarce foreign exchange could grant that factory a license to purchase machinery at a preferred exchange rate while denying foreign currency to importers of finished cloth. That arrangement could encourage local production without creating capacity to manufacture looms or industrial chemicals. Its effects depended on whether administrators could enforce the rules, how much competition remained among domestic firms, and whether factories could operate at efficient scale. In agricultural exporting regions, the same currency regime might create opposition: exporters receiving foreign exchange at an unfavorable official rate obtained fewer domestic pesos or cruzeiros for every sale, effectively transferring purchasing power toward favored importers or consumers. The policy was thus a distributional bargain, not merely a technical trade instrument. A useful comparison is between the emergency controls Argentina introduced in 1931, the managed exchange arrangements used in Brazil, and the stronger emphasis on licenses and protection in Mexico during the 1940s. These cases differed both in political constituencies and in the sequence of their interventions. Students should not assume a single region-wide tariff schedule or one uniform exchange regime.

Division III · Section 75.11

Development Banks, Infrastructure, Public Enterprises, and the Expanding Economic State

Protection alone could not build industry without capital, energy, and transport. Governments increasingly created specialized institutions to supply them. Mexico's Nacional Financiera, established in 1934, evolved into a major vehicle for industrial financing, and later channeled public and foreign funds into steel, electricity, and other enterprises. Argentina created an industrial credit bank in the 1940s, and Brazil expanded public credit through the Bank of Brazil and, later, development institutions. Chile's Production Development Corporation, CORFO, founded in 1939, became the most comprehensive example of a state planning and financing agency in the region at that time.

CORFO's origins lay in a catastrophe and a political coalition. A devastating earthquake struck the Chillán region in January 1939, killing thousands, and the Popular Front government of Pedro Aguirre Cerda, which had taken office in December 1938, seized the moment to create an agency that would handle reconstruction and promote national production. Over the following years CORFO financed or founded enterprises in electric power, including the National Electricity Company (ENDESA), in steel, petroleum, sugar beet processing, and fisheries, and it prepared plans for national development. Some of the projects, such as the Huachipato steel works, were completed in the following decade. CORFO illustrates how state intervention could combine planning, finance, and enterprise ownership.

Infrastructure expansion mattered as much as direct industrial investment. Electricity generation and distribution, roads, ports, and communications were extended, often by public companies or by state-regulated firms. Foreign utilities, including British and North American electricity and telephone companies, faced increasing regulation and, in some places, nationalization. Mexico's later nationalization of electricity and Brazil's expansion of public power belong to the period beyond this chapter's main coverage, but the pattern of state involvement was established in the 1930s and 1940s.

Students should distinguish three forms of state involvement: ownership of enterprises, regulation of private firms, and support for private enterprise through credit and protection. The mix varied. Chile and Brazil moved toward state ownership in strategic sectors such as steel and energy, while Colombia and Mexico supported private manufacturers with credit and protection while also creating public firms, and Argentina combined state enterprises, many under military administration, with private industry. In all cases, the state became a more central economic actor than it had been in the export era.

Division III · Section 75.12

Factories, Consumer Goods, Steel, Energy, and the Growth of National Industrial Sectors

Early import substitution concentrated on light consumer goods. Cotton and woolen textiles, clothing, footwear, processed foods and beverages, soap, matches, furniture, and household goods required relatively modest capital and technology, drew on domestic raw materials, and served an existing market that had been supplied by imports. Cement, glass, paper, and some chemicals followed. Domestic entrepreneurs, many of them immigrants or the descendants of immigrants, dominated these industries in places such as São Paulo, Buenos Aires, and Monterrey, often as family firms that expanded from workshops. Foreign firms also established branch plants, especially in consumer goods, tires, and assembly, to avoid tariffs.

Heavy industry was more difficult. Steel, machinery, chemicals, and engineering industries required large capital, advanced technology, dependable energy, and imported equipment, all scarce in a period of limited foreign exchange. Governments therefore played a larger role. Mexico's private Monterrey steelmaker had operated since 1900, and the state-supported Altos Hornos de México was established in 1942. In Brazil, a long political and diplomatic effort during the war led to the creation of the National Steel Company in 1941, with the Volta Redonda plant, financed with United States credit, producing its first steel in 1946. Argentina's military established a directorate for military manufactures in 1941, which became involved in steel and other industries. Colombia's Paz del Río steel project began in the late 1940s. Chile's Huachipato works, planned through CORFO, entered production around 1950.

Energy and transport underpinned all industrial growth. Electricity generation expanded, though shortages frequently limited output, and the supply of petroleum products and coal affected industry differently by country. Oil-producing countries such as Mexico and Venezuela could use domestic fuel, while others imported it. Railways and roads shaped where industry could locate, favoring port cities and established metropolitan regions.

The roles of different actors varied. Domestic entrepreneurs carried much of the expansion in light industry, governments financed and sometimes owned basic industries, and foreign companies participated through investment, licensing, and technology supply. Foreign capital was often welcomed, particularly where domestic capital was thin, but it also raised concerns about dependence. Entrepreneurs, civil servants, engineers, military officers, and trade unionists all participated in building the new industrial sector, and their relationships were sometimes cooperative and sometimes conflictual.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Global Depression to Domestic Industrial Expansion

Long-term causes

Export concentration, reliance on foreign capital and customs revenue, earlier industrial foundations, narrow domestic markets, and unequal land and income distribution.

Short-term causes

The 1929 crash, collapse of commodity prices and lending, protectionism abroad, and the foreign-exchange shortage that followed.

Event or transformation

Default, devaluation, and exchange controls raised the price of imports and made domestic manufacturing more profitable.

Immediate effects

Recovery led by domestic demand and industry in several economies, new state agencies, and growth of urban labor.

Long-term consequences

A larger industrial sector, an expanded state, and organized labor as a political force, along with continuing dependence on imported machinery and export earnings.

Historical complexity

The Depression did not create industry from nothing. Outcomes depended on prior capacity, policy choices, politics, and wartime conditions, and varied widely by country.

Multiple Perspectives: Industrialization and State Intervention

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or influence over policy.

Industrial workers: Industrial jobs offered steadier wages and, increasingly, legal rights and social benefits, but workers also faced factory discipline, state supervision of unions, and inflation that eroded wages.
Industrialists: Many welcomed protection, credit, and cheaper imported machinery, and some supported labor regulation as a means of social peace, while resisting wage demands and competition from foreign firms.
Exporters and landowners: They often saw exchange controls and overvalued currencies as a transfer of income away from export agriculture and mining, though some benefited from price supports, as coffee growers did in Brazil.
Rural and Indigenous communities: Rural families supplied migrants and food but often saw little benefit from industrial policy, and faced pressures on land and wages as agricultural sectors were taxed or neglected.
Consumers: Urban consumers gained access to domestically produced goods but paid higher prices for protected products, and shortages and quality problems were common.

The distinction between installed capacity and actual industrial production is especially important for this period. Domestic demand could rise because imported consumer goods disappeared, yet factories could not necessarily increase output if a worn transmission belt, spare motor, chemical input, or specialized tool had to be obtained abroad. Engineers and machine operators had to maintain imported equipment longer than expected, making technical schools and apprenticeship systems valuable complements to tariff protection. Where steel and electricity were bottlenecks, one new plant could support many downstream industries, but it could also consume scarce capital and foreign exchange before producing saleable output. The state often assumed this long investment horizon because private lenders were reluctant to finance a project with high upfront costs and uncertain demand. Working conditions mattered as well: expanding output did not guarantee comparable wage gains, and women in textile and food-processing establishments often worked under different pay and promotion arrangements from men in metalworking and heavy industry. Industrial statistics should therefore be read alongside capital-import records, labor surveys, and sector-specific production evidence.

Division IV · Section 75.13

Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina: Comparing the Region's Major Industrial Economies

Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina were the region's largest industrial economies by the mid-twentieth century, but they reached that position through different circumstances. Mexico combined a large population and domestic market with a postrevolutionary state that, as Chapter 74 explained, had created ejidos, strong labor confederations, a state oil company, and a governing party. Industry expanded in Mexico City, Monterrey, and Puebla, supported by the 1941 manufacturing law, Nacional Financiera, and wartime demand, including from the United States. Foreign investment was selective, and the state's role in oil, railways, and electricity grew. Mexican manufacturing was shaped by its large neighbor and by labor exports under the wartime Bracero program of 1942.

Brazil had an agrarian export economy led by coffee but also the largest single concentration of industry in São Paulo, built on coffee wealth, immigrant entrepreneurship, and a growing urban labor force. The policies of the 1930s protected coffee income, which sustained demand, while exchange controls and tariffs favored domestic manufacturing. The federal government expanded its role in steel, mining, and transport, with the Volta Redonda plant and the Vale do Rio Doce mining company, established in 1942, as examples. Brazil's size, resources, and political consolidation under Vargas, treated in Chapter 76, shaped a state-led industrialization that retained a strong role for planters and regional elites.

Argentina began the period as one of the richest economies in Latin America, exporting beef and grain, with an industrial base in food processing, textiles, and metalworking that grew in Buenos Aires and the littoral. The conservative governments of the 1930s pursued policies aimed at protecting traditional export interests while managing exchange and trade, and industry grew in part within that framework. Manufacturing expanded substantially during the 1930s and the war, increasing the industrial workforce, much of it drawn from internal migrants. The politics that this growth made possible, Peronism, belongs to Chapter 77.

Several comparative contrasts stand out. Mexico had the strongest institutional link between organized labor and the postrevolutionary state, Brazil the strongest state-directed corporatist labor system and an unusually large internal market, and Argentina the highest income and most developed consumer market but a political system dominated until 1943 by agrarian-export interests. Access to raw materials also differed: Brazil had iron ore and Mexico petroleum, while Argentina lacked coal and iron and depended on imports of fuel. Foreign investment was important in all three, from British firms in Argentina and United States firms in Mexico and Brazil. The comparison undermines any single model of ISI and underscores that politics and resources, as well as policy, shaped outcomes.

Chile, Colombia, and Uruguay: State Intervention and Industrial Development in Smaller Economies

Chile suffered one of the most severe export collapses in the world, and the state's response evolved from orthodox retrenchment under Arturo Alessandri's second presidency, beginning in late 1932, to broader intervention under the Popular Front. Industrial output, particularly in textiles, food, and metalworking, recovered during the 1930s, and CORFO, as described in Section 75.11, institutionalized state-led development. Chile's industrial sector was constrained by its small domestic market and by the continuing dominance of foreign-owned copper, which remained a major source of state revenue. Labor organization was strong, and the Socialist and Communist parties were important in the Popular Front coalition, as Paul Drake's work has shown. At the same time, rural labor remained largely excluded from the political bargains that benefited industrial and mining workers, and landed elites retained substantial influence.

Colombia relied on coffee, produced mainly by smallholders in the Andean highlands, and the National Federation of Coffee Growers, founded in 1927, became a powerful institution for marketing and technical support. The coffee economy created a rural cash income that supported a domestic market, and Medellín in Antioquia developed a textile industry financed by coffee, commerce, and mining wealth. The Liberal governments after 1930, especially under López Pumarejo, enacted social and land legislation, including a 1936 land law, and the state supported industry. Colombia's rugged geography fragmented its market, and its violence, treated in Chapter 83, would later complicate development. Industrial growth during the war was substantial, though manufacturing remained concentrated in a few regions.

Uruguay was a small, urbanized country with an early welfare state shaped by the reforms of José Batlle y Ordóñez in the early twentieth century, and an economy dependent on meat and wool. Protection and state enterprises, including electricity and banking, supported a modest industrial base in Montevideo, and organized labor and strong welfare institutions gave the country a distinctive social profile. The 1933 coup by President Terra restricted democracy, but the state's economic role persisted. The small domestic market limited the scale of manufacturing and made Uruguay vulnerable to changes in export markets in later decades.

Comparison highlights the constraints of scale and structure. Chile and Uruguay had small markets and powerful organized workers, Colombia had a smallholder export base that built domestic demand but had regional fragmentation, and all three used state institutions to support industry. Government capacity varied, as did the degree to which rural elites could block change. The dependence of each on a particular commodity, copper, coffee, or meat and wool, remained central to their prospects.

These three examples also complicate the familiar division between “industrializing” and “nonindustrializing” countries. In Chile, a mining export sector could finance infrastructure and public programs while remaining foreign-controlled and highly cyclical; the same government could promote domestic manufacturing without removing the original export vulnerability. In Colombia, coffee receipts reached many independent growers rather than a handful of mines, potentially stimulating purchases of locally manufactured goods, although unequal access to land and transport still limited the spread of that demand. Uruguay had an established urban workforce and social legislation, yet its small domestic population limited the size of an efficient production run for some goods. Imported machinery could become expensive for a plant serving only one small national market. Economic policy therefore reflected geography and scale as well as political ideology. The appropriate comparison is not whether one country “adopted ISI,” but which sectors grew, who financed them, who bought their products, and what external constraints remained.

Division IV · Section 75.15

Peru, Bolivia, Venezuela, and the Uneven Prospects for Industrialization

Peru had a varied export base of sugar, cotton, copper, and petroleum, much of it under foreign or large domestic ownership, with a coastal economy tied to export agriculture and mining and a highland interior of Indigenous peasant communities with little access to credit or markets. Lima developed light industry, particularly textiles, but the country's concentration of land and income, weak infrastructure, and the exclusion of much of the rural population from the market limited the domestic demand available to industry. Politics in the 1930s were marked by conflict between military governments and the APRA party, which mobilized workers and the middle class but was repeatedly suppressed. Thorp and Bertram's study shows how an open economy and foreign capital shaped Peru's growth.

Bolivia was an extreme case of concentration, with tin overwhelmingly dominant, a small domestic market, and a population largely Indigenous and rural, living in highland communities and on haciendas with little cash income. State revenue depended on the tin sector, which was controlled by a few large firms, and the state had limited capacity to invest in industry. The Chaco War drained resources and politicized soldiers and workers. The reasons that Bolivia did not follow the Brazilian or Mexican path were structural, and the revolution of 1952, treated in Chapter 78, would address many of these exclusions.

Venezuela was transformed by oil. The foreign-dominated petroleum sector generated large government revenues through royalties and taxes and made Venezuela one of the world's major exporters, but it employed relatively few workers and left agriculture neglected. The death of the long-ruling dictator Juan Vicente Gómez in December 1935 opened a period of political liberalization, and a major oil workers' strike in 1936 and 1937 revealed the growing capacity of labor to organize. A law of 1943 raised the state's share of oil income and renegotiated concessions, and later adjustments increased it further. Oil income financed imports, which could cheapen foreign manufactures and discourage local industry, while also providing resources that the state could use for infrastructure and urban growth, as Tinker Salas and others have shown. Venezuela's industrial base remained small compared with its petroleum wealth. The longer history of the oil state is the subject of Chapter 105.

The experiences of Indigenous and rural populations in these economies were distinct from those of urban industrial workers. In Peru and Bolivia, highland communities supplied labor to mines and plantations, faced land pressure, and had limited access to schools, credit, or political representation. Industrial policy, concentrated on urban markets, rarely addressed their circumstances. The contrast illustrates that the same period could bring opportunity in cities and continuity of exclusion in the countryside.

Venezuela presents a particularly useful counterexample to the simple view that depressed export economies necessarily industrialized. Petroleum exports generated foreign currency and government income, but the oil enclave used capital-intensive equipment, imported technology, and a comparatively small directly employed workforce. Those characteristics weakened the automatic link between export prosperity and a diversified domestic manufacturing sector. Bolivia faced another problem: mining profits and tax receipts were concentrated in a narrow sector, while land inequality and transport obstacles constrained the market for locally made consumer goods. In Peru, coastal exporters, Andean communities, and urban producers were linked unevenly by roads, railways, and credit; a policy advantageous to one zone did not readily transfer gains across the country. This is why the social history of land tenure, Indigenous labor, and regional bargaining belongs alongside industrial statistics. A national rise in factory production could coexist with stagnant rural income and little development in the interior. The cases also demonstrate that heavy exports and weak industrialization were not interchangeable: petroleum, tin, and diversified coastal agriculture posed distinct economic problems.

Division IV · Section 75.16

Cuba, Central America, and the Caribbean: Sugar, Bananas, Petroleum, and Continued Export Dependence

Caribbean and Central American economies generally remained more dependent on agricultural exports, foreign-owned enterprises, and a single external market than the larger South American and Mexican economies. Small domestic markets, concentrated land ownership, and the strong presence of United States capital and trade policy limited the scope for industrialization based on the home market. The differences among them were nevertheless substantial, and students should avoid treating them as a bloc.

Cuba was formally independent but tied to the United States by trade, investment, and the Platt Amendment until its abrogation in 1934. Sugar dominated the economy, with much of the industry owned by United States companies, and the 1934 reciprocal trade agreement and sugar quota system anchored Cuban exports to a protected United States market. Cuba had some light industry, but the structure of trade favored imports of United States manufactures, and rural landlessness and seasonal unemployment were severe. The 1940 constitution, a progressive document, reflected the political ferment of the decade, and labor organized strongly. Puerto Rico, a United States territory, relied on sugar and tobacco, with large landholdings and poverty, and in the 1940s a reform government under Luis Muñoz Marín created an industrial development agency that foreshadowed the later industrialization program called Operation Bootstrap, which promoted export manufacturing with tax incentives for United States firms. This was a different model from ISI, since it relied on external markets.

The Dominican Republic under Trujillo, who took power in 1930, combined dictatorship with state and personal control of important enterprises, including parts of the sugar sector, and with limited industrialization oriented around the ruler's interests. Haiti, whose military occupation by the United States ended in 1934 although United States financial oversight continued into the 1940s, remained overwhelmingly rural and poor, with exports of coffee and sisal and a small industrial base. In Central America, coffee and bananas dominated. Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica differed in landholding, labor systems, and politics: Guatemala and El Salvador combined concentrated coffee estates with repressive states, Honduras relied on banana enclaves with United Fruit and Standard Fruit, and Costa Rica had more smallholders and a more developed state. Dictatorships of the 1930s controlled labor, and the Salvadoran massacre of 1932 recalled earlier in the chapter marked the repressive limits of the period. Panama, with the Canal Zone economy, had a different structure again.

Why did these economies industrialize less? Small populations made domestic markets too limited for efficient local manufacturing, foreign-owned export enclaves left little domestic linkage, landholding concentrated land and income, and United States trade arrangements favored the import of its manufactures. Regional integration, which could have enlarged markets, was limited in this period. Industrialization nonetheless made some progress in food processing and light manufacturing, and later chapters will show how Central American reformers, notably in Guatemala, sought to change the structure of landholding.

Division V · Section 75.17

World War II and the Acceleration of Industrial Transformation, 1939–1945

The Second World War altered Latin America's economic position in two opposite ways. On one side, it disrupted the supply of manufactured goods, machinery, and shipping from Europe and the United States, which turned to war production. Latin American economies found it difficult to buy capital goods, spare parts, and consumer manufactures, and this shortage stimulated domestic production of substitutes. On the other side, the Allies needed raw materials, and demand for copper, tin, rubber, sugar, beef, cotton, and strategic minerals rose, bringing export revenue. Many countries accumulated foreign exchange reserves, often as blocked or inconvertible balances, because there was little to buy with the earnings, a pattern especially notable for Argentina's sterling balances in Britain.

Economic cooperation with the United States grew. After the 1942 meeting of foreign ministers in Rio de Janeiro, most Latin American governments broke relations with the Axis, and Mexico and Brazil entered the war, with Brazil sending an expeditionary force to Italy in 1944. Argentina remained neutral until early 1945, and its relations with Washington were strained. The United States government negotiated purchase agreements, often at fixed prices, for strategic commodities, and extended credits through the Export-Import Bank for projects such as Brazil's steel works and rubber production in the Amazon. The Good Neighbor Policy, the more cooperative approach adopted by Washington in the 1930s, provided the diplomatic framework, and Max Paul Friedman's work has shown how wartime security concerns also led to pressure on Germans and others in the region. Latin American governments bargained, sometimes successfully, over prices and conditions, as when Bolivia and others complained that fixed prices for tin and other goods undercompensated them.

Industrial output expanded in several countries. Textiles, food processing, chemicals, and metal products grew in Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Chile, and Colombia, and in some cases industry exported to neighboring markets that could not obtain goods elsewhere. Labor was mobilized, with more workers in factories and in some countries wartime regulation of wages and strikes. Women entered industrial employment in greater numbers. Mexico's Bracero agreement sent Mexican agricultural workers to the United States, with consequences for rural Mexico and for migration patterns that persisted.

The war also exposed limits. Shortages of machinery meant that existing factories ran at high utilization with aging equipment, and replacement was difficult. Inflation rose in many countries because demand outran supply and reserves grew without imports to absorb them. After the war, renewed imports and the end of strategic purchases forced adjustments. Students should distinguish short-term wartime opportunity from structural change: the war accelerated industrial growth, but it also left a legacy of equipment obsolescence and an expectation of continued protection.

Raúl Prebisch, ECLA, and the Intellectual Foundations of Developmentalism

By the late 1940s, a body of economic thought had emerged that gave industrial policy a theoretical justification. Its most influential exponent was Raúl Prebisch, an Argentine economist who had served in the 1930s as a leading official of Argentina's Central Bank, created in 1935. In that period Prebisch was a pragmatic policymaker, not yet the structuralist critic he would become. After political changes in Argentina in 1943 removed him from his post, he turned to research and international work, and in 1948 the United Nations established the Economic Commission for Latin America, known as ECLA or, in Spanish, CEPAL, headquartered in Santiago, Chile. Prebisch joined it and soon became its leading intellectual figure and, from 1950, its executive secretary, as Edgar Dosman's biography documents.

His Spanish-language report of May 14, 1949 (UN E/CN.12/89), revised for English publication on April 27, 1950 as The Economic Development of Latin America and Its Principal Problems (UN E/CN.12/89/Rev.1), presented the structuralist argument. It described the world economy as divided into an industrial center and a primary-producing periphery. Technological progress, it argued, raised productivity in the center, but the benefits were not passed on to the periphery in the form of falling prices of manufactures. Instead, the periphery faced a tendency for the prices of its primary exports to decline relative to those of manufactured imports, a claim related to the work of Hans Singer. Because primary exports could not alone generate sufficient growth and employment, the study argued that Latin American countries should diversify through industrialization, with state support, while managing the foreign exchange constraints that industrial imports created.

The argument has often been summarized as a defense of ISI, but its meaning is more nuanced. Prebisch emphasized that industrialization required planning, attention to capital goods, regional cooperation, and the improvement of agricultural productivity, and he later criticized the inefficiencies of protectionism pursued without those complements. Equally important, the ideas were formulated after the Depression and wartime industrial growth, drawing on that experience. They did not guide the policies of the early 1930s, which had been improvised. Subsequent scholars have contested the empirical basis of the declining terms-of-trade claim, noting that trends depend on the period and commodities chosen, and critics from both free-market and Marxist traditions disputed other elements. Dependency theorists in later decades radicalized the structuralist critique.

ECLA became a training ground for a generation of Latin American economists and technocrats, and its reports shaped policy debates across the region. It provided data, comparative studies, and a shared vocabulary. Students should therefore treat structuralism as both an interpretation of the past and a force in the later history of policy, and should be careful not to read its categories back into the decisions of 1931.

Achievements and Limits of Import-Substitution Industrialization

The achievements of ISI in this period were substantial. Manufacturing grew faster than the economy as a whole in most large economies, raising its share of output and employment. Domestic firms acquired technical and managerial experience, infrastructure expanded, and urban markets for consumer goods grew. A skilled industrial workforce developed, along with institutions of technical education, and states built agencies capable of planning and financing. In several countries, the economy became less vulnerable to the fluctuations of a single export, although not immune from them. For many urban families, the period brought stable wages, access to consumer goods, and, in some countries, social benefits.

The limits were also visible by the early 1950s. Industrialization depended on imported machinery, fuel, and technology, so a successful industrial sector increased demand for foreign exchange, which still came largely from traditional exports. Albert Hirschman noted that after the easy stage of consumer-goods substitution, the shift to intermediate and capital goods demanded larger scale, more capital, and more technology than small protected markets could easily support. Protection could lead to inefficiency and high costs, and concentrated ownership in some sectors reduced competition. Discrimination against exporters made it harder to earn foreign exchange. Public finances were strained, and inflation became a persistent problem in several countries, as the cost of subsidies and wartime reserves flowed through economies.

Social limits mattered as well. Industrial employment, though growing, absorbed only part of the migrant population, and the informal sector expanded. Rural poverty persisted, because industrial policy neglected agriculture or taxed it implicitly, and land inequality was left largely intact except in places of revolutionary change, as in Mexico and, later, Bolivia and Guatemala. Regional disparities widened, and the benefits of industrial growth were distributed unequally across class, race, and gender. Dependence on foreign finance and technology, including from United States firms, did not end. ISI therefore transformed economic structures without resolving the inequality and external constraints that critics emphasized.

Assessment must distinguish periods. The 1930s and 1940s, the focus of this chapter, saw largely beneficial effects in terms of growth, while the more serious costs, such as inflation, balance-of-payments crises, and industrial inefficiency, became more pronounced in later decades. Evaluating ISI therefore requires attention to the country, the sector, and the time frame, and the debate below reflects this complexity.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was ISI a Necessary Response or an Economically Costly Strategy?

The question: Was the turn toward protected, state-supported industrialization a sound and perhaps unavoidable response to the collapse of the export economy, or did it impose costs that better policies could have avoided?

Interpretation A — A necessary and productive response: Structuralist and many historical accounts emphasize that export markets and capital flows had collapsed, that industrial growth restored output and employment, and that building domestic capacity reduced vulnerability. On this view, the policies of the 1930s and 1940s were pragmatic, produced real growth, and created institutions and skills.
Interpretation B — A strategy with significant costs: Critics, including economists associated with market-oriented analysis, argue that protection and exchange discrimination penalized exports, encouraged inefficiency and monopoly, and produced inflation and balance-of-payments problems. On this view, a more outward-oriented approach would have achieved faster and more sustainable growth, as some East Asian economies later seemed to show.

Evidence: Both interpretations use growth and trade data, industrial censuses, and comparisons across countries and periods. Díaz Alejandro and Bulmer-Thomas emphasize variation and the early recovery of several economies, while Hirschman identified both the dynamics and the difficulties. Many historians now place the costs mainly in later decades and distinguish the circumstances of the 1930s from those of the 1950s and 1960s.

Why it matters: The question shapes how later economic crises and the neoliberal reforms of Volume V are understood. If ISI was largely a rational response, later liberalization looks like a reversal of failed or exhausted models. If it was a costly error, liberalization looks like a correction. The evidence suggests that both claims contain partial truth and depend on country and period.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, then identify what evidence, for example comparative growth data, firm-level productivity studies, or counterfactual analysis, would best help decide between them for one country of your choice.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Export-Led Growth to State-Directed Development

What Changed

  • States took on responsibility for allocating foreign exchange, credit, and protection, and created new banks and enterprises
  • Manufacturing and urban employment became central to economic and political life
  • Organized labor acquired legal standing and political weight
  • Economic ideas shifted from liberal export orthodoxy to developmentalism

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on primary exports for foreign exchange, and on imported capital goods and technology
  • Concentrated landholding and wide inequality of income and opportunity
  • The influence of foreign capital and of the United States in trade and finance
  • Exclusion of many rural, Indigenous, Afro-descendant, and informal workers from the main benefits

Who experienced change most directly: Urban industrial workers, industrialists, and state officials. Greater continuity: Rural and Indigenous communities and large parts of the informal urban population. Why the pattern matters: The mixture of transformation and persistence explains why developmental states were politically powerful yet vulnerable to later crises, and why debates over inequality and dependence continued.

A further methodological difficulty is separating a change in economic structure from an improvement in social welfare. The share of manufacturing in national output might rise simply because export agriculture contracted more sharply during a recession; that compositional effect differs from sustained growth in factory productivity. A rise in employment at protected factories likewise says little about the prices paid by consumers or the opportunity cost of investment that could have gone to farming, transport, or public health. Conversely, an evaluation that looks only at high protected prices may overlook the infrastructure, skills, and supplier networks created by initial investment. Distributional questions are equally important: which urban workers gained social insurance, which rural families paid higher prices for manufactured products, and which entrepreneurs received credit or import licenses? A responsible historical judgment must compare real output, productivity, trade earnings, and household welfare across comparable periods. It should also distinguish contemporary choices made under depression and wartime shortages from later policy regimes that entrenched protection for decades.

Division V · Section 75.20

From Export Dependence to Developmental States: The Transformation of Latin America

By about 1950, the relationship among states, economies, and societies in much of Latin America had changed. The state was no longer chiefly a guarantor of export conditions and debt service but an active manager of exchange, credit, protection, and in many cases production. Industry had become a central sector, cities were larger and more socially complex, and organized workers, industrialists, and military and civilian technocrats were significant political actors alongside traditional exporters. The ideas of developmentalism gave this arrangement an intellectual framework, and regional and international institutions embodied it.

A comparative synthesis must emphasize variation. In Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina, industrial transformation was deepest, linked to large markets and, in different ways, to labor incorporation. In Chile, Colombia, and Uruguay, state intervention supported smaller industrial sectors. In the oil, tin, and mineral economies, change was more limited and tied to extractive revenue. In the Caribbean and Central America, export dependence continued, often under authoritarian regimes and strong United States commercial ties. The experiences of the Depression also differed according to the structure of exports, the capacity of the state, and the balance among social groups.

Structural transformation and continuity coexisted. The transformations included industrial growth, urbanization, expanded state capacity, and new labor institutions. The continuities included inequality, the dependence on exports and foreign technology, and the exclusion of many rural and Indigenous people. Women's participation in paid work and politics grew, though with unequal wages and constraints. Race and region continued to shape opportunity. Neither a simple narrative of modernization nor one of dependency alone accounts for this mixture.

The chapters that follow examine how these changes translated into politics. Chapter 76 follows Brazil from the Revolution of 1930 through the Estado Novo, where industrialization, corporatist labor institutions, and nationalism were joined under Getúlio Vargas. Chapter 77 turns to Argentina, where Juan and Eva Perón built a mass movement on organized labor and social policy. Chapter 78 considers Bolivia, where the exclusions of a tin-dependent economy produced the revolution of 1952, and Chapter 79 examines Guatemala's reform government and the 1954 coup that ended it. Each of these cases is unintelligible without the economic and social background established here, and each shows that the developmental state could take democratic, revolutionary, corporatist, and authoritarian forms.

The transformation did not move all governments along a single political path. A development bank might assist private enterprise under an elected civilian government, while a steel corporation might be supervised by officers under a dictatorship. A national labor law could extend enforceable rights while giving officials discretion over which union counted as the legitimate bargaining representative. Similarly, recovery from an export-price collapse did not by itself establish a long-term strategy of economic diversification. By the early 1950s, many policymakers were arguing more explicitly for industrialization as an enduring objective, while rural inequality and shortages of external finance remained unresolved. Those tensions furnish the comparative questions for the next four chapters: who organized mass support, which institutions managed conflict, who controlled the benefits of development, and when demands for reform collided with coercive power.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Latin America from the export-led economies of the 1920s to the early developmental states of the mid-twentieth century. It has argued that the Great Depression exposed vulnerabilities, that crisis accelerated state intervention, that industrialization expanded through a combination of earlier foundations, policy, and war, and that these changes did not eliminate dependence or inequality.

On the eve of the Depression, Latin American economies relied on a few primary exports, including coffee, sugar, copper, nitrates, tin, beef, grain, bananas, and petroleum. They depended on foreign capital and on customs revenue, and they were integrated into global markets through ports and railways. The degree of concentration varied, with Cuba, Bolivia, and Chile exposed most severely and Argentina more diversified.

The crisis reached the region through falling commodity prices, collapsing trade, and the end of foreign lending, and the timing and severity differed by country. Export earnings fell by roughly half across the region, with much steeper declines in Chile and Cuba. Brazil used coffee purchase and destruction to protect planters, Cuba restricted sugar under international and domestic agreements, and Bolivia and Chile endured mining collapse. Governments suspended debt service, devalued, and imposed exchange controls, mostly as emergency measures.

The human consequences were severe and unevenly distributed. Miners, plantation workers, smallholders, urban households, and Indigenous communities lost income, migrated, and relied on informal survival strategies, while public relief was limited. In El Salvador, the crisis contributed to a rebellion and a massacre of Indigenous and peasant people in 1932. Aggregate statistics conceal these experiences and the differences by class, gender, and region.

Political systems were destabilized across the region. Coups, revolts, and regime changes occurred between 1930 and 1933, and the 1930s saw reformist, corporatist, and authoritarian governments. States expanded economic intervention in many forms, though not always democratically or redistributively. Organized labor grew, and states and parties sought to incorporate unions, producing different relationships between workers and governments. Industrial workers, though a minority, became politically significant.

Urbanization, migration, and the entry of women into paid work and politics reshaped societies. Cities grew with migrants, informal settlements, and new middle classes, while race and region affected access to industrial employment and political benefits. Suffrage for women advanced at different times in different countries.

Import-substitution industrialization emerged gradually. Spontaneous substitution, caused by currency depreciation and import shortages, preceded deliberate policy, and earlier industrial foundations meant that the Depression accelerated rather than created manufacturing. Tariffs, licensing, exchange policy, development banks, and public enterprises such as CORFO, Nacional Financiera, and Brazil's steel company gave policy a more systematic form, while heavy industry required more capital and technology than light consumer-goods industry.

Regional paths diverged. Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina built the largest industrial sectors on large markets and different political foundations. Chile, Colombia, and Uruguay used state intervention in smaller economies. Peru, Bolivia, and Venezuela, shaped by mining and oil and by unequal landholding, had more limited industrial prospects, and Caribbean and Central American economies remained largely agricultural exporters tied to the United States, with Puerto Rico and Cuba following distinct trajectories.

World War II disrupted imports and raised demand for strategic exports, accelerating industrial growth and building foreign-exchange reserves but also exposing capital-goods constraints and inflation. The war brought increased cooperation with the United States, which was an important actor without being the central protagonist. In 1948 ECLA was established, and Prebisch's 1949 study gave the structuralist interpretation, which justified industrialization but followed rather than preceded early practice.

The achievements of ISI included growth, infrastructure, skills, and institutions, and its limits included continuing dependence on foreign exchange and imported technology, inefficiency, inflation, and persistent inequality. The historical debate over its necessity and cost remains open and depends on country and period. The chapter concludes that the developmental state was a genuine transformation that coexisted with deep continuities, and that it provided the setting for the political histories of Brazil, Argentina, Bolivia, and Guatemala examined in Chapters 76 through 79. Students should also take away a method: statistics and policy documents are indispensable evidence, but they reflect the perspectives of those who produced them and say little about many people's lives.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Export-led growth
An economic pattern in which expansion depends on selling primary commodities abroad and importing manufactures and capital.
Primary commodity
A raw or minimally processed product such as coffee, sugar, tin, copper, or petroleum.
Terms of trade
The ratio of export prices to import prices; a decline means exports buy fewer imports.
Balance of payments
The record of a country's economic transactions with the world, including trade, loans, and investment income.
Sovereign default
A government's suspension or reduction of payments on its foreign debt, widespread in Latin America in the early 1930s.
Devaluation
A reduction in the external value of a currency, raising the domestic price of imports.
Exchange controls
Rules rationing foreign currency and setting rates, introduced as emergency measures and later used for industrial policy.
Multiple exchange rates
A system giving different rates to different transactions, used to favor or penalize particular imports and exports.
Bilateral trade
Trade arranged between two countries, often balancing exchange without hard currency, as in German compensation deals.
Tariff
A tax on imports that raises revenue and protects domestic producers.
Import licensing
Government permission required to import specified goods, used to limit quantities and direct foreign exchange.
Import-substitution industrialization (ISI)
Expansion of domestic manufacturing to replace imports, and a development strategy promoting it.
Spontaneous substitution
Domestic production that expands because of shortages or price changes rather than deliberate policy.
Infant-industry argument
The claim that new industries need temporary protection until they can compete.
Capital goods
Machinery and equipment used to produce other goods, difficult to substitute because of cost and technology.
Development bank
A state-backed financial institution that lends to industry and infrastructure, such as Nacional Financiera.
CORFO
Chile's Production Development Corporation, founded in 1939 to plan and finance national production.
Public enterprise
A firm owned or controlled by the state, as in steel, electricity, or petroleum.
Corporatism
A system tying organized interest groups, especially unions, to the state through official recognition and supervision.
Union incorporation
The process by which states or parties brought labor organizations into formal political life, whether led by the state or by parties.
Informal economy
Economic activity outside official regulation and statistics, including street selling and unregistered work.
Structuralism
An economic approach stressing unequal international economic structures, associated with Prebisch and ECLA.
Center–periphery
The structuralist model dividing the world economy into industrial centers and primary-producing peripheries.
ECLA (CEPAL)
The United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America, established in 1948 and based in Santiago.
Developmentalism
The set of ideas and policies favoring state-led industrial diversification as the path to economic development.
Good Neighbor Policy
The United States approach of the 1930s emphasizing diplomacy and nonintervention in the hemisphere, with continuing economic influence.
La Matanza
The 1932 military killing of Indigenous and peasant people in western El Salvador following a rebellion.
Infamous Decade
The Argentine name for the conservative, electorally manipulated period beginning with the 1930 coup.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Great Depression was a turning point in how Latin American states, economies, and societies were organized, but not a clean break. Industry existed before 1929, many policies began as improvisation, and the theory of developmentalism came after practice. What changed was the expectation that governments would manage exchange, credit, and production, and that cities, factories, and organized workers would shape national politics.

The chapter also shows how unevenly the transformation was distributed. Industrial workers and entrepreneurs in a few metropolitan regions gained the most visible benefits, while Indigenous highland communities, Afro-descendant workers, rural laborers, and many women in the informal economy experienced more continuity than change. The violence in El Salvador in 1932 and the exclusions of the tin economy in Bolivia remind students that economic crisis could produce repression and revolt as well as reform.

The developmental state created in these decades would shape the rest of the twentieth century. It formed the setting for Vargas's Brazil, Perón's Argentina, Bolivia's 1952 revolution, and Guatemala's reform, and it left questions about inequality, dependence, and inflation that later crises, including the debt crisis of Volume V, would revive. Chapter 76 begins with Brazil, where the Revolution of 1930 and the Estado Novo joined industrialization, nationalism, and a new relationship between the state and workers.
