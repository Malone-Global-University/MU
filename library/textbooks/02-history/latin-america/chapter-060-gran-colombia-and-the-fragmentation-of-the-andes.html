Gran Colombia and the Fragmentation of the Andes

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Republic That Outlived Its Constitution by Months

In January 1830, Simón Bolívar, the man whose name was attached to the Republic of Colombia, told its Congress that he wished to leave office. By the end of that year Venezuela and Ecuador had declared themselves separate states, the general who had won the battle of Pichincha had been murdered on a mountain road, and Bolívar was dead. The republic that had stretched from the Orinoco delta to the Gulf of Guayaquil, and from the Caribbean coast to the Amazonian frontier, was in the process of becoming three states with no agreed rules for dividing its debts, its borders, or its memory.

Historians call that republic Gran Colombia, a label coined later to distinguish it from the modern Republic of Colombia, which descends from its New Granadan core. Its contemporaries simply called it Colombia. It existed as a legal entity from the Fundamental Law of December 1819, was given a constitution at Cúcuta in 1821, absorbed Quito and Guayaquil in 1822, and fragmented in 1829 to 1831. Within those dozen years it fought wars, borrowed in London, hosted a diplomatic congress at Panama, passed a free-womb law, and governed perhaps two and a half to three million people across some of the most difficult terrain in the Americas.

This chapter has two central questions. Why did a union created by a victorious revolutionary leadership fail to become a durable state? And why did the republics that emerged from its wreckage, together with Peru and Bolivia, follow such different political paths? The answers cannot be reduced to Bolívar's ambition, Santander's legalism, or Páez's regional pride. They lie in distances and fiscal burdens, in competing definitions of sovereignty, in the military patronage created by war, in commercial interests that pulled coasts and highlands in different directions, and in rival ideas about what a republic was for.

An essential distinction organizes the chapter. Peru and Bolivia were never constituent parts of Gran Colombia. They belong here because Bolívar, Sucre, and Santa Cruz moved between their politics and Colombia's, because the Colombian-Peruvian war of 1828 and 1829 was a direct consequence of unfinished independence, and because their rival constitutional experiments, culminating in the Peru-Bolivian Confederation of 1836 to 1839, were part of the same Andean struggle over how large a state could be and who should govern it. Chapter 53 told how the armies of Bolívar won independence in the north, and Chapter 55 told how the Andean war ended; this chapter studies the political order that victory produced and could not hold.

The chapter also insists on social history. Constitutional texts declared citizens equal before the law, yet enslaved people in the Cauca valley and on the Caracas coast, Indigenous tribute-payers in the Ecuadorian sierra and the Bolivian altiplano, women denied the vote, and Afro-descended soldiers like Admiral José Prudencio Padilla experienced the republic very differently from the lawyers who drafted its charters. Rural people supplied recruits and taxes, filed petitions, defended communal lands, and sometimes rose in arms. Their choices, not only those of presidents and generals, shaped how far republican institutions could reach.

The evidence is rich but partisan. Constitutions, congressional journals, and proclamations survive in published collections; Bolívar's voluminous correspondence has been edited many times; memoirs by participants such as José Manuel Restrepo, Daniel O'Leary, and José Antonio Páez are valuable and self-serving. Contemporary newspapers reflect the factions that sponsored them. Nationalist historiography in each successor state later reread the dissolution to serve its own founding story. Students should treat every account of 1826 to 1830 as an argument. This chapter follows the outline's boundaries: it begins with the Colombian union in 1819, studies five republics to roughly 1850, and leaves the War of the Pacific to Chapter 65 and Panama's 1903 separation to Chapter 70.

Learning Objectives

  3. Analyze how distance, public finance, foreign debt, and administrative geography strained the union between 1821 and 1826.

  8. Compare Peru and Bolivia, and interpret the Peru-Bolivian Confederation as a rival Andean union.

Historical Context

Chapter 48 explained how Bourbon reforms strained the Spanish Atlantic empire, Chapters 51 through 55 traced the collapse of imperial legitimacy and the wars that followed, and Chapter 58 introduced the institutional problems facing new republics: sovereignty, citizenship, regionalism, the Church, the army, and fiscal weakness. This chapter applies those general problems to the Andean and Caribbean-facing regions of northern South America. Before 1810 these territories were administered separately: the Captaincy General of Venezuela, the Viceroyalty of New Granada with its capital at Bogotá, and the Audiencia of Quito, itself attached to New Granada after 1739. Each had its own commerce, elites, and Indigenous and African-descended populations.

Independence itself was fought region by region. Venezuelan juntas of 1810 and the first republics of 1811 to 1814 collapsed amid war, earthquake, and social conflict. New Granada's provinces formed rival juntas, and the quarrel between centralist Cundinamarca and federalist provinces in 1812 to 1816, remembered as the patria boba, allowed Spanish reconquest. Quito's 1809 junta was suppressed, and Pasto stayed fiercely royalist. By the time Bolívar proposed union in 1819, the territory had experienced a decade of requisition, conscription, and massacre. Venezuela had lost a considerable share of its population, and military leaders held authority that civilians had not been able to establish.

Colonial institutions remained in place, often modified only in name. Indigenous tribute, the alcabala sales tax, the tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, the tithe, ecclesiastical and military fueros, and slavery all survived into the republic. Cabildos, which had provided local self-government, were reshaped into municipalities. Law books remained largely Castilian. The new state needed revenue from a society impoverished by war and still organized around mining, haciendas, plantations, cattle ranching, small farming, and Indigenous village agriculture. The Atlantic economy, now open to British and North American traders, offered opportunity and competition.

Peru and Upper Peru were different cases. Lima had been the viceregal capital and royalist stronghold; its independence came through the intervention of San Martín, then Bolívar and Sucre, and the final victories at Junín and Ayacucho in 1824 (Chapter 55). Bolivia was created in 1825 from Upper Peru. These countries had larger Indigenous majorities, heavier dependence on tribute and silver, and strong local identities in Lima, Cusco, Arequipa, Chuquisaca, La Paz, and Potosí. Chapter 59 showed Mexico's parallel struggle between federalism and centralism; students should keep it in mind as a comparison, since this chapter does not retell Mexican history. For Brazil and the Southern Cone, see Chapters 61 and 62.

Section 60.1

The Vision of an Andean Republic

Bolívar's argument for union predates 1819. In the Jamaica Letter of 1815 he had imagined the former Spanish empire dividing into several states, and he doubted that Venezuela and New Granada, with their differing populations and histories, could easily form one. By 1819 the strategic calculation had changed. Venezuela's war economy had been wrecked, its eastern plains and Orinoco were the base of patriot forces, and its manpower was exhausted. New Granada's highlands held population, taxable resources, and a defensible interior. Quito, still royalist, controlled the approaches to Peru. A single government could move soldiers, supplies, and credit among these zones in a way that three weak juntas could not.

Union also carried diplomatic weight. A large republic might negotiate with Britain and the United States as a serious power, command respect from Spain, and discourage reconquest. Bolívar and his advisers, including Francisco Antonio Zea and Santander, saw size as protection. The name Colombia, popularized by Francisco de Miranda decades earlier as a term for the whole of Spanish America, gave the project a continental resonance that "Venezuela" or "New Granada" lacked.

Yet aspiration was not sovereignty. In 1819 the union existed on paper while Spanish armies still held Bogotá, Caracas, and Quito. Provincial elites had spent a decade governing themselves through juntas and congresses and had learned to defend local prerogatives. Many New Granadans remembered that the centralist-federalist quarrel of the patria boba had made reconquest possible, and many Venezuelans associated Bogotá with a distant Andean bureaucracy. The union would need to be built through institutions and revenue as well as through victories, and the evidence of its first years shows how thin that foundation was.

Section 60.2

Angostura, the Fundamental Law of 1819, and the Birth of Colombia

The Congress of Angostura, the Orinoco river town now called Ciudad Bolívar, opened on 15 February 1819 with delegates from the parts of Venezuela under patriot control. Bolívar's address urged a strong, stable executive and a hereditary senate drawn from the military heroes of the revolution. He also proposed a fourth branch of government, the Moral Power, to supervise public education and civic virtue. The Congress adopted a Venezuelan constitution that August, but it did not adopt his most unusual proposals, and the Moral Power was never implemented.

After the victory at Boyacá in August 1819 opened New Granada to the patriots, Bolívar returned to Angostura and persuaded the Congress to proclaim the union. The Fundamental Law of 17 December 1819 created the Republic of Colombia from three departments: Venezuela, Cundinamarca (New Granada), and Quito. Each was to have a vice-president, and the whole was to be governed by a president, with Bolívar as the first. Zea became vice-president of the republic, and Santander was installed as vice-president of Cundinamarca. The law promised a general congress at Cúcuta to write a constitution once enough territory was liberated.

The Fundamental Law was a provisional settlement made by one region's congress on behalf of three. New Granadans did not elect the Angostura delegates, and Bolívar and Santander would later disagree about what the document had promised. Bolívar treated it as a wartime union under military leadership. Santander and the lawyers around him saw it as a first step toward a constitutional republic governed by law.

Section 60.3

From Boyacá and Carabobo to the Territorial Union

The campaigns of 1819 to 1821 are the subject of Chapter 53, and this section needs only their political results. Boyacá on 7 August 1819 opened Bogotá to the patriots and gave Santander the capital; Carabobo on 24 June 1821 destroyed the main royalist army in Venezuela and led to the occupation of Caracas. Between them, the armistice negotiated at Trujillo in November 1820 and the "regularization of the war" decrees reduced the savagery of fighting and created a diplomatic precedent for Colombia as a belligerent state.

Military success made the union practically possible. Territories that had been separate under Spain now shared a single army, a single treasury struggling to fund it, and a single diplomatic voice. It also made union seem necessary: a general war required unity of command, and civilian leaders who might have objected to a strong executive found it hard to argue against centralization while royalist garrisons remained at Puerto Cabello, Pasto, and Quito. Cartagena, besieged and defended by the royalists, fell to patriot forces under Admiral José Prudencio Padilla in October 1821.

The war also produced a particular distribution of power. Venezuelan officers, including Páez, Mariño, Bermúdez, and Soublette, had won their rank on the battlefield and expected to exercise authority in the territories they had liberated. New Granadan civilians, notably Santander, had built administrative structures behind the lines. The union therefore joined two kinds of legitimacy, military and legal, that would clash repeatedly. Spanish garrisons held out on the coast until Puerto Cabello fell in 1823, and war with Spain continued formally for years afterward.

Section 60.4

Quito, Guayaquil, and the Incorporation of the Southern District

Quito's incorporation followed Sucre's victory at Pichincha on 24 May 1822. The audiencia's elite, weary of a decade of royalist and patriot occupation, rapidly petitioned to join Colombia, and Quito's formal acceptance gave the republic its southern department. The southern district was difficult to govern: its Indigenous majority paid tribute and provided labor in textile workshops and on haciendas, its highland elites depended on Spanish-era institutions, and the war had left it under military occupation.

Guayaquil presented a harder problem. The port had declared independence on 9 October 1820 under a junta led by the poet-statesman José Joaquín de Olmedo and was, for nearly two years, an autonomous province. Its merchants, whose wealth rested on cacao exports and shipbuilding, wanted free trade and local control. Factions favored independence, union with Colombia, or union with Peru, which San Martín's government also claimed. Bolívar arrived in July 1822, and on 11 to 13 July the city's junta, under the pressure of his troops and political maneuvering, declared Guayaquil under Colombia's protection. The meeting between Bolívar and San Martín on 26 and 27 July, whose content is not fully documented, was followed by San Martín's withdrawal from Peru.

The incorporation of the south shows that Gran Colombia was assembled from regions with competing options. Guayaquil would later be a center of opposition to Bogotá and to Flores, Quito's highland elites would seek protection for textile producers, and Cuenca would feel marginal to both. Their later separations in 1830 were not simply revolts against Bolívar; they were attempts by regions that had never fully chosen the union to define their own terms. Peruvian claims to Guayaquil, Jaén, and Maynas remained alive and would produce the war of 1828 and 1829.

Section 60.5

Panama, the Caribbean, and the Geography of the Republic

Panama joined Colombia without battle. The town of Los Santos declared independence from Spain on 10 November 1821, and Panama City followed on 28 November, deciding to unite with Colombia. The isthmus mattered because its overland routes between Chagres and Panama City connected the Atlantic and Pacific, and its merchants depended on trade moving across them. Its residents joined partly to find protection and partly because no other option seemed viable; Panama's later attempts at autonomy, including brief separations in 1830 and 1840 to 1841, belong to New Granada's history in Division VI.

The republic's geography imposed severe constraints. The Caribbean ports of Cartagena, Santa Marta, Maracaibo, Puerto Cabello, and La Guaira linked it to Atlantic trade. The Magdalena River, the main artery of New Granada, carried goods from the coast to Honda, from which mule trains climbed to Bogotá. The Andes split into three ranges, and the Orinoco plains and Amazonian forests lay beyond. Bogotá was chosen as capital because of its central location, institutions, and wealth, but it was far from both Caracas and Quito, and communications took weeks.

Population estimates for the whole republic in the 1820s range around two and a half to three million, with Venezuela having something under a million after the war and New Granada the largest portion. These figures are approximate and rest on colonial censuses adjusted for war losses. They matter because they show that a state with Spain's institutions had to govern a population scattered across mountain valleys, river corridors, plains, and coasts, with limited roads and a small administrative class.

Section 60.6

The Constitution of Cúcuta and Centralized Republican Government

The Congress of Cúcuta, which met at Villa del Rosario from May 1821, adopted a constitution on 30 August 1821. It established a unitary republic, popular and representative, with a president and vice-president serving four-year terms, a bicameral Congress, and a judiciary headed by a High Court. Three departments, Venezuela, Cundinamarca, and Quito, were subdivided into provinces. Bolívar was elected president and Santander vice-president. The assembly also reaffirmed the abolition of the Inquisition, imposed a property qualification on voting, and set a literacy requirement to take effect later.

It is important to describe the constitution accurately. Although the outline refers to Bolívar's federation, Gran Colombia's constitution created a centralized republic, not a federal system. Provincial governors and department intendants were appointed by the executive, and the nation's laws were uniform. Federalism, the arrangement favored by many provincial leaders and, later, by Santander's allies at Ocaña, was a competing demand, not the system in place. The distinction matters because many critics of centralism blamed Bogotá for decisions that the constitution itself had concentrated there.

Two provisions had long consequences. Article 128 allowed the president extraordinary powers in zones of military operations, a clause that Bolívar's allies later invoked broadly. Article 191 provided that the constitution could not be reformed for ten years, after which a convention could be called, a clause that made the convention called at Ocaña in 1828 legally controversial. Elections were indirect, passing through parish assemblies and electoral colleges, which gave local notables power. The constitution therefore combined a strong executive with local electoral mediation, and that combination shaped much of what followed.

Historical Vocabulary
Centralism and Federalism

Centralism is a form of government in which authority is concentrated in a national capital, with provincial officials appointed by and answerable to the central executive. Federalism divides sovereignty between a national government and constituent states or provinces with their own elected officials and powers.

In Spanish America after independence these were not abstract theories. Centralists argued that a weak, war-damaged state needed unity of command and uniform law, while federalists argued that distant provinces were best governed by their own residents. Cúcuta's constitution was centralist, but the federalist demand grew as provincial elites resented Bogotá's decisions. Students should not equate federalism with liberalism or centralism with conservatism: both were used by competing factions, and their meanings shifted over time.

Section 60.7

Bolívar, Santander, and the Division of Executive Authority

From the first, executive authority was divided in practice. Bolívar was the republic's president but spent most of the years from late 1821 onward on campaigns in the south, then in Peru from 1823 and at Lima through 1826. Santander, as vice-president, exercised the executive in Bogotá, administering the treasury, appointing officials, and negotiating with Congress. The result was a government in which the constitutional head was absent for most of his term and the acting head was a lawyer who relied on law and procedure.

The arrangement worked for a time because both men needed each other. Bolívar needed Santander's administrative skill to raise money and men; Santander needed Bolívar's prestige to hold together a union that many Venezuelan and Quiteño leaders accepted because of him. Santander's nickname, "the man of laws," captures his priorities, including public education, fiscal order, and constitutional procedure, and his governing style differed from that of the military leader who defined Colombia's identity.

Tension was predictable. Bolívar and his Venezuelan lieutenants saw the Peru campaign as the completion of independence and resented what they regarded as insufficient support from Bogotá. Santander and the New Granadan civilian elite worried that the republic was being drained to finance wars of liberation abroad and that the army's prestige would eventually override civil authority. Each man also had regional constituencies that distrusted the other. By 1826 the rivalry had become a political fault line, with Bolivarians and Santanderistas competing for control of Congress, the press, and the army.

Section 60.8

Departments, Provinces, Municipalities, and Administrative Integration

The Law of Territorial Division of 25 June 1824 reorganized the republic into twelve departments: Orinoco, Venezuela, Apure, and Zulia in the Venezuelan region; Boyacá, Cundinamarca, Cauca, Magdalena, and Istmo in New Granada; and Ecuador, Guayaquil, and Azuay in the south. Each was headed by an intendant appointed by the executive, with provinces under governors, cantons under political chiefs, and parishes at the base. The purpose was to make the republic governable by bringing uniform administration to a patchwork of colonial jurisdictions.

The system tried to substitute appointed officials for the colonial cabildos' older self-governing role. City councils survived as municipalities, but their authority was narrower and their members were chosen by an indirect process. Appointments rested on the executive and on Santander's patronage, which Venezuelan and southern leaders sometimes viewed as favoring New Granadans. The administrative class was small, and many officials were military officers or lawyers moved from other regions. The law's uniformity assumed an administrative capacity that did not exist.

The result was a state whose institutional reach exceeded its practical capacity. Communications between Bogotá and distant intendants were slow, and Venezuelan and southern military commanders often exercised civil authority themselves. Local officials collected taxes, recruited soldiers, and kept the peace in ways that varied from province to province. The constitution gave Bogotá formal control, but effective control depended on negotiation with regional notables and officers, a pattern that shaped how La Cosiata unfolded in 1826.

Section 60.9

Public Finance, Foreign Loans, Diplomacy, and the Panama Congress

The republic's revenues came from customs duties, the tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, the alcabala sales tax, the tithe, and Indigenous tribute and its successors. War had destroyed trade and many revenue sources, and most income went to the army. To finance the republic, Colombian agents in London arranged two loans: one in 1822 with a nominal value of two million pounds, and one in 1824 with a nominal value of 4.75 million, both sold at substantial discounts, so that Colombia received much less in cash than it owed. When the London capital market collapsed in 1825 and 1826, Colombia defaulted, leaving a debt that would shape relations among its successor states.

Diplomacy followed. The United States recognized Colombia in 1822, and Britain in 1825, after Canning's government decided that commerce with Spanish America could not be ignored. Zea, Revenga, and other envoys sought recognition and credit in European capitals. Bolívar invited the American republics to a congress at Panama, which opened on 22 June 1826 with delegates from Colombia, Central America, Mexico, and Peru, with British and Dutch observers. The United States appointed delegates, but one died en route and the other arrived too late.

On 15 July 1826 the delegates signed a treaty of union, league, and perpetual confederation, but only Colombia ratified it. The congress moved to Tacubaya in Mexico and never reconvened effectively. It nonetheless shows Colombia's ambition: a leading South American republic proposing a collective defense system and a framework for settling disputes. It also shows the limits, since Colombia's debt, its internal divisions, and its financial dependence on London left it unable to impose such a system. The congress met while La Cosiata was already unsettling Venezuela.

Section 60.10

Citizenship, Slavery, Indigenous Communities, and Social Hierarchy

The Congress of Cúcuta passed a free-womb law on 21 July 1821. Children born to enslaved mothers would be free, but they would owe labor to their mothers' enslavers until adulthood, typically until age eighteen. Manumission juntas, funded partly by inheritance taxes, were to free adult enslaved people gradually. The law reflected the influence of Bolívar, who had promised abolition to Haitian president Pétion in 1816, and of elite fears of both continued slavery and sudden abolition. The republic's enslaved population, perhaps on the order of a hundred thousand with considerable uncertainty, was concentrated in the Cauca valley and Chocó gold fields and on the cacao and sugar estates of the Venezuelan coast.

In practice the manumission juntas freed relatively few, and the labor obligation of the freed-womb children kept many in dependence. Enslaved people used the courts, petitioned authorities, ran away, and in some cases enlisted in the armies, where service offered a path to freedom. Free people of color, known as pardos, had also served in the armies; Admiral Padilla was the most prominent. Leaders like Bolívar feared what contemporaries called pardocracia, a social and political rise of the Afro-descended majority, and tried both to reward service and to contain it.

Indigenous communities faced a different set of burdens. Cúcuta's legislators abolished the colonial tribute and ordered the division of communal resguardos into private plots, an effort inspired by liberal ideas of equality, but replacement taxes and land pressures soon reappeared. Citizenship was limited to free adult men who met property and, later, literacy requirements; women could not vote or hold office, though Castilian law gave them rights to property and inheritance. The constitution declared equality before the law, but the distance between that text and the lives of enslaved people, Indigenous villagers, and women was vast.

Section 60.11

The Structural Sources of Regional Opposition

Opposition to Bogotá grew from material grievances before it acquired a constitutional vocabulary. Venezuela had been devastated by war and emerged with damaged estates, depleted herds, and merchants who depended on Atlantic trade. Venezuelan commercial and landowning families resented customs rules and monopolies set in Bogotá, and Venezuelan officers complained that New Granadan civilians controlled the treasury and law courts. Military conscription fell heavily on rural men for the Peruvian campaigns, and local elites, who often had to supply recruits and provisions, saw little compensation.

The southern departments had parallel complaints. Guayaquil's merchants favored free trade and disliked Colombian tariff decisions; Quito's textile producers wanted protection against imported cloth; and Cuenca's elites felt marginalized by both. The tithe, tribute, alcabala, and monopolies bore heavily on rural producers across the republic. Provincial elites also resented that the executive, under Santander, appointed intendants and governors who were often outsiders. The union's fiscal burdens, including the share of customs revenue spent on the army and on servicing London loans, fell unevenly and generated arguments about who benefited.

These tensions were structural but not inevitable causes of dissolution. Regional grievances can be accommodated through bargaining, fiscal adjustment, or federal arrangements, and many of the same grievances persisted in the successor states. What made them politically dangerous was their combination with military authority and constitutional uncertainty. Venezuelan generals like Páez possessed armed followers and local prestige, and the absence of Bolívar, the one figure acceptable to most factions, removed the arbiter who might have negotiated between them.

Section 60.12

La Cosiata and the Venezuelan Challenge of 1826

La Cosiata, a Venezuelan colloquialism for "the thing," began with a dispute over military recruitment. In late 1825 Páez, then the military commander in Venezuela, ordered the enrollment of men from sixteen to fifty in the militia, including members of prominent families in Caracas. The Caracas municipality complained, and the Colombian Chamber of Deputies, acting on a Caracas petition, accused Páez of arbitrary conduct. In April 1826, the Senate in Bogotá summoned him to appear and answer, and the executive suspended him.

Páez refused to obey. Around 30 April the municipalities of Valencia and Caracas supported him, and Páez assumed civil and military command in Venezuela. Some of his supporters proposed that Venezuela reorganize itself as a federation or adopt a new constitution, while others, including Bolívar's allies, called for Bolívar's return as a dictator who could settle the dispute. The movement's aims were ambiguous. Páez presented himself as defending local rights against Bogotá's overreach, but many of his followers also resented Santander's authority.

La Cosiata's significance is political and constitutional. It showed that a regional commander with popular support could defy the national government and be backed by municipal governments, and that the union's institutions could not discipline him. Santander's decision to prosecute Páez reflected his commitment to civilian authority, but it also helped trigger the crisis. The affair ended with Bolívar's negotiated settlement in January 1827, which left Páez in command and undermined Santander's authority. Historians still debate whether Cosiata was separatist in intent or a dispute over authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Caudillismo

A caudillo was a leader whose authority rested on personal loyalty, regional prestige, and the ability to command armed followers rather than primarily on formal office. Caudillismo names the political pattern in which such leaders dominated early republican politics.

Páez, with his llanero cavalry and patronage networks, is a classic example, and Flores, Santa Cruz, and Gamarra shared aspects of the type. The term can mislead if it implies mere lawlessness: many caudillos used constitutions, elections, and legal forms, and they provided order and protection that weak states could not. The relationship was reciprocal, with followers expecting land, offices, and protection in return for loyalty. Caudillismo was a product of war, weak institutions, and regional fragmentation, not of a particular national character.

Section 60.13

Bolívar's Return and the Failure of Political Reconciliation, 1826–1827

Bolívar left Lima in September 1826 and reached Bogotá in mid-November. There he faced a divided government and a crisis in Venezuela. His priority was to restore unity without a civil war, and he chose negotiation. He moved to Venezuela, reaching Puerto Cabello on the last day of 1826. On 1 January 1827 he issued an amnesty, confirmed Páez as Superior Chief of Venezuela, and accepted a delay in the constitutional reforms that Páez's allies had demanded. The arrangement avoided bloodshed, but it left Páez's command intact and established that Venezuelan officers could defy Bogotá with impunity.

Bolívar's relations with Santander worsened. Santander had been at the center of the effort to prosecute Páez, and the two men now disagreed about how to respond to the crisis and about what constitution Colombia should have. Bolívar had written a constitution for Bolivia in 1826, with a lifetime president and a tricameral legislature, and some of his supporters in Guayaquil, Quito, and Venezuela proposed adopting it in Colombia. Santander and his allies, who feared a monarchical drift, objected. Bolívar offered to resign the presidency in 1827, and Congress refused.

By September 1827 Bolívar was back in Bogotá, resuming the presidency, and the republic was split into rival camps. Bolivarians stressed order, a strong executive, and the army's role; Santanderistas stressed law, civilian government, and limits on executive power. Both factions supported a convention to reform the constitution, which Congress scheduled for Ocaña in 1828. The decision to hold a convention early, before the ten years specified in Cúcuta, reflected the sense that the existing arrangement could not hold, and it began a process neither side could control.

Section 60.14

The Convention of Ocaña and the Constitutional Breakdown of 1828

The Convention of Ocaña opened on 9 April 1828 in a town in the northeast of New Granada, with delegates from across the republic. The Santanderistas held a majority. They favored a constitution that limited the president's powers, strengthened Congress, and gave provinces greater autonomy; some advocated an explicit federal system. Bolivarian delegates, including José Rafael Revenga and Vicente Azuero's opponents, argued for a stronger executive, longer terms, and the preservation of a unified state. Neither side was willing to accept the other's central demands.

The convention's central problem was whether it could reach a compromise. Debate focused on executive power, the structure of the provinces, and what to do about Bolívar, whose authority all sides had to confront. By June it was clear that the Santanderista majority would adopt a constitution unacceptable to Bolívar. Around 10 and 11 June the Bolivarian delegates withdrew, leaving the convention without a quorum, and it dissolved without completing a constitution.

The breakdown was a constitutional crisis, not just a political one. The convention had been called under questionable legal authority, and its collapse removed the one mechanism that might have produced a negotiated settlement. In the following weeks, assemblies in Bogotá and other cities petitioned Bolívar to assume supreme power. Whether these petitions were spontaneous, orchestrated, or both remains debated. Ocaña should be read together with La Cosiata: both were attempts to resolve through legal procedure the same dispute over centralism, and both failed in ways that strengthened military authority.

Section 60.15

Bolívar's Dictatorship, the September Conspiracy, and Political Repression

On 27 August 1828 Bolívar issued an Organic Decree assuming supreme power. It abolished the vice-presidency, concentrating authority in his hands and a council of ministers, and promised a new congress. Bolívar and his supporters justified the measure as necessary to prevent anarchy and civil war. The decree removed Santander from office and placed the legislature under suspicion. Bolívar also restored some colonial institutions, including Indigenous tribute and support for convents, in an effort to build conservative support.

On the night of 25 September 1828 a group of conspirators attacked the presidential palace in Bogotá. Bolívar escaped, helped by Manuela Sáenz, a Quito-born woman who had become his companion and managed his papers; she is credited by many accounts with confronting the attackers and urging him to flee through a window. Her role is documented in later testimonies, and she later received the title "Liberator of the Liberator," though the details vary between sources. The government blamed Santander's circle, and the vice-president was arrested and sentenced to death, a sentence commuted to exile. Admiral Padilla, the Afro-Colombian hero of Cartagena, was executed in October 1828 for his alleged involvement.

Contemporary interpretations differed sharply. Bolívar's defenders held that the dictatorship was a temporary emergency measure to save the republic, and the conspiracy proved the danger. His critics, among them Santanderistas and republicans in Venezuela and Quito, saw tyranny and a betrayal of the republican principles for which they had fought. The repression polarized opinion further. Padilla's execution, in particular, raised questions about race and the treatment of Afro-descended officers, and Santander's exile removed the republic's leading civilian from public life.

Section 60.16

War with Peru, Tarqui, and the Militarization of Andean Politics, 1828–1829

The war between Colombia and Peru grew from unresolved issues of independence. Colombia and Peru disputed the status of Guayaquil, Jaén, and Maynas, whose control would determine access to the upper Amazon. Peru's grievances also included Colombia's role in Bolivia and the Colombian troops who had remained at Lima until the mutiny of January 1827. Peru's president, José de La Mar, born in Cuenca, had a personal stake. In 1828 Peru intervened in Bolivia, forcing Sucre's resignation, and Colombia declared war in July.

Peru blockaded and seized Guayaquil in November 1828, and in early 1829 La Mar invaded Colombian territory in the south with an army. Sucre, commanding the Colombian forces, met him at Portete de Tarqui on 27 February 1829 and defeated him. The Convention of Girón, signed the next day, ended the fighting. In June, Gamarra overthrew La Mar, and the Treaty of Guayaquil of September 1829 restored peace and returned Guayaquil, though it left the border undefined.

The war illustrates the militarization of Andean politics. Both states were governed by officers, financed armies at the expense of civil institutions, and used external conflict to legitimize authority. Venezuelan officers commanded garrisons in the south, and the Colombian army remained poorly paid. The victory at Tarqui strengthened Colombia's military reputation but not its political cohesion, since the south would soon declare separation. The unresolved frontier would continue to be a source of conflict between Ecuador and Peru.

Division IV · 1829–1834Dissolution and Successor States
Section 60.17

Venezuela's Separation from the Union

Venezuela's separation was the work of Páez and the leading figures of Caracas and Valencia, who judged that Bolívar's dictatorship and Bogotá's policies threatened local interests. In 1829, as rumors circulated that Bolívar's allies planned to introduce a monarchy, municipalities across Venezuela passed resolutions calling for separation. On 25 November 1829 an assembly of notables in Caracas declared that Venezuela would no longer obey the government at Bogotá, and Páez accepted the leadership of the movement. A decree or resolution reportedly declared Bolívar unwelcome in Venezuela.

Bolívar tried to respond through conciliation, sending commissioners to negotiate, but Venezuelan leaders insisted on separation. A Constituent Congress met at Valencia in May 1830. Delegates came from the provinces, mostly property-owning men, and their debates concerned the form of the new state and the status of Páez, who served as provisional leader. The Congress approved a constitution in September 1830, and the capital moved to Caracas.

Venezuelan separation shows the role of regional elites and caudillos. Páez drew on a coalition of llaneros, landowners, merchants, and municipal officials. Separation was framed as a defense of republican liberty and local self-government, but it also served the interests of those who had resented Bogotá's authority. It was supported by many who had fought under Bolívar, and its leaders acknowledged his role in independence while rejecting his government. Venezuelan separation therefore divided the revolutionary generation rather than pitting loyalists against rebels.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and Secession

Sovereignty is supreme political authority within a territory. Secession is the withdrawal of a territory from an existing state. In the early republics, who held sovereignty was an unsettled question: the nation as a whole, the provinces, the municipalities, or the people acting through juntas.

Venezuelans and Ecuadorians did not describe their actions as rebellion but as the reassertion of sovereignty that had never been fully surrendered. Bogotá's defenders argued that the constitution bound the departments. The question was neither simple nor settled by law, and it was decided by force, bargaining, and the choices of regional elites. The term "secession" should be used carefully, since contemporaries often spoke of "separation" or "reassumption of sovereignty."

Section 60.18

Ecuador's Separation and the Southern Political Crisis

In the south, the movement toward separation came after Venezuela's. On 13 August 1830 an assembly in Quito declared that the southern departments of Ecuador, Guayas, and Azuay would form a separate state, at least until the union could be restored on acceptable terms. The leading figure was Juan José Flores, a Venezuelan-born officer who had risen through the independence wars and married into a prominent Quito family. As military commander of the south, he had both the force and the local connections to lead the movement.

The three principal regions of the south had different interests. Guayaquil's merchants wanted free trade and a share in decision-making; Quito's highland landowners wanted protection and stability; and Cuenca's elites wanted autonomy from both. Flores balanced these interests, relying on Quito's support, military patronage, and an alliance with prominent Guayaquil figures. The Constituent Congress, which met at Riobamba in August and September 1830, adopted a constitution creating the State of Ecuador.

The new state also sought to extend its territory northward. In late 1830 Flores annexed Pasto, Popayán, and Buenaventura, territory belonging to New Granada's Cauca. This provoked conflict with New Granada, and the disputed region was returned in 1832 after fighting and negotiation. Ecuador's independence thus involved both a break with Bogotá and an attempt to claim territory that Bogotá considered its own, an early example of the border disputes discussed in 60.21.

Section 60.19

The Admirable Congress, Sucre's Assassination, and Bolívar's Death

The Admirable Congress, as it came to be called, met in Bogotá in January 1830, under the presidency of Sucre, to write a constitution and settle the union. Bolívar presented his resignation, which the Congress accepted in March; Joaquín Mosquera became president and Domingo Caycedo vice-president. The Congress adopted a new constitution on 29 April 1830, but it never took effect, since Venezuela and Ecuador had already begun separating.

Bolívar left Bogotá in May, intending to leave the country. On 4 June 1830 Sucre was murdered in the Berruecos woods while returning to Quito. His killers have been identified in several ways, and their motives are debated. Suspicions fell on General José María Obando and others, but the question remains disputed, and the case was politicized in subsequent decades. Sucre's death removed the most respected military figure aside from Bolívar and a symbol of Andean unity.

Bolívar reached the Caribbean coast, ill with tuberculosis, and died on 17 December 1830 near Santa Marta. His last proclamation urged his countrymen to maintain union; his enemies saw hypocrisy and his admirers saw testament. With his death, Gran Colombia lost the one figure around whom a union had been organized. The legacy of his political thought, both centralist and republican, remained in dispute in every successor state.

Section 60.20

The End of Colombia and the Creation of New Granada

After Mosquera took office, a military faction in Bogotá led by General Rafael Urdaneta, a Venezuelan Bolivarian, overthrew the government on 31 August 1830, proposing to restore Bolívar's authority. Urdaneta governed for several months, but without Bolívar and with Venezuela and Ecuador separated, he lacked a base. By April 1831 his regime collapsed and Caycedo assumed power. The resulting settlement, signed at Apulo, restored civilian government in New Granada.

A convention met in Bogotá in late 1831 and, on 17 November, issued a fundamental law creating the State of New Granada from the provinces that remained. The constitution was adopted on 29 February 1832. It established a unitary republic but gave provincial chambers some authority over local matters, a compromise reflecting the federalist demands of Ocaña. The president would serve four years, the Congress was bicameral, and suffrage required property. Santander, recalled from exile, was elected president in October 1832 and took office in 1833.

The dissolution was a process rather than a single act. Venezuela separated in 1829 and 1830, Ecuador in 1830, and New Granada's institutions were reorganized in 1831 and 1832. Panama and the Cauca provinces were brought into the new state through negotiation and, in some cases, military pressure. Contemporaries did not use the term "Gran Colombia" to describe the republic they had lived in; the label came later, when the modern Colombian state needed to distinguish itself from its predecessor.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did Gran Colombia Dissolve?

The question: Was the dissolution principally a consequence of structural regional differences, political choices, constitutional design, or fiscal and military crises?

Structural explanations: Some historians emphasize distance, regional economies, and separate colonial histories. Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito were joined by revolution but not by roads, markets, or institutions. On this view, no constitution could have held them together, and Bolívar's personal authority was a temporary substitute for the structures that did not exist.
Political and constitutional explanations: Others emphasize contingent choices: Santander's prosecution of Páez, Bolívar's absence, the early convention at Ocaña, the dictatorship, and the failure to accept a federal compromise. On this view, a union with more flexible institutions might have survived, and the dissolution resulted from political failures at specific moments.

Evidence: The evidence includes constitutional texts, congressional debates, fiscal records, and the correspondence of the principal actors, all of which are partisan. Comparisons with the successor states, which also struggled with regionalism, support structural views, while the timing of the separations supports contingent ones.

Why it matters: The question affects how Bolívar's project is judged: as visionary but premature, as flawed by his own choices, or as undone by forces beyond any individual. It also shapes national memory in each successor state.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, then identify a third factor, such as debt or military patronage, that neither fully explains, and describe what evidence would test it.

Section 60.21

Borders, National Debts, Diplomatic Recognition, and the Inheritance of Union

The end of the union left unresolved questions about debts, borders, and institutions. The foreign debt contracted in London in 1822 and 1824, which Gran Colombia had defaulted on, was divided by a convention signed at Bogotá in 1834: New Granada accepted 50 percent, Venezuela 28.5 percent, and Ecuador 21.5 percent. The division was negotiated, and each state subsequently struggled to service its share, a recurring problem through mid-century.

Borders were harder. The successor states agreed in principle to the colonial boundaries of 1810, the principle called uti possidetis, but colonial jurisdictions were vague. Venezuela and New Granada disputed the Guajira peninsula and eastern frontier; Ecuador and New Granada disputed the Cauca provinces; and Ecuador and Peru disputed Jaén and Maynas, leaving a conflict that lasted until the twentieth century. Diplomatic recognition also varied, with Spain recognizing Ecuador in 1840 and Venezuela in 1845 but New Granada only in 1881.

Other inheritances were shared. The flag's tricolor, the language of Bolívar's republic, and the army's institutional memory survived in different forms. Each state also claimed the memory of Bolívar differently: Venezuela eventually returned his remains to Caracas in 1842, while New Granada remained more divided between admirers and critics. The union's failure produced competing national histories, but it also left shared legal traditions, trade connections, and diplomatic habits that connected the Andean republics for decades.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Constitutional Union Failed and How Its Collapse Shaped the Successor States

Long-term causes

Distance and poor communications; separate colonial administrations and regional economies; the legacy of war, which created military leaders with local followings; fiscal weakness and foreign debt; unresolved debates over centralism and federalism.

Short-term causes

The Páez prosecution and La Cosiata; Bolívar's absence in Peru; the early convention at Ocaña; the dictatorship and the conspiracy; the war with Peru; Bolívar's declining health.

Event or transformation

Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada separated between 1829 and 1832 and reorganized as distinct republics.

Immediate effects

Debts were divided, borders disputed, and constitutions rewritten; Bolívar and Sucre died; military leaders consolidated regional authority.

Long-term consequences

Successor states adopted constitutions that blended centralism with provincial elements, and some, like Venezuela and Ecuador, were dominated by the caudillos who led separation; unresolved borders and debts created recurring disputes.

Historical complexity

No single cause is sufficient. Structural conditions made union difficult, but the specific timing and form of dissolution depended on choices by Páez, Santander, Bolívar, and regional elites. The successor states faced the same problems and solved them differently, which suggests that union's failure was neither inevitable nor unique.
Division V · 1830–1850Venezuela after Gran Colombia
Section 60.22

Páez and the Constitution of 1830

Venezuela's constitution of 22 September 1830 created a republic that was centralist in structure but gave provinces more voice than Cúcuta had. The president served a four-year term and could not be immediately reelected; Congress had two chambers; and each province had a deputation, a representative body, with a governor appointed by the executive. Suffrage depended on substantial property or income, so that the political nation remained a small minority of free men. The constitution also asserted the state's patronage rights over the Church, continuing the colonial inheritance.

The system rested on Páez's coalition. Páez had the loyalty of the llaneros of the plains, the support of Caracas merchants and landowners, and the cooperation of civilian jurists and administrators such as Carlos Soublette, José Rafael Revenga, and Santos Michelena, who gave the regime legal and financial competence. Historians have called this arrangement the Conservative oligarchy. It combined a caudillo's military prestige with an elite's administrative skill and aimed to keep the army subordinate to civil government while preserving social order.

Páez served as the first constitutional president from 1831 to 1835, and his influence continued afterwards, since he dominated the army and could intervene when he judged the constitution in danger. This arrangement gave Venezuela a stability that New Granada and Ecuador sometimes lacked in the 1830s, but it relied on one man's arbitration. The tension between constitutional rule and the military authority inherited from independence was to be tested in 1835.

Section 60.23

José María Vargas and the Revolution of the Reforms, 1835–1836

The 1834 to 1835 election tested civilian government. José María Vargas, a physician and university rector with a reputation for integrity, was elected president and took office in February 1835. He defeated military candidates, including Santiago Mariño, a Bolivarian general, and his election symbolized a hope that a civilian could govern. Vargas favored reforms to reduce the privileges of the army and the clergy, including their special courts, the fueros.

On 8 July 1835 a group of officers, including Pedro Carujo and Mariño, seized the president and declared a revolution. The so-called Revolution of the Reforms demanded the preservation of military and clerical privileges and a government led by military men. A much-repeated anecdote has Carujo telling Vargas that the world belongs to the brave and Vargas answering that it belongs to the just; the exchange rests on later tradition rather than verified documentation.

Páez, who was in his Apure estates, came to defend the constitution, defeated the rebels, and returned Vargas to office. Vargas, deeply discouraged, resigned in 1836 and went into exile for a time. The episode illustrates that civilian authority still depended on military prestige, since the civilian president needed a general's help to survive a military rebellion. It also reveals what the rebels feared: reductions in the privileges that had accompanied independence and the Church's institutional position.

Section 60.24

The Conservative Political Order, Trade, and Regional Power

After Vargas, power circulated among Páez and his ally Soublette: Soublette served as president from 1837 to 1839 and 1843 to 1847, and Páez from 1839 to 1843. The economic base was agricultural exports, with coffee overtaking cacao as the leading export in the 1830s and 1840s, produced in the central valleys and the Andes of Trujillo, Mérida, and Táchira. Coffee growing favored smallholders and large estates, and merchant houses in Caracas, Maracaibo, and La Guaira financed the trade, often with British, German, and North American partners.

Policy favored creditors and landowners. A law of April 1834, the freedom of contracts law, relaxed colonial limits on interest and made it easier for creditors to foreclose, which benefited merchants but exposed small producers to debt. The state paid its share of the debt inherited from Gran Colombia, with customs revenue providing its main income. Provincial elites controlled local offices, and Caracas dominated finance and politics, though Maracaibo and Valencia retained commercial strength.

The regime's strengths were institutional: it maintained civil order, collected taxes, and avoided the cycle of coups that afflicted neighbors. Its weakness was narrow participation. Property qualifications limited the electorate, and urban artisans, rural workers, and the enslaved had little voice. A generation of Venezuelans who had fought in the independence wars felt shut out of the rewards, and economic downturns in the 1840s sharpened that discontent, preparing the ground for the Liberal opposition.

Section 60.25

Slavery, Free People of Color, Llaneros, and Rural Venezuela

Slavery continued in Venezuela through mid-century. The enslaved, numbering in the tens of thousands, worked on cacao and sugar estates and in domestic service, concentrated on the central coast and valleys. The free-womb law of 1821 continued to operate, and Venezuela's legislators passed supplementary manumission measures, including a law in 1830, but they were slow and compensated enslavers. Full abolition came only in 1854, under José Gregorio Monagas. Enslaved people continued to run away, to petition, and to purchase their freedom.

The plains and cattle economy shaped Venezuelan politics. The llaneros, cowboys and cattle workers who had formed the backbone of Páez's cavalry, remained a source of armed power. Rural society was organized around large cattle estates and dependent workers; laws on vagrancy and rural police controlled mobility and labor. Peasants working small plots on others' land, or conuqueros, were vulnerable to eviction and debt.

Rural discontent could take political form. In 1846, Ezequiel Zamora and others led peasant uprisings in the plains under the banner of land and free men, forcing the government to deploy troops. Free people of color, including pardos who had served in the armies, continued to press claims for equality, while elites feared racial conflict. The relationship between popular mobilization and caudillo power was ambivalent: caudillos mobilized rural followers but also contained them.

Section 60.26

Liberal Opposition, the Monagas Presidency, and the Crisis of 1848

In 1840 Antonio Leocadio Guzmán, a journalist, founded the Liberal Party and its newspaper El Venezolano, criticizing the oligarchy's monopoly of office and its economic policies. Liberals attracted artisans, small producers, and ambitious provincial figures. In the 1846 election Guzmán was arrested for his criticism and sentenced to death, a sentence later commuted; the repression strengthened his cause and divided the Conservative elite.

José Tadeo Monagas, a veteran of the independence wars from eastern Venezuela, was elected president in 1847 with Conservative support, but he soon broke with his patrons and moved toward the Liberals. On 24 January 1848, an armed crowd attacked Congress as it debated efforts to impeach him, killing several legislators. Páez rebelled in defense of the constitution, but Monagas's forces defeated him in 1849 and Páez was captured, imprisoned, and exiled in 1850.

The crisis transformed Venezuelan politics. The Conservative oligarchy was displaced, and the Monagas brothers, José Tadeo and José Gregorio, dominated the presidency until 1858. The change did not produce a more representative state so much as a new set of rulers, backed by an army and by Liberal opponents of the old elite. The episode showed that the constitutional order of 1830 depended on a balance among caudillo, army, and oligarchy that could be upset when one party defected.

Division VI · 1832–1850New Granada and the Colombian Republic
Section 60.27

Santander, the Constitution of 1832, and Republican Reconstruction

Santander returned from exile in late 1832 and took office as president of New Granada in 1833, serving until 1837. He governed according to the 1832 constitution, which combined a unitary state with provincial chambers. His priorities were fiscal discipline, administrative regularity, and public education. He reorganized the treasury, promoted primary schools and colleges, and introduced utilitarian texts, including Bentham's, in university teaching, which provoked clerical opposition.

Fiscal reconstruction was central. New Granada inherited the largest share of Gran Colombia's debt, and its revenue came from customs, the tobacco and salt monopolies, and the tithe. Santander worked to restore credit, negotiate with creditors, and establish a more regular tax collection. The provinces, whose chambers shaped local budgets, gained a role in administration that reflected federalist pressures, yet the executive retained control over appointments.

Santander also managed political succession. His chosen successor, José Ignacio de Márquez, a lawyer from Boyacá, defeated General José María Obando in Congress in 1837, a decision that angered Obando's supporters in the southwest. The contest foreshadowed the civil war of 1839, as it suggested that the republic's lawyers and soldiers could not easily share power. Santander himself remained influential until his death in 1840.

Section 60.28

The War of the Supremes, 1839–1842

The War of the Supremes began over a religious dispute. A law of 1839 ordered the closure of small convents, those with fewer than eight religious, and the use of their property for education. In Pasto, a region with a long royalist and Catholic tradition and a large Indigenous population, the order provoked a local uprising in mid-1839, led by priests and local notables. The rebellion drew on resentments toward Bogotá's authority and the memory of earlier suppressions.

General José María Obando, a Cauca officer who had been denied the presidency in 1837, joined the rebellion, which spread across the republic. Provincial leaders, known as "supremes," declared themselves heads of their own territories: in Cartagena, Santa Marta, Socorro, and elsewhere, often under the banner of federalism. The Isthmus of Panama declared itself a separate state from 1840 to 1841 under Tomás Herrera. The war was an alliance of grievances, including federalism, resentment of Bogotá's fiscal policies, and religious sentiment.

The government of Márquez, with Pedro Alcántara Herrán and Tomás Cipriano de Mosquera as principal generals, defeated the rebels in a series of campaigns, with key victories at Huilquipamba in 1840 and Aratoca in 1841. By 1842 the rebellion was largely over, and Obando went into exile in Peru. The war strengthened the government's case for a stronger executive and Conservative control, but it also revealed the weakness of national authority and the persistence of regional loyalties.

Section 60.29

The Constitution of 1843 and the Strengthening of Executive Authority

After the war, Herrán, president from 1841 to 1845, and his ministers argued that New Granada needed stronger national institutions. The Constitution of 20 April 1843 curtailed the provincial chambers' power, enlarged the president's authority to appoint governors, and lengthened terms. It reaffirmed Catholicism as the state religion and, in 1844, Herrán readmitted the Jesuits, who had been expelled in the eighteenth century. Supporters presented the changes as a return to order; critics saw them as a retreat from the provincial voice acknowledged in 1832.

Mosquera, born into a prominent family in Popayán and a major landowner in the Cauca, succeeded Herrán in 1845. He was an energetic modernizer who promoted steam navigation on the Magdalena, supported roads and public works, and reduced some tariffs to encourage trade. His government took a pragmatic approach to economic development, and he later became a Liberal leader, illustrating the fluidity of political alignments before the formation of parties.

The 1843 constitution and the Mosquera administration established a pattern of strong executive authority combined with a lawyer-led elite. They also created the conditions for a Liberal reaction. Artisans, small producers, and provincial intellectuals, who had little voice in the system, began to organize, and the debate over the nature of the republic reopened in the late 1840s.

Section 60.30

Economy, Slavery, Indigenous Lands, and Provincial Society

New Granada's economy was regional. Gold mining in Antioquia, the Cauca, and the Chocó provided exports and employed enslaved and free workers; the Magdalena valley carried tobacco and other goods; haciendas and ranches supplied internal markets; and the highlands of Boyacá and Santander had textile artisans and farmers. Steamboat service on the Magdalena began in the 1820s and expanded in the 1840s, but roads remained poor, and many regions traded more with neighbors than with Bogotá.

Slavery persisted, concentrated in the Cauca valley and the gold mines, where enslavers held tens of thousands in the aggregate. Manumission under the 1821 law and individual manumissions slowly reduced the enslaved population, but abolition came only in 1851, after Liberal victory. Indigenous communities held lands in resguardos, which liberal reformers wanted to divide and which provincial authorities sometimes dissolved; resistance was strong, notably in Pasto and the southwest, and litigation was frequent.

These economic structures produced unequal consequences of independence. Textile artisans in Socorro and elsewhere suffered from imported cloth after tariffs fell, which turned some into protectionists and later supporters of the Liberals. Cauca landowners, dependent on enslaved labor, were among the Conservatives' and Liberals' crucial constituencies. Provincial society was dominated by local notables who controlled land, credit, and office, while poorer rural families depended on them. National politics reflected these local hierarchies.

Section 60.31

The Emergence of Liberal and Conservative Politics, 1848–1850

Political parties in the modern sense emerged only at mid-century. Earlier divisions, Bolivarians and Santanderistas, ministerials and opposition, were factions with changing membership. In the 1840s artisans in Bogotá formed the Democratic Society in 1847, demanding tariff protection and political voice. The European revolutions of 1848 inspired young radicals, and newspapers multiplied.

In July 1848 Ezequiel Rojas published an article in La Aurora that is often treated as the first Liberal program, calling for individual liberties, the end of slavery, a reduced role for the Church, and federal arrangements. In October 1849 Mariano Ospina Rodríguez and José Eusebio Caro published the Conservative program in La Civilización, defending order, religion, and legal continuity. Neither program existed as a formal party organization in the 1820s, and the chapter should not project them backward.

The 1849 election turned on the pressure of Liberal crowds in Bogotá, who surrounded Congress as it chose between candidates, and José Hilario López, a general from Popayán, became president in 1849. His administration enacted abolition in 1851 and a series of Liberal reforms. The political world of Gran Colombia had thus given way to a new party system rooted in the experiences of the 1830s and 1840s.

Division VII · 1830–1850Ecuador and the Politics of Regional Integration
Section 60.32

The Constitution of 1830 and the First Government of Juan José Flores

The Constituent Congress at Riobamba adopted Ecuador's first constitution on 11 September 1830. It created a unitary state with three departments, Quito (Ecuador), Guayas, and Azuay, a president serving a four-year term, a Senate and Chamber, and property and literacy qualifications for voting. It declared Catholicism the state religion and, in keeping with colonial tradition, gave parish priests a protective role toward Indigenous communities. Quito became the capital, a decision that satisfied the highland elites but irritated Guayaquil.

Flores became the first president and governed from 1830 to 1834. A Venezuelan-born officer who had served the royalists before changing sides, he had no native regional base, but he married into a Quito family and cultivated landowners, clergy, and officers. His government relied on military patronage and an alliance with highland elites, with Guayaquil's leaders included at the margins. He faced the challenge of integrating three regions that had few common institutions: a highland economy linked to Colombia and Peru, a coastal economy oriented to the Pacific and world trade, and a southern city, Cuenca, with its own interests.

Flores' government also tackled external problems. It negotiated the division of Gran Colombia's debt, settled conflicts over the Cauca provinces, and in 1832 claimed the Galápagos Islands. Domestically, it faced a fiscal shortage and a dependence on a small, mostly Indigenous tax base. The constitution of 1830 thus created a state whose institutions depended on a military leader's ability to balance regions, a fragile foundation.

Section 60.33

Coast, Highlands, Indigenous Communities, and the Regional Economy

Ecuador's economy was divided by geography. The coast, centered on Guayaquil, exported cacao, with merchants financing planters and importing foreign goods. Shipyards and the port's trade made it the country's commercial gateway, and its merchants favored free trade. The highlands produced wheat, potatoes, livestock, and textiles, with the Quito obrajes (textile workshops) and household weavers supplying markets in Colombia and Peru. Cuenca's economy rested on agriculture, quinine bark, and later straw hats, and Loja on quinine and local trade. The total population was small, probably well under a million, and mostly Indigenous.

Imports threatened highland textiles, and the tariff became a regional issue. Highland landowners wanted protection; coastal merchants wanted low duties. Indigenous communities paid a personal tribute and labor obligations, and many were bound to haciendas through debt, a system known as concertaje. Tribute was a significant revenue source and remained into the 1850s. Landownership was concentrated in highland estates, and Indigenous communities lost land through sale, debt, and encroachment.

These regional differences shaped politics. Guayaquil's elites, wealthy and mercantile, resisted Quito's dominance and highland landowners' economic priorities. The Sierra elite, tied to haciendas, the Church, and Indigenous labor, formed a different political culture. Flores built his support in Quito, and his opponents found theirs in Guayaquil, so that the contest between presidents was also a contest between regions.

Section 60.34

Opposition, Rocafuerte, and the Constitutional Experiment of 1835

Opposition to Flores began in Guayaquil. Vicente Rocafuerte, a Guayaquil statesman who had served as a diplomat in Mexico and Europe, returned in 1833 and led a coalition of coastal liberals and disaffected officers. In October 1833 a revolt in Guayaquil, known as the Revolution of the Chihuahuas, challenged Flores, and the war continued into 1834 and 1835. Flores defeated his opponents at Miñarica in January 1835.

Rocafuerte and Flores then reached a settlement. Flores accepted a constitutional convention at Ambato in 1835, and Rocafuerte became president while Flores remained head of the army. The agreement joined a civilian reformer and a military leader, a pattern that would shape Ecuadorian politics for a decade. Rocafuerte's government, from 1835 to 1839, stressed education, roads, prisons, and public administration, and he was strongly anticlerical for his time.

The settlement illustrated how Ecuadorian politics worked. Neither a military caudillo nor a coastal civilian could govern alone, and their uneasy alliance gave the new state a degree of stability. The constitution of 1835 also reflected a more restrictive approach to suffrage. But the arrangement depended on cooperation between two men with different regional bases, and the question of who would succeed Rocafuerte remained open.

Section 60.35

Flores's Return, the Constitution of 1843, and Political Centralization

Flores was elected president again in 1839, serving until 1845 and drawing on his control of the army and of patronage networks in Quito. His government faced a fiscal squeeze, reflecting the costs of the army and the burden of Gran Colombian debt, which strained a revenue base dependent on customs, tribute, and monopolies. It also confronted continuing coastal opposition.

In 1843 a convention in Quito adopted a constitution that lengthened the presidential term to eight years and permitted reelection, strengthening executive power. Its opponents called it the "Charter of Slavery," and it provoked opposition from Guayaquil and from liberals. Flores was reelected under its terms. His government used patronage and military power to maintain control, and critics accused him of using the constitution to perpetuate his rule.

The 1843 constitution aimed at stability but intensified opposition. Coastal merchants objected to the tax burden and to Quito's dominance; liberals objected to executive power; and some elites worried about Flores's ambition. Flores's critics also pointed to his foreign origins and to his plans for European support, which fed accusations of monarchism. By early 1845, the conditions for revolt were in place.

Section 60.36

The March Revolution of 1845 and the Reconstruction of the Ecuadorian Republic

On 6 March 1845 a movement in Guayaquil proclaimed a revolution against Flores. Coastal leaders, including Olmedo, Vicente Ramón Roca, and Diego Noboa, joined with military officers and liberals, and the movement spread to other regions. Flores, facing widespread opposition, negotiated the Treaty of Virginia in June 1845, under which he left Ecuador with his rank and a pension. A provisional government of three leaders took office.

A new assembly at Cuenca wrote a constitution in 1845 and chose Roca as president, serving until 1849. The new government sought to heal regional divisions, reduce executive power, and reform finances. It faced the persistent problem of a state with limited revenue and divided regions. In 1846 and 1847 Flores organized an expedition in Europe to recover power, which collapsed, and it demonstrated both his ambitions and the limits of foreign support.

The March Revolution did not resolve Ecuador's regional problem, but it altered the balance toward Guayaquil. Through about 1850, politics continued to be shaped by competition between coast and highlands, between military and civilian leaders, and between liberals and conservatives. Slavery, though small in scale, persisted until abolition in 1851 and 1852, and Indigenous tribute continued into the later 1850s. The republic had survived, but its integration remained incomplete.

Division VIII · 1823–1850Peru, Bolivia, and Competing Andean Unions
Section 60.37

Peru's Early Constitutions and the Rejection of the Bolivarian Model

Peru's first republican constitution, adopted in November 1823, was liberal and legislature-centered, but it was suspended almost immediately because Congress, facing the royalist threat, named Bolívar dictator in early 1824. After the victory at Ayacucho in December 1824, Bolívar governed Peru through delegates, then through Colombian troops, until 1826. His presence was both a protection and a burden: the army he commanded was costly, and many Peruvians resented foreign officers.

In 1826 Bolívar presented the Bolivian constitution to Peru. Its lifetime president and tricameral legislature were adopted by Peruvian electoral colleges in December 1826, naming Bolívar president for life. The decision was unpopular; Colombian troops in Lima mutinied in January 1827, and the constitution was suspended and repealed soon afterward. A new congress elected La Mar president and drew up a liberal constitution, promulgated on 18 March 1828.

The 1828 constitution restored the tradition of legislative supremacy and provincial participation, with prefectures and departmental juntas, and became a model for later Peruvian liberals. Its adoption represented a rejection of the Bolivarian model and a reassertion of Peruvian sovereignty, but it also left the new republic with weak executive authority and a politicized army, and it arrived as war with Colombia loomed.

Section 60.38

José de La Mar and the Republic after Bolívar

José de La Mar, born in Cuenca and a veteran of the Spanish army before joining the patriots, was elected president by Congress in 1827 and served until 1829. His government reconstructed Peruvian authority after the departure of Colombian troops. It faced fiscal problems, since customs revenues and Indigenous contributions had to support an army and a new bureaucracy, and political problems, since factions of Lima liberals and provincial military leaders competed for influence.

Peruvians' perspective on Colombia differed from that in Bogotá. They viewed Bolívar's presence as an occupation, resented the lifetime constitution, and blamed Colombia for retaining Guayaquil, Jaén, and Maynas. La Mar's own origins in Cuenca and his belief that Guayaquil belonged to Peru made the dispute personal. When the war with Colombia came, it was in part an effort to complete what Peruvians understood as the unfinished business of independence.

The defeat at Tarqui and the failure of the campaign damaged La Mar. In June 1829 General Agustín Gamarra overthrew him, and La Mar went into exile, dying in Costa Rica in 1830. His fall demonstrated that military defeat could dissolve civilian authority, and that the Peruvian presidency depended on the loyalty of generals.

Section 60.39

Agustín Gamarra and the Search for a Durable Peruvian Government, 1829–1833

Gamarra, born in Cusco and formerly a royalist officer, ruled Peru from 1829 to 1833. He strengthened the army and sought to centralize authority. His economic policy leaned toward protection of domestic industries, particularly southern textiles and agriculture, which won him support in Cusco and the southern highlands, but it antagonized Lima merchants who favored free trade. The state continued to depend on customs duties and the Indigenous contribution, restored after independence, and on arrears on foreign loans contracted in 1822 and 1825.

His wife, Francisca Zubiaga, known as the Mariscala, was a prominent political figure who accompanied him on campaign, intervened in political maneuvers, and became a subject of both admiration and hostility. Her role shows that women could exercise political influence in elite circles even without formal rights, though contemporaries often used her to attack her husband.

Gamarra's government was challenged by liberals in Congress, including Francisco Javier de Luna Pizarro and Manuel Lorenzo de Vidaurre, and by regional rivalries between Lima and the southern highlands. In 1833 Congress, which had no wish to extend his term, elected Luis José de Orbegoso to succeed him, and the transition marked a rare constitutional succession, though it was soon undermined.

Section 60.40

Orbegoso, Salaverry, and Peru's Constitutional Crisis, 1834–1836

Orbegoso took office in December 1833 but faced a military revolt almost at once, as Gamarra's allies tried to retain power. The crisis was resolved by the so-called Embrace of Maquinhuayo in April 1834, in which rival forces reconciled, and a new liberal constitution was adopted in June 1834. The peace was short. In February 1835 Felipe Santiago Salaverry, a young general, seized Lima and declared himself Supreme Chief.

Orbegoso, besieged in Arequipa and supported by the south, appealed for help to Andrés de Santa Cruz, president of Bolivia, who had long been involved in Peruvian politics. Santa Cruz entered Peru in 1835 and defeated Salaverry at Socabaya in February 1836, and Salaverry was shot soon afterward. Gamarra, in exile, also took part in the maneuvering, and the south's resentment of Lima shaped events.

The crisis demonstrates how constitutional politics in Peru was entangled with regional rivalries and military factionalism. Lima, Arequipa, and Cusco had distinct interests, and generals served as regional power brokers. The invitation to a foreign president reflected the weakness of civilian institutions and the appeal of an alternative political project. It led directly to the Confederation.

Section 60.41

Bolivia after Independence: Sucre, the Constitution of 1826, and Santa Cruz

Bolivia was created in August 1825 from Upper Peru, and its first constitution, drafted by Bolívar and adopted in 1826, established a lifetime president with the right to name a successor, a tricameral legislature of tribunes, senators, and censors, and a Catholic state. Sucre was named president, accepted for two years, and sought to organize a new state with scant resources. He reorganized finances, established schools, restored an Indigenous tribute under a new name, and tried to reform the mining sector.

Opposition grew. Local elites in Chuquisaca resented Colombian officers and soldiers, and Peru's government under La Mar intrigued against Sucre. On 18 April 1828 troops mutinied at Chuquisaca and Sucre was wounded. Peruvian forces under Gamarra entered Bolivia, and by the Treaty of Piquiza in July 1828 the Colombian troops left. Sucre resigned and departed in August 1828.

Santa Cruz, a La Paz-born officer of Indigenous maternal descent who had served the royalists before joining the patriots, became president in 1829 and held office until 1839. He revised the constitution in 1831, abolishing the lifetime presidency, introduced legal codes, and reorganized the army and treasury. He also nurtured the Pacific port of Cobija, hoping to expand Bolivia's access to the sea. His government gave Bolivia a more stable order, and it also nurtured his ambition to reunify the Andean highlands.

Section 60.42

Indigenous Tribute, Silver Mining, Regional Elites, and the Bolivian State

Indigenous tribute was the foundation of the early Bolivian state. The contribución indigenal, as it was called, was paid by Indigenous communities as a per-person tax and was the single largest source of revenue, often estimated at roughly two-fifths to one-half of the state's income. Its continuation reflected the republic's fiscal weakness and the fact that Indigenous peasants, who formed a large majority of the population, were politically excluded but economically essential.

The silver mines of Potosí and Oruro, which had declined during the independence wars, recovered slowly. Flooded mines, scarce capital, and the end of the colonial mita draft made production difficult, and the state tried to support miners through credit, mint operations, and the purchase of silver. Highland valleys such as Cochabamba produced grain, Chuquisaca remained a legal and clerical center, and the eastern lowlands of Santa Cruz and the Beni were distant and sparsely settled.

Indigenous communities, organized in ayllus, retained communal lands and defended them through litigation and negotiation. The state's reliance on tribute also made it a guarantor of communal landholding in practice, since the payment of tribute was tied to recognition as a community member. This relationship, sometimes called a colonial pact carried into the republic, shaped Bolivian politics for decades and distinguished Bolivia from republics that abolished tribute earlier.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous Tribute and Corporate Rights

Tribute was a regular payment owed by Indigenous communities to the state, introduced under Spanish rule. After independence it was often renamed, for example contribución personal de indígenas or contribución indigenal, and reintroduced or retained because the new states needed revenue. Corporate rights refers to the legal recognition of Indigenous communities as collective bodies holding lands, with their own authorities and legal standing.

Liberal reformers sought to abolish tribute and divide communal lands in the name of equality and individual property. But Indigenous communities often defended tribute as part of an arrangement that protected their lands, while states resisted losing revenue. The tension between liberal ideals and fiscal necessity shaped Andean republics' relations with Indigenous peoples. Students should avoid assuming that abolishing tribute was uniformly beneficial or that its retention was merely oppressive.

Section 60.43

The Peru-Bolivian Confederation, 1836–1839

After defeating Salaverry, Santa Cruz organized Peru into two states. Assemblies at Sicuani (south) in March 1836 and Huaura (north) in August 1836 declared the creation of South Peru and North Peru, and a Bolivian assembly at Tapacarí in October joined them. The Confederation was proclaimed on 28 October 1836, and a congress at Tacna in 1837 approved its pact on 1 May. Santa Cruz was named Protector, with extensive powers.

The Confederation had economic logic. Southern Peru and Bolivia had long been connected by trade, with the Pacific ports of Arica and Tacna serving the highlands. Arequipa's wool trade and the altiplano's commerce depended on these routes. Santa Cruz's tariff and free port policies favored these ports and challenged Lima and Valparaíso, Chile's commercial rival. His own Indigenous heritage and Andean base made him a plausible leader of highland interests.

But the Confederation had powerful enemies. Lima elites resented losing their capital's primacy and a ruler from Bolivia; Peruvian exiles, notably Gamarra, plotted against it; Chile's minister Diego Portales saw it as a threat to Chile's commercial and strategic position; and Argentina's ruler, Juan Manuel de Rosas, had his own quarrels with Bolivia over Tarija. These opponents did not need to defeat the Confederation's army alone; they needed only to exploit its internal divisions.

Section 60.44

War against the Confederation and the Battle of Yungay

Chile declared war on the Confederation in December 1836, after a dispute over the seizure of Chilean ships at Callao and an expedition led by Ramón Freire. Argentina, under Rosas, followed in 1837. A first Chilean army, under Manuel Blanco Encalada, landed in southern Peru and was surrounded, and in November 1837 the Treaty of Paucarpata ended the campaign. Chile's government repudiated it, particularly after Portales was murdered by mutineers in June 1837, an event that heightened Chilean determination.

A second army, led by Manuel Bulnes with Peruvian exiles under Gamarra, advanced into Peru. After the Battle of Portada de Guías near Lima in August 1838, and months of campaigning in the highlands, the Restoration Army met Santa Cruz's forces at Yungay, in the northern Andes, on 20 January 1839. Santa Cruz was defeated, resigned in February, and went into exile in Ecuador and then Europe. The Confederation was dissolved.

The war's consequences were lasting. Chile emerged as a naval and commercial power in the Pacific, while Peru and Bolivia were left divided and often hostile. The memory of the Confederation became a part of national narratives: for Chile it was a victory over a threat, for Peru it was an episode of foreign interference or Andean union, and for Bolivia a moment of lost opportunity. Later rivalries, discussed in Chapter 65, built on the geography and grievances this war created.

Section 60.45

Peru and Bolivia after the Confederation, 1839–1850

After Yungay, Gamarra returned as president of Peru and sought to annex Bolivia, a goal that ended on 18 November 1841 at Ingavi, where Bolivian forces under José Ballivián defeated and killed him. Peru then entered a period of instability, sometimes called the "military anarchy," from 1841 to 1845, with competing generals such as Manuel Menéndez, Juan Crisóstomo Torrico, Francisco de Vidal, and Manuel Ignacio de Vivanco, until Ramón Castilla prevailed at Carmen Alto in 1844 and became president in 1845.

Castilla's presidency, from 1845 to 1851, began a new phase. The guano deposits on Peru's coastal islands, exploited under contracts from the early 1840s, produced revenue that allowed the state to stabilize its finances, pay salaries, and settle debts. Guano revenue made Peru less dependent on Indigenous tribute and customs duties, although the contract system enriched merchants and created new dependencies. This transformation, which grew in the 1850s, is a key difference between Peru and its neighbors.

Bolivia, under Ballivián from 1841 to 1847 and then Manuel Isidoro Belzu from 1848, remained fiscally dependent on tribute and silver. Ballivián promoted administrative order and education; Belzu built a populist following among artisans and Indigenous communities, and his government marked a more popular style of caudillismo. Both states continued the search for stability, and both faced the consequences of a decade of conflict.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxes, Recruits, Petitions, and Debts in the Early Republics

In the Andean highlands, an Indigenous household's relation to the state was most visible at tribute time. Community leaders gathered payments, lists of tribute-payers were maintained, and disputes over who owed what were fought out before local officials. Households also provided labor, such as road repair and service for officials, that could be as burdensome as cash payments. In Ecuador and Peru, debts to hacendados or merchants bound families to estates.

Soldiers were recruited from villages, haciendas, and the plains, often by force. Conscription removed workers from fields and left women to manage households and crops. Women also sold food in markets, ran small businesses, and defended family property in court. Petitions to local authorities and courts, including complaints about taxes, requests for pensions after military service, claims to communal lands, and demands for manumission, show that ordinary people used legal institutions even when excluded from politics.

Port towns such as Guayaquil, Cartagena, La Guaira, Callao, and Arica were centers of commerce, rumor, and disease. Civil war disrupted harvests, requisitioned animals, and destroyed markets. The silences in official sources about these experiences, and the dominance of elite voices, mean that historians must read tax records, petitions, and notarial documents carefully to recover the lives of those who bore the burdens of republican state-building.

Section 60.46

Why Some Republican Institutions Survived and Others Collapsed

Comparison begins with the caution that stability is not a measure of merit. Venezuela's Conservative order lasted from 1830 to 1848 because Páez could arbitrate between army, oligarchy, and provinces, and because coffee exports supported a modest but regular revenue. New Granada's order rested on a lawyer-led elite, a tradition of constitutional argument, and a strong executive after 1843, but it endured a major civil war. Ecuador's system depended on a balance between a military caudillo and coastal civilians and was repeatedly challenged by regional rivalry. Peru and Bolivia lacked sustained civilian institutions in the 1830s and relied on generals.

Fiscal bases explain much. Bolivia's dependence on Indigenous tribute created a state that was stable but narrow, with little incentive to change land or labor relations. Peru's guano revenue after the 1840s offered an alternative that allowed the state to reduce reliance on tribute but encouraged contract-based enrichment and new disputes. Colombia's customs and monopolies tied it to Atlantic trade and the Magdalena, and Venezuela's coffee and cattle exports created a different commercial elite. Public finance shaped who could afford to maintain an army and whom the state needed to bargain with.

Other differences include the relationship between military and civilian authority, the strength of provincial autonomy, the role of the Church, and the degree of elite cohesion. In Venezuela, Páez's authority and the Conservatives' unity reduced conflict; in New Granada, religious and regional issues produced rebellion; in Ecuador, the Sierra-Coast division was decisive; in Peru, regional rivalries among Lima, Arequipa, and Cusco prevented consolidation. No simple ranking by stability captures these differences, since each state's institutions reflected particular bargains.

Section 60.47

Citizenship, Social Hierarchy, Slavery, and Indigenous Sovereignty after Independence

Republican citizenship was unequal. Constitutions in all five states limited the vote to adult men with property, income, or literacy, and sometimes all three. Women had no political rights but retained property rights under inherited law, and some, like Manuela Sáenz and Francisca Zubiaga, exercised political influence outside formal channels. In many places, the Afro-descended soldiers who had won independence found their claims to equality unmet, as Padilla's execution suggests.

Slavery's end was gradual and uneven. Colombia's free-womb law of 1821 began a slow process; Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador, and Peru abolished slavery only in the early and middle 1850s, after decades of continued bondage and compensation to enslavers. Enslaved people shaped this process through flight, litigation, military service, and revolt, and their actions pressured elites.

Indigenous peoples were governed under changing mixes of tribute, communal land protection, and liberal attempts at individual property. In Bolivia and Ecuador, tribute and corporate recognition endured; in New Granada, resguardos came under pressure; in Peru, tribute was restored after independence and abolished only in 1854. Indigenous communities used litigation, petitions, and sometimes rebellion to defend lands and customs. The republic's extension of state power was uneven, reaching markets, ports, and elite households more reliably than remote villages, and its promises of equality coexisted with enduring hierarchies.

Section 60.48

The Failure of Political Union and the Persistence of Andean Interdependence

Gran Colombia failed as a constitutional union, but the connections among its regions did not disappear. Trade continued along the Magdalena, the Pacific coast, and the Andean highlands. Legal traditions, military memories, and family ties crossed new borders. Bolívar's remains were transferred from Santa Marta to Caracas in December 1842, a gesture of reconciliation initiated by Páez, who had once opposed him. The memory of Bolívar became a shared symbol, even as each republic claimed it differently.

The unresolved borders and debts kept the Andean republics in a relationship of dispute and cooperation. Ecuador and Peru contested Jaén and Maynas; Venezuela and New Granada negotiated their frontier and the Guajira; the debts were serviced irregularly. The idea of Andean union also persisted: the Lima Congress of 1847 and 1848, attended by Bolivia, Chile, Ecuador, New Granada, and Peru, tried to create common defensive and diplomatic arrangements, though its treaties were not widely ratified. These efforts show that the idea of union survived the failure of the particular constitutional union attempted in 1819.

The chapter's central lesson is that winning independence, forming a republic, maintaining political union, and building an effective state were separate achievements. Gran Colombia achieved the first two and failed at the third and fourth. Its successors built states of different kinds, from Venezuela's caudillo-oligarchy to New Granada's lawyerly republic, Ecuador's regional balance, Peru's guano state, and Bolivia's tribute state, and the variety of outcomes underlines that there was no single path from independence to nationhood.

Chapter Summary

This chapter began with a contradiction: a union formed by revolutionary victory and a charismatic leader that did not survive the leader's own lifetime. Gran Colombia was created in 1819 by a congress at Angostura before most of its territory was liberated, and organized in 1821 at Cúcuta as a centralist republic. Its strategic logic was strong: a single government could mobilize resources, command respect abroad, and complete the liberation of the Andes.

The union's governing structure divided executive authority between Bolívar, often absent on campaign, and Santander, who administered from Bogotá. Administrative integration, culminating in the twelve departments of 1824, imposed uniform institutions on regions separated by mountains and rivers. Public finance depended on customs, monopolies, and tribute, and London loans of 1822 and 1824 produced a debt that could not be serviced after 1826.

Regional opposition grew from fiscal burdens, conscription, commercial interests, and resentment of appointed officials. La Cosiata in 1826 showed that a regional commander could defy Bogotá with municipal backing; Bolívar's compromise left Páez in command. The Convention of Ocaña in 1828 failed to produce a constitution, and Bolívar assumed dictatorial powers, which polarized opinion further. The September conspiracy, Santander's exile, and Padilla's execution deepened divisions.

The war with Peru and the victory at Tarqui reflected the unfinished business of independence and the militarization of Andean politics. Venezuela separated in 1829 and 1830 under Páez, Ecuador in 1830 under Flores, and the Admirable Congress could not save the union. Sucre was murdered and Bolívar died in 1830. New Granada's reorganization in 1831 and 1832 completed the transition, and a convention in 1834 divided the debt.

Venezuela's Conservative oligarchy under Páez maintained order through a balance of caudillo, oligarchy, and army, surviving the Revolution of the Reforms but falling to the Monagas brothers in 1848. New Granada rebuilt under Santander, endured the War of the Supremes, centralized in 1843, and saw Liberal and Conservative programs emerge in 1848 and 1849. Ecuador navigated the division between Sierra and Coast, with Flores, Rocafuerte, and the March Revolution shaping its early politics.

Peru and Bolivia, though never part of Gran Colombia, were part of the same Andean political world. Peru rejected the Bolivarian constitution, adopted a liberal charter in 1828, and entered a period of military politics. Bolivia, under Sucre and then Santa Cruz, depended on tribute and silver. Santa Cruz's Confederation of 1836 to 1839 offered an alternative Andean union and was destroyed by Chile, Peruvian exiles, and internal opposition at Yungay.

Across all five republics, citizenship was unequal. Slavery persisted into the 1850s, Indigenous communities paid tribute and defended land, women were excluded from formal politics but participated in other ways, and Afro-descended soldiers were rewarded unevenly. Colonial institutions continued under republican names, and rural people experienced the state mostly through taxes and recruitment.

Comparing the successor states shows that stability depended on particular bargains among military leaders, elites, and regions, and that fiscal bases, from tribute to coffee to guano, shaped state capacity. The failure of union did not end Andean interdependence, and projects like the Lima Congress show that the idea survived. Interpretations of the dissolution remain divided between structural and political explanations, and students should hold both in view.

Chapter Reflection

Gran Colombia matters because it shows that the end of empire did not settle what would replace it. Bolívar's union was among the boldest political experiments of the independence era, and its collapse within a dozen years is not simply a story of personal failure. It reveals the limits of constitutional design when distance, debt, regional economies, and military patronage pull in different directions.

The chapter also shows that the successor republics were not failed versions of a single model. Venezuela's caudillo-oligarchy, New Granada's lawyerly republic, Ecuador's regional balance, Peru's military politics and guano state, and Bolivia's tribute state each reflected local bargains. Indigenous tribute, slavery, women's exclusion, and racial hierarchy persisted across them, and ordinary people experienced republican citizenship through taxes, recruitment, petitions, and local disputes more than through constitutions.

Many of the questions raised here return in later chapters: the quarrel between centralism and federalism, the relationship between military and civilian authority, the fiscal dependence on export commodities, and the unresolved borders. Chapter 61 turns to a different outcome of independence, the Brazilian Empire, where monarchy and slavery shaped a very different political order, and Chapter 65 returns to the Pacific rivalries that the Confederation's war helped create.

Students should carry forward the lesson that independence, republican government, political union, and effective state-building were separate achievements. The failure of one union did not end the connections among Andean peoples, and the memory of Bolívar's project would be invoked again by generations seeking regional integration.
