Oligarchies, Land, Labor, Immigration, and Indigenous Dispossession

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Port That Exported Wheat and Expelled Workers

In November 1902 the docks of Buenos Aires, among the busiest in the southern hemisphere, stopped working. Within days the strike had spread across the city, and within weeks the Argentine Congress had passed a law allowing the executive to expel foreign residents without a trial. The same state that advertised itself across Europe as a destination for immigrants had written a legal instrument for removing them.

The port was the visible joint of Argentina's export economy. Railways carried grain, hides, wool, and meat products from the Pampas to the river, and steamships carried them to Europe; in the opposite direction the ships brought manufactured goods, capital, and people. By 1902 a very large share of the working population of the capital had been born in Italy, Spain, or elsewhere in Europe. Port workers loaded the cargo that paid for the country's imports and for the interest on its foreign loans. When they struck, they interrupted the system that the political elite regarded as the source of national progress, and the government enacted Residence Law No. 4,144 on 22 November, granting extensive powers to expel foreign residents, and followed it on 24 November with state-of-siege Law No. 4,145. The episode compresses the argument of this chapter: Latin America's export boom created wealth and employment, and it also created a society in which access to land, political voice, and legal protection was sharply unequal.

Chapter 68 explained how commodity exports, railways, foreign investment, and commercial institutions transformed the region's economies between roughly 1850 and 1914. This chapter asks what that transformation did to the people who lived through it. It follows five questions. Who captured the wealth and converted it into political power? How did land, once held in common, by the church, by the crown's successors, or by Indigenous nations, come to be concentrated in private hands? Who supplied the labor, and under what conditions? Why did millions of Europeans, and smaller numbers of Asians and Caribbean and internal migrants, move to export zones? And how did workers, rural communities, and Indigenous peoples resist, bargain, or adapt?

The chapter treats Indigenous peoples as historical actors with political institutions, diplomacy, and strategies of their own, not as obstacles removed from the path of national development. The Mapuche and the peoples of the Pampas and Patagonia had negotiated with American-descended states for generations before the military campaigns of the 1860s–1880s. Andean communities had a long history of petitioning courts and officials. Amazonian peoples met the rubber economy as targets of coercion, but also as workers, intermediaries, and refugees. Afro-descendant communities, freed from slavery in Cuba in 1886 and Brazil in 1888, entered labor markets that often preferred other workers. Women appear throughout, as wage earners, domestic workers, household managers, migrants, organizers, and members of mutual-aid societies, not only as dependents in male-centered labor systems.

Historians read this period through very different sources. Land laws, census returns, and notarial and court records come from states and elites and measure what officials chose to count. Immigration registers record arrivals but not feelings. Newspapers of the labor movement, petitions by Indigenous communities, testimony gathered after atrocities, and photographs and memoirs add other voices, but each has its own purpose and silences. Racial theory of the period is a source in a different sense: it shows how educated elites justified hierarchy, and it must be studied as doctrine, not accepted as description. The chapter distinguishes what laws said, what administrators did, and what ordinary people experienced.

Two cautions run through the narrative. First, privatization of land was not identical to dispossession in every case: some laws recognized existing occupants and some smallholders gained secure title. The outcome depended on administration, cost, legal knowledge, and local power. Second, neither migration nor wage work meant freedom from coercion. A European family under a coffee contract, a Quechua-speaking tenant in a highland hacienda, and a cane cutter on a Caribbean island were all part of one global commodity system, and their situations differed greatly.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each: oligarchic power and property (69.1–69.4); territorial expansion and Indigenous dispossession (69.5–69.8); immigration and labor systems (69.9–69.12); cities, race, and everyday life (69.13–69.16); and resistance and reform (69.17–69.20). It concentrates on 1870–1914, with background from 1850 and limited discussion of what followed. It does not retell the economics of Chapter 68, the foreign policy of Chapter 70, or the political crisis of the Díaz regime in Chapter 71; Mexican examples are used comparatively and left open for that chapter. The last section returns to the Buenos Aires docks and asks why a period of exceptional material growth ended in demands for reform and revolution.

Historical Context

Chapters 58 through 62 described the difficulties of creating stable republics after independence: contested constitutions, regional rivalries, caudillos, civil wars, and fiscal weakness. By the 1870s many of these conflicts had eased in the larger states, partly because export revenues finally gave governments the means to pay armies and officials. Chapter 68 showed how coffee, sugar, nitrates, beef, wheat, bananas, minerals, and rubber, together with foreign capital and railways, tied Latin America to industrializing Europe and the United States. Chapters 61 and 66 traced Brazil's empire and the abolition of slavery in 1888, and Chapter 67 the end of Spanish rule in Cuba and Puerto Rico. This chapter takes those developments as given.

The export boom did not begin from a blank slate. Colonial and early republican land systems included Indigenous community lands, church estates, municipal commons, large private estates, and public land that states claimed but did not control. Labor was organized through slavery in Brazil and Cuba until the 1880s, through tribute and customary obligation in the Andes, through debt and residence on estates in much of Spanish America, and through forced and indentured labor of Asians in Peru and Cuba. A liberal political program, associated with figures such as Benito Juárez in Mexico and Domingo Sarmiento in Argentina, held that private property, immigration, education, and free labor would produce progress. The program had Indigenous and Afro-descendant opponents as well as conservative ones, and its application often depended on the same local elites it claimed to modernize.

Between about 1870 and 1914 several features of the export economy shaped social structure. Railways raised the value of land near their lines and opened frontiers. Foreign and domestic credit favored large borrowers. Commodity prices fluctuated, and workers and smallholders bore the shock first. Governments, funded by customs duties on trade, could afford to expand armies, police, bureaucracies, schools, and public works, and they often did so in ways that favored the export sector. In the 1890s and 1900s the main cities grew rapidly, and wage labor, strikes, and political reform movements followed.

Scholarship has revised older interpretations of the period. Dependency theorists of the 1960s and 1970s emphasized external control and underdevelopment; later economic historians stressed that export growth was real, that some countries achieved high per-capita incomes by 1914, and that domestic institutions mattered. Social historians have added that land, labor, and race were negotiated locally. This chapter follows the view that growth and inequality were produced by the same processes, and that outcomes varied with each country's land tenure, labor supply, and political structure.

Oligarchic Power, Property, and Social Hierarchy

Central question: How did economic elites convert control over land, commerce, and capital into political influence, and why did the resulting systems vary among Latin American countries?

Section 69.1

Export Wealth and the Formation of New Economic Elites

Export booms enriched different groups in different places. In Argentina, the owners of estancias on the humid Pampas profited from wool, then from cattle and, increasingly, from leasing land to tenant farmers who grew wheat and maize. Their organization, the Sociedad Rural Argentina, founded in 1866, was a lobbying body and a social club. In São Paulo, coffee planters in the western plateau, who had moved from the exhausted Paraíba Valley, built a new economy based on fertile soils and railways. In Chile, wealth came from copper, nitrates in the north after the War of the Pacific, and wheat, and landed families invested in banks, mines, and urban property. In Mexico, hacendados in the north and center expanded cattle, sugar, and henequen holdings in the Yucatán, while a group of financiers and officials linked to the regime of Porfirio Díaz handled foreign concessions. In El Salvador, Guatemala, Costa Rica, and Colombia, coffee made landowning families rich, though in Costa Rica and parts of Antioquia smallholder coffee was also important.

The relationship between land and other forms of wealth was close. A landowner needed credit to plant coffee trees that took years to bear, so banks, commercial houses, and exporting firms, many of them foreign, became the owners of mortgages and the arbiters of prices. Merchants often owned land, and landowners founded banks and insurance companies. The railway was a particular nexus: lines raised the value of the land they crossed, and owners and investors lobbied for routes, concessions, and subsidies. Mining entrepreneurs, such as the Chilean and Bolivian owners of nitrate and silver and tin enterprises, formed another group whose wealth depended on foreign capital and on labor recruitment from rural districts.

Historians distinguish old landholding families from newly wealthy commercial and industrial groups, though the two overlapped through marriage and investment. In Argentina, the established families of Buenos Aires province were joined by immigrants who made fortunes in commerce and industry, and a small number of foreign-born businessmen became important, yet political power remained concentrated among native-born landowners. In Brazil, the paulista planter class also controlled finance and, increasingly, industry. In Peru, the Civilista party, formed around Manuel Pardo in 1871, represented coastal planters, merchants, and bankers; its president took office in 1872 as the first civilian elected head of state. Elite composition differed by country, and so did the extent to which landowners were willing to share power with merchants and professionals.

Elites must be distinguished from the middle classes, a much larger and more varied group. Clerks, teachers, small merchants, shopkeepers, military officers, lawyers, physicians, and public employees expanded in number as cities and states grew. Some depended on the patronage of the wealthy; others, especially professionals and immigrants, formed organizations of their own and later supported reformist parties. The Argentine Radical Civic Union, founded in 1891, drew support from this milieu, as did later movements in Chile and Uruguay. Middle-class people were not oligarchs, though they could share elite prejudices against Indigenous peoples and manual laborers.

Evidence for elite formation includes tax and land registers, notarial records, bank and company archives, family papers, and the membership lists of clubs and associations. These documents reveal property and connections but say little about the workers whose labor created the wealth, which is one reason the later sections turn to petitions, strike records, and census returns for other perspectives.

Section 69.2

Oligarchic Republics, Political Institutions, and Restricted Participation

Oligarchy in this chapter is an analytical term for rule by a small, economically powerful group, and it does not name a single type of constitution. Most Latin American states had republican constitutions, elections, and legislatures. What made them oligarchic was that the effective right to choose, and to be chosen, was restricted by literacy and property requirements, open or manipulated voting, patronage networks, and coercion, and that policy generally reflected landowning and commercial interests. Some systems retained real competition within the elite; others concentrated power in a president.

Argentina after 1880 is the clearest case of a government of notables managing elections. The Partido Autonomista Nacional, which consolidated under Julio Roca, dominated national politics until 1916. Its strength lay in agreements among provincial governors, who controlled local police, electoral registers, and officials, and who in turn supported the president. Voting was open and verbal for much of the period, and fraud, purchase of votes, and the use of the so-called "voto cantado" were common. The system was constitutional in form, and some presidents, notably Roca, were genuine national builders. It also provoked opposition: the Revolution of the Park in 1890, which failed militarily but forced the resignation of President Miguel Juárez Celman, led to the founding of the Radicals, who demanded clean elections and boycotted the system for decades.

Brazil's First Republic, established after the military-backed overthrow of the monarchy on 15 November 1889, adopted a federal constitution in 1891 that gave states control over taxes, militias, and land. Voting was open, and the franchise excluded illiterates, who were the majority of adults. Under President Campos Sales (1898–1902), the "politics of the governors" traded federal support for state-level loyalty, and the large states of São Paulo and Minas Gerais exerted the greatest influence. The popular phrase "coffee with milk" captures only part of the arrangement, since presidential succession did not always alternate between the two states, but it points to a structure in which regional oligarchies managed national politics.

Mexico under Porfirio Díaz, president for most of the years from 1876 to 1911, rested on a different arrangement. Elections were held but their results were arranged; power was personalized, with Díaz balancing regional bosses, army officers, and technocratic advisers, and relying on political prefects, the rural police known as the rurales, and the policy summarized by contemporaries as "bread or the club." Chapter 71 examines the regime's crisis; here the point is comparative: the Mexican system was more centralized and personal than Argentina's governor-based machine, and it offered fewer channels for organized opposition. In Central America, regimes varied. Guatemala under Justo Rufino Barrios (1873–1885), and later Manuel Estrada Cabrera (1898–1920), concentrated authority in the president and used coffee policy to build a labor supply. El Salvador's coffee growers controlled the state through a small circle of families and the army. Costa Rica had a more competitive electoral politics, though suffrage was restricted.

Military power operated differently across these states. In Argentina and Brazil, professional armies became more important after the wars of the 1860s–1880s, and civilian politicians controlled them through promotion, patronage, and shared elite background. In Mexico the Díaz regime reduced the army's autonomy but depended on it. In Central America, generals often held the presidency. Civilians in oligarchic systems seldom relied on arms alone, but violence, or its possibility, underlay the arrangements.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Oligarchic Rule Facilitate Development, or Mainly Protect Elite Interests?

The question: Were restricted political systems a stage that provided order, investment security, and administrative capacity for economic growth, or did they mainly entrench the privileges of landowners and financiers while limiting broader development?

Interpretation A — Order and growth: Some historians argue that oligarchic governments ended the civil wars of the early republican decades, built railways, ports, and bureaucracies, protected contracts, and attracted capital and migrants. Argentina, Chile, and parts of Brazil reached high export growth and urbanization, and elites in these cases also invested in schools and public works.
Interpretation B — Elite capture and limited development: Others emphasize that policy favored export interests, that land concentration and restricted suffrage blocked competing demands, that taxes fell on consumption more than on property, and that industrialization, schooling, and rural welfare were underfunded. On this view growth was real but narrowly shared and vulnerable to commodity shocks.

Evidence: Both interpretations use customs and fiscal records, electoral returns, investment data, land registers, literacy and income estimates, and comparisons of countries. Evidence that states built infrastructure and kept order supports A; evidence on land distribution, voting, and taxation supports B. The two are not mutually exclusive: many scholars hold that oligarchic governments both promoted growth and shaped it to elite advantage.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians interpret the later pressures for reform and revolution, and whether those pressures are seen as reactions to failure or as consequences of success.

Student task: Choose one country and identify one fact that supports each interpretation. Then specify what kind of evidence about who paid taxes and who received public spending would help decide between them.

Section 69.3

Land Laws, Privatization, and the Concentration of Rural Property

Liberal governments in the second half of the century treated land held in common, by the church, or by customary right as obstacles to productivity and to the creation of citizens who were independent property holders. Legal change followed in nearly every state, but the consequences depended on who could use the law. Individual title was valuable to smallholders who could register and defend it, and dangerous to communities that had held land collectively without individual titles or the money to survey and register parcels.

In Mexico, the Lerdo Law of 1856 required civil and ecclesiastical corporations to sell land they did not directly use, and the Constitution of 1857 confirmed the principle. The law was aimed first at the church, but it also covered Indigenous villages' corporate lands, whose members often failed to claim titles in time. Later laws on vacant land (terrenos baldíos) and the 1883 law on surveying companies authorized private companies to survey public land in return for a share, commonly one-third, of the territory measured. A small number of holders obtained very large tracts, particularly in the north and south, and the practice also exposed villages whose customary claims lacked documents.

Brazil's Land Law of 1850 ended the acquisition of public land by simple occupation, except for existing holdings that were registered, and required purchase. Its aims included raising revenue to subsidize immigration and ensuring that land would not be available to freed or poor people as an alternative to estate labor. Registration through parish priests was incomplete and fraud was common, and large fazendeiros claimed more than they had occupied. In Guatemala the liberal regime of 1871 encouraged coffee by distributing public and some communal land, and supplemented land policy with labor laws, including a 1877 regulation of day laborers (jornaleros) that required highland Maya communities to provide workers to coffee estates. Land and labor policy worked together there, which was less true in Argentina, where the problem was land distribution on an open frontier.

El Salvador's legislation is among the most debated, and it deserves attention to its actual terms. A decree of 1881 declared the extinction of the legal form of Indigenous and peasant communal landholding (comunidades), and a law of 1882 extended the same logic to municipal ejidos. The texts ordered division of the land into individual parcels, gave priority to those already working plots, and provided for the sale of land that was not claimed. They did not state an intention simply to seize every community's land; they were framed as a conversion to private property that would encourage coffee and agriculture. In practice, legal knowledge, surveying costs, and the power of local officials favored large growers and speculators, and many communal members lost land, while some obtained individual titles. Because Salvadoran coffee required particular altitudes, the effects varied by district. Students should therefore distinguish three things: the legal text, the process of its implementation, and the long-term distribution of land, which was highly concentrated by the early twentieth century.

The two Salvadoran statutes differed in mechanism. The communal-land measure, promulgated on 24 February 1881, directed division among co-owners in proportion to their contributions toward acquisition, or equally per head if contributions could not be established; Article 3 treated existing possessors with legitimate claims as owners of the land they occupied. The 1882 ejido act instead required many current possessors to compensate the municipality by paying the equivalent of six annual ground rents, with a possible term of up to four years bearing nine percent legal interest; holders without an established rent could obtain ownership without that payment. It obliged municipalities to identify undistributed plots within six months, provided for auction, and also set a six-month deadline for possessors to obtain title before risking loss of possessory rights. These were consequential conditions, not a generic instruction simply to privatize unused land.

Historical Vocabulary
Latifundio, Ejido, and Comunidad

A latifundio is a very large landed estate, often only partly cultivated; the corresponding smallholding is a minifundio. An ejido in colonial and nineteenth-century Spanish America was land held in common by a municipality or village for pasture, wood, and other uses. A comunidad (community) was a legally recognized collective with its own authorities and land rights, especially in the Andes and Central America. Note that the twentieth-century Mexican ejido, created by agrarian reform after the Revolution, was a different institution.

These terms matter because liberal laws defined collective holdings as obsolete. Misreading them as simple "unused land" repeats the reformers' own argument.

Section 69.4

Local Power: Hacendados, Coronéis, Caciques, and Regional Political Networks

National governments in this period were small relative to the territory they claimed. Their authority was exercised locally through landowners, municipal officials, judges, police chiefs, and military commanders who combined private power with public office. The relationship ran in both directions: central governments needed local brokers to deliver votes, collect taxes, and keep order, and local brokers needed national patrons to confirm their offices and protect their interests.

In Brazil this system is called coronelismo, from the title coronel given to local notables, historically officers of the National Guard. A coronel was usually a large landowner who offered work, credit, protection, and favors to dependents, and who in return delivered their votes, supported by control over local officials. State governors rewarded coronéis who delivered loyal majorities. The term describes an exchange between a weak but expanding state and powerful local figures, not simple tyranny, though it was often backed by violence and fraud. Mexico had its own regional bosses, from governors and jefes políticos to caciques of rural districts, who were selected or approved by the president and given latitude in return for loyalty. In the Andes, landlords and local political fixers, called gamonales in Peru and Bolivia, held power over Indigenous peasants, courts, and officials, often through control of labor and of access to the law. Central American regimes relied on military commanders, plantation owners, and local officials in similar ways.

It is important to separate several kinds of authority. Personal patronage rested on reciprocity and dependence; coercion rested on threat or force; electoral control rested on manipulation of registers, voting, and counting; and legally recognized authority came from office. In practice they were mixed. A coronel might act as a legal official, a creditor, and a patron, and a peasant might see him as protector and as master at once. Resistance and bargaining happened within these relationships, for example when tenants moved, withheld votes, or appealed to higher officials.

The variety of systems also reflected differences in rural society. In Argentina's Pampas, a commercial economy of estancias and tenant farms with wage labor and immigrants generated strong provincial machines but fewer personal dependents than in the Brazilian interior. In Mexico's southern haciendas and the Andean highlands, resident peons and tenants lived on estate land and were more dependent on the landlord. Where smallholders predominated, as in parts of Costa Rica and Colombia's Antioquia, local power was less concentrated.

Historical Vocabulary
Oligarquía and Coronelismo

Oligarquía was used by contemporary critics, such as Argentine and Peruvian reformers, to describe rule by a narrow circle of wealthy families, and later by historians as an analytical term. It does not indicate a single constitutional form. Coronelismo names the Brazilian system of exchange between local landed notables and state governments in the First Republic, in which votes and loyalty were traded for jobs, favors, and recognition.

Both terms help avoid two errors: treating elites as a unified class that agreed on everything, and treating local power as separate from national politics.

Territorial Expansion and Indigenous Dispossession

Central question: How did states and commercial interests transform Indigenous territories into agricultural, pastoral, and extraction frontiers, and what forms of resistance and adaptation followed?

Section 69.5

Argentina's Conquest of the Desert and the Expansion into Patagonia

The phrase "Conquest of the Desert" was the name given by its promoters to Argentine campaigns against Indigenous peoples of the Pampas and Patagonia. "Desert" in this usage meant land outside state control, not an empty land: the Pampas, Neuquén, and northern Patagonia were home to Mapuche-speaking, Ranquel, Tehuelche, and other peoples, with chiefly leadership, horse and cattle economies, trade with Chile, and relations with Argentine officials. For decades before the 1870s, the frontier had been a zone of raiding and trading, of treaties and rations, and of "friendly Indians" who served in provincial forces. Leaders such as Calfucurá, who died in 1873, built confederations that bargained with Buenos Aires. Argentine officials sometimes paid rations in return for peace, and the frontier was always contested by settlers, ranchers, and the state.

The balance changed after the 1860s. The War of the Triple Alliance had enlarged the army, and the telegraph, railways, and repeating rifles gave the state new means. Minister of War Adolfo Alsina favored a gradual advance marked by forts and a long defensive ditch, the zanja de Alsina. After his death in December 1877, his successor Julio Roca argued for a decisive offensive to the Río Negro. The Congress passed Law 947 in October 1878, authorizing funds for the advance and securing them against the lands to be gained, a financing technique that tied conquest directly to land sales. Roca's 1879 campaign, in several columns, pushed Indigenous groups from the Pampas and fixed a new line along the Río Negro. Scholars point out, however, that the campaigns did not end in 1879: operations continued into Neuquén, Patagonia, and the Chaco through the mid-1880s, and the last major leaders, among them Sayhueque, Inacayal, and Foyel, surrendered in 1884–1885.

Indigenous peoples did not face these campaigns without strategy. Some leaders negotiated with the state, while others, including groups who had once been allies, chose to fight or flee across the Andes. Some Indigenous men served as auxiliaries in Roca's forces; others were killed or captured by them. Casualty numbers are uncertain and contested, and estimates for those killed and captured vary widely. What is well documented is the treatment of the captured. Prisoners were marched to detention sites, including Martín García island in the Río de la Plata. Families were separated, and women and children were distributed as domestic servants to households in Buenos Aires and other cities, while men were sent to labor in sugar mills in Tucumán and to military service. Some leaders and their remains were sent to museums for scientific study. This was a process of dispossession and forced incorporation, not peaceful settlement.

The land taken was largely transferred to private owners. The state sold or granted large tracts, often through the bonds issued under Law 947, to a limited number of buyers, many of them already wealthy and well connected, and the new lands were used for sheep and cattle, and later for grain. The expansion enlarged export capacity and provided the basis of fortunes. It also gave the national government a physical sovereignty it had not had, and strengthened the political prestige of Roca, who was elected president in 1880. Indigenous communities that survived often lived on the margins of estates or in small reservations, and generations later they continued to petition for recognition and land.

The 1878 financing statute made the link between conquest and property especially explicit. Article 1 authorized spending up to 1,600,000 pesos fuertes; Article 2 charged the expense against proceeds of public lands to be incorporated. Articles 5–10 provided for a public subscription, interest-bearing titles and later allocation of surveyed lots, including blocks of 10,000 hectares. The law also included a provision for reserving lands for towns and for Indigenous people who submitted to state authority. These provisions should not be read as proof of equitable settlement: they reveal a framework in which military occupation, anticipated land value, and investor subscription were institutionally connected.

Section 69.6

Chile's Occupation of Araucanía and Mapuche Territorial Dispossession

South of the Bío-Bío River, the Mapuche had retained political autonomy since the sixteenth century (Chapter 38), through warfare, treaty making (parlamentos), and commerce with the Spanish and later the Chilean state. In the 1850s and 1860s, this autonomy came under pressure from several directions: Chilean settlers and speculators eyed fertile land, the wheat boom increased demand for it, and the state, now richer, felt able to extend its authority. A French adventurer, Orélie-Antoine de Tounens, proclaimed a kingdom of Araucanía and Patagonia in 1860 with the support of some Mapuche leaders, which Chilean officials treated as a provocation.

In 1861 Colonel Cornelio Saavedra proposed an advance by stages, with forts, towns, and a gradual occupation, which began in 1862 with the founding of Angol. Between 1862 and the late 1860s the army pushed the frontier to the Malleco River, and Mapuche leaders resisted, notably in the uprising of 1868–1869. The campaign was interrupted by the War of the Pacific (1879–1883), after which the army returned its attention south. Under General Gregorio Urrutia, forces founded Temuco in 1881 and advanced further. Mapuche groups rose in a major rebellion in late 1881 and 1882, attacking forts and settlements, and were defeated; in 1883 the army refounded Villarrica. Mapuche leaders were divided: some fought, some negotiated, and some, such as Venancio Coñoepán, cooperated with the state to protect their communities' interests. Historians describe the process as warfare, negotiated accommodation, colonization, and legal restructuring, and not simply as pacification.

Occupation was followed by land redistribution. The state auctioned large tracts to Chilean and foreign buyers and sponsored European colonists, among them Germans and Swiss in the south, while confining the Mapuche to reducciones, small areas granted to families under títulos de merced (grants of favor). A commission for settling Indigenous people (Comisión Radicadora de Indígenas) worked from 1884 into the early twentieth century, awarding several thousand titles. The frequently cited total, approximately 475,194 hectares in 3,078 titles according to one Chilean National Library series (sometimes rounded to 500,000 hectares), refers to the broader titling process extending from 1884 through 1929, not to land already granted by 1914. It was a small fraction of the territory the Mapuche had used; titles often recognized collectively held reducciones subject to specific legal restrictions. Boundaries were often drawn without regard to families' actual use, and many Mapuche found their land surrounded by estates whose owners encroached further, through purchase, fraud, or force.

The impact on Mapuche life was severe. Cattle trade and seasonal movement were curtailed, communities lost access to forests, rivers, and ceremonial sites, and many men became wage workers on estates or migrated to cities. Women's work in weaving, farming, and exchange continued but within smaller land bases. Yet the Mapuche remained a living society. They used courts, petitions, and eventually organizations such as the Sociedad Caupolicán Defensora de la Araucanía, founded in 1910, to defend land. They preserved language, kinship, and ritual in the reducciones, laying the foundation for later Mapuche political movements.

Section 69.7

Andean Communal Lands, Haciendas, and Indigenous Resistance in Bolivia, Peru, and Ecuador

In the Andes the colonial arrangement that gave Indigenous communities (comunidades) land in return for tribute and labor survived into the republic in modified form (Chapters 40 and 58). After independence, states often continued Indigenous tribute because it was a major source of revenue. Liberal pressure to abolish it, and to replace collective holdings with individual property, grew after mid-century.

Bolivia's Law of Exvinculación of 5 October 1874 is the principal example. It recognized the right of members of Indigenous communities to hold individual parcels, provided for individual titles and related fiscal obligations. Article 7 withdrew legal recognition from a community or ayllu only once individual property titles had been issued: it did not instantaneously abolish every community on the day the statute passed. The law was a reformulation of earlier attempts, among them President Mariano Melgarejo's 1866 decree, which had ordered communal lands sold and had been reversed after his fall. Implementation was slow, since the tin and silver economy and the War of the Pacific diverted attention, but it accelerated in the 1880s and after, when surveyors and officials visited highland districts. Many communities lost land to speculators, estate owners, and officials, who exploited the legal gap between individual and collective claims. Some Indigenous families obtained individual titles and kept land, and some communities avoided registration by using intermediaries.

In Peru, the post-1879 defeat in the War of the Pacific weakened the central state and strengthened regional landlords. Indigenous tribute had been abolished in 1854 and reintroduced in other forms, and highland haciendas expanded at the expense of free communities, aided by gamonales who controlled local courts and officials and recruited labor. A major uprising in Ancash in 1885, associated with Pedro Pablo Atusparia, began as a protest against labor and tax demands and showed the capacity of Indigenous leaders to mobilize across districts. In Ecuador, the hacienda economy of the highlands bound resident families through the huasipungo, a plot of land given in exchange for labor, and the church and the state were large landowners. Fernando Daquilema led a rebellion in Chimborazo in 1871 against labor and tithe obligations, and was executed in 1872. After the Liberal Revolution of 1895, the state restricted the church's lands and later abolished imprisonment for debt, but conditions for most highland Indigenous families changed slowly.

Indigenous communities did not simply lose land; they used law and politics. Community authorities collected colonial-era titles, hired lawyers and agents, petitioned governments, and traveled to capitals to present claims. In Bolivia, representatives known as caciques apoderados (empowered chiefs) assembled documents and pursued cases for communities. Alliances with outside parties could also be tactical: in the Federal War of 1899, Aymara leader Pablo Zárate Willka mobilized communities in alliance with Liberal forces, hoping to recover land, and the alliance broke down when the Liberals won and the new government did not meet Indigenous demands. The episode also showed the fear that Indigenous mobilization produced among elites.

Historical Vocabulary
Gamonalismo and Indigenous Land Claims

Gamonalismo is the Andean system of local landlord-centered power, named for the gamonal (originally a parasitic plant). The Peruvian writer José Carlos Mariátegui later analyzed it as a social and political order, not just the acts of individual landlords. Indigenous land claims in the period rested on colonial titles, on custom and long possession, and on legal arguments about community rights.

The term helps explain why the formal equality of republican law coexisted with rural subordination: law was administered locally, often by those who benefited from the arrangement.

Section 69.8

Amazonian Frontiers, Rubber Extraction, and Indigenous Communities

The Amazon basin was not a single frontier. The rubber boom, which ran from about 1879 to 1912, drew on the latex of wild trees in the forests of Brazil, Peru, Bolivia, and parts of Colombia, and it created several distinct labor systems. Manaus and Belém, on the Brazilian Amazon, grew into prosperous export cities, while Iquitos in Peru and Riberalta in Bolivia became commercial centers. Rubber prices were driven by industrial demand for tires and insulation in Europe and North America, and the collapse after about 1912, when plantation rubber from Southeast Asia entered the market, was abrupt.

The organizing institution in much of Brazil was the aviamento, a chain of credit. A trading house in Belém or Manaus advanced goods on credit to a middleman, who advanced them to the owner of rubber trails (seringalista), who advanced them to tappers (seringueiros). Tappers, many of them migrants from the drought-stricken northeast, particularly Ceará after the drought of 1877–1879, were paid in latex but bought supplies from the estate store at high prices, which kept them indebted. Debt did not always prevent departure, but distance and isolation, and the control of boats, made leaving difficult. Indigenous peoples were pushed from river lands, some worked in the rubber economy, and others were enslaved or exposed to epidemics.

Territorial disputes accompanied extraction. In the Acre region, Brazilian rubber tappers and Bolivian authorities clashed, and the Acre War of 1899–1903 ended with the Treaty of Petrópolis, by which Bolivia ceded the territory to Brazil in return for compensation. Bolivian rubber was largely controlled by firms such as that of Nicolás Suárez in the Beni and Riberalta region, which dominated local trade and labor. Indigenous groups such as the Tacana and Cavineña were recruited, displaced, or enslaved, and recruitment practices varied across districts.

The Putumayo, along the border between Peru and Colombia, became notorious. The Peruvian merchant Julio César Arana built a company, registered in London in 1907 as the Peruvian Amazon Company, that exploited the Witoto (Huitoto), Bora, Andoke, Ocaina, and other peoples through forced labor, flogging, killings, and sexual violence, under armed overseers, many of them recruited from the Caribbean island of Barbados. Allegations were published in 1909 by an American engineer, Walter Hardenburg, and by Peruvian journalists, and the British government sent consul Roger Casement, who investigated in 1910 and reported in 1912. Casement documented widespread brutality and severe population loss. An often repeated estimate of about 30,000 deaths over the period remains unverified: incomplete starting population figures and the difficulty of separating killings, disease, displacement, and flight prevent a reliable precise total. What is established is that systematic atrocity occurred, that company officials were implicated, and that few of those responsible were punished. The case shows the weakness of frontier governments, the role of foreign capital and international publicity, and the use of a language of "civilizing" Indigenous people to justify coercion.

Section 69.9

European Immigration to Argentina, Uruguay, and the Southern Cone

Between the 1850s and 1930 several million Europeans arrived in Argentina, and perhaps half stayed permanently. Argentina's 1876 immigration and colonization law, associated with President Nicolás Avellaneda, created an immigration commission, reception facilities, and subsidies, and officials in Buenos Aires and Europe promoted the country. Italians were the largest group, followed by Spaniards, with French, Germans, Eastern Europeans, Jews, and, from the Ottoman territories, Syrians and Lebanese contributing smaller numbers. Uruguay, with a much smaller population, also drew heavily on Italian and Spanish migrants, and Montevideo had a very large foreign-born share. Chile attracted far fewer, with a notable German settlement in the south tied to land policy in Araucanía.

Passage took weeks on steamships, with third-class passengers in crowded quarters, and arrival was managed through immigrant hotels near the port, where newcomers were housed for a few days while officials arranged employment and transport. Many settled in Buenos Aires, Rosario, and Montevideo, where they found work in construction, ports, workshops, commerce, and domestic service. Others moved to farming districts of Santa Fe, Córdoba, and Buenos Aires province, often as tenants or sharecroppers, because the best land was already in large holdings. Seasonal migrants, called golondrinas (swallows), crossed the Atlantic for the harvest and returned to Italy or Spain, taking advantage of reversed seasons in the two hemispheres. Return migration was substantial, and statistics on arrivals must therefore be distinguished from those on permanent settlement.

Opportunities existed. Wages in Argentina were higher than in southern Europe, and some immigrants built businesses, bought land, or achieved professional status; their children entered schools and the growing middle class. Others faced hard conditions: tenants had short leases and owed landlords crop shares, and the rent and costs of inputs absorbed profits. In cities, many lived in conventillos, crowded tenements where families shared rooms and one courtyard. Discrimination appeared in the form of suspicion of foreign radicals, prejudice against southern Italians, and, for Jewish and Arab migrants, social barriers. Immigrants organized mutual-aid societies, hospitals, schools, and newspapers, and many joined labor and political organizations. Women arrived as wives and as independent migrants, working as domestic servants, seamstresses, factory workers, and shopkeepers.

Another feature of immigration was political: because most immigrants did not become citizens, a large share of the adult male working population could not vote, a point developed in section 69.19. The 1914 census recorded about 29.9 percent of Argentina's population as foreign-born, and a far higher share in Buenos Aires.

Section 69.10

Brazilian Coffee Labor after Abolition: Immigration, Colonato, and Afro-Brazilian Workers

When Brazil abolished slavery on 13 May 1888 (Chapter 66), the coffee economy of the southeast was already shifting toward free labor. In the 1850s some planters had tried a partnership system with European families, and a revolt at the Ibicaba estate in 1856, publicized by the Swiss schoolteacher Thomas Davatz, had given the experiment a bad name in Europe. From the 1880s São Paulo's government and planters organized a subsidized immigration program: the provincial government and a planters' association, the Sociedade Promotora de Imigração, paid passage and operated a reception center, the Hospedaria dos Imigrantes in the Brás district of São Paulo, which opened in 1887. Immigrants arrived at the port of Santos, traveled by railway to the city, and were then assigned to estates. Italians were the largest group, followed by Portuguese, Spaniards, and later others, and the number of European arrivals in São Paulo grew dramatically between the late 1880s and the early twentieth century. Counts vary by dates, place of registration, nationality, and whether re-entries are included; the total processed by the Hospedaria over its many decades cannot be used as a figure for 1888–1914 alone.

The dominant arrangement on estates was the colonato. A family signed a contract that combined several kinds of payment: an annual sum for the care of a given number of coffee trees, a payment per unit of harvested coffee, a house, and a plot on which to grow food and sometimes pasture for animals. The family, not the individual, was the labor unit, so women and children worked in cultivation and picking. Planters valued the system because it tied workers to the estate for the year and secured a flexible, family-based labor force. Workers valued access to food plots and the chance to save, and some did buy land or move to towns.

The system was also an arena of conflict. Disputes arose over wage deductions, store prices, the quality of housing, the measurement of harvests, restrictions on movement, and the treatment of workers by overseers. Some immigrants left estates after a season, moved to other farms, or went to the city. Italian authorities, concerned by reports of abuse, restricted subsidized emigration to Brazil by the Prinetti decree in 1902, which reduced Italian arrivals. Workers' letters and complaints to consuls are valuable evidence of these conditions, though they represent one side, and planters' records are another.

The postabolition experience of formerly enslaved and free Black Brazilians differed. Many freedpeople left plantations in 1888 and after, seeking to control their time, settle with families, or work in different districts. Some remained on estates as workers or tenants, and others moved to cities and towns. In São Paulo, planters and officials preferred European immigrants, justified by racial ideology and by the belief that subsidized Europeans would "improve" the population, as section 69.15 explains. Employment in the coffee zone was thus racialized: Afro-Brazilian workers were often excluded from subsidized recruitment and from the most favorable contracts, and they were concentrated in casual labor, domestic service, and informal work. Black communities responded by forming brotherhoods, clubs, and a Black press in São Paulo in the early twentieth century. Black men and women continued to work in the coffee economy, in many areas, and the generalization that European immigrants "replaced" them is therefore too simple.

Section 69.11

Internal Migration, Asian Migration, and the Changing Geography of Labor

Much of the movement of people in the export era took place within countries. Rural families moved to cities in Argentina, Brazil, Chile, and Mexico; highland Andean villagers worked seasonally on coastal sugar and cotton plantations and in mines; peasants from central Chile moved north to the nitrate fields, where laborers known as pampinos worked in harsh desert conditions; and northeastern Brazilians moved to the Amazon and to São Paulo. Mining districts, such as the silver and tin regions of Bolivia and the copper mines of northern Mexico and Chile, recruited workers from surrounding communities, sometimes through contractors, and workers often combined mining with farming at home. Recruitment through labor brokers using cash advances, called enganche in Peru and Mexico, is examined in section 69.12.

Asian migration had two phases that should not be conflated. From the late 1840s to the 1870s, tens of thousands of Chinese men, roughly 100,000 arrivals in Peru in 1849–1874 and approximately 125,000 survivors of the passage to Cuba in 1847–1874 (out of more than 141,000 embarked according to one reconstruction), were brought under contracts that often began through deception or coercion and imposed terms of eight years or more. They worked on sugar plantations, guano deposits, and railways, in conditions that investigations by Chinese and international officials in the early 1870s condemned, and that led to the end of the Peruvian and Cuban systems. Some Chinese workers resisted collectively or individually, some completed contracts and stayed as small traders and farmers, and during the War of the Pacific, Chinese workers in Peru were targets of violence. Later Chinese migration to Mexico, Peru, and elsewhere was largely voluntary but met increasing hostility.

Japanese migration began later and under different arrangements. The first group of contract workers reached Peru in 1899 on the ship Sakura Maru, and the first group to Brazil arrived on the Kasato Maru at Santos in 1908. Japanese migrants came with intermediary companies and negotiated through their government, and in Brazil they came in family groups for coffee estates. They too faced hard conditions, discrimination, and disputes over contracts, and many moved to small farming, commerce, and towns. In both countries, officials who favored European migration regarded Asian migrants with suspicion, and Brazil had restricted entry of Asians and Africans by a decree of 1890 that required congressional authorization.

The Caribbean and Central America saw other movements. Workers from Jamaica and other British West Indian islands moved to Costa Rica, Panama, Cuba, and the Caribbean coast of Central America to build railways and to work on banana plantations, often for U.S. firms such as those that later formed the United Fruit Company. They brought English-speaking Protestant communities, formed mutual-aid and religious institutions, and faced prejudice from local elites. The construction of the Panama Canal (Chapter 70) drew many more.

Section 69.12

Rural Labor Regimes: Peonage, Tenancy, Sharecropping, and Wage Work

Labor in the export zones was organized in several ways, and the terms used by contemporaries and historians must be kept distinct. Debt peonage bound a worker to an employer by an unpaid debt, often inherited or enlarged by advances and store purchases, and sometimes enforced by law or by threat. In Mexico, it was most severe on the henequen estates of Yucatán, where Maya workers and deported Yaquis labored, and in tobacco and rubber districts in the south; an American journalist, John Kenneth Turner, publicized it in 1910, though his account was polemical, and subsequent research confirms the existence of severe coercion in particular regions while showing that debt servitude was less general across the country than his book suggested. Enganche ("hooking") recruited workers by advancing money to those who agreed to work on distant plantations, and the advance could become a form of bondage when workers could not repay it. In Peru, enganche supplied coastal sugar and cotton estates with highland labor.

Inquilinaje in central Chile gave resident tenants (inquilinos) a house and a plot in return for labor on the estate, for themselves and for hired family members, and gave landlords a stable supply of work and, in many cases, political loyalty. In Ecuador the huasipungo, in Bolivia and Peru the colono (resident tenant) systems, and in Peru the yanaconaje (a form of sharecropping) combined access to land with labor obligations. In Brazil, the colonato (section 69.10) involved family contracts. Sharecropping and tenancy dominated parts of Argentina's wheat zone and Colombia's coffee estates, while smallholders, as in parts of Costa Rica and Colombia's Antioquia, produced coffee on their own plots. Seasonal wage work, such as the harvest of sugar, coffee, and grain, was widespread, and wages were sometimes paid in tokens usable at the estate store.

Family labor and gender shaped each system. Contracts treated the family as a unit, with men often receiving the formal payment, and women, children, and older relatives performing planting, weeding, harvesting, cooking, and care. Women also worked in processing, in markets, and in textile and craft production, and they ran households under conditions of heavy work. Control of housing, plots, and credit gave landlords power to restrict movement, because leaving meant losing a home and a food supply. Yet workers bargained: they changed estates, negotiated terms, withheld labor at critical moments, and used officials and courts when possible.

It would be wrong to treat all wage work as free, or all debt relationships as the same. A debt to a store in a village with competing merchants differed from one in an isolated plantation in which the employer controlled transport and law enforcement. Legal freedom to leave was, in practice, shaped by distance, local authorities, and the cost of departure.

Historical Vocabulary
Colonato, Enganche, and Debt Peonage

The colonato was São Paulo's family-based coffee labor contract described in section 69.10. Enganche is a recruitment system using cash advances to bind workers, especially in Peru and Mexico. Debt peonage (peonaje por deudas) refers to bondage created or maintained by debt. The three are not synonyms: the first was largely contractual, the second a recruitment method, and the third a condition of dependence that varied widely.

Using the terms precisely avoids both minimizing coercion and describing every dependent worker as enslaved.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Export Demand, Land Tenure, and Rural Labor

Long-term causes

Colonial estates, Indigenous tribute and customary obligations, slavery in Brazil and Cuba, and liberal ideas about private property and free labor.

Short-term causes

Rising world demand for coffee, sugar, wheat, meat, nitrates, minerals, and rubber; railway construction; foreign credit; and the end of slavery and tribute.

Event or transformation

Land laws and military expansion concentrated property, and recruiters and contracts assembled workers for export estates and mines.

Immediate effects

New wage jobs, migration, family contracts, and debt systems; loss of communal land for many Indigenous and peasant communities.

Long-term consequences

Persistent rural inequality, organized labor and agrarian protest, and demands for land reform in the twentieth century.

Historical complexity

The same growth created opportunity for some and dependence for others. Outcomes varied by region, crop, and local power.

Cities, Social Inequality, Race, and Everyday Life

Central question: How did modernization transform daily life and social identities, and why did expanding cities and public institutions fail to eliminate entrenched inequalities?

Section 69.13

Urbanization and the Transformation of Latin American Cities

Latin American cities grew quickly after 1880, fed by immigration, rural migration, and export revenue. Buenos Aires, with perhaps 180,000 residents in 1869, reached roughly 1.5 million by 1914. São Paulo, a modest town in 1880, grew with coffee, railways, and industry to a population in the hundreds of thousands by 1900. Santiago, Montevideo, Rio de Janeiro, and Mexico City expanded as well, though at different speeds. Port cities, such as Valparaíso, Santos, Veracruz, Guayaquil, and Havana, handled the commodity trade and became centers of commerce and labor.

Rulers and elites used public works to display progress. Buenos Aires under Mayor Torcuato de Alvear in the 1880s opened boulevards, and the Avenida de Mayo (1894) and later the Teatro Colón (1908) expressed civic ambition. Rio de Janeiro's mayor Francisco Pereira Passos, with the backing of the federal government, cut broad avenues through the old center in 1902–1906, demolishing tenements. Sanitation campaigns, led in Rio by the physician Oswaldo Cruz, fought yellow fever and smallpox; the compulsory smallpox vaccination law of 1904 provoked a violent popular protest, the Vaccine Revolt, reflecting public resentment at forced evictions and state intrusion. Cities built tram and rail networks, markets, water and sewer systems, hospitals, and schools. Buenos Aires's yellow fever epidemic of 1871, which killed many thousands, had been a spur to sanitary works.

These improvements coexisted with severe inequality. Housing did not keep pace with population, and rents rose. Many working families lived in conventillos in Buenos Aires and Montevideo, cortiços in Rio and São Paulo, and vecindades in Mexico City, where overcrowding and shared sanitation spread disease. Clearance of central tenements pushed some of the displaced to hillsides, and in Rio informal hillside settlements grew, including those later called favelas, where some veterans of the 1897 campaign at Canudos and other poor families settled. Elite districts, with paved streets and gas lighting, contrasted with poorer neighborhoods on the periphery and in the older center.

Cities were also centers of political and cultural change: newspapers, theaters, clubs, and unions. They concentrated workers who could organize, and officials who feared disorder. The same boulevards that displayed prosperity were routes for demonstrations.

The urban map was not a simple division between rich center and poor outskirts. A central business district might contain banks, clubs, department stores, print shops, municipal offices, markets, and nearby crowded lodging houses in which immigrant workers rented single rooms. Working-class families often lived close to docks, railway yards, or factories because walking was cheaper than paying a daily tram fare. In Buenos Aires, conventillo residents shared courtyards, wash facilities, and cooking space; landlords subdivided older houses to collect numerous rents. Complaints about crowding and sanitation formed part of a political struggle over rents, housing inspection, and the rights of tenants, not merely a problem of deficient infrastructure.

São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro illustrate different paths of urban change. Coffee revenue, workshops, textile mills, railway employment, and inward migration helped São Paulo expand beyond its old core. In the federal capital, Pereira Passos’s reconstruction of Rio after 1902 widened avenues and altered old neighborhoods, displacing residents who had few affordable alternatives. The demolition of tenements and the regulation of street commerce could be celebrated by officials as hygiene and progress while experienced by residents as loss of home or livelihood. The Revolta da Vacina of 1904 joined resistance to coercive public-health enforcement with grievances over policing and displacement; it should not be reduced to irrational opposition to medicine.

Urban services likewise had uneven boundaries. Tram lines and electric lighting reached profitable or prominent streets before many peripheral settlements; piped water, sewers, clinics, and schools expanded at different rates. Household servants, street vendors, laundresses, and informal sellers made city life function, but their occupations rarely figured in elite portraits of modern industry. Studying a city therefore requires neighborhood maps, rental records, mortality data, and testimony from residents alongside photographs of boulevards. These sources prevent architectural modernization from standing in for the everyday experience of urban populations.

Section 69.14

Industrial Workers, Women, Children, and Household Economies

Urban employment included textiles, food processing, meatpacking, construction, transport, manufacturing, ports, mining, and domestic service. Mexican mills in Puebla and the Orizaba region, Brazilian textile mills in Rio and São Paulo, and Argentine meatpacking plants and flour mills employed thousands. Working days of ten to twelve hours or more were common, wages were low and uneven, and accidents were frequent, in a period before most social insurance. Factories relied heavily on women and children in some sectors; in São Paulo's textile mills women and minors formed a very large share of the work force. Child labor was widespread, and later laws such as Argentina's 1907 law on women's and children's work began to regulate hours and conditions, though enforcement was weak.

Domestic service was the largest category of paid work for women in many cities. Servants, often rural migrants, Afro-descended women, or immigrants, worked long hours, lived in employers' homes, and had little legal protection. Women also worked as seamstresses, laundresses, vendors, midwives, teachers, and, in smaller numbers, clerks and professionals. Much of their labor was unpaid household work or informal, and therefore uncounted. Household economies depended on pooling income from several members, taking in lodgers, doing piecework at home, and using networks of kin and neighbors for support. Gender, citizenship, race, and skill shaped wages: foreign-born skilled men earned more than native-born unskilled workers, women earned less than men in the same trades, and Afro-descendant and Indigenous workers were concentrated in low-paid jobs.

Women were also organizers. Anarchist women in Buenos Aires, including Virginia Bolten, published the newspaper La Voz de la Mujer in 1896–1897; socialist activists such as Carolina Muzzilli studied and campaigned against abuses in women's work; and women led or joined strikes in textile and garment trades. Mutual-aid societies and churches provided welfare, and women ran many of them. Educated women entered professions: Cecilia Grierson became Argentina's first woman physician in 1889. Their demands for civil and political rights are part of the story of the period, though they won suffrage only later.

The household was also a productive unit. A male industrial wage rarely supported every dependent by itself, especially during unemployment or seasonal downturns. Women took in laundry, sewed garments at piece rates, sold food, kept small shops, and rented rooms; children could contribute through errands, street selling, domestic service, or paid workshop labor. Some of this income was invisible in census categories that described women as wives or daughters without occupations. Historians must therefore compare official occupational tables with household budgets, inspection records, court testimony, and workers’ newspapers before concluding that women were economically inactive.

Workplace hazards depended on industry. Textile operatives faced lint, moving belts, and long periods standing; meatpacking workers handled knives, cold rooms, and irregular schedules; printers inhaled lead dust; domestic workers faced isolation in employers’ houses and difficulty leaving abusive positions. Factory fines, wage arrears, unpaid overtime, and accident risk were central grievances. A legally signed work agreement alone tells historians little about a supervisor’s conduct or whether a worker could secure redress. Nor did a minimum age in law guarantee compliance where families needed earnings and inspection staffs were small.

Women’s organizing cut across occupational and political boundaries. Mutual-aid societies, socialist circles, anarchist groups, charitable organizations, and labor newspapers offered different kinds of assistance and visions of change. A domestic worker might rely on kin or neighborhood associations rather than a formal union, while textile workers could join collective disputes over hours and pay. The writing of Virginia Bolten and other activists challenges any claim that early labor politics was exclusively male. At the same time, some male unions limited women’s participation or treated their wages as secondary. A comparative history of labor must analyze those barriers rather than assume universal solidarity.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Working Days: A Domestic Worker, a Coffee Laborer, a Miner, and a Factory Worker

The sketches below are composite, reconstructed from patterns documented by historians. They describe typical conditions and are not accounts of named individuals.

A domestic worker in Buenos Aires or Rio usually rose before the household, lit the stove, prepared meals, washed, cleaned, and attended to children, often with a short rest in the afternoon. She slept in the employer's home, was dependent on the employer's goodwill for food and lodging, and might see her own family only on a day off, if she had one.

An immigrant coffee laborer's family in São Paulo began work at dawn. Men and women cultivated or picked in the rows assigned to the family, children helped, and the rest of the day involved tending food plots, preparing meals, and washing. Pay came by contract and was reckoned at intervals, with advances at the estate store. Time on a food plot was precious.

A miner in northern Chile or Bolivia labored underground or in the open pits and desert nitrate works, in heat and dust, with long shifts and the risk of accident. Housing and supplies were commonly provided by the company, and in some districts workers were paid in tokens redeemable at the company store, which lowered their bargaining power. Families lived in camps, and women sold food and did laundry.

A textile worker in Orizaba or São Paulo started early in a noisy mill, operating machinery for a ten- to twelve-hour day, often with children working alongside. Fines for lateness or defects reduced pay, and accidents with machines were common. Families depended on several wages to pay rent.

These sketches show why household income strategies, not individual wages, explain how working people survived, and why many of them came to support organization.

Section 69.15

Scientific Racism, Whitening Ideologies, Immigration Preferences, and National Identity

Elite thought in this period drew on European theories that ranked human groups, together with positivism, a philosophy of progress associated with Auguste Comte whose slogan "order and progress" appears on Brazil's flag. Writers such as Arthur de Gobineau, who spent time in Brazil, and later biological determinists provided vocabulary for claims that Indigenous, African, and mixed populations were inferior and that whitening through European immigration would raise national "civilization." These claims were doctrines, constructed to justify hierarchy and policy. They were not established knowledge, and modern genetics and anthropology do not support the racial hierarchies they asserted.

Their influence varied. Argentine political thought, from Domingo Sarmiento's contrast of "civilization and barbarism" to Juan Bautista Alberdi's phrase that "to govern is to populate," supported European immigration and treated Indigenous peoples as obstacles; the state's conquest of the Pampas was justified in this language. In Brazil, thinkers such as the physician João Batista de Lacerda predicted at the 1911 Universal Races Congress that Brazil would become white within generations, and the whitening ideal (branqueamento) shaped subsidized immigration and the 1890 restriction on African and Asian entry. In Bolivia, Alcides Arguedas's Pueblo enfermo (1909) portrayed the Indigenous majority as a burden. Mexican "científicos" and writers such as Justo Sierra and Francisco Bulnes debated the role of Indigenous and mestizo populations, with positions ranging from pessimism to the idea of mestizaje as national synthesis. Attitudes to Asian migrants shifted from labor recruitment to exclusionary prejudice.

Not all writers agreed. In Peru, Manuel González Prada, in a speech of 1888 and in later essays, argued that the Indigenous population was the core of the nation and that its exploitation, and not any inherent inferiority, was the problem; Clorinda Matto de Turner's novel Aves sin nido (1889) depicted abuse of Indigenous people by priests, officials, and landowners. In Chile, Nicolás Palacios in Raza chilena (1904) celebrated a mestizo "race" while retaining racial categories. José Enrique Rodó's Ariel (1900) shaped a cultural nationalism that did not rest on biology alone. These writers are part of the history of the idea of the nation, and they were also products of their time.

The doctrines contrasted sharply with real societies. Argentina, whose elites spoke of a white nation, had Indigenous, Afro-Argentine, and mestizo populations, and its immigrants did not form a uniform group. Brazil had the largest African-descended population outside Africa, and its national culture, from samba's antecedents to religious practice, was heavily shaped by Afro-Brazilians. Indigenous peoples and Afro-descendants contested exclusion in many ways: through education and press, religious brotherhoods, military service, voting where permitted, legal petitions, and cultural expression. A notable instance of Afro-Brazilian protest was the 1910 Revolt of the Lash, a mutiny of Black sailors in Rio led by João Cândido against corporal punishment in the navy.

Section 69.16

Education, Public Health, Social Reform, and the Limits of Liberal Modernization

States expanded schools. Argentina's Law 1420 of 1884 established free, compulsory, secular primary education, and Sarmiento's earlier work had created teacher-training colleges. Uruguay's school reform under José Pedro Varela in the 1870s built a public system. Mexico, Chile, and Brazil also expanded primary education, though more slowly. Literacy rose in the largest cities and in Argentina and Uruguay, but remained low in rural Mexico, the Andes, and northeastern Brazil, where schools were scarce and teachers few. Education was a means of integrating immigrant children into national culture, but it also taught a national history that often marginalized Indigenous and Afro-descendant peoples.

Public health improved in some respects. Water and sewer systems, vaccination, quarantine, and campaigns against yellow fever, plague, and tuberculosis reduced mortality in some cities. Cuban physician Carlos Finlay's work on mosquito transmission of yellow fever and the Brazilian physician Carlos Chagas's identification of Chagas disease in 1909 contributed to tropical medicine. Municipal hospitals and charitable institutions, often run by religious orders, associations of immigrants, or women's groups, served the poor. But access was uneven: rural populations and poor neighborhoods were underserved, and reformers often blamed the poor for disease, supporting forced inspections and removal.

Labor regulation began tentatively. Argentina passed Sunday-rest legislation in 1905 and the 1907 law on women's and children's work, and Chile enacted a law on workers' housing in 1906. Uruguay under José Batlle y Ordóñez, who held the presidency in 1903–1907 and 1911–1915, moved furthest in this period, with laws on divorce and the abolition of the death penalty, and later labor and social legislation. Reformers included physicians, lawyers, socialists, and Catholic social thinkers; they described their programs as progress, while workers, Indigenous peoples, and women experienced them partly as restriction and surveillance, and in many places as unavailable.

Schooling illustrates both the reach and the limits of reform. Argentina’s Law No. 1,420 created a framework for compulsory and free primary instruction within the federal capital and national territories, while provincial systems developed under their own laws. Enrolment and basic literacy rose over subsequent decades, but attendance depended on the availability of buildings and teachers, distance, family work requirements, and the language of instruction. A child in an immigrant neighborhood could find schooling a route into national public life; a child on an isolated Andean estate might have little access. Claims of national educational progress need examination of attendance, not simply the number of schools decreed by law.

Urban hygiene campaigns were similarly uneven. Public-health officials mapped disease, inspected housing, regulated refuse, and advocated clean water; bacteriology and vector research strengthened certain interventions. Yet the sanitary benefits of paving a principal avenue were limited if families in adjoining districts lacked water or faced eviction. Officials at times treated tenements, street markets, Afro-descendant cultural spaces, and immigrant neighborhoods as objects of suspicion. The 1904 vaccine revolt in Rio revealed how a measure justified in the name of collective health could become entangled with resentment of intrusive police powers. An adequate assessment must hold together advances in mortality prevention and the coercive practices of particular programs.

Reform laws did not by themselves establish enforceable social rights. Argentina’s Sunday-rest Law No. 4,661 of 1905 initially applied in the federal capital, while Law No. 5,291 of 1907 set rules for women’s and children’s work, again with differentiated geographic reach and exemptions. The national government also began systematically collecting labor information through the Department of Labor in 1907. Employers could contest inspections, and workers in informal service or distant rural districts were much harder to protect. Comparing legal coverage, inspection capacity, and workers’ actual complaints provides a more reliable measure of reform than counting legislation alone.

Programs of social improvement were also shaped by associations outside government. Migrant mutual-aid societies, neighborhood clubs, religious charities, women’s organizations, and professional medical groups financed clinics, libraries, school materials, or emergency relief. Their efforts sometimes compensated for weak public institutions; they could also enforce distinctions between approved and disapproved recipients of aid. The overall picture is not one of reforms imposed from above or demanded solely from below. Social provision emerged through unequal bargaining among officials, professionals, employers, reform associations, and the people meant to benefit.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Social Hierarchy, Work, and Citizenship before and after the Export Boom

What Changed

  • Slavery and tribute ended; wage and contract labor spread
  • Immigration remade the populations of Argentina, Uruguay, and southern Brazil
  • Cities grew and organized labor emerged
  • Frontiers closed as states occupied Indigenous territory
  • Schools, public health, and some labor law expanded the state's role

What Stayed the Same

  • Concentrated landholding and landlord-dominated local power
  • Racialized hierarchy and unequal access to law
  • Restricted political participation and limited rights for women
  • Dependence on commodity exports and foreign capital

Who experienced change most directly: Indigenous communities that lost land, immigrants who crossed the Atlantic, former slaves, and urban workers. Greater continuity: Highland Indigenous tenants and rural women, whose obligations changed in form more than substance. Why the pattern matters: It shows that legal abolition and economic growth altered labor relations without removing the inequalities on which they rested.

Division V · Sections 69.17–69.20 · c. 1890–1914

Social Resistance, Labor Movements, and the Challenges to Oligarchic Rule

Central question: How did the inequalities of the export era generate organized challenges to oligarchic political and social systems?

Section 69.17

Rural Protest, Indigenous Resistance, and the Defense of Community Land

Rural people did not accept the loss of land and autonomy in silence, and the forms of their resistance varied with their situation and options. Armed rebellion was the most visible but not the most common strategy. Communities petitioned, litigated, collected titles, hired agents, negotiated with landlords, withdrew labor, migrated, and cooperated or competed with outside political groups. Combinations of these strategies shaped outcomes, and success or failure often depended on whether a community could obtain legal help and political allies.

In the Andes, community leaders used colonial titles, lawsuits, and delegations to the capital, as in the caciques apoderados movement in Bolivia, and violent episodes such as the Atusparia rising of 1885 in Ancash and the Zárate Willka mobilization of 1899 in Bolivia grew out of the same land and labor disputes. Mapuche communities in Chile, after the 1881–1882 rising failed, turned to the courts and to organization, challenged encroachment on their reducciones, and negotiated with local officials and, later, with national parties. In Argentina, Indigenous survivors adjusted by working on estates, in sugar mills, and in the Chaco, where military campaigns continued into the early twentieth century, and they preserved communities and languages in peripheral territories.

Mexico provides examples of long armed conflict. The Yaqui of Sonora fought for their river lands for decades, under leaders such as José María Leyva, known as Cajeme, who built an autonomous government in the 1870s–1880s and was executed in 1887, and Tetabiate, after whom resistance continued into the 1900s. The state's response in the 1900s included mass deportation of Yaqui families to plantations in Yucatán and elsewhere, which broke up families and caused many deaths. In Yucatán, the war between Maya communities and the state, begun in 1847, ended militarily with the Mexican army's capture of the Maya capital Chan Santa Cruz in 1901, though Maya communities remained and resisted. Local millenarian movements, such as the 1891–1892 rebellion at Tomóchic in Chihuahua, which drew on the charisma of Teresa Urrea, and Brazil's Canudos settlement in Bahia, destroyed by federal forces in 1897 after a long campaign, show a different form of rural response to state expansion and economic change, in which religious leadership organized communities that rejected state authority and taxes. In Central America, land laws led to petitions, local revolts, and repression in highland Guatemala and Salvadoran districts, and communities used legal routes where possible; the tensions would erupt in later violence in the twentieth century.

Women participated in these struggles as petitioners, as defenders of homes and land, as providers of food and information, and sometimes as fighters. Their presence in petitions and in the oral memory of communities is a reminder that resistance was a household and community activity.

Section 69.18

Labor Organization, Strikes, Socialism, and Anarchism

Urban workers formed organizations in the last decades of the century, starting with mutual-aid societies that offered insurance, burial, medical care, and social life, many organized by craft or immigrant community. From these, and from the influence of European ideas, grew unions, federations, and political parties. Anarchism, favoring direct action, strikes, and the abolition of the state, was influential among artisans and port and construction workers in Argentina, Uruguay, Brazil, Mexico, and Chile. Socialism, favoring parliamentary participation and reform, took shape in Argentina's Socialist Party (founded 1896) under Juan B. Justo, with its newspaper La Vanguardia. Syndicalists favored unions as the vehicle of social change. The currents competed, and their disagreements, over whether to engage in electoral politics, whether to accept state arbitration, and how to treat immigrant and native workers, shaped each country's movement.

In Argentina the Federación Obrera Argentina, founded in 1901, became the Federación Obrera Regional Argentina in 1904 under anarchist influence, while socialists formed the Unión General de Trabajadores in 1903. The port strike and general strike of November 1902, which began among dock workers, led the government to adopt Residence Law No. 4,144 on 22 November and to declare a state of siege through the separate Law No. 4,145 on 24 November. Brazil's labor movement held a workers' congress in 1906 and organized strikes in São Paulo and Rio in 1907, including a general strike in São Paulo in May–June 1907 with demands for an eight-hour day; a law of January 1907, associated with the legislator Adolfo Gordo, allowed expulsion of foreigners. In Chile, nitrate workers struck repeatedly, and in December 1907 troops massacred striking miners and their families, with wives and children among them, at the Santa María school in Iquique; the number of dead is disputed, with official counts far lower than other estimates, and was at least several hundred.

Mexico's conflicts show how labor and political opposition met. At the Cananea copper mine in Sonora, owned by an American company, Mexican miners struck on 1 June 1906 over wages and unequal pay compared with U.S. workers, and the strike ended in violence in which several died, after armed Americans from across the border entered at the request of the governor. At Río Blanco in Veracruz in January 1907, textile workers who had struck were ordered by presidential arbitration to return to work, and troops fired on a crowd, killing a number whose total is disputed. Activists associated with the Partido Liberal Mexicano of Ricardo Flores Magón had published a program in 1906 calling for labor and land reforms. These events are discussed here as signs of social pressure; the revolutionary crisis they anticipate is treated in Chapter 71.

Employers' and governments' responses varied. Some employers negotiated, some used strikebreakers, blacklists, and private guards, and many appealed to police and the army. Governments treated strikes as threats to public order and the export economy, while some officials favored arbitration. Union newspapers, leaflets, and meeting halls were part of a labor culture that included women, children, and entire neighborhoods.

Section 69.19

State Repression, Social Legislation, and the Beginnings of Political Reform

Governments answered labor and social protest with force and, increasingly, with reform. Repression took the form of police violence, states of siege, press closures, deportation of foreign activists, and military action: the Residence Law and Brazil's expulsion law of 1907 are examples of the former; Iquique and Río Blanco, of the latter. In Buenos Aires, police fired on a May Day demonstration in 1909, and an anarchist, Simón Radowitzky, killed the police chief, Ramón Falcón, in retaliation; in 1910, at the centennial celebrations, the government again declared a state of siege and passed a law of "social defense" against anarchist activity. These measures reflected a belief among elites that unrest was caused by foreign agitators and not by conditions.

At the same time, some elites concluded that exclusion was unsustainable. Uruguay's president Batlle y Ordóñez used the state to expand labor protection, education, and public enterprise. In Argentina, the Radicals' revolts and abstention pressured a part of the ruling group, and President Roque Sáenz Peña, elected in 1910, argued that the legitimacy of the system required cleaner elections. Law No. 8,871, adopted by Congress in February 1912 and published on 26 March, instituted secret and compulsory voting for registered native-born and naturalized male Argentine citizens aged eighteen or older, using military enrollment to construct the electoral register; its incomplete-list arrangement improved opposition representation. Article 2 retained specified exclusions, including active soldiers of certain ranks, police agents, regular clergy, some detainees, and persons disqualified under enumerated legal categories; Article 7 exempted voters over seventy and election-duty judges from compulsory voting. The reform made fraud harder and opened the way to the victory of the Radical Hipólito Yrigoyen in 1916, which ended the Autonomist dominance.

The limits were equally important. women could not vote in national elections, a right extended in Argentina in 1947, and in other countries later. The law covered citizens, but a very large share of adult male workers were foreign-born and had not naturalized, and so remained outside the electorate. Many Indigenous residents of provinces and territories were poorly integrated into civil registers. Social laws were unevenly enforced, and many applied only to certain sectors or to cities. In Brazil, literacy restrictions continued to exclude most adults, and in Mexico the electoral system remained under presidential control until the revolution. Reforms expanded participation for some and left the structure of property and race largely untouched.

The distinction between repression and reform can be seen particularly clearly in the Argentine sequence. Congress passed the Residence Law on 22 November 1902 during the strike wave, allowing the executive to expel certain foreign nationals; on 24 November it separately enacted the state-of-siege law for the capital and the provinces of Buenos Aires and Santa Fe. Under the expulsion law, conduct judged threatening to national security or public order could trigger administrative removal, and an ordered foreigner was allowed three days to depart. These procedures subjected non-naturalized residents to executive power even when they had lived and worked in Argentina for years. Their children born in Argentina could have different citizenship rights. The legislation made legal status itself a dividing line within the working population.

The movement toward electoral reform arose from different calculations. Clean elections promised opposition parties a peaceful route to office, but they also allowed reform-minded elites to preserve state legitimacy in the face of abstention, revolt, and urban protest. The Sáenz Peña law established a secret ballot and compulsory voting for qualified male citizens, with specified exemptions and disqualifications; it did not enfranchise women or the enormous number of foreign residents who never naturalized. Nor did it directly restructure estates, wages, or Indigenous landholding. Its significance lies in changing the rules under which political competition operated, which could in turn alter policy choices, rather than in instantly completing democracy.

Uruguay provides another comparison. Batlle y Ordóñez associated republican legitimacy with regulation of public utilities, welfare measures, and protection for organized labor, although the sequence and reach of his policies varied. Other governments preferred repression or limited concessions, and none of these cases should be treated as representative of an entire continent. Reform was often selective: a protected factory worker in the capital might have stronger formal safeguards than a migrant farm laborer, domestic servant, or Indigenous tenant. The growing gap between social claims and the restricted enforcement of rights helps explain why collective action persisted even where governments adopted reformist language.

Section 69.20

Historical Synthesis: Inequality, Resistance, and the Contradictions of Export Modernization

The chapter began with a strike in Buenos Aires and a law that allowed expulsions. That juxtaposition was not an accident. The same system that built the port, the railways, and the estates also required a labor force, produced concentrated power, and defined who counted as a citizen. Comparing the groups discussed shows the pattern. Elites, whether landed, commercial, or financial, gained most, and converted wealth into political influence through machines, patronage, and law. Landowners expanded estates through legal reform and conquest. Rural laborers, in debt systems, tenancy, and wage work, gained access to cash and sometimes land but remained dependent. Indigenous communities lost land and autonomy, yet survived and used law, politics, and arms to defend what they could. Immigrants found opportunity and hardship, and in Argentina they largely lacked political rights. Women worked in fields, mills, households, and associations, and were excluded from the vote. Urban workers gained jobs and housing, and organized to resist low wages and repression.

The effects on political power and social opportunity were mixed. Growth created new middle classes, schools, and cities, and some mobility. Yet property and power remained concentrated, racial hierarchies were reproduced in new forms, and political participation remained limited. Variation mattered: Argentina and Uruguay had mass immigration and relatively open urban societies; Brazil had a freed population facing racialized labor markets; Chile and Bolivia combined mining with unresolved Indigenous land questions; Mexico and Central America combined strong presidents with intense rural dispossession. Continuity was strong in rural landholding and local power, while change was greatest in cities and in the frontier zones.

These tensions fed the demands of the following decades: land reform in Mexico and, later, in Bolivia and Guatemala; Indigenous movements across the Andes; labor legislation and populist politics; democratization and the extension of suffrage; and economic nationalism in response to the vulnerability of export dependence. The interpretation advanced here is that the export boom produced substantial economic and infrastructural modernization, but its social consequences were profoundly unequal. The concentration of property and political power, the dispossession of Indigenous communities, the restructuring of labor markets, and the expansion of immigration and urban employment generated both new opportunities and new conflicts. These struggles increasingly challenged oligarchic systems and helped prepare the region's subsequent movements for reform, democratization, and revolution. Chapter 70 turns to the external political order of the same period, and Chapter 71 to the crisis of the Mexican regime in which many of these tensions became revolutionary.

Returning to the Buenos Aires waterfront makes the chapter’s logic concrete. The immigrant worker whose labor kept export traffic moving might gain a wage, acquire a trade, organize with colleagues, and send children to school, yet remain outside national elections and vulnerable to expulsion. An Indigenous family whose territory became a profitable estate might possess an old title and petition a court but find the costs of a survey or the influence of a local magistrate decisive. A woman whose earnings sustained an urban household might have neither an electoral vote nor the protection granted to a more visible industrial occupation. Economic integration created new social possibilities without distributing the power to define their rules equally.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that export-led economic development transformed Latin American societies between about 1870 and 1914 through the concentration of land and political power, the expansion of labor markets, mass migration, and state-directed territorial consolidation. The same processes produced employment, urban growth, and opportunity, and dispossession, exploitation, racial exclusion, and organized resistance.

Export wealth formed and reshaped elites: estancieros and merchants in Argentina, paulista coffee planters, Chilean landowner-financiers and mining entrepreneurs, Mexican hacendados and officials, and Central American coffee families. These elites overlapped with, but were distinct from, expanding middle classes of clerks, professionals, officers, and shopkeepers.

Oligarchy is a category, not a single regime. Argentina's Partido Autonomista Nacional ran a governor-based machine, Brazil's First Republic a federal system of state oligarchies with literacy-restricted voting, and Mexico a centralized personal system. Central American states varied from presidential concentration to relatively competitive politics. Local power operated through coronéis, caciques, and gamonales, mixing patronage, coercion, electoral manipulation, and office.

Liberal land legislation converted communal, church, and customary holdings into private property. Mexico's reforms and survey companies, Brazil's 1850 law, Guatemala's coffee policies, El Salvador's laws of 1881–1882, and Bolivia's 1874 law worked differently in practice, because administration, cost, legal knowledge, and local power determined who gained title and who lost land. Privatization was not identical to dispossession everywhere, but it exposed collective holders disproportionately.

States and commercial interests turned Indigenous territories into frontiers. Argentina's campaigns of 1878–1885 and Chile's occupation of Araucanía were military conquests accompanied by negotiation, detention, family separation, forced labor, land sales, and reductions. Indigenous peoples fought, negotiated, and adapted, and survived as communities. In the Andes, communities defended land through lawsuits, titles, and sometimes rebellion, and in the Amazon, rubber extraction created debt and coercive systems, culminating in the Putumayo atrocities.

Immigration, subsidized and unsubsidized, remade Argentina, Uruguay, and São Paulo. Opportunity and hardship coexisted; return and seasonal migration were large; and São Paulo's colonato combined family contracts with conflict. Afro-Brazilians, free of slavery, faced racialized labor markets. Asian migration moved from coercive indenture to contract and family migration, and Caribbean workers staffed railways and banana enterprises.

Rural labor regimes, including peonage, enganche, inquilinaje, colonato, huasipungo, tenancy, sharecropping, and wage work, differed in the degree of coercion and in the role of debt, housing, and land. Family labor and gender shaped each. Cities grew, with modern centers and crowded tenements, and industrial and domestic work engaged women and children. Racial ideologies of whitening and Indigenous inferiority justified exclusion but did not describe the multiethnic societies of the region, and writers and communities contested them.

Resistance took legal, economic, cultural, and armed forms: petitions and lawsuits, mutual-aid societies, unions, anarchist and socialist organizations, strikes, rural rebellions, and millenarian communities. States responded with repression, expulsion laws, and massacres, and with limited reform: Uruguay's early social legislation and Argentina's 1912 electoral law, which expanded participation for male citizens while leaving women, many immigrants, and others outside.

The legacy was a set of unresolved conflicts over land, labor, citizenship, race, and political participation. They shaped the revolutionary and reformist movements of the twentieth century, beginning with the Mexican Revolution, and they illustrate a method used throughout this book: reading laws, censuses, and elite writings critically alongside the records and voices of those they governed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what export prosperity meant for the people who lived through it. The ports, railways, and boulevards of 1900 were real achievements, and they were built on land that had been taken, labor that was often constrained, and political systems that excluded most residents. Understanding both halves of that statement is necessary for understanding the century that followed.

The chapter also reveals a set of connected processes across very different regions: the privatization of communal land from Mexico to Bolivia, the closing of Indigenous frontiers in the south, the recruitment of workers across oceans, and the creation of urban working classes that built their own institutions. Brazil's postabolition labor markets, Argentina's immigrant society, Andean highland estates, and Amazonian extraction fit within one commodity system but not one pattern, and the variation is part of the explanation.

Many continuities survived the period. Land concentration and local power structured rural life for decades; Indigenous communities continued their legal and political defense, which later became organized movements; racial hierarchies continued to shape labor and citizenship; and labor organizations, women's activism, and reform currents grew in strength. The gains of some groups, notably through electoral reform, were real but partial.

Chapter 70 turns from internal social structures to the hemispheric political order, in which the United States became a more prominent power, and Chapter 71 returns to Mexico, where many of the tensions examined here broke into revolution. Students should carry forward the view that the export era was not simply a prelude to later crises, but a period whose own choices, and the resistance they generated, shaped the conflicts of the twentieth century.
