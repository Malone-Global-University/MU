African Slavery and the Making of Afro-Latin America

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Petition from the Fazenda Algodões

On 6 September 1770, an enslaved woman in the captaincy of Piauí, in the Brazilian interior, wrote to the governor. She described beatings, a separation from her husband, a child who had been injured, and sacraments her community had been denied. The letter is a rare surface on which the whole system of Atlantic slavery becomes visible at the scale of one household, and it is also evidence that the people held within that system made claims on the authorities who governed them.

The woman was Esperança Garcia. Her petition, discussed in Section 44.15, survives in a colonial archive and has been transcribed and published. It says that she had been taken from the Fazenda Algodões, where she lived with her husband, to serve as a cook in the house of the estate's administrator; that she and her child were beaten; that she and other women had gone three years without confession; and that her children had not been baptized. She asked the governor to order her return to her husband and to the estate so that her daughter could be baptized. A text of a few dozen lines therefore touches labor, violence, gender, family, religion, law, and the structure of colonial authority at once. It also reminds students that what follows in this chapter is a history of people with names, relationships, and demands, and not only of shipping totals.

This chapter studies the enslavement of Africans and their descendants in colonial Latin America from about 1500 to the late eighteenth century. The scale is large. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (Voyages) estimates that about 12.5 million people were embarked from Africa between 1501 and 1866 and about 10.7 million disembarked in the Americas, with more than 1.8 million deaths on the crossing. These are figures for the entire period of the trade, much of which falls in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and therefore partly outside this chapter. Brazil received the largest single share, and the database attributes roughly 5.8 million embarkations to Portuguese and Brazilian carriers across the whole period, more than to any other national flag. Spanish America, the British, French, Dutch, and Danish Caribbean, and North America received the rest. A textbook of Latin American history must therefore place Africa and Africans at its center, and it must not confine them to the plantation or reduce them to the status that law imposed.

The chapter's central argument has two parts. First, slavery was fundamental to the formation of colonial Latin America. It supplied labor for sugar, mining, ranching, shipping, construction, and households, financed merchants and monarchs, and shaped law, religion, and ideas of status. Second, Afro-Latin American history cannot be reduced to enslavement. Africans arrived with languages, technical knowledge, religious practices, kinship ties, and political experience. Under extreme coercion they formed families, mutual-aid associations, markets, communities, and routes to freedom, and their descendants became integral to Brazil, the Caribbean, Mexico, Central America, and South America. Afro-Latin America emerged from both coercive institutions and the actions of African-descended people themselves.

The chapter also insists on difference. Portuguese, Spanish, and French colonial slavery were not variations of one system. Brazil's engenhos and later its gold and diamond districts, the early Spanish Caribbean and its towns, the ports and haciendas of mainland Spanish America, and Saint-Domingue's explosive eighteenth-century plantation economy produced different demographic patterns, legal practices, urban and rural labor regimes, rates of manumission, and sizes of free African-descended populations. Understanding those differences is a major learning goal, because they shaped later struggles over abolition, citizenship, and racial identity.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. The enslaved left few narratives of their own in this period, and the records that survive were generated by owners, merchants, officials, and clergy: ship manifests, customs and tax records, notarial sales, wills, parish registers of baptism and marriage, inventories, judicial files, Inquisition cases, royal correspondence, and legislation. Historians read these sources against their purposes and look for the rare documents, such as petitions and freedom suits, in which enslaved and free African-descended people speak through or around the authorities. The modern estimates of the trade rest on cross-checked voyage records and carry margins of uncertainty that this chapter notes where it uses them.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It begins with African societies and Iberian expansion and ends in the late eighteenth century, before the Haitian Revolution of 1791, which belongs to Chapter 50; Section 44.10 explains the society that preceded it without narrating the revolution. Chapter 43 treated the economics of sugar, estates, and cities, so this chapter concentrates on workers and communities. Chapter 45 analyzes race, casta, and social hierarchy; Chapter 46 treats Christianity and African religious traditions; and Chapter 47 treats rebellion, Palmares, and maroon societies. Because slavery cannot be explained without violence, family, religion, and resistance, those subjects appear here wherever they are necessary, and the later chapters deepen them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of West and West Central African societies and explain why enslaved Africans cannot be treated as an undifferentiated population.
  2. Trace the development of Atlantic slavery from Iberian and island experiments through the early Caribbean to hereditary, racialized colonial slavery.
  3. Distinguish the roles and responsibilities of African rulers and merchants, Luso-African intermediaries, and European traders in the supply of captives, without collapsing differences of power.
  4. Explain the Middle Passage, the regional geography of embarkation and arrival, and the evidence and limits of modern estimates.
  5. Compare slavery in Brazil, the Spanish Caribbean, mainland Spanish America, and Saint-Domingue in terms of labor, law, demography, and freedom.
  6. Analyze plantation, urban, mining, ranching, and transport labor and the differing conditions of work they produced.
  7. Interpret slave law as written norm, enforcement, and lived experience.
  8. Assess the experiences of enslaved women, children, and families, including the evidence of Esperança Garcia's 1770 petition.
  9. Evaluate why some slave societies depended on repeated African arrivals and how forced migration transformed colonial populations.
  10. Explain manumission, self-purchase, and the formation of free African-descended communities, and why legal freedom did not mean equality.
  11. Connect African identities, cultural knowledge, and everyday resistance to the making of Afro-Latin American societies and to the chapters that follow.

Historical Context

Part IX showed how Iberian empires built colonial states and economies. Chapter 40 described Spanish institutions, Chapter 41 Portuguese Brazil, Chapter 42 the silver economies, and Chapter 43 the estates, sugar mills, ports, and cities through which American production reached global markets. Part IX concentrated on structures. Part X turns to the people who lived within, sustained, and contested them, and this chapter opens by asking who supplied labor and under what conditions.

The answer shifted over time. Early Spanish colonization relied on Indigenous labor, and Chapter 29 described the demographic collapse that followed contact. Indigenous peoples remained a large labor force in Mesoamerica and the Andes through forms such as the encomienda and the mit'a, but in the Caribbean, coastal Brazil, and other lowland regions where Indigenous populations were devastated or could escape, planters and officials turned increasingly to African captives. Chapter 26 introduced the Portuguese and Atlantic origins of this shift. Slavery itself was not new. It existed in Iberia, in Africa, and across the Mediterranean world. What changed in the Atlantic was the scale, the commercial organization, the association with African descent, and the hereditary character of the status.

Africa was an active historical setting and not a source of undifferentiated supply. West and West Central Africa contained states, confederations, commercial networks, farming systems, and religious traditions of great variety, and the Atlantic trade interacted with their politics in different ways in different places and times. Europeans for much of this period did not control the African interior and relied on African partners and Luso-African intermediaries to obtain captives. This chapter treats that fact as a matter of historical accuracy that does not diminish the moral weight of the trade or the unequal power relations in which it operated.

Finally, the chapter works within the legal and religious frameworks the Iberian monarchies applied to enslaved people. Roman-derived law treated the enslaved as property but also recognized that they were persons with souls; the Catholic Church claimed jurisdiction over baptism and marriage; and royal officials claimed a responsibility to regulate treatment. The gap between those claims and practice runs through the chapter. The French monarchy adopted its own Code Noir in 1685, and Saint-Domingue's slave society, which grew from French colonial law and commerce, is part of the Latin American story because of its scale, its connection to Hispaniola, and the revolution that Chapter 50 will examine.

Section 44.1 · Division I — Africa and the Formation of the Atlantic Slave Trade

African Societies Before and During Atlantic Expansion

The captives who reached Latin America came from a long stretch of Atlantic Africa and its interior, and the first lesson of this chapter is that the region contained many societies. Senegambia and Upper Guinea, on the northwest coast, included Wolof, Mandinka, Fulbe, and other peoples, some in Islamic states with long trans-Saharan ties and others in decentralized rice-growing communities. The Gold Coast and Bight of Benin were home to Akan-speaking polities, the Gbe-speaking kingdoms of the Fon and Ewe, and the Yoruba towns and Oyo state. The Bight of Biafra, further east, held Igbo, Ibibio, and other peoples who organized themselves largely through village councils, lineages, and trading networks and not through large kingdoms. South of the Equator, West Central Africa included the Kingdom of Kongo, with its capital at Mbanza Kongo, and neighboring Ndongo, Loango, and other Kimbundu- and Kikongo-speaking polities.

These societies had agricultural, craft, and commercial economies. Farmers grew millet, sorghum, yams, rice, and later American crops such as maize and cassava, which Atlantic exchange introduced. Ironworkers produced tools and weapons. Weavers made cloth that circulated as a trade good and, in some regions, as currency. Salt, gold, copper, and kola nuts moved along regional trade routes, and coastal and riverine peoples fished and traded by canoe. Political organization ranged from centralized monarchies with courts and tribute systems to confederations and stateless communities governed through councils and age-sets. Religious life included ancestor veneration, spirit cults and healing practices, Islam in the Sahel and Senegambia, and, from 1491, a Christian monarchy in Kongo whose rulers adopted Catholicism on their own terms.

Servitude existed in many of these societies before Atlantic demand. Forms included captives taken in war, debtors and people pledged as security (pawnship), criminals sentenced to servitude, and dependents attached to households or courts. These arrangements differed from the chattel slavery that developed in the Americas, in which a person was legal property, could be sold without limit, and passed the status to children. Historians debate how far African practices resembled or prepared the ground for Atlantic slavery, and the answer varied by region. The point for students is that enslavement was a familiar institution in the societies Europeans encountered, which shaped the way African and Portuguese partners negotiated the first sales, and that the Atlantic market greatly expanded and transformed it.

The evidence for these societies comes from archaeology, oral traditions, linguistics, and European accounts, which are often biased and always partial. Portuguese descriptions of Kongo, for example, record royal diplomacy, including letters from King Afonso I to the king of Portugal in the early sixteenth century complaining about the disruption caused by slave traders, but they also reflect European expectations. Students should treat such sources as valuable and limited, and recognize that African historical knowledge also survives in oral and artistic form and in the cultural practices that Africans carried to the Americas.

Section 44.2

The Origins of Atlantic Slavery and Iberian Colonial Expansion

Slavery was an established institution in medieval Iberia, where Muslim and Christian kingdoms enslaved war captives and bought others through Mediterranean and Saharan trade. Castilian law, especially the thirteenth-century Siete Partidas, descended from Roman law and treated slavery as a condition contrary to natural liberty that nonetheless existed in law. It regulated ownership and also recognized that the enslaved had souls, could marry, and could in certain cases seek protection. Portuguese law followed similar principles. These legal traditions were later carried to the Americas and shaped the ambiguous status of the enslaved as both property and persons.

Portuguese maritime expansion down the African coast gave Iberian slavery a new geography. After the capture of Ceuta in 1415, Portuguese captains probed the Atlantic coast, and in the 1440s they began bringing captives from West Africa to Portugal, where enslaved Africans worked in households, workshops, and fields and formed a visible part of Lisbon's population. At the same time the Portuguese settled Atlantic islands. On Madeira, in the Cape Verde archipelago, and above all on São Tomé in the Gulf of Guinea, colonists experimented with sugar cultivation using enslaved labor on estates that needed large work forces. São Tomé's sugar economy, organized around mills and gangs of workers drawn from the nearby African mainland, is often called a prototype of the American plantation. Students should be careful with the word, since it reflects hindsight, but the islands did show Iberians that a sugar economy could be built on coerced African labor.

Spanish settlement in the Caribbean reproduced the pattern. Africans, including some who were already Christians and Spanish-speaking (ladinos), arrived in Hispaniola in the early 1500s, and Africans born in Africa and brought directly across the Atlantic (bozales) followed. In 1518 a royal license authorized shipments straight from Africa, and sugar mills were established on Hispaniola in the following years. Enslaved Africans worked in mines, mills, cattle estates, and Spanish households. The Spanish Crown was also trying, unevenly, to restrict Indigenous enslavement, notably through the New Laws of 1542, and the decline of the Indigenous population made African labor more attractive to colonists, though the Crown's attitude toward African slavery itself was mostly one of regulation and taxation and not of challenge.

During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries slavery became hereditary and increasingly associated with African descent. Iberian law followed the principle partus sequitur ventrem, by which a child took the legal condition of its mother, so that the children of enslaved women were enslaved whatever the father's status. Over time, in the Americas, "Black" and "African" came to be associated in colonial thought with slave status, even though free Africans existed and some Indigenous people and others were enslaved. The racialization of slavery was gradual and uneven and varied by place, and Chapter 45 explores how colonial societies classified people. For this chapter, the key point is that the legal and economic institution of American slavery emerged from specific Iberian practices and Atlantic experiments, not from any fixed destiny.

Section 44.3

Captivity, African Political Economies, and the Slave-Trading Networks

How were people enslaved and sold? The answer differed by region and period, and the most reliable generalization is that most captives were taken through violence or legal coercion within Africa by African actors, to be sold at the coast or inland markets to European and Luso-African buyers. The principal mechanisms were warfare, raiding and kidnapping, judicial enslavement for debts or alleged crimes, and the sale of dependents or pawns. Wars were sometimes fought partly to obtain captives, and in other cases captives were a byproduct of conflicts over territory, succession, or trade. Historians debate how far Atlantic demand caused wars and how far it merely exploited existing ones. The evidence suggests that demand intensified and prolonged violence in many regions while political motives remained important.

Regional variation was large. In Senegambia and Upper Guinea, exports were drawn partly from conflicts among Islamic and non-Islamic states and from raiding. In the Bight of Benin, expanding states such as Oyo and Dahomey became important suppliers; Dahomey's conquest of the coastal kingdoms of Allada and Whydah (Ouidah) in the 1720s gave it direct access to the sea trade. In the Bight of Biafra, the trade was organized largely through decentralized commercial networks, with the Aro merchants of the Igbo hinterland among the best-known intermediaries and with coastal city-states such as Calabar acting as ports. On the Gold Coast, Akan states traded gold and later captives, and the later rise of Asante affected supply. Each polity pursued its own interests and made its own decisions about whether, when, and on what terms to sell.

West Central Africa, the region Portuguese called Angola, was the largest single source of captives for Brazil, and its history shows the interaction of African and Portuguese power. The Kingdom of Kongo's early Christian diplomacy with Portugal turned sour as slave traders operated outside royal control, a problem King Afonso I raised with Lisbon in the early sixteenth century. Portugal founded Luanda in 1576 and, with African allies and mercenaries, fought the Kingdom of Ndongo, whose most famous ruler, Queen Njinga, spent decades negotiating, fighting, allying with the Dutch during their occupation of Luanda, and building a new state in Matamba. Njinga participated in the regional captive trade, as most of the region's rulers did, while also resisting Portuguese conquest. Luso-African traders known as pombeiros traveled to inland markets to purchase captives, and the Portuguese conquest and trade in Angola produced a long-lived colonial system that sent very large numbers of people to Brazil. Kongo itself suffered from the defeat at Mbwila in 1665 and from civil wars that provided further captives later in the century and in the eighteenth.

What Europeans brought in exchange also mattered. African sellers received textiles from India and Europe, metals, firearms, cowrie shells, alcohol, and, in the Brazil trade, tobacco and spirits from Bahia, goods that were often chosen by African buyers to meet local demand. This commerce gave African rulers and merchants real bargaining power, since European traders usually could not enter the interior without permission and depended on local partners for supplies, safe passage, and the delivery of captives. It did not make the trade equal or voluntary for the people sold. Those who were seized were victims of coercion, and European buyers knew it, designed their institutions around it, and profited from it. Assigning responsibility therefore requires distinguishing among actors with different powers: African rulers and merchants who sold captives, European and Brazilian merchants who financed and demanded them, crowns that licensed and taxed the trade, and the enslaved themselves.

Section 44.4

The Middle Passage and the Experience of Forced Transportation

The experience of enslavement began with capture and a journey that could last weeks or months before the sea voyage. Captives were marched, sometimes in coffles bound together, to coastal or river ports, and many died on the way. At the coast they were held in forts such as the Portuguese castle of Elmina, in trading posts, or in temporary enclosures sometimes called barracoons, until a ship was ready. Traders examined, branded, and sold them. Portuguese and Spanish authorities required baptism before embarkation, and at ports such as Luanda large groups were baptized in rapid, often perfunctory ceremonies whose meaning for the captives is uncertain. The sources, written by the buyers, say little about what the captives understood.

The Atlantic crossing, the Middle Passage, was an ordeal of confinement, disease, hunger, and violence. Captives were packed below decks, often shackled, with little space or air. Dysentery, smallpox, fevers, and dehydration spread, and mortality on voyages varied widely, so that the average of about 14.5 percent that Voyages estimates for the whole trade conceals voyages in which many died and voyages in which few did. Women and children were sometimes kept less tightly confined than men but were exposed to sexual violence by crews. Shipboard resistance occurred, including refusals of food, suicides, and revolts, which crews met with force and with security measures such as extra guards and weapons. People who endured the crossing formed bonds with their shipmates, and in Brazil the Portuguese word malungo and related terms are associated with such relationships, though students should treat the details of that vocabulary as scholarly interpretation.

Geography shaped the experience. The South Atlantic, where winds and currents carried ships between Angola and Brazil, offered generally shorter crossings than the northern routes between West Africa and the Caribbean, which could last two months or more. Brazilian traders, who sailed from Salvador, Recife, and Rio de Janeiro without needing to pass through Europe, developed a direct bilateral trade with Angola and later with the Bight of Benin. Voyages to the Spanish Caribbean and the French and British islands crossed the northern and equatorial Atlantic, with arrivals at ports such as Cartagena, Havana, Veracruz, Port-au-Prince, and Cap-Français. On arrival, captives were quarantined, sold at auctions, in markets, or privately, and sent to estates, mines, or households, often separated from shipmates and sometimes from relatives.

Historical Vocabulary
Middle Passage

The Middle Passage refers to the sea crossing from Africa to the Americas, the middle leg of the "triangular" circuits that some historians used to describe Atlantic commerce. The term is conventional, and the triangle model fits some trades, such as those of northern European ports, far better than others, such as the direct Brazil-Angola trade.

The term helps students identify the voyage as a distinct stage, but it risks concealing the longer process of forced migration, which included capture, overland marches, coastal detention, the crossing, quarantine, sale, and the later journey to a place of labor. Historians therefore speak of forced migration and Atlantic trafficking as well, to include the whole system that moved people.

Section 44.5

Imperial Regulation, Asientos, and the Growth of the Atlantic Trade

The trade was organized differently by each empire. Portugal's merchants and crown controlled the African coast in Angola and, to a degree, in other regions, and Brazilian colonists, especially in Bahia, Pernambuco, and Rio de Janeiro, financed their own voyages to Africa with locally produced goods. This Brazil-centered system required less intermediation by Lisbon than other empires' systems did and gave the colonial merchant community of Rio and Salvador enduring economic and political power. Spain, having no major African outposts of its own after the Treaty of Tordesillas allocated Africa to Portugal, depended on foreign suppliers, and the Spanish Crown used the asiento, a contract granting a merchant or company the right to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America in return for fees and conditions.

During the Union of the Crowns (1580–1640), Portuguese merchants held asiento contracts and supplied captives to Spanish American ports, chiefly Cartagena de Indias and Veracruz, with onward movement to Panama, Peru, and other regions, and some arriving through Buenos Aires. After Portugal's separation from Spain in 1640, the contracts passed among Genoese, Dutch, and other suppliers. The Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621, seized Pernambuco in 1630, took the fort of Elmina in 1637 and Luanda in 1641, and became a major carrier, until the Portuguese retook Luanda in 1648 and expelled the Dutch from Brazil in 1654. The French and British established colonies in the Caribbean and entered the trade, and the British South Sea Company obtained the Spanish asiento in the Treaty of Utrecht (1713). A royal decree of 28 February 1789 opened the slave trade to Cuba, Santo Domingo, Puerto Rico, and the Caracas region, allowing Spanish subjects and, under conditions, foreign suppliers to import captives; the liberalization was renewed and extended in the following years. It contributed to the expansion of Cuban plantations after the 1790s. A separate royal instruction of 31 May 1789 addressed the treatment of the enslaved, as Section 44.14 explains.

Financing involved merchant houses, credit networks, and insurance. Voyages were risky, and investors expected high returns. Crowns taxed the trade, and officials at ports recorded arrivals for fiscal purposes, producing the customs and tax documents that historians now use. Smuggling was widespread, since the asiento system provided a cover for contraband and since colonists often preferred cheaper or faster foreign suppliers. The legal structures were important less for restricting the trade than for creating revenue and monopoly rights, and they show that crowns, merchants, and colonists were all invested in it.

How do historians know the trade's scale? The Voyages database, built by generations of scholars led most prominently by David Eltis and David Richardson, compiles records of tens of thousands of voyages (nearly 35,000 in its 2008 version) from ship registers, customs records, newspapers, insurance data, and merchant papers, and uses statistical methods to estimate voyages for which records are missing. Its estimates are not exact. Records are better for some flags and periods than others, particularly for British and French voyages in the eighteenth century, and weaker for Portuguese and Brazilian voyages and for the earliest centuries. The overall pattern is nonetheless clear: the trade grew, with fluctuations, across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and reached its highest annual levels in the final decades of the eighteenth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Asiento

An asiento (Spanish, "contract" or "agreement") was a license by which the Spanish Crown granted a merchant or company the right to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America, usually in exchange for fees and often with a stipulated number of captives. The system existed in various forms from the sixteenth century and was most famously held by Portuguese merchants (1595–1640 and in some phases), then by Genoese, Dutch, French, and British contractors.

The asiento matters because it shows how a monarchy that did not control African coasts regulated and profited from the trade while relying on foreign merchants. It should not be confused with a monopoly that prevented all other trade, since contraband flourished, and it should not be read as evidence that Spanish America received only the captives the contracts specified.

Section 44.6 · Division II — The Regional Slave Societies of Colonial Latin America

Brazil: Sugar Plantations and the Expansion of African Slavery

Portuguese settlement in Brazil first depended on Indigenous labor. Colonists enslaved Indigenous people in raids and wars, and Indigenous laborers worked in the early sugar mills of Pernambuco and Bahia. Epidemics, flight, violence, and the intervention of Jesuits and the Crown, who periodically restricted Indigenous enslavement while allowing exceptions, reduced this labor supply in the coastal zone. From about the 1570s, as sugar expanded, planters in the northeast turned increasingly to Africans, imported mainly from Angola and, in smaller numbers, from other regions, so that by the early seventeenth century enslaved Africans formed the dominant labor force on the engenhos. Indigenous enslavement continued in the interior, in the south, and in the Amazon region, a point Chapter 39 introduced.

The sugar economy of the northeast, centered on Pernambuco and Bahia, was organized around the engenho, which meant both the mill and the estate. The planter (senhor de engenho) owned the mill and often the land, and independent cane growers (lavradores de cana) supplied cane that the mill processed. Chapter 43 described the economics. For the enslaved, the engenho meant long, dangerous work: planting and cutting cane, hauling it to the mill, feeding the grinding machinery, and tending the boiling houses, with the harvest running through much of the year and requiring workers day and night. An engenho's workforce also included skilled enslaved workers, such as sugar masters, carpenters, and coopers, and a smaller number of household servants.

The Dutch occupation of Pernambuco (1630–1654) did not alter the dependence on enslaved Africans. The Dutch, who captured sugar-producing regions and the African posts of Elmina and Luanda in order to supply them, relied on enslaved labor and moved captives across the Atlantic. Their rule, especially under Johan Maurits of Nassau in the 1640s, brought urban construction in Recife and a degree of religious toleration for Jews and Protestants, but the plantation economy was intact. The war that expelled them also produced military roles for free and enslaved men of African descent, including Henrique Dias, a Black commander whose forces fought on the Portuguese side and who was later honored by the Crown, a case Section 44.18 revisits. After 1654, Pernambuco's sugar declined relative to Caribbean competitors, and Bahia remained a major center.

Slave society in the sugar zone was hierarchical and tightly bound to the estate. Africans from Angola and Kongo predominated early; later Bahia developed particularly strong links to the Bight of Benin. Plantation owners, local officials, and the Church shaped daily life, and the engenho's chapel and the priest's visits (when they occurred) were part of its routines. The enslaved lived in communal housing, grew some of their own food where owners allowed, and maintained relationships within and beyond the estate. Their labor produced much of the wealth that sustained Brazil's colonial elite and the Portuguese Crown, and their presence made the northeast the heart of Afro-Brazilian history. Sections 44.11 and 44.15 return to labor and family.

Section 44.7

Beyond the Sugar Coast: Slavery in Rio de Janeiro, Minas Gerais, and Interior Brazil

Brazil cannot be represented by northeastern sugar alone. In the seventeenth century, enslaved Africans worked in tobacco cultivation in Bahia, cattle ranching in the northeastern backlands (sertão) and the south, subsistence and commercial farming near ports, and a growing range of urban trades. Southeastern Brazil, around Rio de Janeiro, developed sugar, food production for local markets, and a commercial economy tied to the Angola trade. These regions diversified Brazilian slavery before the great mining boom.

The discovery of gold in the 1690s in the interior region that became Minas Gerais transformed Brazil. Prospectors from São Paulo and elsewhere, and a flood of migrants from Portugal and other colonial regions, entered the mountains, and miners bought enslaved Africans in large numbers to work in streams and pits. Diamonds, found in the district around Tijuco in the 1720s, became a royal monopoly worked by enslaved labor under strict supervision. Enslaved workers in alluvial mining panned, dug, and carried material under harsh conditions, while some enslaved people were permitted to prospect independently and to keep part of what they found, an arrangement that could lead to self-purchase. Free African-descended people also joined the mining population. The eighteenth century therefore saw a southward and inland shift of Brazil's economic and demographic center, symbolized by the transfer of the viceroy's seat from Salvador to Rio de Janeiro in 1763.

Rio de Janeiro became the pivot of the new system. The port received large numbers of captives, particularly from Angola, and merchants there financed voyages, purchased captives, and sent them inland to Minas and to the farms and plantations of the region. Rio-based traders developed a close relationship with Luanda and Benguela, and the city's population of enslaved and free people of African descent, who worked on the docks, in households, and in trades, grew accordingly. Salvador remained important, with its own connection to the Bight of Benin, where Bahian tobacco and spirits were exchanged for captives. The bilateral system gave Brazilian merchants considerable influence in the Angolan trade, and the money generated by the gold economy fueled the further importation of enslaved Africans.

The interior's slave society differed from the sugar coast's in important ways. Mining districts were more urbanized, with towns such as Vila Rica and Mariana developing around the gold economy, and they included large populations of free and freed people of African descent, women who worked as vendors and in households, and enslaved artisans. Mining slavery could offer greater mobility and opportunity for self-purchase than a sugar mill did, though it was also dangerous and unstable. The rapid growth of the region also created fears of unrest and led authorities to regulate movement and punish flight. The district's best-known woman of African descent, Chica da Silva, was freed in the early 1750s and lived with the diamond contractor João Fernandes de Oliveira. Her documented career, discussed in Section 44.18, illustrates possibilities and constraints, but it was exceptional and should not be taken as typical.

Section 44.8

The Spanish Caribbean: Hispaniola, Cuba, and Puerto Rico

Africans were among the earliest non-Indigenous inhabitants of the Spanish Caribbean, and their number grew as the Indigenous population of the islands collapsed (Chapter 29). In sixteenth-century Hispaniola, enslaved Africans worked in the first sugar mills, in gold washing, in cattle ranching, and in the households and workshops of Santo Domingo, and they were early participants in the flight and resistance that Chapter 47 discusses. By the middle of the sixteenth century, however, the Spanish colony's fortunes were declining. Settlers left for Mexico and Peru, the island's sugar industry weakened, and Hispaniola's economy came to rest on cattle, hides, and small-scale agriculture, with a population that included many free and enslaved people of African descent.

Cuba and Puerto Rico followed a different course for most of the colonial period. Cuba's economy centered on Havana, the port where the treasure fleets gathered, which developed shipbuilding, provisioning, and defense industries that employed enslaved and free workers. Cattle ranching, tobacco farming, and small sugar mills operated in the countryside, and the copper mines at El Cobre, near Santiago, were worked by enslaved people whose legal status changed over time. After the Crown took the mines and their workers from a private contractor in 1670, the community became "royal slaves" (esclavos reales) who held land, formed a village, served in a militia, and litigated for rights. When the heirs of the former contractor later claimed them as private property, the cobreros sent one of their own, Gregorio Cosme Osorio, to Madrid to argue their case. In María Elena Díaz's study of the community, a royal decree of 7 April 1800 recognized their collective freedom and the town's status, and its implementation is documented in 1801. The outcome followed more than a century of litigation and negotiation, and individual cases, including claims of re-enslavement, were more complicated than a single grant of freedom suggests. Puerto Rico, a garrison and agricultural island, relied on a mixed economy of small farms, ranching, and some sugar and coffee, and its population included a substantial free population of color. Enslaved Africans in both islands were a significant but not overwhelming portion of the population for much of the colonial era.

The British occupation of Havana in 1762–1763, which brought thousands of enslaved Africans into Cuba in a short time, and the subsequent Bourbon reforms prepared the ground for change. The collapse of Saint-Domingue after 1791, treated in Chapter 50, then created an opening for Cuban planters, who expanded sugar production enormously around and after the turn of the nineteenth century, importing large numbers of Africans, particularly after Spain's 1789 liberalization of the trade. That transformation, which turned Cuba into a major plantation society, lies mostly beyond this chapter's period, and students should be careful not to project it backward onto the earlier island, where slavery was embedded in urban, port, ranching, and small-farm economies.

Free people of African descent were prominent in the Spanish Caribbean. They served in militias, worked as artisans and small farmers, and in Havana formed mutual-aid associations known as cabildos de nación, organized by African origin, that provided burial, support, and a public cultural presence. Manumission through self-purchase, including the practice called coartación discussed in Section 44.17, was well established in Cuba, and the Spanish legal tradition provided avenues for the enslaved to seek protection and, sometimes, to change owners. These features did not abolish slavery's violence, but they shaped a society with a large and visible free African-descended population, a pattern that Cuba's later plantation boom would strain and transform.

Section 44.9

African Slavery in Mainland Spanish America

On the Spanish American mainland, African slavery developed in the shadow of larger Indigenous populations and the silver economies, but it was nonetheless central. Cartagena de Indias, on the Caribbean coast of New Granada, became the principal entry port for captives bound for northern South America and, through the isthmus, the Pacific coast. The Jesuit Alonso de Sandoval, who worked in Cartagena, wrote a treatise in the early seventeenth century describing the African peoples who arrived there, one of the most valuable contemporary sources on the trade, though it reflects a missionary's purposes and Spanish categories. His colleague Pedro Claver ministered to newly arrived captives and was later canonized. Their work shows the Church's presence at the port but also its acceptance of the institution it served.

New Spain received captives through Veracruz, and enslaved Africans and their descendants formed a significant part of the population of Mexico City, Puebla, and the coastal lowlands. Estimates of the colonial total vary with method and scope. Work building on Philip Curtin's census of the trade suggests about 200,000 arrivals; Mexican historiography associated with Gonzalo Aguirre Beltrán cites figures of roughly 200,000 to 250,000; and some counts for the period to 1650 alone give about 110,000 to 120,000. The differences reflect whether authors count registered imports, estimate contraband, or include arrivals through secondary ports such as Campeche and Acapulco, so students should treat all totals as approximations. They worked in sugar mills in regions such as Veracruz and Morelos, in silver mining at places such as Zacatecas, in textile workshops (obrajes) and trades, in transport and ranching, and in households. In Peru, the Lima census of 1614 counted roughly 11,100 residents of African descent and about 11,900 Spaniards, so that people of African descent were nearly as numerous as the city's Spanish population. Enslaved Africans worked on the sugar and wine estates of the coastal valleys, in the city's trades, and in domestic service. Jesuit and other religious estates owned large numbers of enslaved people.

Other regions complete the picture. Panama, a key transit point where goods and silver crossed the isthmus, relied on enslaved boatmen, porters, and laborers, and the region's difficult terrain provided refuge for those who fled. In New Granada, enslaved Africans and their descendants worked the gold-bearing river valleys of the Pacific lowlands, in the districts of Chocó and Popayán, in the eighteenth century, forming mining communities distinct from both plantations and Spanish towns. Venezuela's coastal cacao estates used enslaved labor, as did the sugar regions of the Cauca valley. Buenos Aires and the Río de la Plata received captives, many of whom were then sent overland, and Chile had a smaller enslaved population. In these areas slavery adapted to local products and geography.

Why does the Spanish American mainland belong in a chapter on Afro-Latin America when Africans there were often a minority? The answer is that the significance of slavery does not rest on population share alone. Enslaved and free people of African descent supplied much of the skilled labor, urban service, transport, and domestic work on which cities depended, and in certain mining and plantation districts they were the main labor force. Their presence also shaped colonial law, the classification of status explored in Chapter 45, and the social worlds of mixed families. Many early modern Spanish cities, especially ports, included African-descended populations that were large and highly visible. Treating mainland Spanish America as "Indigenous" or "mestizo" and the Caribbean and Brazil as "African" simplifies a more complicated geography.

Section 44.10

The French Caribbean and the Rise of Saint-Domingue

French colonization of the Caribbean began in the early seventeenth century on islands such as Saint-Kitts, and then Martinique and Guadeloupe, where French settlers at first relied on European indentured servants and then, as sugar spread, increasingly on enslaved Africans. French buccaneers and settlers also occupied territory in western Hispaniola, and in the Treaty of Ryswick (1697) Spain recognized French possession of that part of the island, which became Saint-Domingue. The Spanish colony of Santo Domingo remained on the eastern side, so the island was divided between two colonial systems, a division that shaped Haitian and Dominican history later.

In 1685, Louis XIV issued the edict known as the Code Noir for the French Antilles, later extended in modified forms to Louisiana. The Code defined the legal status of the enslaved as property but imposed duties on owners: baptism and instruction in the Catholic faith, rules about marriage, and limits on certain punishments, alongside harsh penalties for flight, theft, and violence against whites, including branding, mutilation, and execution. It also provided for manumission and, in its text, granted freed people the rights of free subjects. Historians stress that the Code was an expression of royal and colonial authority, that colonial planters and courts enforced it selectively, and that later regulations narrowed the rights of free people of color. Like the Spanish and Portuguese laws, it should be read as both a norm and a statement of what authorities intended.

Saint-Domingue's growth in the eighteenth century was extraordinarily fast. Planters developed sugar in the northern plain and the western region, and coffee and indigo in the hills, and the colony became by the 1780s the world's leading producer of sugar and coffee. The labor force grew by massive importation of Africans, many of whom came from West Central Africa in the later decades, and the colonial census of 1789, as reported in recent scholarship, enumerated about 434,000 enslaved people, about 30,800 whites, and about 24,800 free people of color. Modern reconstructions that allow for undercounting give higher estimates for the enslaved population, around 500,000 or more, so census enumerations and scholarly estimates should not be treated as identical. The enslaved population was dominated by people born in Africa because mortality was so high and imports so heavy that the population did not reproduce itself. Both kinds of figure are approximations for the years just before the revolution.

The free people of color, known in French as gens de couleur or affranchis, formed a distinct and important group. Some were wealthy planters and slaveholders, others artisans and small farmers, and many had white fathers and Black or mixed mothers. In the later eighteenth century, white colonists and the colonial administration imposed increasing restrictions on them, limiting their occupations, dress, and public roles, even as they gained economic strength. The society that resulted combined extreme exploitation of the enslaved majority, tension among whites, free people of color, and the enslaved, and deep dependence on transatlantic commerce. Understanding it is essential for Chapter 50, but this chapter stops at the threshold of the revolution: Saint-Domingue shows what an intensive plantation slave society could become when demographic, commercial, and legal conditions aligned.

Section 44.11 · Division III — Labor, Ownership, and the Institutions of Enslavement

Plantation Labor and the Organization of Enslaved Work

Sugar plantations demanded relentless, seasonal, and hierarchical labor. In the field, workers cleared land, planted cane cuttings, weeded, and cut the mature cane with machetes, usually in organized gangs under drivers who enforced the pace. Cane had to reach the mill quickly after cutting, so the harvest and milling season required long hours, often continuing through the night, with grinding rollers that could maim careless workers and boiling vats that scalded them. Skilled workers, such as sugar boilers and masters who judged when the juice crystallized, carpenters, blacksmiths, and coopers, held positions of greater responsibility and sometimes of greater prestige, though they remained enslaved.

Regional and temporal differences mattered. In the Caribbean, particularly in Saint-Domingue, planters used the gang system extensively and imported people in very large numbers, and the intensity of work, together with disease and poor nutrition, produced high mortality. In Brazil, the engenho likewise demanded heavy labor, but some plantations allowed workers to cultivate provision grounds, small plots on which they grew food for themselves and, sometimes, for sale. These arrangements, which scholars of Brazil have called a "peasant breach" in the slave system, reduced owners' costs for feeding workers and gave the enslaved some economic independence, which could be used for self-purchase, while also reinforcing the owner's control by making workers' livelihoods dependent on his tolerance. Provision grounds and the markets they supplied are an important example of how enslaved people shaped the economy.

Work shaped bodies and family life. Cane cutting and mill work caused injuries, exhaustion, and illness. Women worked in the fields alongside men, and in the mill and household, and pregnancy and childbirth were hazardous under such conditions. Poor diet, disease, and overwork produced high death rates, which Section 44.16 examines. Work also structured time: planters regulated the length of the workday, the allocation of days for religious observance, and the grant of free time, and the enslaved bargained over these rules, as the example of the Engenho Santana in Section 44.20 shows. Enslaved workers in different roles, such as field laborers, skilled artisans, drivers, and domestic servants, had different experiences and sometimes different loyalties.

A contemporary description of the Brazilian mill survives in Cultura e opulência do Brasil (1711), by the Jesuit João Antônio Andreoni, who wrote under the name Antonil. The book was written for estate owners and officials, and the Crown suppressed it soon after publication, so it reflects an owner's perspective and a concern with profit as much as description. It nonetheless sets out the division of labor from cane field to purging house, treats enslaved workers as indispensable to the estate, and advises owners to supply food, clothing, and rest while also accepting corporal punishment as normal management. Antonil recorded a bitter saying of the period that Brazil was hell for Black people, purgatory for whites, and paradise for people of mixed ancestry, a judgment that reveals how color mapped onto labor and opportunity.

Regional organization differed. French planters in Saint-Domingue commonly divided workers into gangs (ateliers) by strength and age, with the strongest in the great gang on the hardest tasks such as cane cutting and ditching, and children and the elderly in lighter gangs. Drivers, often enslaved men, supervised the gangs under white managers. The Code Noir required owners to provide weekly food allowances, but many planters also gave workers plots and time to grow food, and enslaved cultivators sold their produce in Sunday markets that became central to the colony's internal economy. In Spanish America, labor on coastal Peruvian estates and in Mexican sugar and workshop settings was often mixed. Enslaved workers labored beside free, debt-bound, and Indigenous workers, and in workshops (obrajes) they could be confined for long hours. These differences meant that an enslaved person's daily experience depended on the crop, the estate, the region, and the owner.

Section 44.12

Urban Slavery: Artisans, Markets, Households, and Maritime Work

Slavery in colonial Latin America was never only rural. In Salvador, Rio de Janeiro, Havana, Cartagena, Lima, Mexico City, and other cities, enslaved people formed a significant portion of the workforce. They worked as domestic servants, cooks, and nurses; as artisans such as masons, carpenters, tailors, and shoemakers; as porters and carriers who moved goods and people; as boatmen, dockworkers, and sailors; and as street and market vendors. Owners who held only one or a few enslaved people, often in households of modest means, were common, and enslaved labor supported the lifestyles of the urban middle and upper classes.

Urban slavery included a practice of hiring out. In Brazil, enslaved workers known as negros de ganho were sent into the streets to find work as carriers, craftsmen, or vendors and returned a fixed sum, usually daily or weekly, to their owner, keeping any surplus. Owners could also rent out workers to others. Similar arrangements, often under the name jornal (a daily wage payment), existed in Spanish American cities. Market women, called quitandeiras in Brazil, sold food and goods in the streets and markets, and their trade made them central to the urban economy and a visible public presence. Skilled enslaved artisans sometimes ran workshops for their owners, and in some cities, guilds restricted or controlled the participation of people of African descent in the trades.

The urban environment offered both opportunities and dangers. Hiring out and independent earnings allowed some workers to accumulate money for self-purchase, to support relatives, or to maintain a degree of autonomy. Cities also offered anonymity, social networks, and the presence of free Black communities and brotherhoods, which could assist people seeking freedom or evading owners. But legal bondage remained. An owner could sell a worker, reclaim earnings, or impose punishments, and urban conditions exposed enslaved people to surveillance by officials, to the public punishment of the whipping post, and to the risk of separation from family through sale. The same city that offered mobility also enforced the boundaries of status.

Skilled trades gave enslaved people unusual leverage and exposed them to particular risks. Notarial records from cities such as Lima and Mexico City show owners contracting enslaved youths to master craftsmen for a fixed term of training, and skilled workers were valued more highly in sales and estate inventories. Owners sometimes ran their own workshops or hired out skilled workers to others. Free artisans resented the competition, and some guilds sought to restrict the participation of people of African descent, so urban labor was shaped by conflict among enslaved, free, and corporate interests. Women dominated food vending and many market trades, working under municipal licensing rules, and their earnings supported households and, at times, the purchase of relatives' freedom.

Ports required large workforces. Enslaved dockworkers, boatmen, caulkers, and sailors loaded and unloaded ships and moved cargo in Havana, Cartagena, Salvador, and Rio de Janeiro. In Cartagena and Havana, both private and royal enslaved workers took part in building and maintaining fortifications, work that connected urban slavery to imperial defense. On the Magdalena River and the Panama isthmus, enslaved boatmen and porters carried goods between the Caribbean and the interior. These jobs gave workers mobility and contact with free communities, but they were hazardous, and they depended on the city's tolerance of workers whose legal condition allowed owners to recall them at any time.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Day in the Life of an Enslaved Worker in a Colonial Port Town

A note on method. No single document describes a typical day, and no individual is being invented here. What follows is a composite reconstruction drawn from patterns that appear in notarial contracts, municipal regulations, travelers' accounts, court cases, and parish records for Brazilian and Spanish American port cities in the eighteenth century. Details varied by city, occupation, and owner.

Consider a woman who sells food in a market. Her day might begin before dawn with preparing the goods she will sell, perhaps cooking or gathering produce, in the household of an owner who assigns her a daily sum. She carries her tray or sets up a stall in the market or on a street corner, and she bargains with customers, many of them free and enslaved neighbors who share her language or origin. By the end of the day she must hand over the required amount to her owner. Anything beyond it is hers, and she may use it to buy food and cloth, to pay a brotherhood's dues, to support a child, or to save toward her own freedom.

Now consider a man who works as a dockworker or porter in the same town. He may belong to a work group organized by origin, sharing a common language, and may carry loads between ships, warehouses, and houses, bringing back his earnings. At night, he may return to his owner's house or to rented quarters, and on feast days he may participate in a brotherhood's procession or gatherings. Town officials impose curfews and regulate public assembly, and he can be stopped by patrols. The sources record regulations and punishments more than inner life, which is why historians read them closely for evidence of what people did despite the rules.

These reconstructions show the social texture of urban slavery: constant exposure to supervision, daily negotiation, work that created networks across free and enslaved communities, and the permanent shadow of sale. They also remind students that urban Africans and their descendants built institutions, friendships, and families in the same streets that confined them.

Section 44.13

Mining, Ranching, Transportation, and Other Forms of Enslaved Labor

Beyond plantations and cities, enslaved labor supported a wide range of enterprises. In mining districts, such as the alluvial gold fields of Minas Gerais and the Pacific lowlands of New Granada, enslaved workers dug, washed, and panned in streams and pits, usually in groups supervised by overseers, and sometimes in smaller teams working on their owners' behalf. Mining labor was physically demanding and hazardous, with exposure to water, disease, and the collapse of excavations. In Brazil, as noted in Section 44.7, some mining owners allowed enslaved people to work independently for a set return, which opened a path to self-purchase while preserving the owner's profit. The Spanish silver mines of Mexico and Peru relied far more heavily on Indigenous and free wage labor, as Chapter 42 explained, but Africans worked in some mining districts and in related activities such as refining, transport, and supply.

Livestock raising employed enslaved and free workers on large estates. In the Brazilian northeast and south, in the Venezuelan plains, in northern Mexico, and in the Río de la Plata region, cattle estates needed herders, hide processors, and others, and enslaved cowhands could enjoy greater mobility than plantation field workers. Later, in the late eighteenth century, southern Brazil developed meat-salting works that used enslaved labor. Agricultural estates producing tobacco, cacao, wheat, wine, or food crops likewise employed enslaved workers, often alongside free laborers and Indigenous workers. These enterprises were important to regional economies, and they show that Africans were embedded in diverse rural settings.

Transportation required labor in the absence of roads and draft animals in many areas. Enslaved porters carried goods and people in cities and on rough tracks, and boatmen and canoemen moved cargo on rivers and coasts, such as the Magdalena River in New Granada and the Chagres River in Panama. Muleteers and others drove pack animals between mining districts and ports. Maritime work included sailing, shipbuilding, and fishing, with Havana's shipyards and the fleets of the Atlantic ports employing both enslaved and free workers. Comparing Brazil and Spanish America, one finds that Brazil's dependence on enslaved labor was broader and deeper across sectors, while Spanish America combined African labor with Indigenous tribute and wage workers, producing more varied labor systems and more complicated legal categories. In both, enslaved Africans were essential to the movement of goods and the working of the colonial economy.

Mining labor was organized in several ways. In Minas Gerais, owners worked larger permanent workings (lavras) with water channels and gangs, while smaller prospectors worked streams using the pan (bateia). Some owners required enslaved miners to deliver a fixed quantity of gold and allowed them to keep what they found beyond it, a system that gave workers an incentive to find more and a route to self-purchase. In 1735 the Crown introduced a capitation tax on enslaved workers in the mining districts, which tied royal revenue directly to slave ownership, and it was in force until 1750. Diamond mining in the Tijuco district was supervised more closely, under a royal contract, and its workers had less independence.

In the Pacific lowlands of New Granada, the gold-mining districts of Chocó and Popayán relied on gangs (cuadrillas) of enslaved workers owned by proprietors who often lived in Popayán and left day-to-day management to administrators. William Frederick Sharp's study of the Chocó describes a frontier economy in which the enslaved were sometimes allowed days to work for themselves, bought their freedom with what they found, and escaped into forested terrain. Ranching and meat production offered other patterns. Venezuelan cattle estates (hatos) employed herders in the plains, and from the 1780s meat-salting works (charqueadas) developed in southern Brazil that used enslaved workers for slaughter and processing. In both empires, therefore, the enslaved moved goods, tended animals, and mined metals in settings quite unlike a sugar mill.

Section 44.14

Law, Property, Violence, and Colonial Slaveholding Authority

Colonial law treated the enslaved as property that could be bought, sold, mortgaged, and inherited, and as persons with legal duties and, in limited respects, rights. Slavery rested on the principle that status passed through the mother, so that children of enslaved women were born enslaved. Sales were recorded in notarial documents, and inventories of estates listed enslaved people along with livestock and tools. Because they were property, enslaved people could be separated from relatives through sale or inheritance, a threat that hung over family life. The law also made slaveholders responsible for punishing crimes by those they enslaved in some circumstances, giving private owners wide authority, while public courts handled serious offenses.

The Iberian legal tradition also acknowledged limits. The Siete Partidas recognized that enslaved people could marry, that owners should not kill or torture them without cause, and that in cases of extreme cruelty an enslaved person could seek to be sold to another owner. In Spanish America, this principle underlay petitions to authorities for a change of owner, and the Church held that the sacrament of marriage could not be denied because of status. Portuguese law, in the Ordenações Filipinas, treated the enslaved as property but also recognized baptism and, in principle, marriage; Brazil had no comprehensive slave code in this period, and practice followed local custom and the decisions of owners and officials. The French Code Noir, discussed in Section 44.10, imposed duties on owners and severe penalties on the enslaved. Spain issued on 31 May 1789 an instruction on the education, treatment, and occupations of the enslaved, a measure distinct from the trade-liberalizing decree of 28 February. It recognized the humanity of the enslaved and imposed duties on owners concerning religious instruction, food, clothing, care in sickness, and treatment. Planters objected, as in the statement that Havana's mill owners sent the king in January 1790, and the Crown temporarily suspended its application. Historians nonetheless find that enslaved litigants continued to invoke it in court, which shows how a regulation weakened in enforcement could still serve as a legal resource.

Violence was integral to slaveholding. Owners and overseers used whipping, shackles, iron collars, confinement in stocks, and other punishments, and sexual coercion of enslaved women by owners and others was common. Authorities also employed public punishment as a deterrent. Control relied on surveillance: restrictions on movement, curfews, bans on carrying weapons, and in Brazil the capitães do mato (bush captains), who tracked and recaptured fugitives. These mechanisms also depended on cooperation among free people, including free people of African descent who served in militias and patrols. Written law and lived experience diverged, since owners often ignored limits, officials often failed to enforce them, and the enslaved lacked easy access to courts. At the same time, the existence of legal limits gave some enslaved people a language and a procedure in which to make claims.

The three systems offered different formal channels. In some Spanish jurisdictions, officials called síndicos or protectors of slaves could represent enslaved litigants, and enslaved people could ask authorities to compel an owner to accept a price or to allow a sale to a new owner. The French Code Noir allowed enslaved people whose owners failed to feed and clothe them to bring complaints to royal prosecutors, and it forbade owners from torturing or mutilating them while authorizing chains and whipping; the Code itself prescribed mutilation and death as judicial penalties for repeated flight. Portuguese Brazil had no equivalent office, so enslaved Brazilians relied more on priests, local magistrates, and petitions such as that of Esperança Garcia. In all three systems, access depended on knowing the procedure, having an intermediary, and surviving the owner's retaliation.

Enforcement shows the distance between text and practice. In Minas Gerais the governor issued regulations in 1722 for the bush captains who pursued fugitives, and cities such as Lima enacted ordinances restricting movement, assembly, and the carrying of weapons. Owners resisted regulations that limited punishment or required them to provide specified goods, and officials needed planters' cooperation to govern, as the reaction to Spain's 1789 instruction shows. Authority therefore rested on a combination of private power, public punishment, and the cooperation of free people, and legal limits on owners existed more as claims that the enslaved could invoke than as constraints that officials reliably imposed.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Regulation of Slavery

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom.

Enslaved people: They lived under the owner's authority and had few means to enforce legal limits, but some used petitions, appeals to priests, requests to be sold to another owner, and flight to press claims about punishment, family, and religion.
Slaveholders: They regarded the enslaved as property and workers whose control was essential to profit and security. Many resisted royal limits on punishment and treatment as interference with their authority, while some also invoked paternalist language about care.
Royal officials and clergy: They sought to preserve order and revenue, claimed a responsibility to regulate cruelty and ensure baptism and marriage, and often enforced these aims weakly because of local pressure and limited resources.
Free people of African descent: They were legally free but often held a precarious place, serving in militias and patrols that policed the enslaved, supporting relatives and brotherhoods, and facing discrimination themselves, with some owning enslaved people and others working to free relatives.
Section 44.15 · Division IV — Families, Demography, Manumission, and Free Black Communities

Women, Children, Marriage, and Family Life Under Slavery

Gender organized enslaved life. Men outnumbered women among captives shipped across the Atlantic, by roughly two to one in many periods though with regional variation, and they filled many field and mining positions. Women worked in the fields, in sugar mills, in households as cooks and servants, in markets, and as skilled workers such as seamstresses and nurses. They also bore children, who, under the rule that status followed the mother, were enslaved. Women were vulnerable to sexual coercion by owners, overseers, and other men, and the records of baptism show many children of mixed ancestry born from such relations. Mothers sometimes sought to protect or free their children, and mothers' ties were often the strongest in the sources.

Families were formed under pressure. Church teaching encouraged marriage among the enslaved, and parish registers record marriages and baptisms. In Spanish America, in principle, owners could not forbid marriage, and spouses were not to be separated, though sales often broke these ties. In Brazil, formal marriage among enslaved people was less common on large estates, and many couples formed lasting unions without a church ceremony. Baptism created godparent relationships that linked enslaved people to one another and, at times, to free people, giving children additional protectors. Large Caribbean plantations with high mortality and skewed sex ratios had less stable families than smaller holdings or regions with more balanced populations. Historians stress that enslaved people maintained kinship and community despite repeated threats, and that family formation is evidence of persistence and not an exception to suffering, since both existed together.

Children were at risk from birth. Infant mortality was high, and children began work at young ages, as helpers and then as field and household laborers. Some were sold away from their mothers. Enslaved midwives and healers played important roles in childbirth and care. Religious practice could provide some protection, since baptized children were recognized in the Church's records and godparents were bound by spiritual kinship. The challenges mothers faced, and the ways they tried to defend their children, appear in the rare surviving documents in which enslaved women speak, of which the petition of Esperança Garcia is a notable example.

Sexual coercion shaped the family histories of enslaved women. Baptismal registers record many children of enslaved women as "natural," meaning born outside a church marriage, and often list the father as unknown or unnamed, and historians read these entries cautiously, because lasting consensual unions also went unrecorded and priests' categories were imprecise. Children fathered by owners or other free men sometimes received better treatment, and some were freed at baptism, but the rule that the child followed the mother kept most in bondage. Enslaved women also worked as wet nurses for the children of free families, which separated them from their own infants and gave their labor an intimate and exploited place in elite households.

Church doctrine and Iberian law supported marriage among the enslaved, and parish registers in Spanish America and Brazil record weddings of enslaved couples. Marriage rates varied by region and estate size, and on large Brazilian estates and in heavily male mining districts, couples faced obstacles to wedding or to living together. Baptism created ritual kinship (compadrazgo) that linked enslaved parents to godparents who might be enslaved, free, or of higher status, and these ties shaped networks of protection. Sale, inheritance, and the owner's decisions about work assignment could break households apart, as Garcia's petition shows. Even so, historians find that enslaved people maintained extended kin networks across estates and towns, using the Church's records and rituals to formalize relationships that law did not fully protect.

Primary Source Window

The Petition of Esperança Garcia, Piauí, 6 September 1770

Creator: Esperança Garcia, an enslaved woman, addressing the governor of the captaincy of Piauí · Date: 6 September 1770 · Place: Piauí, in the Brazilian interior · Type: A petition from an enslaved person to a colonial authority

Context: Piauí was a cattle-ranching region in northeastern Brazil. The royal estates there had been taken over by the Crown after the Jesuits were expelled in 1760, and they were run by administrators. Garcia names the administrator, Captain Antônio Vieira do Couto, and the estate from which she had been moved, the estate called Fazenda Algodões, where she lived with her husband (the estate's name does not by itself establish what it produced). The document was identified by historians in the late twentieth century and has been widely studied and transcribed. The text below follows the transcription published by the Brazilian Bar Association (OAB), which presents it in modernized spelling; students should consult scholarly editions and the original archival manuscript for orthographic and paleographic detail.

Excerpt (Portuguese, in transcription): "Eu sou uma escrava de Vossa Senhoria da administração do Capitão Antônio Vieira do Couto, casada. Desde que o capitão lá foi administrar que me tirou da fazenda algodões, onde vivia com o meu marido, para ser cozinheira da sua casa, ainda nela passo muito mal."

Closing request (Portuguese, in transcription): "Peço a Vossa Senhoria pelo amor de Deus ponha aos olhos em mim ordinando digo mandar ao procurador que mande para a fazenda aonde me tirou para eu viver com meu marido e batizar minha filha."

English paraphrase (by the editors): She identifies herself as a married enslaved woman under the administration of the captain, says she was removed from the Fazenda Algodões, where she lived with her husband to work as a cook in the captain's house, and says she is badly treated there. She describes severe beatings of herself and of her child, a fall while shackled from which she escaped by chance, and the fact that she and other women have gone three years without confession and that her children are unbaptized. She begs the governor, for the love of God, to order the estate's attorney to send her back to the estate so she can live with her husband and have her daughter baptized.

Analysis: The petition is valuable because it was composed by, or in the name of, an enslaved woman about her own circumstances, and it makes specific demands. It combines complaints about violence with appeals to the Church's norms of marriage and baptism, which were also norms that colonial authorities claimed to uphold. This is characteristic of how enslaved people used the language of Catholic duty and royal justice. The document shows labor reassignment, family separation, bodily violence, and religious deprivation as parts of one experience.

It also has limits. We do not know whether the governor acted on it or what happened to Garcia and her family. Scholars discuss whether she wrote the letter herself or dictated it, a question the document alone does not settle. Later accounts of her life and her modern recognition, including an honorary title of lawyer granted by Brazilian legal bodies in the twenty-first century, are separate from the eighteenth-century document and should not be read back into it. She was not a licensed lawyer in 1770, and the description of her letter as a forerunner of habeas corpus is a modern interpretive label. The source shows what a person could ask for and the logic of the request, not how far such requests were honored.

Section 44.16

Demographic Transformation: Population, Mortality, and Reproduction

The forced migration of Africans transformed the populations of the Americas. Imports were heavily male, so many slave societies began with skewed sex ratios, which lowered birth rates. Death rates were high because of overwork, disease, malnutrition, and violence, and infant mortality was severe. In the most intensive sugar-producing regions, such as Saint-Domingue and parts of the Caribbean and northeastern Brazil, deaths often exceeded births, a condition called natural decrease, so that planters maintained and expanded their workforce through continuing imports. Because the new arrivals were African-born, these societies had large proportions of people born in Africa (bozales in Spanish, africanos in Brazil) alongside a growing population born in the Americas (criollos, crioulos, or creoles).

Not every slave society followed this pattern. Where work was less intensive, sex ratios more balanced, and conditions more stable, as in some urban areas, in small holdings, and in parts of mainland Spanish America, enslaved populations could grow, and birth rates in some regions of Brazil and later Cuba rose above death rates at certain times. Quantitative evidence is uneven. Censuses, parish registers, estate inventories, and import records exist for some places and years and not others, and historians estimate rates cautiously. The general conclusion, that intensive plantation regions depended on continuing arrivals while others achieved partial natural increase, rests on many local studies.

Population composition changed across regions and time. By the late eighteenth century, Saint-Domingue's population was overwhelmingly enslaved and African-born. Brazil had a very large population of African descent, both enslaved and free, with major concentrations in the northeast, Rio de Janeiro, and Minas Gerais. Mainland Spanish America combined Indigenous majorities in highland regions with large African-descended and mixed populations in ports, lowlands, and mining districts. Because mixed families formed throughout, the boundaries between categories were fluid, which Chapter 45 examines. The demographic effects of the trade also reached Africa, where the loss of people, especially young adults, and the violence that accompanied capture reshaped communities, though historians continue to debate the long-term demographic and political consequences.

Regional evidence shows how varied the outcomes were. In New Spain, imports declined after the first decades of the seventeenth century, and the African-descended population increasingly mixed with Indigenous and Spanish people. Because the rule that status followed the mother meant that children of free women, including free Indigenous and free Black women, were born free even when the fathers were enslaved, the free population of African descent grew in ways that did not depend on manumission. By the early nineteenth century, estimates place people identified as Black at roughly a tenth of New Spain's population, though such figures depend on how observers classified a mixed population. Lima and other Spanish American cities followed comparable patterns of mixture.

The contrast with the intensive plantation zones is sharp. In Saint-Domingue, the demand for labor was so heavy and mortality so high that a majority of the enslaved in the late eighteenth century had been born in Africa. In Minas Gerais, a male-dominated mining population and large imports kept the proportion of African-born people high, though historians continue to debate how far the region's enslaved population reproduced itself. These differences affected culture and politics. A population of recently arrived Africans maintained languages, religions, and ties to specific origins, while a population born in the Americas had different connections to local society, Church, and law.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Forced Migration and Demographic Transformation

Long-term causes

Iberian traditions of slavery, Atlantic island plantations, the decline of Indigenous labor supplies, expanding European markets for sugar and other goods, and African political economies able to supply captives.

Short-term causes

Booms in particular products, such as sugar, gold, and coffee; imperial contracts and licenses; and merchants' access to credit and shipping.

Event or transformation

The forced transportation of millions of Africans to the Americas over several centuries, concentrated heavily in the eighteenth century.

Immediate effects

Skewed sex ratios, high mortality, dependence on African-born arrivals in plantation regions, and rapid growth of African-descended populations in cities, mines, and ports.

Long-term consequences

Large Afro-Latin American populations, free and enslaved; new cultural and religious forms; racial hierarchies; and later struggles over abolition and citizenship.

Historical complexity

Outcomes varied by region and economy, and enslaved people shaped them through families, communities, and strategies for freedom. The trade also affected African societies.
Section 44.17

Manumission, Self-Purchase, and Paths to Legal Freedom

Freedom could be obtained in several ways. In Brazil, an owner could grant a letter of freedom (carta de alforria), sometimes without payment, sometimes in exchange for money or service, and sometimes conditionally, for example on serving the owner until death. Freedom could be granted in a will, at the baptismal font for an infant, or after years of loyalty, and owners could in some circumstances revoke it for "ingratitude." Studies of Bahia in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries found that freedom was more often obtained by women, by people born in Brazil, and by those of mixed ancestry than by African-born men, and that many letters were purchased and not simply given.

Self-purchase was central. Enslaved workers who earned money in urban hiring-out, in markets, in mining, or on provision grounds could save to buy themselves or relatives, sometimes in installments. In Spanish America and especially Cuba, the practice known as coartación allowed an enslaved person to agree on a price with the owner and pay it in installments, during which the person gained partial protection against being sold at a higher price. In the Brazilian mining region, a similar status was called coartação. Enslaved people could also go to court, sometimes with the help of a protector or a lawyer, to seek freedom or fix the price. Freedom suits, which became more common later, show how law could be turned to account, though owners and courts held the advantage.

Obstacles were severe. Prices were high relative to earnings, and owners could refuse to sell or raise the price. People who began the process remained vulnerable to sale or abuse. Freed people might be required to remain in service, might lose property or family members still enslaved, and might face re-enslavement if documents were challenged. French practice, after the Code Noir, increasingly required official permission and fees for manumission as the colonies grew wealthier and more anxious about the free population of color. These conditions mean that manumission was neither a general trend toward abolition nor an act of simple generosity. It was a negotiated and uneven process.

The most influential quantitative study of manumission in colonial Brazil is Stuart Schwartz's analysis of 1,160 letters of freedom from Bahia between 1684 and 1745. He found that freedom went disproportionately to women, to the Brazilian-born and those of mixed ancestry, and to household and skilled workers, and that many letters recorded payment and not a gift. The pattern suggests that proximity to the owner, skills, and opportunities to earn money mattered as much as the owner's generosity. Studies of other regions, such as Minas Gerais, find that mining towns and cities offered more openings than sugar plantations, while a plantation's isolated field workers had few chances to save.

Spanish American records illustrate parallel routes. Notarial letters of freedom (cartas de libertad) document purchases by enslaved artisans and vendors in Mexico City, Lima, and other cities, and in Cuba the practice of coartación allowed an enslaved person to fix a price, pay by installments, and seek the help of municipal officials if the owner raised it. Collective routes also existed: the royal slaves of El Cobre won freedom through decades of litigation (Section 44.8), and military and other service could support individual petitions. Conditional freedom, in which a person had to serve until the owner's death, and the possibility that an owner could challenge a freed person's status, meant that manumission did not always secure permanent liberty. Chica da Silva's freedom in the early 1750s, which followed her acquisition by her partner, shows that personal relationships with powerful men could also open this route, at a cost to those who lacked such ties.

Historical Vocabulary
Manumission, alforria, and coartación

Manumission is the legal act by which an owner freed an enslaved person. The Portuguese term is alforria, documented in a carta de alforria. Coartación (Spanish; coartação in Portuguese) refers to a status in which an enslaved person agreed with the owner on a price and paid it in installments, gaining some protection from resale in the meantime.

These terms matter because they identify freedom as something obtained through specific procedures, often with payment, and not a single event of release. They should not be mistaken for emancipation, which refers to the general legal abolition of slavery in a society, or for equality, since freed people continued to face discrimination.

Section 44.18

Free Black and Free African-Descended Communities

By the eighteenth century, free people of African descent were a large and important part of many colonial societies. They included those who had obtained freedom through manumission, their descendants born free, and people of mixed ancestry. They worked as artisans, small farmers, vendors, muleteers, sailors, and in some cases as owners of land, shops, and enslaved people. In Brazil's mining region, in Cuban and Puerto Rican towns, along the Venezuelan coast, in Mexican and Peruvian cities, and in Saint-Domingue, they formed households and commercial networks and built local reputations. Some, such as Chica da Silva in Minas Gerais, rose to prominence as partners of wealthy men and as property owners, though her success was exceptional and depended on a relationship with a powerful patron.

Institutions gave them standing. Militias organized by color, such as the Henriques regiments in Brazil, named for Henrique Dias, and the pardo and moreno militias of Spanish America, offered rank, privileges, and a claim on the Crown's recognition in exchange for military service, including service against maroons, foreign invaders, and sometimes enslaved rebels. Religious brotherhoods (irmandades, cofradías) organized by color or African origin provided mutual aid, burial, and festivals, a subject Chapter 46 treats in more detail. Free people of color also used notaries and courts to protect property and family.

Freedom did not bring equality. Free people of African descent faced restrictions on dress, weapons, and public office, exclusion from some guilds, schools, and religious posts, and the stigma of slave ancestry. Spanish regulations requiring "purity of blood" for certain positions affected them, and in 1795 the Crown issued a fee schedule for royal dispensations known as gracias al sacar. Through it a few petitioners of mixed ancestry could obtain, at cost and after a legal process, a dispensation from the disabilities attached to their status. These dispensations were selective, expensive, and legally complex, and not a general right to buy whiteness. They nonetheless show how status could be negotiated and how closely it was tied to race, and elite councils in places such as Caracas protested when they were granted. Relations with the enslaved were complex. Free people of color sometimes helped relatives and friends obtain freedom and supported brotherhoods that included enslaved members, but they also owned enslaved people and served in forces that policed them, and their own security depended on distinguishing themselves from slavery.

Chica da Silva has become a legend in Brazilian popular culture, so her case needs careful treatment. Research by Júnia Ferreira Furtado, based on archival records from the diamond district, shows that Chica was free by 1754 and owned property, including enslaved people, and that she and João Fernandes de Oliveira had many children. She was a woman of standing in a hierarchical district who managed her household and her children's prospects. The popular image of her as a seductress who dominated the district is later mythology that the documents do not support. Her status also depended on a powerful patron, and her situation after João Fernandes returned to Portugal around 1770 remained fragile in a society that kept racial hierarchy in place. She was exceptional, and her life illustrates both the possibilities of freedom and its constraints, not the typical experience of freed women.

Institutions gave free people of color influence. The war against the Dutch in Pernambuco produced the Henriques regiments, whose members enjoyed rank and privileges in exchange for service; after the British occupation of Havana in 1762, Bourbon military reforms expanded Black and pardo militias in Cuba. Religious brotherhoods built churches and managed funds, as the Rosary brotherhood of Black men in Salvador did over much of the eighteenth century. Free women of color worked as market vendors and tavern keepers and, in mining towns, headed households. Their independence did not remove the legal and social barriers they faced, and when militias or brotherhoods asked the Crown for equal treatment, the answers they received were usually partial.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did Free African-Descended Populations Grow in Societies That Kept Slavery?

The question: What explains the size of free African-descended populations in Brazil and Spanish America and the differences among regions? Slavery's existence and its coercive character are not in dispute. The debate concerns the relative weight of law and religion, economic and demographic conditions, local custom, and the strategies of the enslaved and their owners in producing manumission.

Interpretation A — Law, religion, and culture: In the mid-twentieth century, Frank Tannenbaum argued that Iberian law and Catholic teaching, which recognized the enslaved as moral persons with rights to marry and to be freed, made manumission easier in Latin America than in the plantation societies of North America. Later scholars criticized the thesis for overstating the practical force of law and underplaying the brutality of Latin American slavery, but they still treat legal traditions, church practice, and local custom as relevant.
Interpretation B — Economic and demographic conditions, and individual strategies: Many historians, including Herbert Klein and Stuart Schwartz in studies of Brazil and elsewhere, emphasize that manumission rates varied with local economic structure, urbanization, the balance of the sexes, the supply of new Africans, and the opportunities to earn money. Owners freed people for reasons of affection, kinship, convenience, or control, and the enslaved bargained, saved, and sued. On this view, freedom depended heavily on who was enslaved (women, the Brazilian-born, and those with skills or ties to owners had advantages) and on the local economy.

Evidence: Both interpretations use notarial letters of freedom, baptismal and parish registers, wills, court cases, and censuses, and compare regions. Quantitative studies, such as Schwartz's analysis of manumission in Bahia from 1684 to 1745, show patterns by sex, origin, and price, while case studies reveal individual strategies. Most current scholarship combines the approaches and does not treat them as mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: The question affects how Latin American slavery is compared with other slave societies and how the origins of free Black communities are understood. If law and culture dominated, free populations are a product of institutions. If economy and individual agency dominated, they result from specific conditions and from the persistent efforts of enslaved people, and they were compatible with a brutal system.

Student task: Write a short case for each interpretation using the evidence in Sections 44.12, 44.15, and 44.17, and identify what additional records, such as comparative price data or court files, would help decide among them.

Section 44.19 · Division V — African Cultural Survival and the Making of Afro-Latin America

African Identities, Cultural Knowledge, and New Communities

Africans in the Americas were identified by labels that traders, owners, and clergy attached to them, often based on the port of embarkation or a perceived ethnic group. Terms such as Angola, Congo, Benguela, Mina, Nagô, Jeje, Carabalí, Lucumí, Arará, and Mandinga appear in baptismal and sales records. They do not map neatly onto African peoples, and many reflect the commercial geography of the trade. Yet enslaved people also adopted and reshaped these labels, forming "nations" (nações, naciones) as communities of mutual aid, worship, and identity. The names thus recorded both European classification and African self-organization, and students should read them as evidence of a process of creating new Afro-Atlantic identities from diverse origins.

Africans brought languages, technical knowledge, and artistic and musical traditions. Languages of West Central Africa such as Kimbundu and Kikongo contributed words to Brazilian Portuguese (including quilombo), and African languages and creoles were spoken in enslaved communities. Skills in ironworking, textile production, agriculture, cattle herding, healing, and construction were applied in the Americas, and enslaved craftsmen contributed to the building of churches, ships, and houses. Foodways combined African crops and techniques with American and European ingredients, and music and dance based on percussion and call-and-response, practiced in gatherings and festivals, evolved into forms that later became central to Caribbean and Brazilian culture. The origins of particular practices, such as the martial art later called capoeira, are debated, and the earliest firm documentation often comes from after this chapter's period.

Culture changed as it was carried. People from different African regions met in the Americas, and shared experiences of enslavement, shipmate ties, and religious and associational life produced new forms. Names were given at baptism, often a Christian first name with an African origin label. Brotherhoods, the cabildos de nación of Cuba, and other associations became institutions of community, a topic reserved for Chapter 46's treatment of religion. Healing practices and spiritual beliefs persisted and blended with Catholic forms and with Indigenous and European elements. Both continuity and transformation are essential: Africans were neither stripped of culture nor able to reproduce African societies intact under slavery.

Language offers some of the clearest evidence. In Minas Gerais, Antônio da Costa Peixoto recorded a Gbe-based African language in manuscripts dated 1731 and 1741, including the better-known Obra nova de língua geral de mina (1741). Together, they document the continued use of an African language among people from the Mina coast in colonial Brazilian mining communities, and they suggest that it served as a community lingua franca. The preference of many owners for captives from the Mina coast in the mining district rested on a belief that they had special mining skill, an idea historians treat cautiously because it may reflect owners' stereotypes. In Cuba, Lucumí (from Yoruba-speaking regions) and Carabalí (from the Bight of Biafra) became labels for communities that organized cabildos, which held public festivals, including Epiphany processions, documented in Havana by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Technical and artistic knowledge is harder to trace. Historians have argued that West African expertise shaped American practices, and Judith Carney has argued that West African rice-growing knowledge influenced rice cultivation in the Americas, a claim other historians have qualified. In Brazil, enslaved blacksmiths, carpenters, and masons built the towns of the mining district, and drummers, singers, and dancers developed forms such as the lundu, a song and dance documented in the eighteenth century. Brotherhoods sometimes crowned a king and queen from their members, an arrangement that gave enslaved and free African-descended people a public form of leadership. Such evidence shows that culture was reshaped constantly, and that the new communities used African and Iberian forms together.

Section 44.20

Agency, Negotiation, and Resistance Within Slave Societies

Resistance took many forms, and much of it was not open rebellion. Enslaved people bargained over tasks, hours, and conditions, slowed work, feigned illness, damaged tools, petitioned officials and priests, sought new owners, sued for freedom, and ran away, sometimes briefly and sometimes permanently. They also resisted by preserving family ties, religion, and cultural practices against owners' efforts to control them. Historians emphasize that such everyday negotiation shaped slave societies as much as did the occasional large revolt, and that it took place within severe limits, with punishment, sale, and death as constant risks.

Collective action could win concessions, and the best-documented case in colonial Brazil comes from the Engenho Santana in Ilhéus, in southern Bahia. The estate had belonged to the Jesuits, and after their expulsion in 1759 it passed to a private owner, Manoel da Silva Ferreira. In 1789, after a violent confrontation with the estate's management, a large group of the roughly three hundred enslaved people on the estate fled to the forest and left the mill idle, and Stuart Schwartz's study in the Hispanic American Historical Review (1977) reports that about fifty had escaped. While in revolt they drafted a set of terms on which they would return, the document historians call the "treaty" proposed to their master; the date of the treaty and of the negotiations that followed is less certain than the date of the revolt. Scholarship has associated the negotiations with 1791, while the catalog of the Universidade Federal Fluminense's Impressões Rebeldes project, which reproduces the document, assigns the surviving text an attributed date of January 1793, so students should not treat these dates as interchangeable.

The terms are unusually concrete. According to published excerpts, the rebels demanded that they be given Fridays and Saturdays each week to work for themselves, without losing those days to holy days; that they be supplied with nets and canoes so they could fish; that the estate's current overseers be replaced by others acceptable to them; that they be free to plant rice in any marsh and to cut timber without asking permission; and that they be allowed to play, rest, and sing whenever they wished. They also specified how many workers should staff the mill's rollers, which shows that they were negotiating the organization of labor itself. Gregório Luís, described in the records as a cabra (a person of mixed ancestry) and as one of the leaders, was later imprisoned in Salvador, and the owner asked that he not be released except by a sentence of exile, because he believed Gregório would incite further disorder.

Schwartz interpreted the document as a view of slavery from below: the rebels did not demand abolition but sought better terms within the system, including time to cultivate food and sell it, a measure of control over work and supervision, and recognition of their own customs. Other scholars read the same text for the divisions it reveals within the enslaved community, including differences among workers by occupation and origin. The case shows how collective negotiation differed from individual strategies such as petitioning or self-purchase: it relied on the threat of stopped production, a shared set of demands, and a written text. It also shows the limits of the evidence. The document survives because authorities preserved it, and the surviving record does not show how fully the owner conceded its terms.

Flight created autonomous communities. Fugitives established settlements known in Brazil as quilombos or mocambos, in Spanish America as palenques or cimarrón communities, and in the French Caribbean as maroon groups. Some were small and short-lived, while others lasted for decades and negotiated with colonial authorities. San Basilio de Palenque, near Cartagena, for example, became a long-lived community whose negotiated recognition by Spanish authorities is usually dated to 1713, though accounts also refer to earlier agreements and to a reported royal decree in 1691, so the sequence is more complicated than a single date suggests. This chapter introduces these communities as part of the range of responses to slavery. Chapter 47 treats Palmares, the major revolts, and the comparative history of maroon societies, and students should read Section 44.20 as setting up that chapter and not as covering it.

Section 44.21

Afro-Latin America by the Late Eighteenth Century: Diversity, Freedom, and Continuing Enslavement

By the late eighteenth century, the Atlantic trade was at its historic peak, and Afro-Latin America was diverse and deeply embedded in colonial life. Brazil, the largest slave society in the hemisphere, combined northeastern sugar, southeastern and interior mining and farming, and large cities, with enslaved and free African-descended people forming a very large share of the population. Saint-Domingue was the most intensive plantation society, with a vast, mostly African-born enslaved majority, a small white elite, and a wealthy and restricted free population of color. The Spanish Caribbean, with Cuba on the verge of a plantation boom, had urban and small-farm slavery and a large free population. Mainland Spanish America had smaller proportions of enslaved people but essential roles for them and for free people of African descent in cities, ports, mining districts, and estates.

The ratio between enslaved and free people of African descent varied sharply. In Saint-Domingue, enslaved people formed the overwhelming majority of people of African descent. In Brazil, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Venezuela, and many Spanish American cities, free African-descended populations were large, in some places equaling or exceeding the enslaved. These contrasts mattered for what followed. In regions where free populations were large and had militias, property, and institutions, they became actors in politics and in the independence era. In plantation societies with tiny free populations and a huge enslaved majority, the dynamics of conflict were different.

This chapter has told two interconnected histories. One is the expansion of a coercive Atlantic system built on violence, commerce, law, and the demand for labor. The other is the formation of African-descended societies whose families, institutions, knowledge, and struggles became inseparable from Latin American history. The following chapters deepen each strand: Chapter 45 on race and social hierarchy, Chapter 46 on religion and culture, Chapter 47 on resistance and survival, and later Chapter 50 on the revolution that destroyed Saint-Domingue's slave society.

These structures did not determine later events, but they shaped the coalitions and conflicts that Volume III will examine.

The chapter's evidence also changed with the societies it describes. For the African origins of the trade, historians use oral and archaeological evidence together with European accounts; for the scale of the trade, voyage records; for family and freedom, parish registers, notarial letters, wills, and court cases; for resistance and negotiation, petitions and rare documents such as the Engenho Santana treaty. Each type of source records a different side of the system, and none shows enslaved people's experience without the filters of the people who made the record. The synthesis offered here rests on combining them, and on remaining open about what each cannot show.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: African Cultural Survival and the Formation of New Afro-Latin American Societies

What Changed

  • Diverse African peoples were reorganized into American "nations" and communities with new names and identities
  • Languages, music, foodways, and healing practices blended with Iberian, Indigenous, and other traditions
  • Status became hereditary and increasingly tied to African descent, and free African-descended populations grew
  • Birth in the Americas replaced African birth as the majority experience in many regions

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship ties, mutual aid, and communal practices persisted in new forms
  • African technical skills continued in agriculture, metalwork, building, and healing
  • Coercion, violence, and the threat of sale remained basic features of slavery
  • Resistance, negotiation, and the pursuit of freedom continued throughout

Who experienced change most directly: Africans transported across the Atlantic and their children, who had to build communities across language and origin. Greater continuity: Many technical skills and family and community values carried forward, and the structures of coerced labor persisted for owners and the enslaved alike. Why the pattern matters: Afro-Latin America was neither a transplanted Africa nor a product of slavery alone. It emerged from African knowledge, American conditions, and the actions of people who made new societies under constraint.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the enslavement of Africans and their descendants was fundamental to the formation of colonial Latin America, and that Afro-Latin American history cannot be reduced to enslavement. Slavery supplied labor for sugar, mining, ranching, transport, shipping, construction, and households, financed merchants and crowns, and shaped law, religion, and status. At the same time, enslaved Africans arrived with languages, knowledge, and relationships, and they and their descendants built families, associations, markets, and paths to freedom that made them integral to Brazil, the Caribbean, and Spanish America.

The chapter began in Africa, where West and West Central African societies were politically, economically, and religiously diverse. Servitude existed there, but it differed from the hereditary chattel slavery of the Americas, and Atlantic demand expanded and transformed existing practices. Iberian slavery, rooted in Roman-derived law, and Portuguese experiments on Atlantic islands such as São Tomé provided models for American colonization, and as Indigenous labor declined in the Caribbean and coastal Brazil, African captives became the principal labor force in many regions. Slavery gradually became hereditary and associated with African descent.

Captives were obtained mainly through violence and coercion in Africa, by African rulers, merchants, and Luso-African intermediaries in different regional systems, with European and Brazilian merchants providing demand, credit, and goods. Regional histories differed: Senegambia, the Bights of Benin and Biafra, and West Central Africa each had distinct political economies, and the history of Kongo, Ndongo, and Queen Njinga shows African states negotiating, fighting, and trading with the Portuguese. Responsibility for the trade must be assigned with attention to differing powers, without minimizing the coercion borne by the captives.

The Middle Passage was an ordeal of confinement, disease, and violence, with mortality that averaged about 14.5 percent across the whole trade but varied widely. The Voyages database estimates about 12.5 million embarkations and 10.7 million disembarkations between 1501 and 1866, figures that cover much more than this chapter's period. Atlantic geography created distinct circuits, such as the direct Brazil-Angola trade, while Spain relied on asientos held by foreign merchants and other empires organized their own trades. Estimates rest on cross-checked records and carry uncertainty, especially for earlier centuries and for Portuguese and Brazilian voyages.

Brazil's slavery began with Indigenous labor and shifted to Africans on northeastern engenhos from the late sixteenth century. The Dutch occupation of Pernambuco changed rulers and routes but not dependence on enslaved labor. The eighteenth-century gold and diamond economy of Minas Gerais, and the rise of Rio de Janeiro, extended slavery into the interior and shifted the center of Brazil southward. In the Spanish Caribbean, Africans were present from the early sixteenth century, but for most of the colonial era slavery was embedded in urban, port, ranching, and small-farm economies, and Cuba's plantation boom came largely after this chapter's period.

On the Spanish American mainland, enslaved Africans worked in ports, cities, workshops, estates, and mining districts, and were significant even where they were a minority. Saint-Domingue, governed under the 1685 Code Noir and expanding rapidly in the eighteenth century, became the most intensive plantation society, with an enslaved population enumerated at about 434,000 in the 1789 colonial census (and estimated higher by modern scholars), a small white elite, and a prosperous but restricted free population of color. Together these societies show that Portuguese, Spanish, and French slavery differed in law, economy, demography, and the size of free populations.

Enslaved labor was organized differently in different settings. On plantations, gangs and mill work, with skilled positions and provision grounds, shaped bodies and family life. In cities, hiring out, markets, and domestic and maritime work created both earnings and surveillance. Mining, ranching, and transport used enslaved workers in varied arrangements. Law treated the enslaved as property while recognizing limited rights, and the gap between written norms, enforcement, and lived experience was wide. Violence, sexual coercion, and the threat of sale were integral to slaveholding.

Women, children, and families stood at the center of enslaved life. Gender shaped labor and vulnerability, and church teaching on baptism and marriage offered both a claim and a constraint. The 1770 petition of Esperança Garcia shows an enslaved woman invoking family and religious norms against violence and separation, while the limits of the source remind students that we do not know its outcome. Demographically, the trade and the conditions of work produced skewed sex ratios and high mortality, so intensive plantation regions depended on repeated arrivals while other regions saw partial natural increase.

Freedom was obtained through manumission, self-purchase, and sometimes litigation, in processes that were negotiated, costly, and uneven. Free African-descended communities grew large in many regions, working as artisans, vendors, farmers, soldiers, and sometimes owners, and organizing militias and brotherhoods, yet they faced discrimination and restrictions. Historians debate the relative roles of law, economy, custom, and individual strategies in explaining manumission, and most combine these explanations.

Africans in the Americas formed new identities, "nations," and communities, blending African knowledge with Iberian, Indigenous, and other traditions. Resistance included everyday bargaining, petitions, flight, and collective negotiation, as at the Engenho Santana, and it produced autonomous communities that Chapter 47 will examine. By the late eighteenth century the Atlantic trade was at its peak, and Afro-Latin America was diverse, with differing ratios of free to enslaved people in Brazil, Spanish America, and the French Caribbean. The chapter's two histories, the expansion of a coercive system and the making of African-descended societies, run together into the chapters on race, religion, resistance, and revolution that follow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Chattel slavery
A condition in which a person is legal property who can be bought, sold, and inherited, with the status passing to children.
Pawnship
An African practice in which a person was pledged as security for a debt, differing from hereditary chattel slavery.
Partus sequitur ventrem
The legal principle that a child takes the condition of its mother, so that children of enslaved women were enslaved.
Siete Partidas
A thirteenth-century Castilian legal code that treated slavery as law-sanctioned while recognizing the enslaved as persons with certain protections.
Ordenações Filipinas
The Portuguese legal compilation of 1603 that governed Portugal and Brazil, treating the enslaved as property within a framework that also recognized baptism.
Code Noir
The 1685 French royal edict regulating slavery in the Antilles, imposing duties on owners and severe penalties on the enslaved.
Luso-African
Of mixed or acculturated Portuguese and African background, applied to intermediaries who linked interior markets to coastal posts.
Pombeiro
A Luso-African trader who traveled to interior markets in Angola to obtain captives.
Barracoon
A temporary enclosure at African coastal trading sites in which captives were held awaiting shipment.
Middle Passage
The sea crossing of the Atlantic that carried enslaved Africans to the Americas.
Asiento
A Spanish Crown contract granting a merchant or company the right to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America.
Voyages database
The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database compiling records of slaving voyages and estimating the trade's scale.
Engenho
A Brazilian sugar mill and the estate that surrounded it.
Bozal and ladino
Spanish terms for Africans newly arrived from Africa (bozal) and those already acculturated to Spanish language and Christianity (ladino).
Crioulo / criollo
A person of African descent born in the Americas, as distinct from one born in Africa.
Negro de ganho
In Brazilian cities, an enslaved worker who earned wages or income in the streets and paid a fixed sum to the owner.
Quitandeira
A Brazilian market woman, often enslaved or formerly enslaved, who sold food and goods in streets and markets.
Provision ground
A plot on which enslaved people grew food for themselves and sometimes for sale.
Capitão do mato
A Brazilian "bush captain" employed to track and recapture fugitives.
Natural increase / decrease
The difference between births and deaths in a population, which determined whether a slave society could reproduce itself without imports.
Manumission / alforria
The legal act by which an owner freed an enslaved person, documented in a letter of freedom.
Coartación
A status in which an enslaved person agreed on a price with the owner and paid by installments, gaining some protection against resale.
Free person of color / affranchi
A legally free person of African or mixed descent; affranchi is the French term for a freed person.
Militia (pardo and moreno)
Military units, in Spanish America organized by color, that gave free African-descended men rank and privileges in exchange for service.
Henriques
Brazilian militia regiments of Black soldiers, named for Henrique Dias.
Cabildo de nación
A Cuban mutual-aid association organized by African origin.
Nation (nação / nación)
A community of enslaved and free Africans identified by a regional label that both reflected trade categories and became a basis of self-organization.
Quilombo / palenque
Communities of fugitives from slavery, called quilombos or mocambos in Brazil and palenques in Spanish America.
Gens de couleur
The French term for free people of color in Saint-Domingue and other French colonies.
Sex ratio
The proportion of men to women in a population, which in slave societies was often heavily male because of the composition of the trade.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it places Africans and their descendants at the center of Latin American history. Colonial economies, cities, laws, and societies were built in large part on the labor and knowledge of enslaved people, and the regions that Chapter 43 described as productive landscapes were also landscapes of coercion and community. The movement of millions of Africans linked Latin America to Africa and the wider Atlantic world and made Brazil and the Caribbean among the largest African-descended societies outside Africa.

The chapter also shows that enslaved people were historical actors. They bargained, petitioned, saved, sued, fled, formed families and associations, and created new identities. Esperança Garcia's petition reminds students that such actions took place under severe constraint, and that sources recording them must be read with attention to what they cannot tell us. The history of free African-descended communities shows that freedom was neither a simple escape nor a guarantee of equality, but a contested status shaped by law, money, and discrimination.

Chapter 45 takes up the classification of people that slavery, conquest, and mixture produced, examining race, casta, family, gender, and social hierarchy and their relation to legal status. Chapter 46 treats religion and culture, including African religious traditions, and Chapter 47 examines resistance, maroon societies, and survival. Chapter 50 will return to Saint-Domingue, whose slave society this chapter described, to trace the revolution that ended it. Students should carry forward the view that Afro-Latin America was made both by a coercive system and by the people who lived within and against it.
