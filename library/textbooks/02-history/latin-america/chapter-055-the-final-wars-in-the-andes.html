The Final Wars in the Andes

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Independence Declared, Independence Contested

Peru declared independence in 1821, yet the largest royalist army in South America remained in the highlands for three more years. The final victories of 1824 and 1825 came in a landscape of rival governments, mutinies, requisitions, and shifting loyalties, and they produced not one Andean republic but two.

When José de San Martín proclaimed Peruvian independence in Lima in July 1821, the declaration described a political intention more than a military fact. The viceregal army under José de la Serna had withdrawn into the sierra and established its headquarters at Cusco, the old Inca capital, from which it controlled the southern highlands, the silver districts, and much of the population. Independence existed on the coast and in the city of Lima, but it was neither secure nor widely consolidated. This chapter explains how, between 1822 and 1826, that unfinished independence was turned into the defeat of Spanish military power in the central Andes.

The chapter's central problem is double. First, how did a divided and financially exhausted Peruvian republic overcome royalist armies that for a time held the strategic advantage? Second, why did the victories of independence produce two separate republics, Peru and Bolivia, rather than one political order spanning the old viceroyalty's southern core? The answers involve battlefields at Junín and Ayacucho, but they also involve governments that dissolved one another, officers who changed sides, councils that debated sovereignty, and civilians who fed, carried, hid, and sometimes fought for armies they had not chosen.

The cast was international. The armies that fought at Ayacucho on 9 December 1824 included Peruvians, Colombians, Venezuelans, Chileans, men from the Río de la Plata, and officers from Europe, serving alongside Indigenous and Afro-descended soldiers, auxiliaries, and porters. The royalist army, for its part, was overwhelmingly American in its rank and file, recruited and conscripted in Peru and Upper Peru, and commanded by a mix of Spanish-born and American-born officers. The final wars of independence in the Andes were, in this sense, civil wars as much as wars between Americans and Spaniards, and they were also among the clearest cases of coalition warfare in the history of independence.

Two figures dominate the traditional narrative. Simón Bolívar arrived in Peru in 1823 as an outside liberator invited by a government that could not protect itself, and he accumulated extraordinary powers in 1824. Antonio José de Sucre, his most trusted lieutenant, commanded in the field at Ayacucho and then carried the republic's authority into Upper Peru. This chapter treats their roles as distinct and complementary, and it treats both within a larger system of Peruvian politicians, regional commanders, guerrillas, and royalist rivals. It also takes seriously a political event that is sometimes compressed into a footnote: the royalist civil war that opened when General Pedro Antonio de Olañeta rebelled against the viceroy in Upper Peru in 1824.

The chapter has two climaxes, and they are different kinds of victory. Ayacucho dismantled the principal royalist army in Peru and produced a negotiated capitulation. The Declaration of Independence of Upper Peru on 6 August 1825, issued by an assembly at Chuquisaca, resolved a constitutional question that military victory had only opened. The first was a military settlement; the second was a political creation, contested at the time and by later historians. Neither ended the fighting immediately, because the royalist garrison at the Real Felipe fortress at Callao did not surrender until January 1826.

The evidence is uneven and often partisan. Military reports (partes) were written to claim credit and to shape reputations. Memoirs, notably those of the British officer William Miller, were composed from participant knowledge but for audiences abroad. Congressional records, decrees, and the text of the capitulation are more reliable for what authorities decided, though not for what ordinary people experienced. Indigenous communities, women, and enslaved and free Afro-descended people appear mostly in the margins of these papers, in requisition orders, muster lists, tribute accounts, and later petitions. Attention to those margins is part of the method of this chapter.

The chapter's boundaries follow the Authoritative Outline. Chapter 53 narrated Bolívar's northern campaigns and Chapter 54 San Martín's southern ones, including the Proclamation of 1821; both are assumed here. Chapter 56 turns to Brazil. The longer constitutional and social history of republican nation-building belongs to Chapters 58 and 60, which take up Bolívar's constitutional projects and the fragmentation of the Andean republics. This chapter foreshadows those problems but does not resolve them. Its task is to explain the final wars, the creation of Bolivia, and the sense in which victory left the work of independence unfinished.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the declaration of Peruvian independence in 1821 did not secure independence, and describe the military and political situation after San Martín's withdrawal in September 1822.
  2. Trace the failed Intermediate Ports campaigns, the Balconcillo intervention, and the rivalry of governments in 1823, identifying why civilian authority proved unstable.
  3. Distinguish Bolívar's political and strategic role from Sucre's battlefield command, and assess how they complemented one another.
  4. Analyze the royalist split between the viceroy José de la Serna and Pedro Antonio de Olañeta as a product of Spanish constitutional conflict and regional ambition.
  5. Describe how the United Liberation Army was recruited, financed, and supplied, and identify the burdens it imposed on civilians.
  6. Interpret the Battles of Junín (6 August 1824) and Ayacucho (9 December 1824) in relation to terrain, command, coalition warfare, and royalist weaknesses.
  7. Evaluate the terms and significance of the Capitulation of Ayacucho, distinguishing a military settlement from diplomatic recognition of independence.
  8. Explain the competing claims over Upper Peru and why the Assembly of Chuquisaca chose independence on 6 August 1825.
  9. Assess whose preferences the assembly represented and which populations were excluded from its deliberations.
  10. Describe the siege of Callao and the human and economic costs of the final wars.
  11. Contextualize the experiences of Indigenous, Afro-descended, and female participants in the final wars and their aftermath.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XII's argument that independence was a set of interconnected but distinct wars with unsettled political outcomes.

Historical Context

The Viceroyalty of Peru had been the center of Spanish power in South America since the sixteenth century. Its wealth rested on the silver of Potosí and the labor system that supported it, discussed in Chapter 42, and on the tribute and labor of Andean communities organized through ayllus and colonial Indigenous republics. In the eighteenth century the Bourbon reforms (Chapter 48) detached Upper Peru, the territory of the Audiencia de Charcas, from the Viceroyalty of Peru and attached it to the new Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in 1776. The great Andean rebellions of 1780–1781 (Chapter 49) and the fiscal pressure of the reforms left lingering fears among Creole elites, who worried that an insurrection could escape their control.

That fear helps explain why Lima, unlike Caracas or Buenos Aires, did not produce an early juntas movement. Peru was the principal royalist base for the suppression of insurgencies in Quito, Chile, Upper Peru, and the Río de la Plata after 1809. Peruvian resources and troops supported the Spanish cause for a decade. By the time San Martín's expedition landed in 1820 (Chapter 54), the Peruvian elite was divided: some favored continued Spanish rule under the Constitution of Cádiz, which had been restored in Spain in 1820; some favored independence under some form of monarchy or republic; and many preferred whichever option protected their property and status. San Martín entered Lima in July 1821 and proclaimed independence, but the royalist army was not destroyed. It withdrew into the highlands, and it controlled a territory that included Cusco, Arequipa, Huamanga, and much of Upper Peru.

The geography of the war favored the defenders of the sierra. Peru is divided into the desert coast, the high Andes, and the eastern slopes and Amazonian lowlands, and communication among them is slow and costly. Armies moving at altitude suffered from cold, thin air, and lack of forage. Control of valleys, mule trains, and food-producing communities mattered as much as control of cities. The royalists held interior lines and the dense highland population; the patriots held the coast, the port of Callao for part of the period, and access to the sea, which supplied weapons, money, and reinforcements.

The surrounding conflicts were also decisive. Bolívar's victories in Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito (Chapter 53) had created a large, experienced Colombian army and a political structure, Gran Colombia, that could contribute troops. San Martín's departure from Peru in September 1822, after the Guayaquil meeting with Bolívar, left Peruvian patriots without their principal commander. In Spain, the liberal constitutional regime of 1820–1823 was destroyed by the French-backed restoration of absolute monarchy under Ferdinand VII, an event that cut across the viceroy's political legitimacy and gave Olañeta a justification for rebellion. The Andes were therefore part of Atlantic politics, not a remote theater.

Division I · 1822–1823

Peru's Unfinished Independence and Political Crisis

Five sections explain why independence declared in Lima remained militarily insecure and politically unstable after San Martín's departure.

Section 55.1

Peru after San Martín: Independence Declared but Not Secured

San Martín's protectorate, established in Lima in August 1821, governed a coastal territory while the royalist army held the sierra. The protector's government abolished the Indigenous tribute in principle, declared that children born to enslaved mothers after 28 July 1821 would be free, and created a fledgling bureaucracy. Its legitimacy was thin. The Lima elite resented the cost of the army and the influence of foreign officers and ministers, and the protector's monarchist inclinations alienated those who wanted a republic. Spanish naval and military power had not disappeared: the viceregal army retained thousands of soldiers, supported by taxes and labor from the highlands.

San Martín withdrew in September 1822, after his meeting with Bolívar at Guayaquil in July. What was said at that meeting is not recorded in a reliable contemporary transcript, and later accounts differ, a reminder of the limits of the sources. What is clear is that San Martín concluded that his continued leadership was a political obstacle and that he lacked the resources to finish the war. He resigned before the first Peruvian Congress and left the country. The Congress, installed in September 1822, assumed sovereignty and appointed a governing junta, but it did so without an army it could command and without a clear plan for ending the war.

The military balance was less favorable than the independence proclamation implied. The royalist forces were divided into several commands, in the southern highlands, the central highlands around Jauja, and Upper Peru, but they were numerous and experienced, and their officers had defeated numerous insurgencies. The patriot forces in Peru were a mixed body: Chilean and Río de la Plata veterans who had come with San Martín, newly recruited Peruvian units, and Colombian auxiliaries sent by Bolívar. They were poorly paid, inconsistently supplied, and politically fractious. The question for Peruvians was whether the republic could end the war on its own or would have to seek the help of the outside commander who had won the northern campaigns.

Section 55.2

The Governing Junta and the First Intermediate Ports Campaign

The junta that the Congress appointed, led by José de la Mar, sought a quick military result. Its strategy, adopted in late 1822, was an expedition by sea to the "Intermediate Ports" (puertos intermedios) on the southern coast, namely Arica, Iquique, Ilo, and neighboring harbors, from which the force could advance into the southern highlands and threaten the royalist heartland. The reasoning was that a landing in the south would draw the royalists away from the center and could link with insurgent or sympathetic communities in Upper Peru and Arequipa. The campaign was entrusted to Rudecindo Alvarado, an Argentine officer who had served with San Martín.

The expedition sailed in October 1822 with several thousand men. It encountered logistical problems almost immediately: transport was inadequate, supplies were thin, and local support was weaker than planners had assumed. The royalists responded by concentrating forces under General Jerónimo Valdés and General José de Canterac, and they used their interior lines to move against the patriot columns in the highlands. Alvarado's army advanced but did not find the popular support it hoped for, and in January 1823 it was defeated in two battles, at Torata on 19 January and at Moquegua on 21 January. Losses were heavy, and the army retreated to the coast and returned by sea to Callao in disorder.

The consequences were political as much as military. The defeat of the first Intermediate Ports campaign suggested that the Peruvian government's reliance on a Chilean-Argentine-Peruvian army had failed and that the republic's own resources were inadequate. The junta, discredited, faced a Congress already uneasy about its authority and an officer corps that blamed civilians for supply failures. The expedition also illustrated a recurring problem of the independence wars: strategies devised by coastal governments underestimated the logistics and politics of the highlands, where armies depended on local food, labor, and goodwill that could not be commanded by decree.

Section 55.3

The Balconcillo Mutiny and the Rise of Riva Agüero

In late February 1823, officers of the returned army, gathered at the camp of Balconcillo near Lima, demanded that the Congress replace the junta and appoint a single executive. Their pressure was explicit and armed. The Balconcillo military intervention began on 26 February 1823; following an interim transfer of authority, Congress named José de la Riva Agüero president on 28 February under military pressure. Riva Agüero, a Lima-born Creole who had conspired for independence early and had been a prefect under San Martín, became the first person to hold the title of president in Peru.

The episode matters because it established a precedent that would recur in Peruvian politics: the army, rather than a civilian assembly, determined who would hold executive power. Historians debate how far the intervention was a coup in a modern sense, since the Congress ratified the decision under pressure, but it clearly shifted the balance of authority toward the military and toward a leader supported by officers. It also marked the entry of competing visions of the republic. Some deputies favored a strong civilian Congress; some officers favored a strong executive able to wage war; and some politicians looked to Bolívar as a source of both money and troops.

Riva Agüero's government attempted to organize a new campaign and to improve the army's finances through forced loans and requisitions, which fell on Lima merchants and landholders and on highland communities. He also sought assistance from Colombia. The political problem was that his legitimacy rested on an officer-backed imposition and that his administration quickly came into conflict with the Congress and with other commanders. The question of who could command the army and speak for the republic would be answered not by constitutional procedure but by competition among men with soldiers and money.

Section 55.4

The Second Intermediate Campaign and the Royalist Recovery of Lima

In the first half of 1823, Riva Agüero's government prepared a second expedition to the southern ports, this time commanded by Andrés de Santa Cruz, a Peruvian officer of Andean origin who had served in the royalist army before joining the patriots, together with Agustín Gamarra. The force landed on the southern coast in mid-1823 and advanced into the highlands, while Colombian troops under Sucre, sent by Bolívar, had reached the Lima area in the spring. Meanwhile, the royalist commanders recognized that the patriot capital was weakly defended and that the departure of the main patriot units had left Lima exposed.

In June 1823, Canterac led a royalist column from the central highlands down to the coast and occupied Lima, which the patriot government had evacuated, taking refuge in Callao. The royalists remained for only about a month, during which they requisitioned goods and money from the population, and then withdrew into the highlands in July. The episode demonstrated both the vulnerability of the patriot capital and the limits of royalist strategy: the viceregal army could seize the city but could not hold it while the patriot army threatened its rear and while its own supply lines were stretched. For Lima's residents, the repeated change of occupiers brought exactions, uncertainty, and disruption of trade.

The southern campaign itself was inconclusive. Santa Cruz's army advanced into the highlands and fought an engagement at Zepita in August 1823, near Lake Titicaca, which both sides claimed as a success. Royalist forces then pressed the patriots, and by the end of the campaign Santa Cruz's army had been forced to retreat, its numbers depleted by combat, desertion, disease, and exhaustion. The second Intermediate Ports campaign thus failed, like the first, to dislodge the royalists from the southern highlands. It did, however, show that royalist forces could be maneuvered and engaged, a lesson that would shape later planning, and it kept part of the royalist army occupied in the south while the patriots reorganized.

Section 55.5

Rival Governments, the Peruvian Congress, and the Crisis of Republican Authority

While the armies maneuvered, political authority fractured. When the royalists approached Lima in June 1823, the Congress, dissatisfied with Riva Agüero's leadership, vested Sucre with military authority and moved to remove Riva Agüero. Torre Tagle exercised interim executive responsibilities in July and was formally appointed president by Congress on 16 August 1823. Riva Agüero refused to accept his removal. He withdrew to Trujillo with allied officers, dissolved the Congress by his own declaration, and set up a rival government. For several months in 1823, Peru had two governments claiming to be the legitimate republic, one in Lima and Callao under the Congress and Torre Tagle, and one in the north under Riva Agüero.

The rivalry was not merely personal. It expressed the difficulty of reconciling three sources of authority: the Congress as the body of deputies representing the nation; the executive as a war leader; and the army as the guarantor of independence. Riva Agüero's supporters argued that the Congress had abandoned the capital and was subservient to foreign commanders, while Congress's supporters argued that Riva Agüero was usurping authority. Both sides appealed, in different ways, to Bolívar, who was approaching from Guayaquil and whose arrival promised to resolve the conflict by force of arms and prestige.

Riva Agüero's government attempted negotiations with the royalists, a fact that his opponents used to charge him with treason, though historians debate the extent and purpose of the contacts. After Bolívar arrived, Riva Agüero's position collapsed; he was arrested by his own officers and sent into exile in late 1823. The crisis left durable consequences. It demonstrated that independent Peru could not yet organize a stable civilian government in wartime, and it strengthened the argument, advanced by Peruvian politicians themselves, that an outside commander with extraordinary powers was necessary. It also left the impression, important in later debates about Bolívar, that his rise in Peru was invited rather than imposed, though the invitation came from a government in crisis.

Division II · 1823–1824

Bolívar, Royalist Division, and the Reconstruction of the Patriot Army

Four sections examine Bolívar's arrival, the royalist split, the crisis of early 1824, and the construction of a coalition army.

Section 55.6

Bolívar's Arrival in Peru and the Struggle to Unify Patriot Command

Bolívar arrived in Callao and Lima on 1 September 1823. He came at the invitation of the Peruvian Congress and with the authorization of the Colombian Congress, but his presence raised questions that were not answered by the invitation. What authority did a foreign head of state have in another country's war? How far could he command Peruvian officers and use Peruvian resources? Torre Tagle's government welcomed him formally and soon conferred supreme military authority, but political leaders disagreed about how much power to concede and for how long.

Bolívar's position was strategically strong and politically delicate. He controlled a Colombian army, the most experienced force in the patriot camp, and he had prestige from his campaigns in the north. He did not command Peruvian loyalty automatically, and Peruvian officers, some of whom had fought in the royalist army or under San Martín, had their own interests. The Chilean and Río de la Plata troops, many of whom were unpaid and resentful, were a particular concern. Bolívar's early weeks in Lima were spent negotiating command arrangements, securing resources, and assessing a situation that was worse than he had expected, with a depleted treasury, divided leaders, and an army that had been defeated twice.

His own health complicated matters. In the winter of 1823–1824, Bolívar fell seriously ill, and he spent weeks in Pativilca and Trujillo on the northern coast recovering and organizing. These months are sometimes narrated as a period of heroic resolve, but their historical importance lies elsewhere: from the north, he could organize recruitment, supplies, and communications, and he could build a political base outside the turbulent capital. The woman who accompanied and assisted him in this period, Manuela Sáenz, managed correspondence and papers and became a visible figure in his household, though the extent of her political influence is disputed and often romanticized in later writing.

Section 55.7

Divisions within the Royalist Army: La Serna, Olañeta, and the Spanish Political Crisis

The royalist army in Peru, led by Viceroy La Serna, was an experienced force, but it was not politically united. La Serna and many of his senior officers, including Canterac and Valdés, were constitutionalists who supported the liberal regime that had governed Spain since 1820 under the Constitution of Cádiz. Pedro Antonio de Olañeta, who commanded royalist forces in Upper Peru, was an absolutist who opposed constitutional government and who had personal and regional interests in Upper Peru, where his family held property and influence. The difference was ideological but also political and economic, since command in Upper Peru gave Olañeta control of resources, particularly the silver districts.

The break came after news arrived from Spain. In 1823, a French army, with the backing of the European powers of the Holy Alliance, invaded Spain and overthrew the liberal government, restoring Ferdinand VII as an absolute monarch in the autumn. When this news reached Peru in the early months of 1824, Olañeta used it to justify his open rebellion against La Serna, whom he accused of serving an illegitimate constitutional order. He declared himself in revolt in Upper Peru in January 1824, and in the following months he refused to obey orders from Cusco and consolidated his control over much of the territory.

The viceroy was now fighting on two fronts. He sent a substantial force under Valdés, one of his best commanders, to the south to suppress Olañeta, which took thousands of experienced soldiers away from the central theater for much of 1824. The royalist civil war has often been described as a gift to the patriots, and it did reduce the forces available to La Serna in the decisive months. Historians caution, however, against treating it as a sufficient explanation of royalist defeat, since the patriots also had to organize and execute a successful campaign. The question of how much weight to give royalist division is revisited in the Historical Debate later in this chapter.

Section 55.8

The Callao Mutiny, Torre Tagle, and Bolívar's Extraordinary Powers

On 5 February 1824, the garrison of the Callao fortresses, which included Río de la Plata and Chilean soldiers who had not been paid for months, mutinied. The mutineers released royalist prisoners held in the fortresses and, in the confusion, the Real Felipe and other forts passed into royalist hands. The immediate cause was arrears of pay and the resentment of the soldiers against their officers and the government, but the effect was strategic: a principal port and the largest fortress on the coast fell to the enemy, and the royalist cause gained a base and a symbol on the coast.

The crisis deepened the political dilemma. Torre Tagle, president since the Congress had deposed Riva Agüero, was widely suspected of seeking an accommodation with the royalists as his government's position worsened. Over the following weeks, he and some officials entered into contact with the royalist command, and he eventually went over to the royalists in Callao, an episode that Peruvian patriots later condemned as betrayal and that his defenders explained as an attempt to protect his family and property. He died in the besieged fortress in 1825. His defection helped convince the remaining Peruvian leaders that a single strong authority was necessary.

On 10 February 1824, the Peruvian Congress granted Bolívar supreme political and military authority, often described as an extraordinary wartime dictatorship, and withdrew from active deliberation. The decree provided for a possible reconvening of Congress; the constituent body remained legally identifiable but did not continue exercising ordinary legislative oversight. This was an admission that civilian institutions could not manage the crisis. Bolívar used the powers to reorganize the army, to appoint officials, to requisition resources, and to enforce discipline, and he acted with severity against those suspected of collaboration or desertion. The grant of powers is examined as a Primary Source Window below. For historians, it marks the point at which the Peruvian republic delegated the war to an outsider while retaining the formal structure of a congress and a presidency that no longer governed in practice.

Section 55.9

Building the United Liberation Army: Recruitment, Finance, Supply, and Coalition Warfare

The army that Bolívar assembled in 1824 is called the United Liberation Army (Ejército Unido Libertador). It combined Colombian divisions, including veterans of Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito, with Peruvian units and with remnants of the Chilean and Río de la Plata contingents. The Colombian troops, many of them from the Venezuelan plains and from the Caribbean and Pacific coasts of New Granada, included numerous Afro-descended soldiers, some of whom had been recruited from enslaved populations in exchange for freedom, a practice that had been controversial since the early years of Bolívar's campaigns. Peruvian units included coastal recruits and Indigenous soldiers from the highlands.

Recruitment depended on a combination of volunteering, conscription, and coercion. Authorities issued levies on provinces and used press-gangs to fill ranks. Highland communities were required to provide men, food, mules, and porters, and the demands often fell on Indigenous ayllus, which were also supplying the royalists and which had to decide, or be forced to decide, which side to support. Women accompanied armies as cooks, nurses, launderers, and porters, and their labor, often unrecorded, sustained both armies. Enslaved and free Afro-descended people on the coastal estates were recruited, sometimes under promises of freedom, and their estates were required to provide food and money.

Financing the army was an enduring crisis. The Peruvian treasury was nearly empty, and Bolívar relied on forced loans, confiscations, sequestration of royalist property, and contributions from the Colombian government, as well as on the sale and rental of lands. In 1824 he issued decrees on the use and disposal of public lands, discussed further in the section on costs. Weapons and clothing came from Colombia, Chile, and Europe by way of Pacific ports. The practical problems of moving men and supplies at altitude were severe: mules and llamas were requisitioned in large numbers, depots were established at Huaraz, Cerro de Pasco, and elsewhere, and the army trained in the northern highlands during the first half of 1824.

Coalition warfare was both a strength and a source of friction. The army's diversity brought experience and numbers but also tension among Colombian, Peruvian, and other officers over rank, pay, and recognition. Bolívar's command of Colombian officers was strong but not absolute, and his political decisions in Peru were closely watched in Bogotá, where Vice President Francisco de Paula Santander managed the Colombian government and was uneasy about the cost of the Peruvian commitment. These relationships would shape the political consequences of victory, as discussed in Chapters 58 and 60.

Division III · 1824

Junín, Ayacucho, and the Destruction of Royalist Military Power

Five sections form the military center of the chapter, from the opening of the campaign to the capitulation that ended the viceregal army.

Section 55.10

The Campaign into the Central Andes: Strategy, Terrain, and the Royalist Position

By mid-1824, the United Liberation Army had assembled in the northern highlands, in the departments around Huaraz and Cerro de Pasco, and numbered some 8,000 to 9,000 men by most estimates, though figures differ and effective strength was lower after illness and desertion. The royalist forces were divided. Valdés had marched south against Olañeta with several thousand soldiers, leaving Canterac, in the central highlands around Jauja, with perhaps 7,000 to 9,000 men, including a strong cavalry. La Serna remained at Cusco, with additional forces. The patriots' opportunity lay in striking the central royalist army before its detachments could reunite.

The campaign was an Andean problem of altitude and logistics. Moving thousands of men and animals across the high puna, a cold plateau above 4,000 meters, required careful planning of routes, water, fodder, and shelter. Soldiers from tropical and coastal regions suffered from altitude sickness, exposure, and frostbite. The patriots depended on Indigenous guides, porters, and communities of the central sierra, where montoneras, local irregular forces, had resisted royalist exactions and supplied information. The royalists, who knew the terrain, had interior lines but also faced the rigors of a long campaign and the burden of provisioning large forces from a limited agricultural base.

Bolívar's strategic aim was to advance from the north through Cerro de Pasco toward the Mantaro valley and the Jauja area, forcing Canterac either to fight or to withdraw toward Cusco. The route was difficult, and the army's passage over the heights in July 1824 involved severe hardship. Canterac, informed of the approach, moved north from Jauja with his cavalry and infantry to meet it, and the two forces maneuvered in the plateau around Lake Junín (Chinchaycocha), a shallow high-altitude lake at roughly 4,100 meters. The contest was to be decided there by cavalry.

Section 55.11

The Battle of Junín, August 6, 1824

The Battle of Junín was fought on 6 August 1824 on the plain near Lake Junín. It was a cavalry engagement fought almost entirely with lances and sabers; according to accounts by participants, no firearms were used in the decisive phase, partly because of the conditions and the haste of the encounter. Bolívar commanded the patriot army in the campaign and was present with the cavalry, which included Colombian hussars, Argentine and Peruvian units, and other mounted formations, perhaps 900 men. Canterac's royalist cavalry, probably numbering over 1,000, was among the best in the Spanish army.

Accounts of the sequence differ in detail, as is common with battles recorded by partisans. Most agree that the royalist cavalry, advancing across a narrow stretch of terrain, charged and threw the patriot cavalry into disorder, so that some units were driven back and others cut off. Then a counter-charge by a Peruvian cavalry squadron under Isidoro Suárez, an officer born in the Río de la Plata, struck the royalists as they dispersed in pursuit and helped turn the engagement. Other patriot cavalry included the Colombian Hussars under Laurencio Silva and Peruvian mounted units associated with William Miller. Students should note that accounts attribute leading roles to several officers, including the Argentine Mariano Necochea, who was badly wounded, and that the sources reflect competing claims to credit.

Casualties are reported variously; the royalists are generally said to have lost a few hundred killed and wounded and many prisoners, while patriot losses were much smaller, though numbers should be treated as estimates. Canterac withdrew with his infantry southward, abandoning positions and equipment. Junín did not destroy the royalist army, but it gave the patriots a decisive moral and strategic advantage. It showed that the royalist cavalry could be defeated, it restored confidence after the setbacks of 1823 and the mutinies of 1824, and it forced Canterac to retreat toward Cusco. Peru later gave the name Hussars of Junín to the Peruvian hussar regiment, an expression of the importance of the victory to national memory. Students should recognize that this memory, like most battle commemoration, simplifies a complicated event in which Colombian, Peruvian, and other soldiers fought together.

Section 55.12

The Campaign after Junín: Sucre, Maneuver, and the Road to Ayacucho

After Junín, the royalist army withdrew to the southern highlands, and the patriots followed. Bolívar's attention was increasingly drawn to political problems, including the instability of Peru's government, the pressure of Colombian politics, and the need to organize the army's supply. In the autumn of 1824 he left the immediate field command to Sucre and returned toward the coast, an arrangement that gave Sucre responsibility for the campaign while Bolívar kept strategic oversight. The division of labor was not seamless. Bolívar issued instructions from a distance, and Sucre exercised independent judgment on the ground, with the support of officers such as Córdova, Lara, La Mar, and Miller.

The royalist forces regrouped. Valdés returned from Upper Peru with part of his force after a campaign against Olañeta that had not resolved the rebellion, and Canterac and La Serna concentrated their army near Cusco. In late 1824, the viceroy decided to move against Sucre's army, which was marching in the southern highlands, and a campaign of maneuver followed in the valleys and passes between Andahuaylas and Huamanga. Sucre sought to avoid a battle under unfavorable conditions, to preserve his army, and to find a position in which the royalists would be compelled to attack. The armies shadowed one another for weeks, with each seeking to cut the other's supply lines.

On 3 November 1824, at Corpahuaico, royalist forces under Valdés attacked the patriot rear guard while it was crossing a river canyon and inflicted heavy losses, in a clash that threatened to disrupt Sucre's army. The patriots recovered and continued their withdrawal and maneuver. By early December, both armies had converged on the high plateau near Ayacucho, a district whose name in Quechua is often translated as "corner of the dead," though the translation is conventional and sometimes disputed. Sucre took a position on the Pampa de la Quinua, a plain beneath the ridge of Condorcunca, where the royalist army descended to meet him.

Section 55.13

The Battle of Ayacucho, December 9, 1824

The Battle of Ayacucho was fought on the morning of 9 December 1824 on the Pampa de la Quinua. Sucre commanded roughly 5,800 men in three infantry divisions—Córdova on the right, La Mar on the left, and Lara in reserve—with cavalry under William Miller near the center. His army included Colombian, Peruvian, Venezuelan, and other troops. The royalist army under La Serna, with Canterac and Valdés as principal subordinates, is usually said to have numbered between 6,500 and 9,300 men, with figures varying according to source and the way in which auxiliaries are counted. Royalist forces enjoyed a numerical advantage, but they had to attack from the heights, which carried tactical risks.

The viceroy chose to descend and attack. Accounts agree on the broad pattern: the royalist right, under Valdés, pressed the patriot left under La Mar; Córdova's division on the patriot right struck against the royalist center and left as the battle developed; and Lara's reserve and Miller's cavalry helped complete the collapse. The details of the sequence are disputed, as participants wrote partisan reports. The battle was brief, lasting perhaps one or two hours, and the royalist army disintegrated rapidly once its center failed, with many units surrendering on the field.

Casualty figures vary, but royalist losses were heavy, probably well over a thousand killed, in addition to several thousand prisoners, while patriot losses were a few hundred killed and wounded. La Serna was wounded and captured. Numerous senior royalist officers were taken, including Canterac, Valdés, and other generals. The magnitude of the defeat is clear in the fact that, within hours, the principal Spanish army in South America had ceased to exist as an effective force.

Explaining the result requires attention to several factors: Sucre's choice of ground and his tactical discipline; the quality and morale of the patriot divisions, especially Córdova's; the royalist decision to take the offensive; the weakening of royalist forces by the Olañeta rebellion; and the fatigue, supply problems, and political divisions of the royalist command. Historians disagree about the relative weight of these factors, a question taken up in the Historical Debate. All agree that Ayacucho ended Spanish military power in Peru, but they also emphasize that it did not end the war, as the next sections show.

Section 55.14

The Capitulation of Ayacucho and the Collapse of the Viceregal Army

On the day of the battle, 9 December 1824, Sucre and Canterac, acting for the wounded and captured La Serna, agreed to a capitulation, a written military settlement. The agreement provided for the surrender of the royalist army, the territory it controlled, its garrisons, depots, and military property, in exchange for guarantees for the defeated soldiers and officers. According to its generally reported terms, the royalists would hand over the territory of Peru still held by the Spanish army up to the Desaguadero, the river that divides modern Peru and Bolivia, along with armaments and supplies; Spanish officers and soldiers who wished to return to Spain would be given passage; and some officers could retain their ranks or receive other consideration under the Peruvian government. Students should consult the primary text, summarized in a Primary Source Window below, for the precise articles.

The capitulation was a significant document in several respects. It was a negotiated end to a military campaign, not an unconditional surrender, and it reflected Sucre's willingness to be generous to a defeated army whose officers, many of them American-born, could contribute to the new order or leave the country. It transferred control of Cusco and the southern highlands to the patriots, and Sucre entered Cusco, the old Inca capital, on 24 December. But it was a military agreement among commanders, not a treaty between states. Spain did not recognize Peruvian independence by this act, and recognition would come only in the following decades, after long diplomatic delays.

Nor did the capitulation end all resistance. Two royalist forces refused to be bound by it: the garrison of Callao under Rodil, who rejected the authority of the captured viceroy and the terms, and the forces of Olañeta in Upper Peru, who did not accept that the defeat of La Serna affected his command. The viceregal army, as the structure that had governed Peru for three centuries, was gone, but fragments of royalist military power remained. The next division examines the first of these, in Upper Peru, where the final military and political questions of the Andean wars were decided together.

Division IV · 1824–1825

Upper Peru and the Creation of Bolivia

Five sections explain how the liberation of Upper Peru became a constitutional question, and why it ended in a new republic.

Section 55.15

Sucre's Advance toward Upper Peru and the Collapse of Remaining Royalist Authority

Upper Peru, the territory of the old Audiencia de Charcas, included the silver city of Potosí, the administrative city of Chuquisaca (also called La Plata, and later Sucre), the commercial centers of La Paz and Cochabamba, the lowlands of Santa Cruz, and a large Indigenous population of Aymara, Quechua, and other peoples. It had been the scene of an irregular war since 1809, in which guerrilla bands, the republiquetas, controlled rural zones and harassed royalist forces. Leaders such as Manuel Ascencio Padilla and his wife Juana Azurduy fought in these campaigns, and Azurduy, who commanded troops and was wounded in battle, became a symbol of women's participation in the independence wars.

After Ayacucho, Sucre moved into Upper Peru in early 1825 with a force of Colombian and Peruvian troops. The region was politically fragmented. Olañeta controlled the south and the central cities, but his authority was weakening, as officers and communities recognized that the royalist cause was collapsing. Several royalist commanders and cities accepted the capitulation and recognized the patriot authorities, while others vacillated. In La Paz, Oruro, and Cochabamba, civilian and military leaders declared for independence in the weeks after Ayacucho. The collapse of royalism in Upper Peru was not simply a conquest by Sucre's army. It was also a series of local decisions by officers and councils who judged that resistance was futile.

Sucre's advance, supported by local guerrillas and by defectors, brought him through Puno and La Paz and toward Potosí and Chuquisaca. He faced the problem of governing as he advanced: Upper Peru had no recognized government, he had no instructions from Bolívar covering its political status, and the Peruvian and Argentine governments each had claims. This political question, rather than the remaining military resistance, would occupy him in the following months.

Section 55.16

Olañeta, Tumusla, and the End of Organized Royalist Resistance in Upper Peru

Pedro Antonio de Olañeta had rebelled against La Serna, but he was also a royalist, and he refused to accept the capitulation. In early 1825 he held the south of Upper Peru with a force of perhaps 2,000 men and saw his authority erode as officers defected. His nephew, Casimiro Olañeta, a lawyer and politician, abandoned him and joined the patriots, a move that illustrated the pragmatic calculations of regional elites and that would give Casimiro an important role in the creation of Bolivia. Other officers, including Colonel Carlos Medinaceli, turned against their commander.

The final events occurred on 1 April 1825 at Tumusla, in the south of what is today Bolivia. According to the best-supported chronology, Medinaceli, who had defected to the patriot cause, confronted Olañeta's forces in a clash in which Olañeta was mortally wounded and died soon after. Whether the encounter was a battle, a mutiny, or a skirmish in which royalist officers turned on their commander is debated, and accounts vary in detail, scale, and responsibility. Participants wrote partisan narratives, and later Bolivian national memory has sometimes presented Tumusla as the last battle of the independence wars, a claim that historians qualify. What is established is that Olañeta died in early April 1825 and that his force collapsed shortly afterward, ending organized royalist resistance in Upper Peru.

The death of Olañeta did not end armed conflict in every locality, and scattered bands and local disorder persisted in some regions. But it marked the end of royalist military authority in Upper Peru. Sucre, who arrived in Chuquisaca in early April, was now the effective ruler of the territory, and the question of what political order would replace the royalist regime became the central issue.

Section 55.17

Who Should Govern Upper Peru? Sovereignty, Historical Jurisdictions, and Competing Claims

On 9 February 1825, from his headquarters in La Paz, before the final royalist collapse, Sucre issued a decree calling an assembly of deputies from the provinces of Upper Peru to consider a provisional government and the territory's future. The decree, examined in a Primary Source Window below, did not itself declare independence, and it reflected Sucre's belief that the provinces, not an outside army, should determine their political status. The decree was controversial. Bolívar, who had not authorized it, was uneasy about its implications and concerned about its legality, and he regarded the question as one for the Peruvian and Argentine governments and for the broader Spanish American community.

Several outcomes were possible. Upper Peru could be incorporated into the Río de la Plata, to which the Audiencia de Charcas had belonged since 1776; it could be attached to Peru, with which it had been united for most of the colonial period and to which it had commercial and economic links; or it could become an independent state. Each claim relied on a principle. The Argentine position appealed to the doctrine of uti possidetis juris, that new states inherit the colonial administrative boundaries of 1810; Peruvian arguments appealed to history, geography, and commercial ties; and a growing group of Upper Peruvian elites preferred separation. The Argentine government, in the Congress of the United Provinces, eventually acknowledged that the provinces should decide for themselves, a position that facilitated the independence outcome.

The preferences of local elites were crucial. Upper Peruvian lawyers, landowners, mining entrepreneurs, clergy, and officials had interests in autonomy. Many feared domination by Lima or Buenos Aires, and some resented the decades of war and requisition. Others, particularly in Cochabamba and La Paz, had links to Peruvian markets. The preferences of the Indigenous majority, who made up the large part of the population, are far harder to recover. They had been taxed, conscripted, and used as labor by both sides, and the debates in Chuquisaca were conducted in Spanish by literate men. This is a recurring issue in the history of independence: assemblies claimed to speak for populations whose own preferences were rarely consulted.

Section 55.18

The Assembly of Chuquisaca and the Declaration of Bolivian Independence

The assembly opened at Chuquisaca on 10 July 1825 with deputies from the provinces, numbering roughly four dozen, mostly lawyers, clergy, landowners, and officials. The deputies were chosen under rules that excluded the large majority of the population. Voting qualifications and the practical difficulties of travel and wartime disruption meant that the assembly represented a narrow elite. It is nevertheless an important example of an independence process conducted through a representative body rather than by military decree alone.

The deliberations centered on the question of whether to join Peru, join the Río de la Plata, or form a separate state. Sucre, who attended as a guest and as the military authority, remained neutral in public, but his preference for independence and his respect for the assembly's autonomy were widely understood. Casimiro Olañeta, now a prominent advocate of separation, argued strongly for independence. On 6 August 1825, the assembly adopted the Declaration of Independence of Upper Peru, which proclaimed the provinces independent of Spain, of Buenos Aires, of Lima, and of any other foreign power. The date is commonly said to have been chosen to coincide with the first anniversary of the Battle of Junín, a link to the Bolivarian narrative that the new state's founders sought to emphasize, though students should treat the claim of deliberate choice as tradition rather than documented fact.

The assembly chose the name Republic of Bolívar, soon changed to Bolivia, in honor of the Liberator, and it invited him to draft the first constitution. Bolívar, who had been in Lima, welcomed the decision. The choice of name, in a republic founded by an assembly of Creole elites, reflected both genuine admiration and the political calculation that identification with Bolívar offered protection against the claims of neighbors. The Declaration is examined in a Primary Source Window. Students should remember that independence was declared by an assembly that spoke in the name of a population, and that the legitimacy of this claim depended on the authority of the deputies, not on a popular vote.

Section 55.19

Founding Bolivia: Bolívar, Sucre, Political Representation, and the Colonial Inheritance

Bolivia's creation was a political process that combined several elements: the geography of the old Audiencia de Charcas; the authority of Sucre's army; the interests of local elites; the symbolic prestige of Bolívar; and the diplomatic acquiescence of Peru and the Río de la Plata. None of these alone was sufficient. The army had no authority to create a state, the elites had no army, and the neighbors had claims they were unwilling or unable to enforce. The outcome emerged from their interaction.

Bolívar traveled to Upper Peru in late 1825, visiting Potosí, where he was received with ceremonies, and Chuquisaca, and he entrusted the new state's first government to Sucre, who became Bolivia's first head of state, first as head of a provisional government and then, in 1826, as constitutional president. Bolívar prepared a constitution for Bolivia, which proposed a lifetime president and a three-chamber legislature. This constitution, and the political experiments connected to it, belong to the story told in Chapter 60. For present purposes, the point is that the founding of Bolivia involved the extension of a particular vision of republican order, centralized and authoritarian, to a society with deep inequalities.

The new state inherited the colonial economy and its labor relationships. Silver mining at Potosí had been devastated by war, flooding, and capital flight. The Indigenous tribute, which San Martín and then Bolívar had abolished in principle, remained fiscally essential, and the new government's treasury depended on it. Haciendas, tribute-paying communities, and mining enterprises continued to organize rural life. Indigenous communities faced pressure on their lands and demands for labor and taxes from both the state and landowners. These continuities, rather than the declaration of independence, shaped most people's experiences in the years that followed, and they are discussed in the continuity and change feature later in this chapter.

Division V · 1825–1826

The Last Strongholds and the Consequences of Independence

Four sections follow the last royalist garrison, count the costs of the wars, and ask what victory settled and what it left open.

Section 55.20

The Siege of Callao and the Last Major Royalist Stronghold in Peru

José Ramón Rodil, who had held command of the Real Felipe fortress since the mutiny of February 1824 brought it under royalist control, refused to accept the Capitulation of Ayacucho. He argued that the captured viceroy could not bind a garrison that had not been present at the battle, and he determined to hold the fortress for the Spanish crown. The Real Felipe was a large, star-shaped stone fortress built in the eighteenth century to defend Callao, the port of Lima, and it was well designed for a long defense. Within its walls were soldiers, civilian refugees, including families who had fled Lima, and officials who had gone over to the royalists, among them Torre Tagle.

The patriots established a siege by land and sea, with the Peruvian government and Bolívar's officers directing operations and the Peruvian naval squadron sustaining a prolonged blockade of the harbor, despite shortages and the difficulty of policing maritime access. The siege lasted more than a year. Bombardments and sorties were intermittent, but the principal weapons were hunger and disease. As supplies ran out, the garrison and refugees suffered severe malnutrition and scurvy, and mortality rose steeply. Contemporary and later accounts describe the consumption of animals and the deaths of many inside the fortress, among them Torre Tagle, who died during the siege. Estimates of the numbers who died vary widely, and the accounts, written by both besiegers and survivors, are shaped by competing narratives of heroism and cruelty.

Rodil surrendered on 23 January 1826, on terms that permitted the surviving garrison to leave. The fall of the Real Felipe ended the last major royalist stronghold in Peru and, with it, the last organized Spanish military presence in the former viceroyalty. A few small Spanish positions persisted elsewhere in the Americas, notably in Chiloé off the Chilean coast until 1826, and Spain retained Cuba and Puerto Rico, which are discussed in Chapters 57 and 67. But the siege of Callao closed the military chapter of the Andean wars. It also left a lasting reputation: in Spain, Rodil was honored for his tenacity; in Peru, he remained a figure of controversy.

Section 55.21

The Human and Economic Costs of the Final Independence Wars

The wars of 1822–1826 added to a decade and a half of conflict that had already strained the region's population and economy. Precise casualty figures are unavailable, and estimates for the entire period vary widely, but the burdens were evident in the countryside. Battles killed relatively few compared with disease, hunger, exposure, and the effects of displacement. Armies moving through the highlands carried epidemics, and the requisitioning of food, animals, and labor left communities with reduced harvests and herds. Mules, llamas, and pack animals were taken in large numbers, disrupting trade and agriculture.

Economic life suffered across regions. Mining in Peru and Upper Peru had declined during the wars, as mines flooded, mercury supplies were interrupted, and capital and skilled labor left. Textile production in the highlands, already weakened by competition from imports, was further disrupted. Commerce suffered from the movement of armies, forced loans, and the collapse of regional trade networks that had linked Cusco, Potosí, and the coast. Governments financed wars by seizing property, imposing contributions, and borrowing; the Peruvian state's first foreign loans, contracted in London in the 1820s, burdened the new republic with debt before it had established a stable fiscal system. These financial problems are examined further in Chapter 58.

The costs were unequally distributed. Landowners and merchants lost property and income, but they also found opportunities in contracts, confiscated royalist estates, and offices. Indigenous communities bore the brunt of requisitions and labor demands and had little access to compensation. Enslaved people on coastal estates were taken for the armies or lost to flight, but slavery itself endured in Peru and Upper Peru, and the promise of freedom made to some soldiers was inconsistently honored. Women, who had served as camp followers, nurses, and suppliers, and who had managed households and properties when men were away or killed, received little recognition. Veterans, particularly the Colombian and Río de la Plata soldiers left in Peru, faced uncertain futures, and many were never paid in full.

Section 55.22

Victory and Republican Authority: Peru, Bolivia, and the Problems of Sovereignty

Victory raised, rather than answered, the question of political authority. In Peru, Bolívar held extraordinary powers granted for the duration of the war, and as the fighting ended, the Congress and Peruvian politicians disagreed about how long he should retain them. Bolívar called for a new constituent congress to meet in 1826, but its preparatory meetings did not result in a regularly installed legislature. He withdrew from Peru in September of that year, leaving a Colombian-influenced governing structure and a divided political class. The Peruvian elite resented the continued presence of Colombian troops, which were costly and politically sensitive, and by 1827 these troops had left, an episode that heightened tensions between Peru and Gran Colombia and that contributed to the war between them in 1828–1829, discussed in Chapter 60.

Bolivia faced a parallel problem. The new state, founded by an assembly of elites and led by a foreign general, needed to establish institutions, raise revenues, and defend its borders. Its government under Sucre introduced reforms, including educational initiatives and fiscal measures, but faced opposition from landowners, regional elites, and the army, and it depended on Colombian troops that were also unpopular. Sucre's administration would end in 1828 amid a military revolt, and Bolivia entered a period of instability that is examined in later chapters.

The questions that victory left open were basic. What were the borders of Peru and Bolivia, and who would resolve disputes? What was the relationship between civilian government and the military? How would new states manage Indigenous communities and tribute, slavery, and the Catholic Church? How would regions with different economies and interests, including Cusco, Arequipa, Lima, Potosí, La Paz, and Santa Cruz, be integrated? These problems, which preoccupied the Andean republics for decades, show that the defeat of Spanish military power was only the first stage of state formation. Their detailed treatment belongs to Chapters 58 and 60.

Section 55.23

The End of Spanish Military Rule and the Unfinished Work of Independence

By early 1826, Spanish military rule in the central Andes had ended. The viceregal army had been destroyed, its commanders had surrendered, and the last major garrison had capitulated. The institutions that had governed Peru for three centuries, the viceroyalty, the audiencias, and the apparatus of royal finance, had been replaced by republican governments, though with extensive continuities in personnel and practice. Many former royalist officers, officials, and landowners remained influential in the new order, a reminder that the wars had been civil conflicts in which loyalties shifted and in which victors often sought reconciliation with former enemies.

The end of Spanish rule was not the end of Spanish claims. Spain refused to recognize the independence of its former colonies for years, and formal recognition of Peru did not come until 1879, after prolonged diplomacy and a later naval conflict in the Pacific in the 1860s. For the people of the Andes, the immediate significance of independence was less diplomatic than practical: whether taxes, labor demands, and landholding practices would change, whether rights and citizenship would be extended, and who would govern.

The answers were mixed. Formal legal equality was proclaimed, and the Indigenous tribute, abolished in principle, was reintroduced under another name when the state needed revenue. Slavery persisted, with gradual measures such as the free-womb law of 1821 reducing but not ending it, and abolition came to Peru only in 1854. Indigenous communities gained some protection from the abolition of colonial institutions and lost others, as liberal policies eroded communal landholding. Women remained excluded from formal political life. These patterns illustrate the argument that runs through Part XII: that independence ended the structures of Spanish military and imperial authority but left unresolved who would hold power in the new republics and how historically subordinate populations would experience it. The next chapter turns to Brazil, where independence took a different political form.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitulation

A capitulation (capitulación) was a written agreement by which a military commander surrendered forces, positions, or territory on stipulated terms. In Spanish and Spanish American practice, the term also described agreements between the crown and conquerors, so students must distinguish context.

The Capitulation of Ayacucho, signed on 9 December 1824, settled the fate of the defeated army and its territory but was an agreement between commanders, not a treaty of recognition between sovereign states. The distinction explains why Spanish recognition of Peruvian independence came only decades later, and why Rodil could claim that the capitulation did not bind the Callao garrison.

Historical Vocabulary
Montonera

A montonera was an irregular armed band, commonly mounted or locally recruited, used in the independence wars in the Río de la Plata, Upper Peru, and the Peruvian sierra. In Peru, montoneras of the central highlands harassed royalist columns, gathered intelligence, and sometimes cooperated with patriot armies.

The word describes a mode of organization, not a single social group. Some montoneras were led by Creoles and mestizos, others were community defense forces, and some had their own agendas that did not coincide with those of either army. The term is useful for recognizing that the war was fought by civilians as well as regular soldiers, but it should not be taken to imply a unified popular movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Uti possidetis juris

Uti possidetis juris (Latin, roughly "as you possess under law") is the principle that new states inherit the administrative boundaries of the colonial units from which they emerged. Spanish American governments often invoked colonial jurisdictions as of 1810, though the boundaries were frequently vague.

In the case of Upper Peru, the principle supported Argentine claims, since the Audiencia de Charcas belonged to the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata after 1776, while Peruvian arguments drew on older ties. The principle did not settle the question because the provinces themselves claimed a right to decide, an assertion of self-determination that complicated the doctrine.

Primary Source Window

The Peruvian Congress and Extraordinary Powers for Bolívar, February 1824

Creators: The Peruvian Congress · Date: 10 February 1824 · Type: A legislative decision conferring political and military authority

Context: Following the Callao mutiny and amid suspicions surrounding Torre Tagle, Congress granted emergency powers to Bolívar and went into recess. Later decrees provided for its reconvening.

Paraphrase: Facing military emergency, the Congress entrusts Bolívar with supreme political and military authority. It enters recess rather than continuing to meet in ordinary session, and provides a mechanism for Bolívar to recall it in extraordinary circumstances. The measure is justified as necessary to preserve the republic.

Analysis: The document reveals how civilian leaders understood emergency powers and how they justified delegating authority to a foreign commander. It does not show the divisions within Congress, the pressure applied by officers, or the views of those excluded from politics. Historians read it alongside letters and memoirs to assess how far the grant was voluntary. Students should consult the published text in a verified collection of documents from the Peruvian independence period.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the document say about the purpose and duration of Bolívar's authority?
  2. Who created it, and under what pressures?
  3. What can historians learn about Peruvian political thought from it?
  4. What can they not establish from this document alone?
Primary Source Window

An Official Report (Parte) on the Battle of Ayacucho

Creator: Antonio José de Sucre · Date: 11 December 1824 · Type: Official report of the battle (parte), subsequently printed in the Gaceta del Gobierno

Context: Sucre's report described the battle for Bolívar and for the Peruvian and Colombian governments. Official reports shaped reputations and rewards, and officers competed for credit.

Paraphrase: Sucre recounts the maneuvering and withdrawal before Ayacucho, explains the deployment and conduct of his troops, and credits officers and formations for the victory. He reports approximately 5,780 soldiers in the patriot force against approximately 9,310 royalist troops, figures that reflect the victorious commander's official accounting. He presents the victory as securing Peru's independence and ending the principal campaign.

Analysis: Sucre's dated report is valuable for chronology, commanders, claimed troop strengths, and the victor's immediate interpretation. It is also partisan: numerical estimates and praise of individual officers must be compared with royalist reports, Miller's memoirs, and other evidence. Its claim of a completed campaign does not account for the subsequent resistance at Callao and in Upper Peru.

  1. What kinds of information does a military report contain?
  2. Who wrote it and why?

  4. What can they not establish from this source alone?
Primary Source Window

The Capitulation of Ayacucho, 9 December 1824

Creators: Sucre and José de Canterac, acting for the captured viceroy · Date: 9 December 1824 · Type: A military capitulation in articles

Context: The agreement was written on the field of battle, soon after the royalist collapse, by commanders who needed to settle the disposition of thousands of soldiers and large areas of territory.

Paraphrase of the generally reported terms: The royalist army surrendered, with the territory it held in Peru up to the Desaguadero, its garrisons, and its military property. Provisions covered prisoners, the treatment of officers and soldiers, and the passage of those who wished to return to Spain. The agreement is framed as a military settlement and does not itself recognize Peruvian independence in the sense of a treaty with the Spanish state.

Analysis: The text shows Sucre's pragmatism and the royalist command's recognition of defeat. It does not show how the terms were implemented, how the soldiers felt, or why Rodil rejected it. The precise articles should be read in a verified edition, such as those preserved in Peruvian diplomatic and historical archives.

Primary Source Window

Sucre's Decree Calling an Assembly for Upper Peru, 9 February 1825

Creator: Antonio José de Sucre · Date and place: 9 February 1825, La Paz · Type: A decree of a military commander

Context: Issued before the final collapse of Olañeta's forces, the decree set out how deputies from the provinces would be chosen and convened.

Paraphrase: The army's purpose in entering Upper Peru is to free the provinces from Spanish rule, not to decide their domestic affairs. Deputies elected through parish and provincial procedures should assemble to establish provisional government and deliberate on the provinces' future. The decree anticipates an agreement involving the congresses of Peru and the Río de la Plata, and states that military intervention in the assembly's decisions would invalidate them.

Analysis: The decree is evidence of Sucre's argument for provincial deliberation, but it also acknowledges competing Peruvian and Río de la Plata claims and places the provinces under the army's authority until the assembly deliberates. Bolívar had not approved the initiative when it was issued. The text does not establish how representatives were selected in practice or whether the Indigenous majority's preferences shaped their decisions.

The Declaration of Independence of Upper Peru, 6 August 1825

Creators: The deputies of the assembly at Chuquisaca · Date: 6 August 1825 · Type: A founding political declaration

Context: The assembly, after weeks of deliberation, issued a statement of independence for the provinces it claimed to represent.

Paraphrase of the source's central claim: The deputies declare that the provinces of Upper Peru, free from Spain, wish also to be independent of Buenos Aires, Lima, and any other state, and form a sovereign nation. The declaration invokes the provinces' will and the fatigue of long war.

Analysis: The text reveals the self-image of the founding elite and the arguments used against rival claims. It does not demonstrate popular consent, and its language of "the will of the provinces" must be read alongside the narrow electorate and the limited Indigenous participation.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Ayacucho Primarily a Military Achievement, or Was Royalist Defeat Increasingly Likely?

The question: To what extent did the patriots win independence by battlefield success, and to what extent did the royalist cause collapse because of internal division and the political crisis in Spain?

Interpretation A — A military achievement: Some historians emphasize the rebuilding of the patriot army, Bolívar's organization, Sucre's generalship, and the coalition's ability to defeat a numerically comparable army. On this view, royalist division mattered but did not determine the outcome; victory required skill, discipline, and logistics.
Interpretation B — Royalist division and structural weakness: Others emphasize the Olañeta rebellion, the withdrawal of Valdés's force, the royalists' dependence on a strained Peruvian economy, the loss of metropolitan support after the Spanish restoration of absolutism, and the declining legitimacy of the constitutionalist viceroy. On this view, the royalists were already weakened, and Ayacucho confirmed an outcome that political conditions had made likely.

Evidence: Both interpretations use military reports, memoirs, royalist correspondence, and Spanish political records. Troop numbers, especially royalist strength, are uncertain and vary by source.

Why it matters: The question affects how independence is remembered, whether as a triumph of liberating armies or as the outcome of imperial crisis. Most scholars argue for combinations: royalist division created opportunity, and the patriots exploited it. The debate is therefore less a contest of camps than a question of emphasis.

Student task: Identify two additional kinds of evidence that would help assess the relative weight of each factor.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Final Wars and Their Aftermath

Note: these viewpoints are analytical reconstructions that synthesize patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom.

Peruvian republicans: Many welcomed independence but disagreed about authority. Some resented the dependence on Bolívar and Colombian troops, while others judged his powers necessary.
Royalists: Constitutionalists such as La Serna and absolutists such as Olañeta defended the monarchy on different terms, and many American-born royalists later accepted the new order.
Indigenous communities: They supplied food, labor, and information to both sides under pressure and calculated their interests locally. Their goals, including relief from tribute and protection of land, were rarely met.
Afro-descended participants: Free and enslaved Black and mixed-race soldiers, many in Colombian ranks, fought in the army and sometimes gained freedom, while slavery persisted in the republics they helped establish.
Elites of Upper Peru: They sought autonomy against Lima and Buenos Aires and framed their choice as the will of the provinces.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Sovereignty, Tribute, Slavery, Property, and Political Participation

What Changed

  • Spanish royal sovereignty was replaced by republican governments in Peru and Bolivia
  • The viceregal army and colonial institutions were dismantled
  • A new state, Bolivia, appeared where the Audiencia de Charcas had been
  • Free-womb legislation began to reduce slavery's long-term reach

What Stayed the Same

  • Indigenous tribute, formally abolished, remained fiscally important in practice
  • Haciendas, mining, and labor arrangements persisted
  • Slavery continued in Peru until 1854
  • Political participation remained restricted by wealth, literacy, and gender

Who experienced change most directly: Creole officeholders and military officers gained the most. Greater continuity: Indigenous peasants, enslaved people, and women experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: It explains why independence is often described as political rather than social revolution, and why later movements pressed for the rights it had promised.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Ayacucho Produced Two Republics

Long-term causes

The administrative separation of Upper Peru from the Viceroyalty of Peru in 1776, regional economic identities, and the guerrilla war in the provinces since 1809.

Short-term causes

The collapse of royalist authority after Ayacucho, Sucre's decree of February 1825, and the defection of local officers and elites.

Event or transformation

The assembly at Chuquisaca declared the provinces independent on 6 August 1825.

Immediate effects

A new republic took the name of Bolívar; Sucre became its first head of state; Peru and the Río de la Plata acquiesced.

Long-term consequences

Bolivia's boundaries, political instability, and later wars with neighbors shaped its history, as examined in later chapters.

Historical complexity

Bolivia was not the inevitable result of Ayacucho. Different decisions by Sucre, Bolívar, the assembly, or the neighboring governments could have produced other outcomes.
Everyday Life and Social History

Recruits, Porters, Camp Followers, and the Besieged

Armies in the Andes were sustained by civilians. Highland villages were ordered to deliver maize, potatoes, meat, mules, and fodder, and men were drafted as porters and soldiers. Families lost labor at planting and harvest, and women took on additional work in fields and households. Women also followed armies as cooks, nurses, and carriers, and some, like Juana Azurduy in the earlier wars of Upper Peru, fought. Their contributions rarely appear in official reports but surface in musters, supply lists, and later pension petitions.

In Lima and Callao, residents endured occupation, requisition, and the movement of armies. Inside the Real Felipe, soldiers, refugees, and officials faced hunger and disease for months. The surviving accounts of the siege come largely from officers and are shaped by their purposes, which makes the experiences of women, children, and servants within the walls hard to recover. Silence in the sources about these groups is itself evidence of whose lives were recorded.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the declaration of Peruvian independence in 1821 began, rather than ended, the struggle for the central Andes, and that the final victories of 1824–1826 depended on coalition warfare, political concentration of authority, and the internal collapse of royalist unity as much as on any single battle.

After San Martín withdrew in September 1822, the Peruvian republic lacked a unifying commander, a stable treasury, or a settled constitutional order. The failed Intermediate Ports campaigns of 1822–1823 ended in defeats at Torata and Moquegua and in an inconclusive second expedition. The Balconcillo intervention of February 1823 showed the army overriding civilian institutions, and the royalist reoccupation of Lima in June 1823 showed the capital's vulnerability.

Rival governments in Lima and Trujillo exposed the problem of authority. Congress, president, and army competed for legitimacy, and Riva Agüero's collapse and Torre Tagle's later defection led the remaining leaders to accept an outside commander. Bolívar's arrival in September 1823 and the grant of extraordinary powers on 10 February 1824 concentrated authority in a figure who could command a coalition army, but at the cost of delegating the war to a foreigner.

The royalist camp was divided between the constitutionalist viceroy La Serna and the absolutist Olañeta, whose rebellion in Upper Peru, justified by the restoration of absolute monarchy in Spain, diverted forces under Valdés. The patriot army that Bolívar rebuilt combined Colombian, Peruvian, Chilean, and Río de la Plata troops, supplied by forced loans, requisitions, and the labor of highland communities. Its composition and burdens reveal the social basis of the war, including the roles of Indigenous communities, Afro-descended soldiers, and women who followed the armies.

The campaign into the central Andes culminated in the cavalry battle of Junín on 6 August 1824, which restored patriot initiative, and in Sucre's victory at Ayacucho on 9 December 1824, which destroyed the principal royalist army. The Capitulation signed by Sucre and Canterac was a military settlement, not a treaty of recognition, and it did not bind Rodil at Callao or Olañeta in Upper Peru. The distinction between military victory and political resolution is the chapter's second main theme.

In Upper Peru, the collapse of royalism was driven by local defections as well as by Sucre's advance. Olañeta's death in April 1825, after the events at Tumusla, ended organized royalist resistance, though the nature of the clash is disputed. Sucre's decree of 9 February 1825 opened a political process that was uncertain in outcome. Competing claims from Buenos Aires and Lima, the principle of uti possidetis, and the preferences of provincial elites framed the debate.

The Assembly of Chuquisaca declared independence on 6 August 1825 and named the new state for Bolívar. Bolivia's creation was thus a contested political decision involving elite negotiation, military authority, and the acquiescence of neighbors. It was made by a narrow electorate that excluded the Indigenous majority, and it inherited a colonial economy of mining, haciendas, and tribute.

The siege of Callao, which ended with Rodil's surrender on 23 January 1826, demonstrated that Ayacucho did not end all fighting and that the last royalist strongholds fell through hunger and attrition. The human and economic costs of the final wars, borne unequally by Indigenous communities, enslaved people, women, and veterans, left both republics fiscally weak and politically unstable.

The chapter's overall conclusion is that victory ended the principal structures of Spanish military power in the Andes but did not settle who would hold authority in the new republics or how subordinate populations would experience independence. The issues of constitutions, caudillismo, Indigenous tribute, slavery, and regional rivalry would continue into Chapters 58 and 60 and well beyond.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that independence in the Andes was achieved through a prolonged and uneven process in which declarations, battles, and negotiations each did different work. Ayacucho ended an army. The Assembly of Chuquisaca created a state. The fall of Callao closed a siege. None of these events, alone, answers the question of who would govern or how.

The chapter also shows that the wars were fought by many hands: Colombian and Peruvian soldiers, royalist officers who changed sides, Indigenous communities that fed and guided armies, Afro-descended soldiers, and women who sustained camps and fortresses. The sources, shaped by commanders and officials, record them unevenly, and learning to read the silences is part of historical method in Volume III.

The consequences extended well beyond 1826. The relationship between army and civilian government, the fiscal dependence on tribute, the persistence of slavery, the contest between centralism and regional autonomy, and the unsettled borders of Peru and Bolivia would shape both republics for generations. Chapter 56 turns to Brazil, where the end of colonial rule followed a different path, and Chapters 58 and 60 return to the problems of republican order that this chapter has opened.
