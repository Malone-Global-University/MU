Teotihuacan and the Great Mesoamerican City

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A City the Aztecs Could Not Explain

Centuries after its great pyramids had been abandoned to grass and wind, the Mexica who built their own capital at Tenochtitlan looked north across the Basin of Mexico at the ruins of an enormous, orderly city they had not built and could not fully explain, and they gave it a name in their own language: Teotihuacan, "the place where the gods were made" or "the place of those who have the road of the gods." That name is the one historians use today. It is not the name the city's own inhabitants used for themselves, their city, or their gods, and no securely deciphered writing system from the city itself has yet told us what they called it.

By the time the Mexica arrived in the Basin of Mexico in the fourteenth century, the enormous pyramids, plazas, and grid of streets that had once organized the daily lives of tens of thousands of people had long since ceased to function as the great political metropolis they had once been. The monumental core was not simply empty in the intervening centuries — later peoples reoccupied and reused parts of the site in more modest ways, including occupations archaeologists associate with the Coyotlatelco period of roughly the seventh through ninth centuries CE and the later Mazapa period — but the city's role as a dominant political and demographic capital had ended many centuries before the Mexica arrived. The Mexica did not experience Teotihuacan as a functioning contemporary society; they experienced it as a monumental ruin, as sacred and mysterious as anything in their own religious universe, a place where, according to Mexica tradition later recorded by Spanish friars, the gods had gathered in darkness to sacrifice themselves and set the sun and moon in motion. This textbook studies Teotihuacan as a functioning ancient city, not as the Mexica understood it centuries after its collapse, and one of the central tasks of this chapter is to keep those two very different bodies of knowledge — archaeological evidence from the city's own lifetime and much later Mexica religious tradition about the ruins — clearly separated rather than blended into a single, misleadingly seamless story.

What archaeologists have recovered from beneath the volcanic soil of the Basin of Mexico is a city of a scale unlike anything examined so far in this textbook. Chapter 6 introduced the Olmec centers of the Gulf Coast lowlands, impressive but limited in population and geographic footprint. Chapter 7 examined Monte Albán, a genuine hilltop capital and regional state, but still a city of thousands rather than tens of thousands. Teotihuacan, by contrast, grew during the first centuries of the Common Era into one of the largest cities anywhere in the ancient world, a planned metropolis whose broad central avenue, soaring pyramids, and thousands of multifamily residential compounds housed a population that most current estimates place at roughly 100,000 people at its height, a figure that remains an estimate rather than a certainty and that is considerably more securely grounded than older textbook claims of 150,000 or 200,000 inhabitants sometimes repeated in earlier scholarship.

This chapter asks a set of questions that will recur, in different forms, throughout the remainder of this textbook: how does a city grow to such an extraordinary scale, what does it take to plan, provision, and govern a population of tens of thousands of people without the wheel, draft animals for hauling loads, or a monetary economy in the modern sense, and how do historians reconstruct the life of a city whose inhabitants left behind extraordinary murals, monumental architecture, and thousands of excavated households, but no confidently deciphered king list, dynastic inscriptions, or royal portraits comparable to those that will appear in Chapter 9's discussion of the Classic Maya? Teotihuacan presents historians with an unusual and instructive imbalance: an enormous, richly documented material record of how people actually lived, worked, worshipped, and organized their neighborhoods, set against a genuinely uncertain picture of who held ultimate political power and how that power was exercised, transferred, or contested.

Teotihuacan also forces this textbook to confront, more directly than any previous chapter, the problem of multiethnicity within a single Mesoamerican city. Archaeological and biochemical evidence recovered from specific neighborhoods within Teotihuacan — most famously a district long associated with people connected to the Valley of Oaxaca examined in Chapter 7, and another multiethnic neighborhood called Teopancazco — demonstrates that people from different regions of Mesoamerica lived within the city for generations, maintaining aspects of their home region's material culture, mortuary customs, and, in some cases, genetic ancestry, while also participating in the shared institutions, religious life, and economy of the larger metropolis. Teotihuacan was not simply a Mesoamerican city; it was, in important respects, a city of Mesoamericans, drawing people and goods from a wide surrounding region into a single, dense, planned urban space.

Finally, Teotihuacan's reach extended well beyond the Basin of Mexico itself. Objects, architectural styles, and, in some well-documented cases, people connected with Teotihuacan appear at sites hundreds of miles away, including, as later chapters will explore in greater depth, dramatic and still-debated interventions in the political life of Early Classic Maya kingdoms. This chapter introduces that wider Teotihuacan world carefully and with appropriate caution, distinguishing between the presence of Teotihuacan-style objects, the movement of actual people, diplomatic or commercial relationships, and outright political or military domination — categories that are easy to blur but that carry very different historical implications. The deeper political history of the Maya kingdoms themselves, including their writing systems, dynastic records, and the specific and closely studied case of a Teotihuacan-linked political intervention at Tikal in 378 CE, belongs to Chapter 9 rather than to this one; this chapter's task is to establish what Teotihuacan itself was, how it worked, and how far its influence is known to have reached, not to narrate Maya political history in its own right.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Teotihuacan not as a mysterious lost civilization defined chiefly by its pyramids, but as a genuine ancient metropolis: planned, socially stratified, economically specialized, religiously elaborate, ethnically diverse, and, in ways that still puzzle specialists, politically organized in a manner that does not fit neatly into models built from either the Classic Maya kingdoms or the Aztec empire that would rise in the same valley many centuries later.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain where and when Teotihuacan developed, and clarify that "Teotihuacan" is a later Nahuatl name rather than the city's own name for itself.
  2. Describe the environmental and demographic processes that concentrated population in the Basin of Mexico and contributed to Teotihuacan's rapid growth.
  3. Analyze the city's grid plan, the Avenue of the Dead, and the degree of centralized spatial organization Teotihuacan's builders achieved.
  4. Evaluate the evidence for and limitations of population estimates for ancient Teotihuacan, including why older figures of 150,000–200,000 are less securely supported than a more conservative estimate of roughly 100,000.
  5. Explain the significance of Teotihuacan's apartment compounds for reconstructing household life, social inequality, and craft production.
  6. Assess the archaeological and biochemical evidence for multiethnic neighborhoods within Teotihuacan, including the Oaxaca Barrio and Teopancazco.
  7. Describe Teotihuacan's economic base, including obsidian working, ceramic production, and the provisioning of a large urban population.
  8. Compare competing scholarly models of Teotihuacan's political organization, including single-ruler, corporate or oligarchic, and district-based models of governance.
  9. Analyze Teotihuacan's religious iconography, monumental architecture, and the sacrificial burials associated with the Feathered Serpent Pyramid as evidence for the relationship between religion and political power.
  10. Distinguish between the presence of Teotihuacan-style objects, the movement of people, diplomatic relationships, and direct political control when evaluating Teotihuacan's influence beyond the Basin of Mexico.
  11. Explain the significance of the Great Fire and Teotihuacan's political and demographic transformation without reducing the collapse to a single cause.
  12. Connect Teotihuacan's urbanism to the regional state examined in Chapter 7 and to the dynastic, literate world of the Classic Maya examined in Chapter 9.

Historical Context

Chapter 6 examined the Olmec centers of the Gulf Coast lowlands, among the earliest Mesoamerican societies to construct substantial monumental architecture and support specialized artisans, priests, and rulers. Chapter 7 turned to Oaxaca, where Zapotec communities built Monte Albán atop a defensible hilltop and developed one of Mesoamerica's earliest genuinely urban capitals, complete with writing, a calendar, and a centralized regional state — a development this textbook was careful to describe as an independent Oaxacan achievement rather than a simple offshoot of Olmec influence. This chapter turns to the Basin of Mexico, a large, relatively enclosed highland valley roughly 2,200 meters above sea level, ringed by mountains and volcanoes and containing, in ancient times, a system of interconnected lakes that shaped settlement, agriculture, and transportation for every society that later occupied the valley, including the Mexica examined in Part III of this volume.

By the last centuries before the Common Era, the Basin of Mexico already supported a number of competing population centers, including the site of Cuicuilco in the southern basin, a settlement whose political and demographic decline, for reasons discussed further in Section 8.2, contributed to a wider redistribution of population within the valley. As Cuicuilco's regional importance declined, population and political influence increasingly concentrated in the northeastern portion of the basin, in a location with reliable springs, fertile agricultural land, and access to nearby obsidian sources — the volcanic glass that Mesoamerican societies prized for cutting tools, blades, and ritual objects. It was in this location that the city later known as Teotihuacan began its remarkable growth, reaching, by the first few centuries of the Common Era, a scale that had no precedent anywhere else in Mesoamerica and few parallels anywhere in the ancient world at that time.

Historians and archaeologists conventionally divide Teotihuacan's history into several broad phases, extending from initial village settlement through a period of rapid urbanization, a long era of mature metropolitan life during which the city's major monuments took their essentially final form, and a final period of political crisis, contraction, and eventual abandonment as a functioning political capital. These phase names and their associated dates, discussed further in the Chronology Preview below, are useful chronological tools, but students should understand that the boundaries between phases are scholarly constructs imposed on a more gradual and unevenly documented process of change, not sharp lines the city's inhabitants themselves would have recognized.

Understanding Teotihuacan also requires understanding the nature of the evidence available. Unlike the Classic Maya world examined in Chapter 9, Teotihuacan has not yielded a substantial corpus of confidently deciphered historical or dynastic writing. The city's murals and carved monuments contain rich pictorial and symbolic content, and specialists continue to debate whether some of Teotihuacan's iconographic signs functioned as a genuine writing system capable of recording specific names, dates, or historical events, or whether they operated more as a shared symbolic and religious vocabulary without the same narrative or bureaucratic functions writing served among the Maya. This uncertainty is not a gap in scholarly effort; it reflects the actual character of the surviving evidence, and it is one of the central historical problems this chapter addresses directly, particularly in the sections on Teotihuacan's political organization.

Section 8.1

What Was Teotihuacan?

Teotihuacan lies in the northeastern portion of the Basin of Mexico, roughly forty kilometers northeast of the site later occupied by the Mexica capital of Tenochtitlan, in a location chosen, as Section 8.2 explains in more detail, for its access to reliable water, fertile agricultural land, and nearby obsidian deposits. At its height, probably sometime between the fourth and sixth centuries CE, the city covered an area of roughly twenty square kilometers and organized a population that most current specialists estimate at approximately 100,000 people, though estimates in the scholarly literature have ranged considerably and remain sensitive to how densely particular residential compounds are assumed to have been occupied. This textbook treats 100,000 as a reasonable working figure while emphasizing that it is an estimate, not a precise census, and while noting that some older accounts proposing figures of 150,000 or 200,000 rested on demographic assumptions that most current specialists regard as less securely supported than the more conservative range now generally favored.

The name "Teotihuacan" is not the name the city's own inhabitants used for themselves or their home. It is a Nahuatl name, applied centuries later by the Mexica, who encountered the site as an ancient and already-abandoned ruin. Its most commonly cited meaning is approximately "the place where the gods were made" or "the place of those who have the road of the gods," reflecting Mexica religious beliefs about the site's cosmic significance rather than any direct knowledge the Mexica possessed of the city's original inhabitants, language, or self-designation. No confidently deciphered writing from Teotihuacan itself has yet revealed what its own residents called their city, their state, or the language or languages they spoke, though specialists have proposed candidates including early forms of Nahuan languages, Otomanguean languages, and Totonacan languages, without scholarly consensus on any single answer.

This uncertainty about self-designation and language is important for how this textbook proceeds. Later chapters examining the Classic Maya will be able to draw on deciphered hieroglyphic texts that record specific royal names, dates, marriages, wars, and dynastic successions. Teotihuacan offers no comparable textual record. This does not mean Teotihuacan is any less historically significant than Maya city-states; it means that historians must rely on a different, and in some ways more challenging, combination of archaeological, architectural, iconographic, and biochemical evidence to reconstruct how the city worked, who lived there, and how it was governed — a methodological challenge that runs throughout this entire chapter.

Students should also distinguish clearly between two very different bodies of knowledge about Teotihuacan that are sometimes blended together in popular accounts: archaeological evidence recovered from the city during the centuries it actually functioned as a living metropolis, roughly the first six centuries of the Common Era, and much later Mexica religious tradition about the ruins, recorded by Spanish friars and Indigenous scribes in the sixteenth century, more than eight hundred years after Teotihuacan's political collapse. Mexica accounts describing the gods gathering at Teotihuacan to create the sun and moon are valuable evidence for Mexica religious thought and for how a much later society interpreted an ancient ruin, discussed further in Chapter 13's treatment of Mexica religion and historical memory, but they are not reliable evidence for how Teotihuacan's own inhabitants understood their city, their rulers, or their gods during the period this chapter examines.

Section 8.2

Before the Metropolis: The Basin of Mexico and the City's Rise

Teotihuacan did not appear suddenly or fully formed. Its rise unfolded over several centuries, against the backdrop of a Basin of Mexico that had already supported village and town-level settlement for many generations before Teotihuacan became the valley's dominant center. During the last centuries before the Common Era, the largest and most politically significant settlement in the basin was Cuicuilco, located in the southern part of the valley near several active volcanic vents. Cuicuilco supported substantial public architecture of its own and appears to have functioned as an important regional center in its own right.

Cuicuilco's decline as a major regional center and the eventual burial of significant portions of the site beneath lava flows from the nearby Xitle volcano are both well documented archaeologically, but historians should be careful not to collapse these into a single, precisely timed event. Xitle is a monogenetic volcano, meaning it erupted once rather than through a recurring cycle of episodes, but the exact date of that eruption remains genuinely disputed among specialists: some archaeomagnetic reconstructions place it in a range spanning roughly the late first century BCE to the early first century CE, while other geological and radiocarbon evidence points to a considerably later date, possibly in the third or fourth century CE. Because the eruption's timing is unsettled, this textbook treats Cuicuilco's broader political and demographic decline — which need not have depended on the eruption itself — as the development most directly relevant to explaining population redistribution within the Basin of Mexico, while treating the Xitle eruption's precise date, and therefore its exact causal relationship to Teotihuacan's initial rise, as an open question rather than a settled part of the chronology. What is reasonably clear is that the decline of a major competing center in the southern basin removed one significant rival for regional dominance and likely contributed migrants to other, growing settlements, including the site that would become Teotihuacan.

The location that became Teotihuacan offered specific ecological advantages: reliable springs capable of supporting intensive agriculture even during drier periods, access to fertile land for maize cultivation, and proximity to important obsidian sources in the surrounding highlands, discussed further in Section 8.7. These advantages help explain why population and political importance concentrated at this particular location rather than elsewhere in the basin, but they do not by themselves explain why the resulting settlement grew to a scale so far beyond any other contemporary Mesoamerican center. That question — addressed more fully in the Cause and Effect feature later in this chapter — involves a combination of environmental advantage, deliberate political and religious investment in monumental construction, economic specialization, and the gravitational pull that a large, successful urban center exerts on migration from smaller surrounding communities, a pattern that would recur, in different forms, throughout the urban histories examined in later chapters of this textbook.

By the early centuries of the Common Era, in the phase archaeologists generally call Tzacualli, Teotihuacan had already begun the rapid transformation from a growing town into a genuinely planned metropolis, a transformation examined in detail in the following section.

Section 8.3

Planning a New Kind of City

What most distinguishes Teotihuacan from the Mesoamerican centers examined in previous chapters is not merely its size but the degree of deliberate spatial planning its builders imposed on the landscape. At some point during the city's early phase of rapid growth, its inhabitants and leaders undertook a large-scale reorganization of the urban landscape around a single dominant axis, a broad north-south thoroughfare that colonial-era and later visitors named the Avenue of the Dead — again, a modern name rather than the city's own term, inspired by the mistaken later belief that the structures lining it were tombs.

The Avenue of the Dead extends for several kilometers through the heart of the city, oriented along an axis that deviates by roughly fifteen and a half degrees east of true north, an orientation that appears to reflect astronomical and cosmological considerations connected to the rising and setting positions of particular celestial bodies at specific times of year, though specialists continue to debate the precise astronomical logic involved. This same basic orientation was extended across the entire city, so that residential compounds, workshops, and secondary temple platforms throughout Teotihuacan were aligned to a shared urban grid rather than growing up haphazardly along the pattern of individual streets and lanes typical of many other ancient cities.

Achieving this degree of citywide alignment required sustained coordination over an extended period of construction, sufficient political or religious authority to compel or persuade a large population to build according to a shared plan, and considerable investment in leveling, filling, and reorganizing terrain that had not originally been laid out this way. Historians and archaeologists differ over exactly what kind of authority accomplished this feat — a question addressed directly in Section 8.8 — but the physical evidence of the grid itself demonstrates that some form of centralized planning authority existed at Teotihuacan, whatever its precise political character.

The city's layout also reveals meaningful social and economic differentiation rather than a uniform, egalitarian townscape. Compounds nearer the ceremonial core of the city and along the Avenue of the Dead tend to show evidence of greater wealth, larger and more elaborately decorated construction, and closer association with the city's major religious monuments, while compounds in more peripheral districts tend to show more modest construction and, in some cases, closer association with specific craft activities or with residents connected to particular outside regions, discussed further in Section 8.6. Recent spatial and economic analysis of the city's residential compounds has reinforced the conclusion that where a household lived within Teotihuacan correlated with meaningful differences in wealth and access to resources, complicating any picture of the city as a purely egalitarian, uniformly planned urban space. Teotihuacan's planners achieved remarkable spatial order, but that order coexisted with, and in some ways reinforced, real social and economic inequality among the city's residents.

Section 8.4

The Pyramid of the Sun, Pyramid of the Moon, and Monumental Landscape

Teotihuacan's skyline was, and in its ruined form remains, dominated by two enormous pyramidal structures: the Pyramid of the Sun, one of the largest structures by volume in the pre-Columbian Americas, and the somewhat smaller Pyramid of the Moon, situated at the northern end of the Avenue of the Dead and fronted by a large plaza. As with the Avenue of the Dead itself, these names are modern conveniences rather than the structures' original names, and students should be cautious about assuming that the Pyramid of the Sun was necessarily dedicated to a solar deity, or the Pyramid of the Moon to a lunar one, in the specific way these later names suggest; the actual religious functions and associated deities of these monuments remain the subject of ongoing scholarly investigation rather than settled fact.

Both pyramids were built up in stages over an extended construction sequence, with later builders enlarging and encasing earlier versions of the structures — a common pattern in Mesoamerican monumental architecture also documented at sites examined in earlier chapters. Beneath the Pyramid of the Sun, archaeologists have identified a subterranean cave or tunnel feature that appears to have held religious significance connected to the pyramid built above it. Whether this feature began as an entirely natural cavity later modified and extended by Teotihuacan's inhabitants, or whether it was substantially artificial from an early stage, remains a subject of genuine scholarly debate; excavation-based studies have argued for significant human construction and modification, while other assessments favor a natural origin later reshaped by human activity. What most specialists agree on is that the tunnel functioned as a meaningful ritual space connected to concepts of caves, water, and the underworld, whatever its precise geological origin, and that this connects Teotihuacan's monumental landscape to a broader Mesoamerican pattern of associating pyramids and temples with sacred, cave-like spaces beneath them. A separate tunnel beneath the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, understood with more confidence to be an artificial excavation, discovered and extensively explored by archaeologists in the twenty-first century, has yielded thousands of ritually deposited objects, including reflective minerals, sculpted figures, and organic materials, interpreted by excavators as evidence of an elaborate ritual program connecting the underground tunnel to concepts of a watery underworld and cosmological creation.

The Ciudadela, a large enclosed plaza complex south of the Pyramid of the Sun along the Avenue of the Dead, houses the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, whose façade is decorated with alternating sculpted heads generally interpreted as representing a feathered serpent deity and a second creature sometimes identified as a war serpent or a version of the Storm God discussed further in Section 8.9. The construction of this pyramid was accompanied by one of the most extensively documented episodes of dedicatory human sacrifice anywhere in ancient Mesoamerica, examined in detail in the Material Source feature later in this chapter.

Constructing monuments of this scale required an enormous, sustained mobilization of labor, extending over generations and drawing on a workforce that must have included both specialized builders and a much larger body of laborers engaged in quarrying, hauling, and construction. This labor investment is itself significant historical evidence: whatever form Teotihuacan's political authority took, it was capable of organizing, over an extended period, the kind of large-scale collective labor project that, in most well-documented ancient societies, required either powerful centralized coercive authority, strong shared religious motivation, some combination of the two, or an organized system of communal or corporate obligation that did not necessarily depend on a single all-powerful ruler.

Section 8.5

Apartment Compounds and Everyday Urban Life

If Teotihuacan were understood only through its pyramids and avenues, students would come away with a badly distorted picture of the city, since the great majority of its population did not live in or immediately around its monumental core but within a distinctive residential form archaeologists call the apartment compound. These were large, walled, roughly rectangular multiroom complexes, typically organized around interior patios, that housed extended family groups or other coresident households, along with associated kitchen areas, storage spaces, small shrines, workshops, and, in many cases, the burials of deceased household members interred beneath patio floors.

Archaeologists have identified several thousand apartment compounds across the city, varying considerably in size, construction quality, and apparent wealth, from modest compounds with simple plastered floors to larger, more elaborately decorated compounds featuring painted murals, better-quality construction materials, and more spacious layouts. This variation provides some of the most direct evidence available for social and economic inequality within Teotihuacan, since differences in compound size, construction quality, and the range of goods recovered from associated burials and refuse deposits correlate with differences in household wealth and status.

The apartment-compound evidence is also central to how archaeologists estimate Teotihuacan's overall population, since population estimates depend heavily on assumptions about how many people typically resided in a given compound and what proportion of excavated or mapped compounds were occupied simultaneously at the city's height. Because these assumptions can vary, and because not every compound has been excavated, population estimates for Teotihuacan carry real uncertainty, and this textbook's working estimate of approximately 100,000 inhabitants should be understood as a reasonable order-of-magnitude figure informed by decades of survey and excavation rather than a precise census total.

Apartment compounds also functioned as productive economic units. Excavations at numerous compounds have recovered evidence of craft production — obsidian working, ceramic manufacture, shell working, and other specialized activities, discussed further in Section 8.7 — suggesting that many households combined domestic life with organized economic production, sometimes apparently oriented toward supplying goods to the wider city or to more distant markets rather than purely for household consumption. This pattern of household-based craft production organized within a planned residential architecture is one of the more distinctive features of Teotihuacan's urban economy compared to other ancient cities, and it provides historians with an unusually rich, if geographically uneven, body of evidence about the economic lives of ordinary Teotihuacan residents rather than only its rulers and elite.

Section 8.6

A Multiethnic Metropolis

One of the most historically significant discoveries about Teotihuacan is that its population was not culturally or ethnically uniform. Certain neighborhoods within the city show sustained, multigenerational occupation by residents whose material culture, burial customs, and, in some cases, biological or genetic evidence connect them to specific outside regions of Mesoamerica, indicating that Teotihuacan drew population from a wide surrounding area and that at least some of these communities maintained distinct identities and practices for generations after settling within the city.

The best-documented example is a district in the western part of the city generally known as the Oaxaca Barrio, or Tlailotlacan, whose material culture — including ceramic styles, tomb construction, and other features — closely resembles patterns documented at Monte Albán and other sites in the Valley of Oaxaca examined in Chapter 7. Isotopic analysis of human skeletal remains from this district, examining chemical signatures in bone and dental tissue that reflect the geographic origin of an individual's childhood diet and water sources, has long provided evidence that at least some residents of this neighborhood were migrants who spent their early years outside the Basin of Mexico, consistent with an Oaxacan origin. A genetic study using mitochondrial DNA published in the mid-2020s has further reinforced the conclusion that individuals buried within the Oaxaca Barrio show maternal genetic ancestry patterns consistent with sustained biological continuity connecting this neighborhood to Oaxacan populations, complementing the material and isotopic evidence gathered over previous decades.

A second especially well-documented multiethnic neighborhood, known as Teopancazco, has yielded skeletal and material evidence indicating residents connected to multiple different regions of Mesoamerica, including areas associated with the Gulf Coast lowlands discussed in Chapter 6. Isotopic and osteological studies of burials from Teopancazco have identified individuals whose chemical and skeletal signatures suggest origins outside the Basin of Mexico, alongside individuals who appear to have been born and raised locally, indicating a genuinely mixed resident population living, working, and, in some cases, apparently intermarrying within a single neighborhood over an extended period.

These findings should not be taken to mean that Teotihuacan's various ethnic communities simply dissolved into a single homogeneous urban culture, nor that they remained entirely separate and unchanged. The evidence instead suggests a more complicated pattern in which specific neighborhoods maintained distinctive material culture and mortuary practices connected to a home region for multiple generations, while their residents also participated in the shared institutions, religious practices, and economic life of the wider city — obtaining housing within the same planned urban grid described in Section 8.3, participating in citywide ritual and construction projects, and, in some cases, adopting elements of Teotihuacan's dominant artistic and architectural styles alongside their own regional traditions. Teotihuacan should be understood as a genuinely plural metropolis rather than either a single unified ethnic community or a loose collection of entirely separate enclaves.

Section 8.7

Craft Production, Commerce, and Provisioning the City

Supporting a population of roughly 100,000 people required an economy capable of provisioning the city with food, raw materials, and manufactured goods on a scale unprecedented in earlier Mesoamerican history. Agricultural provisioning depended on intensive cultivation of the fertile lands surrounding the city, supplemented by resources drawn from the basin's lake system, and, quite plausibly, by agricultural production and tribute drawn from a wider hinterland whose precise political relationship to the city remains debated, a question connected to the broader discussion of Teotihuacan's regional reach in Section 8.10.

Obsidian working was one of Teotihuacan's most economically significant and archaeologically visible craft industries. The city controlled access to, or maintained especially close relationships with, several important nearby obsidian sources, and archaeologists have identified numerous workshops throughout the city specializing in the production of obsidian blades, projectile points, and other tools, along with more specialized ritual and ornamental obsidian objects. This textbook resists the temptation to reduce Teotihuacan's economic success to a single explanation centered on obsidian alone; scholarship on the organization of obsidian production at Teotihuacan indicates a more complicated picture involving both workshops apparently connected to state or elite sponsorship and other workshops that appear to have operated with greater independence, embedded within ordinary apartment compounds rather than under direct centralized control. Obsidian was clearly an important part of Teotihuacan's economy and of its long-distance exchange relationships with other regions, but it functioned within a broader and more diversified economic system rather than serving as the city's sole source of wealth and power.

Ceramic production, textile manufacture, shell and stone ornament working, and other craft industries are similarly documented across numerous apartment compounds, often showing evidence of household-level specialization in which particular compounds or neighborhoods concentrated on particular products. Teotihuacan-style ceramics, along with objects made from imported materials such as marine shell, exotic feathers, and specific stones, have been recovered both within the city and at considerable distances from it, indicating that Teotihuacan participated in exchange networks extending across much of Mesoamerica, discussed further in Section 8.10.

The precise mechanisms by which goods moved within the city and between the city and its wider hinterland — whether through centrally administered markets, more decentralized forms of exchange, tribute arrangements, or some combination of these — remain incompletely understood and connect directly to the unresolved questions about political organization examined in the following section. What is clear from the archaeological record is that Teotihuacan supported an economically diverse population of specialized artisans, agricultural producers, and, very likely, merchants or intermediaries responsible for moving goods within and beyond the city, all operating within the ordered residential and spatial framework described earlier in this chapter.

Section 8.8

Who Ruled Teotihuacan?

Given the extraordinary scale of Teotihuacan's monumental architecture, the sophistication of its urban planning, and the clear evidence of organized large-scale labor mobilization, students might reasonably expect archaeologists to have identified a dynasty of powerful individual rulers comparable to the kings examined in Chapter 9's discussion of the Classic Maya. They have not. Unlike Maya kingdoms, which left behind carved stelae, painted vessels, and architectural inscriptions naming specific rulers, recording their genealogies, and commemorating their military and ceremonial achievements, Teotihuacan has produced no confidently identified royal portraits, no securely deciphered king list, and no dynastic inscriptions comparable to Maya hieroglyphic texts. This absence is one of the central historical puzzles this chapter addresses, and it has produced genuine and ongoing scholarly disagreement.

One family of interpretations holds that Teotihuacan was governed, at least during some periods of its history, by powerful individual rulers whose images and identities have simply not yet been securely recognized in the surviving material and iconographic record, perhaps because Teotihuacan's political ideology emphasized institutional and religious authority over individual royal personality in ways that discouraged the kind of personalized royal portraiture and text so prominent among the Maya. Proponents of this view point to certain figures depicted in Teotihuacan murals and on portable objects, sometimes interpreted as possible rulers or high officials, though these identifications remain contested and are not supported by accompanying texts that would confirm a specific individual's name or reign.

A second, increasingly influential family of interpretations proposes that Teotihuacan was governed through some form of corporate, collective, or oligarchic political structure, in which power was shared among a council of elite lineages, temple institutions, or district representatives rather than concentrated in a single divine or semi-divine ruler. Proponents of this view point to the relative absence of monumental art celebrating individual rulers, the prominence of shared religious and cosmological imagery over personal royal iconography, and the apparent importance of specific institutional complexes, including large administrative-looking compounds near the city's ceremonial core, as evidence that Teotihuacan's political system may have distributed authority more broadly than the single-ruler model common among the Classic Maya and, later, the Mexica.

A third approach, not necessarily incompatible with either of the first two, emphasizes that Teotihuacan's political organization may have varied significantly over the course of the city's several centuries of existence, moving between periods of more centralized authority — plausibly including the coordinated effort required to lay out the city's grid and construct its major early pyramids, discussed in Sections 8.3 and 8.4 — and periods in which power was more genuinely distributed among competing elite groups or neighborhoods, a pattern that some specialists connect to the political tensions eventually associated with the Great Fire examined in Section 8.12.

Students should take from this section not a confident answer but an accurate sense of genuine scholarly uncertainty. Teotihuacan demonstrates, perhaps more clearly than any other site examined so far in this textbook, that the scale and sophistication of a society's material remains do not automatically translate into a clear historical record of its political leadership. Reconstructing Teotihuacan's government requires careful, comparative reasoning from indirect evidence — architecture, iconography, spatial organization, and comparison with better-documented Mesoamerican political systems — rather than the kind of direct textual evidence historians can draw on for many other ancient societies.

Section 8.9

Religion, Murals, Sacrifice, and Political Ideology

Religion at Teotihuacan was deeply embedded in the city's public architecture, art, and, as this section explains, its exercise of political power. Teotihuacan's surviving murals — painted on the interior and exterior walls of numerous apartment compounds and public buildings, and preserved in unusually rich quantity compared to most other ancient Mesoamerican sites — depict a dense visual world of deities, ritual processions, water and fertility imagery, feathered serpents, felines, and elaborately dressed figures engaged in ceremonial activity.

Certain recurring figures in this iconographic repertoire have been given modern interpretive names by archaeologists, including a goggle-eyed figure long associated with storm, rain, and lightning, generally referred to as the Storm God, and another figure, sometimes called the Great Goddess, associated with water, fertility, spiders, and the underworld. Students should treat both of these labels with appropriate caution. While the Storm God interpretation rests on reasonably strong comparative iconographic grounds, connecting Teotihuacan imagery to later, better-documented rain and storm deities elsewhere in Mesoamerica, the so-called Great Goddess remains a more contested identification; some specialists question whether the imagery associated with this label represents a single, coherent deity at all, as opposed to several distinct figures or symbolic complexes that modern scholars have grouped together. This textbook presents the Great Goddess as a debated interpretive category rather than a securely established ancient deity.

The clearest and most extensively documented connection between religion and political power at Teotihuacan comes from the Feathered Serpent Pyramid within the Ciudadela complex, introduced in Section 8.4. Extensive excavation of this structure has revealed more than one hundred individuals interred as dedicatory sacrifices in connection with the pyramid's construction, many found bound, arranged in symmetrical patterns, and accompanied by weapons, shell ornaments, and other objects associated with military status. This evidence provides some of the strongest support available anywhere at Teotihuacan for a direct connection between monumental religious construction, organized state violence, and the assertion of political and military authority, suggesting that whatever specific form Teotihuacan's government took, it was capable of organizing large-scale sacrificial ritual in the service of a shared political and religious ideology centered on the pyramid and its associated imagery.

Teotihuacan's religious and artistic vocabulary — including its feathered serpent imagery, water and fertility symbolism, and ritual regalia — also appears, in modified and locally reinterpreted forms, at sites far beyond the Basin of Mexico, a pattern examined further in Section 8.10. This wide diffusion of Teotihuacan religious imagery does not by itself indicate direct political control over the distant places where such imagery appears; it indicates, at minimum, a shared or widely admired religious and artistic vocabulary that other Mesoamerican societies found meaningful enough to adopt, adapt, and, in some cases, use to bolster their own local political authority.

Section 8.10

Teotihuacan Beyond Teotihuacan

Teotihuacan's influence extended well beyond the Basin of Mexico, but describing the character of that influence requires careful analytical distinctions. Archaeologists working on interregional interaction generally distinguish among several different kinds of connection that can produce similar-looking material evidence: the exchange of goods between independent societies; the movement of individual people, whether through trade, marriage, diplomacy, or migration; the diffusion of religious or artistic styles admired and adopted by other societies without any accompanying political relationship; formal diplomatic relationships between Teotihuacan and other polities; and, at the far end of this spectrum, direct military intervention or political domination. All of these forms of connection can leave behind similar categories of evidence — a Teotihuacan-style vessel, a Teotihuacan-style building, an image incorporating Teotihuacan iconography — and one of the central historical skills this section develops is learning not to assume the most dramatic explanation, direct conquest or control, merely because Teotihuacan-connected material appears at a distant site.

Within the Basin of Mexico and its immediate surroundings, the evidence for direct Teotihuacan political dominance is considerably stronger, reflected in the consistent presence of Teotihuacan-style architecture, ceramics, and settlement patterns across a substantial surrounding region. Moving outward from this core area, Teotihuacan-connected material appears at varying levels of intensity across highland and coastal Mesoamerica, including areas of the Gulf Coast, Oaxaca (already examined in Section 8.6 through the lens of migration into Teotihuacan itself, rather than Teotihuacan's presence in Oaxaca), and parts of highland Guatemala.

Specialists studying these wider connections have increasingly cautioned against assuming that Teotihuacan directly administered a large, continuous territorial empire in the way later historians will describe the Aztec Triple Alliance in Chapter 14. The evidence more often supports a picture of an extensive and variable interaction sphere, in which Teotihuacan's economic weight, religious prestige, and, in specific and well-documented cases, direct political intervention shaped developments across a wide area without necessarily requiring permanent garrisons, resident administrators, or formal territorial annexation comparable to later Mesoamerican or Old World imperial systems. Some specific interactions, however — including the case examined in the following section — involved documented, direct, and dramatic political intervention rather than mere stylistic influence, indicating that Teotihuacan's capacities for projecting power at a distance, at least on particular occasions, went well beyond simple trade and cultural admiration.

This more cautious, differentiated picture of Teotihuacan's regional reach — economically and culturally influential across an enormous area, directly administratively dominant across a more limited zone, and capable of decisive, if apparently occasional, political intervention at a considerable distance — is more consistent with current evidence than either an image of Teotihuacan as a passive cultural influence with no real political reach, or an image of Teotihuacan as a unified territorial empire comparable to later Mesoamerican or Old World states. Even specialists who favor this more differentiated picture note that exactly how directly Teotihuacan administered its more distant connections, and how consistently, remains an open question rather than a fully settled matter.

Section 8.11

Teotihuacan and the Maya World

Among Teotihuacan's most striking and best-documented distant relationships is its connection to the Maya world of the Early Classic period, examined in full political and dynastic detail in Chapter 9. This section introduces that relationship only briefly and at the level of general pattern, since a fuller treatment belongs to the chapter specifically devoted to Classic Maya political history, writing, and dynastic records.

Archaeological and iconographic evidence indicates that people, objects, and artistic and religious motifs connected with Teotihuacan reached Maya kingdoms in both the lowlands and highlands during the fourth and fifth centuries CE. This evidence includes Teotihuacan-style architecture and ceramics found at Maya sites, Maya artistic representations of individuals wearing Teotihuacan-associated military and religious regalia, and, in at least one especially well-documented and closely studied case involving the major Maya kingdom of Tikal, textual and iconographic evidence for a dramatic political intervention traditionally associated with the year 378 CE, in which a figure linked to Teotihuacan appears to have played a decisive role in a change of ruling authority at Tikal.

This textbook deliberately does not narrate the details of Tikal's dynastic politics, the specific Maya historical figures involved, the content of the relevant hieroglyphic inscriptions, or the broader scholarly debate over how to interpret this episode in this chapter. That material belongs to Chapter 9, where it can be examined properly within the context of Maya writing, chronology, and dynastic record-keeping, tools students have not yet encountered at this point in the textbook. What matters for this chapter is the broader pattern the Tikal case illustrates: Teotihuacan's connections to the Maya world went, on at least this occasion, well beyond stylistic admiration or ordinary trade, and Teotihuacan-linked actors appear to have played a decisive role in a major political transition at Tikal, though the exact identity, sponsorship, and relationship of those actors to any central Teotihuacan authority remain interpretive questions taken up more fully in the next chapter.

This raises an important interpretive question that Chapter 9 will take up directly: what did Teotihuacan look like from the perspective of societies far outside central Mexico? For distant Maya elites, Teotihuacan evidently represented a source of powerful religious and military prestige, worth incorporating into local political self-presentation, and, on at least one documented occasion, a source of outside political influence connected to a change in ruling authority at a major kingdom hundreds of miles from the Basin of Mexico. Holding this outside perspective in view, alongside the internal, Basin-of-Mexico-centered perspective this chapter has developed, will help students appreciate Teotihuacan's full historical significance as they move into Chapter 9's study of the Classic Maya world.

Section 8.12

Crisis, Great Fire, and the Transformation of the City

Teotihuacan's dominance of the Basin of Mexico and its wider interaction sphere did not last indefinitely. Sometime in the range of approximately 550 to 570 CE, a major fire, generally referred to by archaeologists as the Great Fire, damaged or destroyed a significant number of structures concentrated along the Avenue of the Dead and in areas closely associated with the city's ruling and religious elite, including monumental temple platforms and elite residential compounds. The distribution of burning, concentrated in precisely the areas most closely associated with political and religious authority rather than spread evenly across the entire city, has led many archaeologists to interpret the fire as the product of deliberate, targeted destruction rather than an accidental blaze or a natural disaster.

Recent scholarly synthesis, including work by archaeologist Linda Manzanilla, situates the Great Fire within a broader interpretation involving internal political conflict, in which population groups, competitive neighborhoods, or intermediate elite factions within the city rose against, or otherwise violently displaced, the individuals and institutions most closely associated with centralized political and religious authority. This interpretation connects the Great Fire to longer-term social and political tensions within the city, including the kinds of inequality among apartment compounds discussed in Section 8.5 and the unresolved questions about the concentration or distribution of political power discussed in Section 8.8, without requiring historians to specify the exact grievances or motives of those who rose against the ruling elite, which the current evidence does not securely establish.

This textbook presents the internal-conflict interpretation as a serious and currently influential scholarly position rather than as an uncontested, single-cause explanation for Teotihuacan's transformation. Other specialists emphasize additional or alternative contributing factors, including longer-term environmental stress, agricultural strain associated with supporting a large population over several centuries, disruption to long-distance trade and interaction networks, and gradual shifts in regional power balances that may have reduced Teotihuacan's ability to maintain its earlier dominance even before the fire itself occurred. These longer-term stresses may have interacted with internal political conflict, but their precise relationship to the targeted destruction associated with the Great Fire remains uncertain.

In the aftermath of the Great Fire, Teotihuacan did not disappear immediately or completely. Archaeological evidence indicates continued, though substantially reduced, occupation of parts of the city for a considerable period afterward, alongside clear evidence of political fragmentation, as the centralized authority that had once coordinated citywide planning, monumental construction, and long-distance interaction lost its former capacity to do so. Population appears to have declined significantly in the decades and centuries following the fire, and political and economic influence in central Mexico gradually redistributed among other, smaller centers, setting the stage for the more politically fragmented central Mexican landscape that later societies, including the eventual builders of Tula examined in Part III of this volume, would inherit.

Students should resist any single-sentence explanation for Teotihuacan's transformation — "invasion destroyed the city," "drought destroyed the city," or "rebellion destroyed the city" each captures, at most, part of a more complicated process that unfolded over years or decades rather than in a single dramatic episode, and that current scholarship continues to actively investigate and debate.

Historical Vocabulary
Teotihuacan

Teotihuacan is the modern scholarly name, derived from Nahuatl and applied centuries after the city's political collapse by the Mexica, for an ancient metropolis in the northeastern Basin of Mexico that flourished from roughly the first century BCE through the sixth or seventh century CE. The city's own name for itself, and the language or languages spoken by its inhabitants, remain unknown, since no confidently deciphered historical writing from the city's own lifetime has survived or been identified.

At its height, Teotihuacan supported a population most current specialists estimate at roughly 100,000 people across a planned urban area of approximately twenty square kilometers, organized around a central axis modern scholars call the Avenue of the Dead and dominated by the Pyramid of the Sun, the Pyramid of the Moon, and the Feathered Serpent Pyramid within the Ciudadela complex. The term "Teotihuacan" is used throughout this chapter, and throughout this textbook, as the conventional scholarly designation for both the site and the ancient city and society that once inhabited it, while students should remember that this convenience obscures our uncertainty about how the city's own inhabitants understood themselves.

Historical Vocabulary
Corporate Governance (Political Model)

In the context of ancient Mesoamerican political history, corporate governance refers to a proposed model of political organization in which authority is exercised collectively by a group of elite lineages, institutions, or representatives rather than concentrated in a single ruler whose personal identity and achievements dominate public art and monumental inscriptions. This model is one of several competing explanations scholars have proposed for Teotihuacan's political organization, discussed in detail in Section 8.8, and it stands in contrast to more centralized, individual-ruler models better documented among the Classic Maya, examined in Chapter 9, and later among the Mexica, examined in Part III of this volume.

Evidence sometimes cited in support of a corporate model at Teotihuacan includes the relative absence of monumental portraiture celebrating named individual rulers, the prominence of shared religious and cosmological imagery over personalized royal iconography, and the presence of large, apparently administrative or elite residential compounds near the city's ceremonial core that may have housed governing institutions rather than, or in addition to, a single royal household. This model remains a scholarly hypothesis rather than an established fact, and it should be understood as one serious possibility among several, not as the textbook's final answer to the question of who ruled Teotihuacan.

Material Source Window

The Feathered Serpent Pyramid and Its Sacrificial Burials

Location: The Ciudadela complex, Teotihuacan · Approximate date of construction and associated burials: c. 150–250 CE (Miccaotli–Early Tlamimilolpa phases) · Type of evidence: Monumental architecture, sculpted façade, and excavated dedicatory human burials

Context: The Feathered Serpent Pyramid stands within a large enclosed plaza south of the Pyramid of the Sun. Its stepped façade is decorated with alternating sculpted stone heads generally interpreted as a feathered serpent deity and a second creature associated with warfare or the Storm God discussed in Section 8.9. Extensive twentieth- and twenty-first-century excavation beneath and around the pyramid has revealed more than one hundred individuals buried as part of the structure's construction, many arranged in symmetrical groupings, with hands bound behind their backs, and accompanied by weapons, shell and stone ornaments, and other objects associated with military status.

Rather than reproducing excavation data verbatim, students should understand the central archaeological findings in summary form: excavators identified multiple mass burials positioned symmetrically around and beneath the pyramid, containing individuals interred at or near the time of the structure's construction, many showing evidence of restrained posture at burial and association with martial equipment, patterns that excavating archaeologists have interpreted as consistent with organized, ritualized human sacrifice conducted in connection with the pyramid's dedication.

Analysis: The scale, symmetry, and consistency of these burials distinguish the Feathered Serpent Pyramid from more isolated or ambiguous evidence of sacrifice elsewhere in Mesoamerica, providing historians with unusually direct material evidence connecting monumental religious construction to organized state violence at Teotihuacan. The presence of military equipment among the buried individuals suggests a connection between this ritual program and martial or warrior identity, reinforcing the interpretation, discussed in Section 8.9, that Teotihuacan's rulers or ruling institutions used large-scale sacrificial ritual to assert and legitimate political and military authority. This evidence does not, however, tell historians who ordered the sacrifices, whether the victims were captives, community members, or some combination, or precisely how this ritual program relates to the broader questions about Teotihuacan's political structure discussed in Section 8.8.

  1. What does the symmetrical arrangement of the burials suggest about the degree of planning involved in this ritual program?
  2. Why might the presence of martial equipment among the sacrificial burials support an interpretation connecting this event to military or political ideology rather than purely religious devotion?
  3. What questions can this evidence not answer about who held ultimate political authority at Teotihuacan?
  4. How does this evidence compare to the kinds of evidence, discussed in Chapter 9, that historians use to reconstruct Maya political history?
Evidence & Interpretation

Identifying Migrants in a Multiethnic City

Section 8.6 draws on several distinct kinds of evidence to identify migrants and multiethnic communities within Teotihuacan, and it is worth pausing to consider what each kind of evidence can, and cannot, establish on its own. Ceramic style and other material culture — the shapes, decoration, and manufacturing techniques of pottery, for example — can indicate cultural connection or shared tradition with a distant region, but similar objects can also travel through trade or imitation without any accompanying movement of people, so material culture alone cannot prove that an individual was born outside the Basin of Mexico.

Burial customs, including tomb construction and the arrangement of grave goods, often persist across generations even after a community has resided in a new location for some time, making them a useful indicator of cultural affiliation, but, like ceramics, they reflect learned tradition rather than biology and can be adopted or modified over time.

Isotopic analysis of bone and tooth enamel examines chemical signatures, particularly of strontium and oxygen, that reflect the geology and water sources an individual consumed during specific periods of childhood development. This method can provide relatively direct evidence that a particular individual grew up outside the Basin of Mexico, but it identifies where a person spent their early years, not their ethnic identity, ancestry, or the language they spoke, and it cannot by itself explain why they later moved to Teotihuacan.

Ancient DNA, including mitochondrial DNA, can reveal biological ancestry and maternal lineage connections between a buried individual and other sampled populations, as the 2025 study of burials from the Oaxaca Barrio demonstrates in finding maternal genetic continuity with Oaxacan populations. Genetic evidence, however, reflects only one line of inheritance (in the case of mitochondrial DNA, the maternal line alone), depends on the availability of comparative samples from other regions, and cannot on its own reconstruct a person's cultural identity, language, or self-understanding.

No single kind of evidence discussed above is sufficient by itself to establish a person's complete ethnic or cultural identity. Historians and archaeologists build the strongest case for a multiethnic Teotihuacan precisely because material culture, burial customs, isotopic evidence, and genetic evidence from neighborhoods such as the Oaxaca Barrio and Teopancazco tend to point in the same direction when considered together, even though each form of evidence, examined in isolation, has real and different limitations.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might ceramic style alone be insufficient evidence that a specific individual migrated to Teotihuacan from another region?
  2. What specific question can isotopic analysis answer that ceramic and burial evidence cannot, and what question can it not answer?
  3. Why does mitochondrial DNA evidence reveal only maternal ancestry, and why does this matter for interpreting a multiethnic neighborhood's population history?
  4. Why is converging evidence from multiple independent methods more persuasive than any single method alone?

Who Ruled Teotihuacan?

The question: Given the absence of a confidently deciphered dynastic record at Teotihuacan, how should historians interpret the city's political organization, and how much confidence should students place in any single model?

Interpretation A — Teotihuacan was governed by powerful individual rulers not yet confidently identified: This view holds that Teotihuacan's scale of monumental construction and evidence of large-scale sacrificial ritual are more consistent with strong, centralized individual authority than with a more diffuse corporate system, and that specific figures depicted in Teotihuacan art may represent rulers whose identities and historical roles have simply not yet been securely established, perhaps because Teotihuacan's artistic and religious conventions downplayed individual royal portraiture in favor of collective religious imagery even under centralized rule.
Interpretation B — Teotihuacan was governed through a corporate or collective political structure: This view emphasizes the striking absence of monumental art celebrating named individual rulers, comparable to what exists at many Classic Maya sites, and argues that this absence reflects a genuinely different form of political organization in which authority was distributed among elite lineages, religious institutions, or district representatives rather than concentrated in a single ruling figure.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of architectural, iconographic, and mortuary evidence, but differ in how they weigh the significance of the absence of individual royal portraiture and inscribed dynastic records.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes broader interpretations of Teotihuacan's capacity for sustained large-scale planning, its relationship to distant regions such as the Maya lowlands discussed in Section 8.11, and the causes of the political crisis associated with the Great Fire discussed in Section 8.12, since a corporate governance model might make internal elite conflict a more readily available explanation for that crisis than a single-ruler model would.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 8.3, 8.4, 8.8, and 8.9, and the Material Source feature on the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, construct an argument for each interpretation and explain what kind of future archaeological discovery would most effectively resolve this debate.

Multiple Perspectives

Life in a Metropolis: Perspectives within Teotihuacan

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across many excavated households and neighborhoods. They are not quotations from any specific named historical individual.

From the perspective of a resident of an elite compound near the Avenue of the Dead: Daily life was organized around proximity to the city's major religious monuments and, quite possibly, to whatever governing institutions coordinated citywide construction and ritual, with household wealth and status reflected in larger, more elaborately decorated living space than most other residents of the city enjoyed.
From the perspective of a craft-specialist household in an obsidian-working compound: Household life combined domestic activities with organized workshop production, supplying blades and tools to other residents of the city and, quite possibly, to more distant exchange networks, within an apartment compound whose architecture accommodated both family life and craft production side by side.
From the perspective of a resident of the Oaxaca Barrio maintaining ties to a home region: Life in Teotihuacan meant participating in the city's shared institutions, grid-planned neighborhoods, and religious life, while also maintaining distinctive burial customs, ceramic styles, and other material connections to the Valley of Oaxaca across multiple generations, reflecting a layered identity rather than a simple choice between assimilation and separateness.
From the perspective of an individual selected for sacrifice at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid: Whether such an individual was a captive taken in conflict, a member of a subordinate community, or a person selected through some other social or religious process remains uncertain from the archaeological evidence alone, underscoring the limits of what monumental construction and burial evidence alone can reveal about the lived experience and consent, or lack of consent, of those whose deaths made Teotihuacan's monuments possible.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Monte Albán to Teotihuacan: Different Forms of Mesoamerican Urbanism

What Changed

  • Urban scale increased dramatically, from Monte Albán's hilltop capital of several thousand residents to Teotihuacan's planned metropolis of roughly 100,000
  • Spatial organization shifted from a defensible hilltop layout adapted to a mountain summit to a deliberately planned lowland grid organized around a monumental central avenue
  • The evidentiary basis for political history changed as well: Monte Albán has yielded early Mesoamerican writing and calendrical inscriptions, while Teotihuacan has produced abundant iconography but no confidently deciphered dynastic text
  • Ethnic and regional diversity within a single urban center became far more archaeologically visible at Teotihuacan, with distinct, long-term multiethnic neighborhoods documented within the city itself

What Stayed the Same

  • Both cities required organized, large-scale labor mobilization to construct monumental public architecture
  • Both cities combined religious, political, and economic functions within their public and residential architecture
  • Both cities show clear evidence of internal social and economic inequality visible in the archaeological record
  • Both cities maintained economic and cultural relationships extending well beyond their immediate home region

Why the pattern matters: Comparing Monte Albán and Teotihuacan demonstrates that Mesoamerican urbanism did not follow a single template; different regions, working with different environments, resources, and political traditions, produced genuinely distinct forms of city life, each requiring its own combination of evidence and interpretive caution to reconstruct.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Teotihuacan Grow So Large?

Long-term causes

Reliable springs, fertile agricultural land, and proximity to major obsidian sources in the northeastern Basin of Mexico created durable environmental and economic advantages for sustained population growth at this specific location.

Short-term causes

The decline of Cuicuilco's regional political and demographic influence in the southern basin — a decline whose relationship to the still-disputed date of the Xitle eruption remains uncertain — removed a major competing center and likely redirected migrants and political influence toward the growing settlement in the northeast.

Effect

Rapid urbanization, large-scale monumental construction, the development of a citywide planned grid, and the eventual concentration of roughly 100,000 people within a single metropolis unprecedented in earlier Mesoamerican history.

Reinforcing dynamics

Successful early monumental construction and religious prestige likely attracted further migration and investment, while growing economic specialization, craft production, and long-distance exchange relationships reinforced the city's continued growth over multiple generations.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Teotihuacan's extraordinary growth; environmental advantage, the decline of a regional rival, religious and political investment in monumental construction, and self-reinforcing migration and economic specialization all interacted over more than two centuries to produce the metropolis archaeologists study today.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside an Apartment Compound

A typical Teotihuacan apartment compound organized daily life around one or more interior patios, providing light, ventilation, and shared space for a household or group of related households living within a single walled complex. Excavated compounds reveal sleeping and living quarters, kitchen areas with evidence of grinding stones and cooking hearths, small domestic shrines where household members likely conducted family religious observances, and, in many cases, workshop spaces where residents produced obsidian tools, ceramics, textiles, or other craft goods, discussed further in Section 8.7.

Burial practices at Teotihuacan frequently placed deceased household members beneath the floors of the compound itself, a practice that kept the dead physically present within the spaces of ongoing daily life rather than removing them to a separate cemetery, and that has provided archaeologists with an unusually rich body of skeletal, isotopic, and associated material evidence for reconstructing household composition, diet, health, geographic origin, and, as discussed in Section 8.6, migration history and evidence relevant to cultural or ethnic affiliation when combined with material and mortuary evidence. Because most surviving evidence for Teotihuacan's political leadership remains genuinely uncertain, as Section 8.8 explains, it is in these household-level apartment compounds — rather than in royal inscriptions or dynastic monuments — that historians have found their richest and most reliable evidence for how most of Teotihuacan's population actually lived.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Teotihuacan as one of the largest and most historically significant cities of the ancient Americas, a planned metropolis whose scale and organization mark a genuine departure from the societies examined in earlier chapters of this volume. Teotihuacan grew, over roughly two centuries beginning around the turn of the Common Era, from a growing settlement in the northeastern Basin of Mexico into a city of perhaps 100,000 inhabitants, organized around a monumental central avenue and dominated by some of the largest pyramids constructed anywhere in the pre-Columbian Americas.

Students have learned that Teotihuacan's own name for itself remains unknown, and that "Teotihuacan," along with "Avenue of the Dead," "Pyramid of the Sun," and "Pyramid of the Moon," are later names applied by the Mexica or by modern scholars rather than terms the city's own inhabitants would have recognized. The chapter has distinguished carefully between archaeological evidence from Teotihuacan's own lifetime and much later Mexica religious tradition about the ruins, insisting that only the former can serve as direct evidence for how the city's own society actually functioned.

The chapter has examined Teotihuacan's remarkable degree of urban planning, reflected in its citywide grid and the deliberate orientation of the Avenue of the Dead, while emphasizing that this spatial order coexisted with genuine social and economic inequality, visible in the varying size, construction quality, and material wealth of the city's thousands of apartment compounds. These compounds, rather than the city's monumental core alone, housed the great majority of Teotihuacan's population and provide historians with their richest evidence for household organization, craft production, and everyday life.

Students have examined the strong archaeological and biochemical evidence that Teotihuacan was a genuinely multiethnic city, with neighborhoods such as the Oaxaca Barrio and Teopancazco maintaining distinctive material culture, mortuary practices, and, in some cases, biological connections to distant regions of Mesoamerica across multiple generations, while their residents also participated in the shared institutions and urban life of the wider city. The chapter has also examined Teotihuacan's economic base, including obsidian working and other craft industries, cautioning against reducing the city's prosperity to any single commodity or trade good.

A central theme of this chapter has been the genuine uncertainty surrounding Teotihuacan's political organization. Unlike the Classic Maya kingdoms examined in the following chapter, Teotihuacan has produced no confidently deciphered dynastic record, and this chapter has presented competing scholarly models — powerful but unidentified individual rulers, corporate or collective governance, and politically variable arrangements over time — as genuine, unresolved historical debate rather than settled fact. The extensive dedicatory sacrificial burials associated with the Feathered Serpent Pyramid provide some of the clearest available evidence connecting monumental religious construction to organized political and military authority, even though they cannot by themselves resolve the broader question of who held ultimate power.

The chapter has also examined Teotihuacan's relationships beyond the Basin of Mexico, distinguishing carefully among economic exchange, cultural and religious influence, migration, diplomacy, and direct political intervention. It introduced, without narrating in full, evidence for consequential involvement by Teotihuacan-linked actors in the political transition at Tikal around 378 CE, reserving the detailed political and dynastic history of that episode, and of the Classic Maya world more broadly, for Chapter 9.

Finally, the chapter has examined Teotihuacan's political and demographic transformation following the Great Fire of approximately 550 to 570 CE, presenting current scholarship's emphasis on internal political conflict as an influential but not exclusive explanation, and cautioning students against reducing this complex transformation to any single cause such as invasion, drought, or rebellion alone.

Chapter 8 has asked how Mesoamerican urbanism could produce a city of unprecedented scale, social complexity, and ethnic diversity, while leaving historians with only an incomplete picture of its political leadership. Chapter 9 turns to a very different but closely connected Mesoamerican world — the Classic Maya kingdoms of the lowlands and highlands — where deciphered hieroglyphic writing allows historians to reconstruct named rulers, dynasties, wars, and the very political intervention at Tikal that this chapter has only briefly introduced.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Teotihuacan
The modern scholarly name for an ancient planned metropolis in the northeastern Basin of Mexico, flourishing roughly from the first century BCE to the sixth or seventh century CE; the city's own name for itself is unknown.
Basin of Mexico
The enclosed highland valley containing an ancient lake system within which Teotihuacan, and much later Tenochtitlan, developed.
Avenue of the Dead
The modern name for Teotihuacan's broad central axis, around which the city's major monuments and many residential compounds were organized.
Apartment compound
A large, walled, multiroom residential complex housing extended families or other coresident groups; the dominant residential form across most of Teotihuacan.
Barrio
A neighborhood or district within Teotihuacan, some of which show sustained material and biological connections to specific outside regions of Mesoamerica.
Talud-tablero
An architectural style combining a sloped (talud) base with a vertical, framed panel (tablero), closely associated with Teotihuacan monumental architecture and widely imitated at other Mesoamerican sites.
Obsidian
Volcanic glass central to Teotihuacan's craft economy, used for blades, tools, and ritual objects.
Multiethnic neighborhood
A district within a city, such as the Oaxaca Barrio or Teopancazco at Teotihuacan, whose residents maintained material or biological connections to more than one region or ethnic community.
Corporate governance
A proposed model of political organization in which authority is shared among elite lineages or institutions rather than concentrated in a single ruler; one of several competing models proposed for Teotihuacan.
Oligarchy
Rule by a small group of powerful individuals or families, one possible variant of corporate governance considered for Teotihuacan.
Feathered Serpent Pyramid
A monument within the Ciudadela complex at Teotihuacan, associated with elaborate façade sculpture and extensive dedicatory sacrificial burials.
Ciudadela
A large enclosed plaza complex at Teotihuacan housing the Feathered Serpent Pyramid.
Isotopic analysis
A scientific method examining chemical signatures in bone and teeth to reconstruct an individual's geographic origin and diet, used to identify migrants at Teotihuacan.
Mortuary evidence
Evidence derived from burial practices, including body position, grave goods, and burial location, used to reconstruct social status, identity, and ritual practice.
Dedicatory burial
A burial, often involving sacrifice, performed in connection with the construction or consecration of a building or monument.
Storm God
A modern name for a recurring goggle-eyed deity figure in Teotihuacan iconography, associated with rain, lightning, and storms.
Great Goddess (debated)
A modern and contested interpretive label for a figure or group of figures in Teotihuacan iconography associated with water, fertility, and the underworld; some specialists question whether this represents a single coherent deity.
Iconography
The study of visual images and symbols and their meanings, central to interpreting Teotihuacan's murals and sculpture in the absence of deciphered text.
Interaction sphere
A zone of shared cultural, religious, or economic connection among distinct societies that does not necessarily imply unified political control.
Enclave
A distinct community or neighborhood maintaining its own identity while situated within a larger, different society, such as the Oaxaca Barrio within Teotihuacan.
Great Fire
A major conflagration, generally dated to roughly 550–570 CE, that damaged elite and ceremonial precincts at Teotihuacan and is associated by many specialists with internal political conflict.
Political fragmentation
The breakdown of centralized political authority into smaller, competing units, a process associated with Teotihuacan's decline after the Great Fire.
Urban planning
The deliberate organization of a city's spatial layout, streets, and structures according to a shared design, exemplified by Teotihuacan's citywide grid.
Craft specialization
The concentration of particular households, workshops, or neighborhoods on the production of specific goods, well documented within Teotihuacan's apartment compounds.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, name, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply, relevant to the use of names such as "Teotihuacan" and "Avenue of the Dead."
Tzacualli, Miccaotli, Tlamimilolpa, Xolalpan, and Metepec phases
Conventional archaeological phase names, derived primarily from ceramic sequences, used to divide Teotihuacan's history into broad chronological periods (approximately Tzacualli 0–150 CE, Miccaotli 150–200 CE, Early Tlamimilolpa 200–250 CE, Late Tlamimilolpa 250–350 CE, Early Xolalpan 350–450 CE, Late Xolalpan 450–550 CE, and Metepec 550–650 CE); specialists vary somewhat in exactly where they place these boundaries, and the ranges given here should be treated as approximate scholarly convention rather than fixed fact.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to grapple with one of ancient Mesoamerica's most impressive and, in important ways, most puzzling achievements. Teotihuacan demonstrates that Mesoamerican societies were capable of organizing urban life on a scale comparable to the largest cities anywhere in the ancient world, coordinating monumental construction, citywide planning, specialized craft production, and a genuinely multiethnic population within a single, densely settled metropolis. At the same time, Teotihuacan reminds students that the scale of a society's physical remains does not automatically produce a clear historical record of its political leadership, and that historians must sometimes accept genuine, well-reasoned uncertainty rather than force ambiguous evidence into a tidy narrative.

Teotihuacan's significance extends well beyond the Basin of Mexico itself. Its economic and religious influence touched societies across a wide swath of Mesoamerica, and its documented political intervention in the affairs of the Maya kingdom of Tikal demonstrates that this central Mexican metropolis was capable of shaping the course of events in politically sophisticated, geographically distant societies. This chapter has deliberately left the fuller exploration of that relationship, and of Maya political history more broadly, to Chapter 9, so that students can approach the Classic Maya world with the analytical tools — writing, dynastic record-keeping, and named historical rulers — that Teotihuacan itself does not provide.

The chapter has also reinforced a theme introduced in Chapter 7: that no single Mesoamerican society or region should stand in for the whole. Monte Albán and Teotihuacan, developing at roughly overlapping periods in different regions of Mesoamerica, produced genuinely different forms of urban life, political organization, and evidentiary legacy. Holding both cities in view, alongside the Classic Maya kingdoms examined next, gives students a more accurate and more richly comparative picture of ancient Mesoamerican civilization than any single case study could provide on its own.

Understanding Teotihuacan — its planned grid, its thousands of apartment compounds, its multiethnic neighborhoods, its uncertain rulers, and its dramatic transformation after the Great Fire — equips students to appreciate both the extraordinary achievements and the genuine interpretive challenges that define the study of ancient Mesoamerica. The story of Mesoamerican urbanism continues, in a very different key, in the chapter that follows.
