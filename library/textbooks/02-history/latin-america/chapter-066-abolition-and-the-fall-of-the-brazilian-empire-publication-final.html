Abolition and the Fall of the Brazilian Empire

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Two Dates Eighteen Months Apart

On 13 May 1888 a two-article law ended slavery in Brazil. On 15 November 1889 a group of army officers ended the monarchy that had signed it. The puzzle of this chapter is how one regime could preside over the first transformation and be destroyed within eighteen months by the second.

On the afternoon of 13 May 1888, Princess Isabel, regent for her ailing father Emperor Pedro II, signed Law No. 3,353 at the imperial palace in Rio de Janeiro. The text was among the shortest major laws in Brazilian history. Its first article declared slavery extinct from the date of the law, and its second repealed contrary provisions. Crowds gathered in the streets, and Rio and other cities celebrated for days. Brazil had received more enslaved Africans than any other society in the Americas, and it was the last independent country in the hemisphere to abolish slavery, a distance in time that shaped how its contemporaries, and later historians, judged the moment.

Eighteen months later, on 15 November 1889, the monarchy fell. Troops led by Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca, supported by a small circle of younger officers and a handful of civilian republicans, deposed the cabinet of the Viscount of Ouro Preto. Within hours the political change turned into the end of the empire; within two days the imperial family was on its way into exile. The monarchy that had endured since independence in 1822 collapsed with little fighting and almost no defense. Many of the people who had celebrated in May were not among those who acted in November, and many who acted in November had no stake in emancipation at all.

The chapter's central question is how the combined pressures of enslaved people's resistance, abolitionist organizing, economic change, political reform, and military republicanism brought down two foundational institutions of nineteenth-century Brazil within eighteen months of each other. The answer developed here is that the two events were connected but distinct. Abolition was pushed by those who fled plantations, sued in court, hid fugitives, raised money, spoke in parliament, and finally by an empire that judged resistance to the pressure more dangerous than yielding. The fall of the monarchy came from a different set of conflicts, over the army, federalism, the Church, and the succession, in which abolition was one factor among several.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, Princess Isabel did not single-handedly end slavery; the Golden Law was the last act of a campaign that had been won in streets, courts, railway stations, and plantations. Second, abolition alone did not cause the monarchy's fall, though it cost the empire the support of some planters. Third, the republic did not automatically bring wider democracy: the Constitution of 1891 left most Brazilians, including most formerly enslaved people, without a meaningful vote. Both transformations ended old institutions while leaving many hierarchies of land, race, and political power in place.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. The legal record is rich: the laws of 1850, 1871, 1885, and 1888, parliamentary debates, and the 1891 Constitution survive and can be read. So do newspapers such as the Gazeta da Tarde, abolitionist books such as Joaquim Nabuco's O Abolicionismo (1883), notarial records, manumission letters, police files, and thousands of freedom suits. Yet many voices in the story, especially those of enslaved and formerly enslaved people, survive mainly through the records of courts, police, planters, and reformers. Memoirs of politicians and officers written after 1889 were often shaped by the need to explain, or to claim credit for, what had occurred.

The chapter's boundaries matter. Chapter 61 described the Brazilian Empire's institutions in general, and Chapter 64 narrated the War of the Triple Alliance; this chapter takes up the war only for its effects on the army and on the question of slavery. Chapters 68 and 69 will compare export economies, land, labor, immigration, and dispossession across the region; here the post-emancipation transition is treated only within Brazil and only to 1891. Throughout, Afro-Brazilian women and men remain visible as actors, not only in the sections on resistance and abolitionism, but also as the narrative moves toward the politics of generals, ministers, and planters.

Historical Context

Chapter 56 described how Brazil became an independent monarchy in 1822 under Pedro I, and Chapter 61 examined the empire under the regency and under his son Pedro II, who took the throne in 1840 and reigned until 1889. That empire was a constitutional monarchy with an elected Chamber of Deputies, a life-tenured Senate, a Council of State, and an emperor who held the "moderating power," allowing him to dismiss cabinets and dissolve the Chamber. Politics was conducted by two elite parties, Conservative and Liberal, whose differences were real but narrower than their rhetoric. The vote was restricted by income, and property-owning slaveholders dominated the political class. The Constitution of 1824 had protected property without addressing slavery by name, and the empire's institutional stability rested heavily on the unspoken agreement that slavery would not be threatened.

By mid-century that agreement was bound up with coffee. Sugar, centered in the Northeast, had defined colonial Brazil, but from the 1830s coffee grown in the Paraíba Valley of Rio de Janeiro province, and then in São Paulo and the Zona da Mata of Minas Gerais, became the leading export. Coffee made new fortunes, financed railways and ports, and supported the Court's finances. It also drew enslaved labor southward, and its planters became the empire's most politically powerful economic interest. Brazil's slavery was never limited to plantations: enslaved people worked in cities, in households, on cattle ranches, in mines, in ports, and in small workshops and shops, and they were owned by people of widely varying wealth.

Free and freed people of African descent were a large part of Brazilian society. By the 1872 census, the first national count, Brazil had roughly ten million inhabitants and about 1.5 million enslaved people, a minority of about fifteen percent. Free people of color outnumbered the enslaved. They occupied a wide range of positions, from poor rural dependents to artisans, soldiers, musicians, clergy, and a few professionals. The social boundary between free and enslaved was therefore constantly crossed and contested, and manumission was an everyday phenomenon that planters used as a tool of discipline. That fact helps explain why slavery could be socially embedded and politically fragile at once.

Atlantic politics changed the setting. Britain's campaign against the slave trade pressed on Brazil throughout the 1840s. The United States abolished slavery in 1865, after a civil war. Spanish authorities enacted a free-womb law in Cuba and Puerto Rico in 1870 and ended slavery in Cuba in 1886, leaving Brazil isolated as the last major slaveholding society in the Americas. Brazilian elites, who prized the empire's reputation as a stable and civilized exception in a continent of republics, were increasingly sensitive to this isolation. Meanwhile, European ideas of progress, race, and science, including positivism and evolutionary racial thought, offered new vocabularies for debating labor, citizenship, and national identity.

Historians approach this subject with different emphases. An older tradition focused on parliamentary politics and on figures such as Princess Isabel, Rio Branco, and Nabuco. A later social history, drawing on court records, police files, and notarial documents, emphasized the actions of enslaved people and of free Black communities. Recent scholarship in Brazil and abroad tends to combine these views, treating emancipation as the result of pressure from below that reshaped what elites considered possible. This chapter follows that integrated view while remaining alert to the limits of each type of evidence.

Section 66.1

Slavery, Coffee, and the Social Order of the Brazilian Empire

Slavery was not an outlying institution in the Brazilian Empire; it structured the economy, the household, the law of property, and the social ranks that Brazilians used to measure one another. Between the sixteenth century and 1850, roughly five million Africans were landed in Brazil, far more than in any other American colony or nation. By the middle of the nineteenth century, the engine of this system was coffee. In the Paraíba Valley, on estates that stretched along the border between Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo, hundreds of enslaved workers planted, weeded, harvested, dried, and carried the beans under the supervision of overseers and the owner's authority. Coffee became the empire's leading export and a principal source of customs revenue, so that the Court's finances, the port of Rio, and the planters' political influence all rose together.

The regional geography matters. Rio de Janeiro province held the oldest and largest coffee estates and the largest concentrations of enslaved labor. São Paulo's plantations were expanding west from Campinas into fertile "new lands," financed increasingly by railways, beginning with the Santos–Jundiaí line completed in 1867. Minas Gerais combined coffee in its southern and eastern zones with an older economy of mining, cattle, and subsistence agriculture, and it held the largest enslaved population of any province. The Northeast, once the heartland of sugar, remained heavily dependent on slavery but was in relative economic decline, a fact that would soon matter for the movement of people.

Enslaved labor extended beyond the plantation. In Rio, Salvador, Recife, and smaller ports, enslaved men and women worked as porters, carters, craftsmen, vendors, and domestic servants. Many urban enslaved workers operated under the system known as ganho, in which they hired themselves out or sold goods on the street and returned a fixed sum to their owners, keeping the remainder. This system gave some of them mobility and an opportunity to save, and it also made them visible, networked, and difficult to control. Enslaved women worked as cooks, laundresses, wet nurses, and market sellers, and also in the fields; they were exposed to sexual exploitation and to the forced separation of families. Skilled enslaved artisans, barbers, musicians, and seamstresses lived in a different world from field hands, though both lived under the threat of sale.

Slaveholding was widely distributed. The great planters shaped national policy, but most slaveholders owned only a handful of people, and slaveholding extended to shopkeepers, artisans, clergy, widows, and in some cases to freed people themselves. This dispersion helps explain why defenses of slavery could appeal across social groups, and why the political class saw property in people as the foundation of order. Slaveholders also benefited from the law's reliance on private power: the owner's authority over punishment was broadly recognized, and the state generally supported the recapture of fugitives.

Free people of color stood between these worlds. The census of 1872 showed that free Black and mixed-race Brazilians were a numerous population, many in poverty, many working in the households and fields of others, and many with kin who were enslaved. Manumission (alforria) was common, and owners used it to reward loyalty, to enforce obedience by holding out the prospect of freedom, and sometimes to respond to the payments of enslaved people who bought themselves. Freed people were legally citizens under the 1824 Constitution, but their status remained shadowed by the memory of enslavement and by informal racial hierarchy.

Historians reconstruct this world from inventories attached to wills, notarial sales, baptismal and marriage registers, police records, travelers' accounts, and the census. Each source reflects the point of view of the people who produced it, usually owners, officials, priests, or foreign observers. The voices of enslaved people appear in court testimony, petitions, and a few narratives, but they are mediated by clerks and lawyers. This is why the actions of the enslaved, visible in the records of escape, litigation, and self-purchase, are so important evidence: they document choices made under constraint.

Historical Vocabulary
Alforria (manumission)

Alforria was the act of freeing an enslaved person, formalized in a letter of freedom (carta de alforria). It could be granted outright, bought by the enslaved person or by family, or made conditional, requiring continued service until the owner's death or for a fixed term. In English, the general term is manumission.

Alforria mattered because it linked slavery to a hierarchy of statuses rather than a simple boundary. It rewarded and disciplined at the same time, produced a large free Black population, and, because owners could revoke conditional grants in some circumstances, did not always guarantee security. A modern equivalent such as "emancipation" can mislead, since alforria was an individual act under the owner's authority, while emancipation in the national sense meant the legal abolition of the institution.

Section 66.2

The End of the Atlantic Slave Trade and the Persistence of Slavery, 1850–1870

The transatlantic trade survived long after it became formally illegal. Brazil had promised Britain to end it in 1826–1827, and a law of 1831 declared Africans landed after that date free, but enforcement was weak, and trafficking grew during the 1830s and 1840s as coffee demand rose. Many of the Africans landed in these decades were legally free under Brazilian law and held in slavery in violation of it, a contradiction that would shape later litigation. British naval pressure intensified after the Aberdeen Act of 1845, which authorized British warships to seize slave ships and try captured crews in British courts, and the 1850 crisis brought Royal Navy seizures into Brazilian ports and waters.

On 4 September 1850, the government of Eusébio de Queirós, Minister of Justice, secured passage of Law No. 581 declaring the trade piracy and authorizing the navy and courts to seize vessels and punish participants. This time the law was enforced. Several causes converged: foreign naval pressure, fear among elites of a growing African-born majority in a country that remembered rebellions like the Malê revolt in Salvador in 1835, the 1850 yellow fever epidemic, which added to anxiety about the sea trade, and the availability of capital that had been tied up in the trade. Landings largely ended within a few years, with a handful of later cases. The measure was a landmark, but it ended importation, not slavery.

Without Africans arriving, the enslaved population could not be replenished by trade, and its distribution changed. A domestic market in enslaved people expanded. Owners in declining regions, especially in the Northeast and in parts of the south, sold people to buyers in the coffee provinces, by sea and by overland routes, tearing families apart and forcing journeys of hundreds of miles. Prices rose. The southeastern provinces of Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, and Minas Gerais held a growing majority of Brazil's enslaved people, which meant that the political center of slavery became ever more concentrated in the very region that dominated the empire. This concentration would later matter, because the Northeast and far south could lose their slaves without ending the system, while the Southeast could not.

The Land Law of 18 September 1850 (Law No. 601), passed two weeks after the trade law, belongs to the same story. It forbade acquisition of public lands except by purchase, required registration of existing holdings, and directed revenue from land sales to fund immigration. Scholars debate how closely the law was designed to prevent poor and formerly enslaved people from gaining land, and its enforcement was uneven. What is clear is that, by making unclaimed land costly, it protected the large estates, and that a future in which workers were free would be one in which access to land was tightly controlled. The link between ending the trade, controlling land, and recruiting future labor shows planners thinking, early on, about life after slavery in terms that favored landowners.

The persistence of slavery was therefore a deliberate outcome. The empire had ended the trade under foreign pressure and from domestic fear, but its political class, especially in the coffee provinces, concluded that the domestic supply and the improved productivity of enslaved labor would suffice. The practical effect was to bind the state, planters, and the national economy even more tightly to a shrinking, aging, and increasingly valuable enslaved population.

Section 66.3

The Paraguayan War, Military Service, and the Question of Emancipation

The War of the Triple Alliance (1864–1870), narrated in Chapter 64, was the empire's largest and costliest conflict. This section takes up its effects on slavery. The imperial army was small, poorly paid, and unpopular, and the government quickly found that volunteers would not fill it. Under the "Voluntários da Pátria" decree of 1865, the government promised bonuses and land to volunteers, but it also resorted to forced recruitment, which fell hardest on poor free men, many of them of African descent. Wealthier families paid substitutes to serve in their place, and some planters sent enslaved men as substitutes, promising freedom in return.

The imperial government itself began freeing enslaved men for service. A specific imperial decree, No. 3,725-A of 6 November 1866, granted freedom without payment to enslaved men owned by the state who were assigned to serve in the army; if they were married, the benefit also extended to their wives. This was not a general promise of compensated emancipation for privately enslaved volunteers. Other recruitment arrangements involved substitutes provided by private slaveholders, the purchase or manumission of enslaved men for military service, and payments negotiated with owners. These mechanisms have to be distinguished in recruitment records: the number of men emancipated through each pathway is contested, although the wartime total reached the thousands. Afro-Brazilian men also formed volunteer units on their own initiative, as in Bahia's Zuavos Baianos, which drew on Black volunteers from Salvador's streets and workshops. For enslaved men, enlistment was a path to freedom that carried the risks of disease, hunger, and battle; for the state, it was an acknowledgment that the army needed the labor and loyalty of people the law treated as property. Wives and children left behind faced uncertain status, and some owners resisted honoring promises, so that the question of what freedom through military service meant became a matter of litigation and petition.

The war also affected the empire's political discussion of emancipation. In 1866 the Emperor asked the Council of State for opinions on gradual abolition, and a Council member, José Antônio Pimenta Bueno, drafted proposals for a free-womb law. The ministers shelved them while the war continued, and later reformers revisited them. Pedro II himself privately favored eventual emancipation but insisted that it should be gradual, orderly, and led by the state. Foreign developments reinforced the pressure: the end of slavery in the United States in 1865 and the movement toward gradual emancipation in Spanish Cuba made Brazil increasingly conspicuous. Conservatives and Liberals alike understood that the country's allies in the war, Argentina and Uruguay, were free of slavery, so the army fought alongside soldiers of republics that had ended it.

The war's institutional effects were significant. It enlarged the army, gave it a sense of corporate identity and grievance, and left the government with heavy debts. Officers who had fought in Paraguay felt that civilian politicians neglected them, and the experience of serving alongside Black soldiers and of leading men drawn from the free poor shaped attitudes that appeared later in the army's refusal to hunt fugitives. These were long-term developments, not immediate ones, and their importance must not be overstated: army officers were not abolitionists as a group, and many came from slaveholding families. But the war created a military institution with its own interests and a record of Afro-Brazilian service that abolitionists would cite.

Historians who study this period use recruitment records, decrees, military reports, and the petitions of soldiers and their families. They show that a policy designed to meet wartime needs opened an unplanned path to freedom and a precedent for state action. They also show that it fell unequally. The people who bore the burdens of the war, Afro-Brazilian soldiers, their families, and the free poor, saw few of its rewards, and the sacrifices of the war did not produce a national program of emancipation.

Section 66.4

The Free Womb Law of 1871 and the Limits of Gradual Emancipation

When the war ended, the question returned. In 1871 the Conservative cabinet of the Viscount of Rio Branco, José Maria da Silva Paranhos, introduced a bill to free the children born to enslaved women. The Emperor was in Europe, and Princess Isabel served as regent. The proposal split the Conservative Party, since many coffee planters, especially in the Paraíba Valley, saw it as an attack on property, and it was opposed by deputies who warned of social disorder. Rio Branco, who had experience in diplomacy and a strong majority, pressed the bill through the Chamber and the Senate. The result, Law No. 2,040 of 28 September 1871, is known as the Lei do Ventre Livre, the Free Womb Law, or, from its sponsor, the Rio Branco Law.

Its terms deserve precision. Article 1 declared that children born to enslaved women from the date of the law would be of free condition. But these children, called ingênuos, remained with their mothers' owners until age eight. At that point the owner could either surrender the child to the state in exchange for compensation of 600,000 réis in government bonds, or keep the child's services until age twenty-one. The law did not free the enslaved mothers. Very few owners chose the indemnity, and most kept the children, so in practice a large number of ingênuos worked under conditions little different from slavery for the first two decades of their lives, though they were legally free.

The law also established mechanisms that went beyond gradual abolition. It created an emancipation fund, financed by taxes, lotteries, and other sources, to free enslaved people in order of priority, such as families. It required the registration of all enslaved people in a national roll, the matrícula, with penalties for non-registration, so that anyone not enrolled could be presumed free. And it gave legal recognition to the pecúlio, the savings that enslaved people accumulated, and to their right to purchase their own freedom, with a court-set price if the owner refused. In this way the law acknowledged what had long been practice, and gave enslaved people a statutory basis for claims they could bring to court. It also forbade separating married couples at sale, a limited protection that built on an earlier decree.

Interpretations of the law differ. Some historians have seen it as a conservative measure intended to slow the end of slavery, a controlled concession that preserved owners' interests by gradually extinguishing slavery within a generation. Others emphasize that it altered the legal terrain by recognizing enslaved people's savings and legal standing, and that it was a major step in an empire that had long avoided regulating slavery. Both views have merit. The law preserved planters' control over the labor of ingênuos and enslaved adults, and the compensation clauses reflected the political need to protect property. But it also opened courts and registers to enslaved litigants and shifted political debate from whether slavery should end to how.

The contradiction at its core would define the next decade and a half. The law promised an end to slavery within a lifetime, but it did nothing for the roughly 1.5 million people then enslaved and little for the children it declared free. The government gave itself the authority to regulate the master-slave relationship, but it did so slowly, with weak enforcement. Slaveholders, who believed they had made a major concession, resented any further interference, while enslaved people and abolitionists saw an opening. The law thus exposed the contradiction of an empire that had committed itself in principle to emancipation without committing itself to its practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Ingênuo

An ingênuo was a child born to an enslaved mother after 28 September 1871 and therefore legally free. The word comes from Roman law, where it described someone born free, as opposed to a freedman (liberto), who had been freed from slavery.

The term captures the law's tension: these children were free in status but remained under their mothers' owners' authority until eight and often until twenty-one. It matters because it shows that legal freedom and practical freedom could diverge sharply, and a modern term like "free-born" does not convey the compromise.

Section 66.5

Enslaved Resistance, Freedom Suits, and the Struggle for Self-Emancipation

Enslaved people resisted slavery throughout its history in Brazil, and the decades after 1850 were no exception. Resistance took forms from the everyday to the collective. Work slowdowns, feigned illness, theft, sabotage, and appeals to the owner's reputation for fairness were constant. Flight was common, whether short-term escapes to negotiate better conditions or permanent flight to cities, to other regions, or to communities of fugitives. Violence against overseers and owners occurred often enough to haunt planters' imaginations, and fear of revolt was a persistent theme of political discussion. After the Free Womb Law, as the legal position of enslaved people altered, the boundary between private resistance and public claims became more porous.

Quilombos, communities of fugitives, existed across Brazil. The seventeenth-century Palmares was the most famous, but smaller settlements continued in forests, hills, and swamps into the nineteenth century, and in the 1880s some formed near cities, where they relied on networks of free and enslaved supporters. Such communities traded with neighbors, grew food, and sometimes sheltered new arrivals. They posed no simple military threat, but they challenged owners' authority and drew armed expeditions. Historians reconstruct them from police reports, newspaper accounts, and the testimony of captured fugitives, all of which reflect the perspective of the forces opposing them.

Self-purchase and manumission represent a quieter strategy. Enslaved people, especially in cities, saved money through the ganho system, through work on their days off, or through family networks, and bought their freedom or that of kin. Women, who often controlled household and market resources, were prominent in these transactions. The 1871 law's recognition of the pecúlio gave such efforts legal footing, and the courts became an arena of struggle over price, over conditional manumissions, and over whether promises made by owners could be enforced. Freedom was often purchased in installments, and families planned carefully which member's freedom to secure first. Such strategies reveal the constraints within which choices were made, since freeing one relative could mean leaving others behind.

Litigation was the most visible legal form of resistance. In freedom suits (ações de liberdade), enslaved people, represented by a curator, a lawyer, or a sympathetic ally, argued that they were free by law, because they had been illegally enslaved, promised freedom, or entitled to purchase. Many cases drew on the law of 1831, under which Africans landed after that year had been free from the moment of arrival, so that thousands of people held in slavery since then had a legal claim. The courts did not always follow their own statutes, and judges were often slaveholders' relatives or associates, but the record of these suits shows that enslaved people understood the legal system and used it.

Luís Gama (1830–1882) embodied this struggle. Born free in Salvador to a free African mother, according to his own account, he was sold into slavery as a child by his father and taken to São Paulo, where he learned to read, gained his freedom, and became a self-taught lawyer, journalist, and republican. Gama defended enslaved clients in court, often without fee, arguing that those held illegally in slavery were entitled to freedom, and he is credited with helping to free hundreds, though the exact number cannot be verified. His funeral in São Paulo in 1882 drew a large crowd, and his legal arguments and writings gave the abolitionist movement a model of rights-based activism. He also illustrates how Black Brazilians were leaders, not only beneficiaries, of the abolitionist cause.

These forms of resistance mattered politically because they raised the cost of slavery and altered the climate. Each escape, suit, and purchase demonstrated that enslaved people did not accept their condition and could use any opening. They also made owners' dependence on state enforcement visible, since slavery relied on police, courts, and the willingness of free men to capture fugitives. As that willingness weakened in the 1880s, the system's vulnerability became obvious. The narrative of reform from above, in which the state gradually freed a docile population, misses the pressure from below that made reform necessary and eventually made gradualism impossible.

Section 66.6

Abolitionist Societies, Black Intellectuals, Women, and the Public Campaign

The abolitionist movement of the 1880s was new in scale and style. Earlier critics of slavery, such as the statesman José Bonifácio at independence, had argued in parliament or in pamphlets; the movement that emerged around 1880 was public, urban, and multiracial. In 1880 Joaquim Nabuco, a young deputy from Pernambuco, helped found the Brazilian Society Against Slavery in Rio de Janeiro. In 1883 a federation of local clubs formed the Confederação Abolicionista. Newspapers, public lectures, concerts, and theater benefits turned abolition into a form of popular politics, and the campaign used techniques of mass mobilization that no earlier Brazilian movement had matched.

Three figures dominate the traditional account. Nabuco, from an elite family, was a gifted orator and the author of O Abolicionismo (1883), which argued that slavery had corrupted Brazil's economy, politics, and society and that abolition must be accompanied by broader reform. José do Patrocínio, the son of a priest and an enslaved woman, was a journalist whose newspaper, the Gazeta da Tarde, became a principal voice of the movement, and a fierce speaker who raised funds and rallied crowds. André Rebouças, an engineer and one of the best-known Black professionals of the empire, advocated not only abolition but "rural democracy," a land tax and reform that would give freed people access to land. Rebouças's friendship with Pedro II gave him a rare connection to the throne.

These leaders worked with many others. Luís Gama remained a model until his death. Black newspapers, lay brotherhoods, and workers' associations carried the message, and artisans and printers played prominent roles. Women were active as writers, fundraisers, and organizers. Maria Firmina dos Reis, a Black schoolteacher in Maranhão, had published the novel Úrsula in 1859, one of the first antislavery novels in Brazil, and written from an Afro-Brazilian woman's perspective. Others, including the composer Chiquinha Gonzaga, took part in benefit events and sold works to raise money for manumission. Women's associations, often tied to churches and charity, organized to buy freedom for individuals and to host public events. Enslaved women, who bore the greatest burden of the Free Womb Law's compromises, were also among the litigants and fugitives who gave the campaign its constituency.

The campaign used a broad repertoire. Meetings in theaters and halls, parades, and festivals drew crowds; newspapers published lists of those freed through donations; and societies sold tickets, held lotteries, and organized "flower battles" and other events to raise money. Petitions and parliamentary speeches pressed the case in the legislature. At the local level, abolitionist clubs hid fugitives, brought cases to court, and pressured owners to free their slaves in public ceremonies. Such ceremonies became a symbolic currency, with owners rewarded by public praise for conditional or unconditional manumissions, and the campaign built a moral climate in which holding slaves was increasingly criticized.

Abolitionists did not agree on all things. Some, like Patrocínio at times, demanded immediate and unconditional abolition. Others accepted gradual plans or compensation to owners as the price of agreement. Nabuco emphasized parliamentary action and distrusted violence, while others tolerated or encouraged the hiding of fugitives and civil disobedience. Rebouças and Nabuco wanted social reform, such as land and education, to accompany emancipation, whereas many allies were content with legal abolition alone. Many abolitionists were monarchists who believed the emperor and the princess could lead emancipation; others, such as Gama, were republicans. This diversity of aims shaped both the movement's strengths and its limits.

Section 66.7

Ceará, Amazonas, and the Expansion of Free Territories, 1884

While the national movement gathered strength in Rio and São Paulo, a campaign in the northeastern province of Ceará showed that abolition could be won locally. Ceará had a small enslaved population relative to the coffee provinces, and its economy was hit by the drought of 1877–1879, which led many owners to sell enslaved people to the south. The slave trade out of the province, carried on from the port of Fortaleza, became the target of an abolitionist society, the Sociedade Cearense Libertadora, formally established amid the January 1881 mobilization and drawing on the support of students, journalists, Black workers, and officials.

The refusal to transport enslaved people was not a single August episode. From 27 to 31 January 1881, Fortaleza's raftsmen (jangadeiros) and their supporters resisted the embarkation of people being sold to southern provinces. The freed Black port worker José Luís Napoleão was prominent in organizing the first action, as was Francisco José do Nascimento, later celebrated as the Dragão do Mar; sources differ in how they distribute individual leadership, and the action depended on collective labor solidarity. A renewed stoppage on 30 August 1881 made Nascimento especially prominent in the public abolitionist campaign. The raftsmen refused to take enslaved people from the beach to ships offshore, interrupting the crucial last stage of the coastal traffic. The boycotts demonstrated that workers could use their control of transportation to weaken a legal trade, at personal economic and political risk. They were not merely symbolic gestures: the port struggle circulated through newspapers, abolitionist networks, and public meetings, and later patriotic accounts often condensed the two stoppages into a single heroic episode.

The movement then expanded town by town. Local societies urged owners to free their slaves, offered public recognition to those who did, and pressed municipal governments to declare their jurisdictions free. The town of Acarape, renamed Redenção, declared itself free in January 1883, and others followed. The province's abolitionists coordinated their campaign through the society and the press, and the president of the province, Sátiro Dias, supported the effort. On 25 March 1884 Ceará was declared free of slavery, the first province to end it within its borders. In the Amazon, the province of Amazonas, with a small enslaved population and a rubber economy beginning to expand, followed with its own declaration on 10 July 1884.

These provincial victories had national effects. They did not end slavery in the empire, and in some areas their results were incomplete, since owners sold enslaved people out of the province, and some freed people were bound by conditional contracts. Still, they showed that abolition was possible and that local mobilization, combined with public pressure, could achieve it. Fugitives from neighboring provinces sought refuge in free territory, and slaveholders elsewhere worried that the example would spread. The campaign influenced the political crisis of 1884–1885, when the government of Manuel Pinto de Souza Dantas introduced a reform plan that proved too radical for the Chamber.

The Ceará campaign also illustrates the role of Afro-Brazilian workers. The jangadeiros acted as free laborers refusing to take part in a trade they opposed, and the decision cost them income and risk. Women, too, joined the movement in Ceará's abolitionist societies. The episode underlines the point that abolition was not simply a matter of elite reformers: it depended on coordination between public figures and people whose work and everyday decisions could halt or sustain the system.

Section 66.8

The Sexagenarian Law of 1885 and the Failure of Controlled Abolition

The crisis of 1884–1885 showed the limits of controlled emancipation. In June 1884, the Emperor appointed a Liberal cabinet under Manuel Pinto de Souza Dantas of Bahia, who proposed that enslaved people aged sixty and over be freed without compensation to owners. The project, drafted with support from Rui Barbosa and other Liberals, was a modest measure by abolitionist standards, since few enslaved people reached sixty. But it was attacked as a violation of property rights and divided the Liberal party. The Chamber, with many Liberals voting against the government, passed a motion of no confidence in 1885, and Dantas fell.

The Emperor turned to another Liberal, José Antônio Saraiva, whose cabinet reworked the bill to include compensation. When Saraiva resigned after passing the bill in the Chamber, the Emperor appointed the Conservative Baron of Cotegipe, a leading defender of the planters, who allowed it to pass. The resulting law, No. 3,270 of 28 September 1885, was known as the Sexagenarian Law or the Saraiva–Cotegipe Law. Article 3, paragraphs 10–11, declared enslaved people aged sixty and over free but generally required another three years of service to their former owners as indemnification, with the obligation ending by age sixty-five. Paragraph 13 required former owners to provide food, clothing, and care to qualifying elderly freedpeople who remained with them after the service period, while still allowing owners to benefit from work compatible with the freedperson's strength; judicial authorization could permit an individual to support themselves elsewhere. A distinct five-year municipal domicile rule in paragraph 14 applied to people emancipated through the emancipation fund, rather than to every person freed under the age provisions. Other clauses imposed policing and labor-contract pressures on freedpeople more broadly. The statute revised the emancipation fund, set a declining schedule of valuations by age, and required new registration. In 1886, Law No. 3,310 repealed judicial sentences of flogging for enslaved defendants; that procedural change did not by itself end private corporal coercion by owners.

The law's weaknesses were obvious. Relatively few enslaved people reached sixty, and many who did faced declining health, poverty, and continued dependence. The law formally imposed duties of maintenance on former owners for specified elderly beneficiaries, but the adequacy and enforcement of that protection cannot be assumed; it also let owners continue to use their labor. Compulsory service perpetuated dependence, while domicile rules for emancipation-fund beneficiaries and broader policing provisions constrained freedom and mobility in distinct ways. Abolitionists denounced it as a measure to rescue slavery. Nabuco and Patrocínio pointed out that it did nothing for the mass of working-age enslaved people and that it was designed to calm opinion without changing the system. The new registration process also gave owners an opportunity to adjust the declared ages of their slaves, and officials sometimes assigned ages arbitrarily.

The law nonetheless altered the political landscape. By making the first general emancipation of adults a matter of law, it conceded that slavery could be ended by statute, and it failed to satisfy anyone. Planters, who had accepted it reluctantly, argued that no further concessions were necessary. Abolitionists, who had been marginal in parliament, now argued that gradual measures had been exhausted. Enslaved people, who followed political debate through the networks of the urban poor and free Black workers, understood that the government had acted under pressure and that its resolve was limited. Within months, the movement to escape plantations began to accelerate, showing that the failure of controlled abolition was also a failure to contain people's own actions.

The debates of 1884–1885 also revealed the divisions among elites. Some favored compensation and the retention of control over the labor of freed people; others, such as the Paulista planters who began to explore immigrant labor, saw slavery as obsolete. The Emperor, who preferred gradual reform, found his options narrowing. Each attempt at gradual change provoked planter resistance, while each concession failed to appease the movement from below. This situation, in which controlled abolition proved politically impossible, helps explain the sudden collapse that followed.

Section 66.9

Mass Flight, the Caifazes, and the Breakdown of Plantation Discipline

After 1885, the pace of escape increased dramatically, especially in São Paulo. Where earlier fugitives had mostly gone alone or in small groups, in the late 1880s groups of dozens and sometimes hundreds left plantations together, often on the same night, and many were helped by networks that supplied them with information, transport, and shelter. The coffee frontier of western São Paulo, where the largest concentrations of enslaved workers had been brought in the previous decades, became the scene of repeated mass departures. In these years, the capacity of owners to hold people by force collapsed, not through a single event but through the accumulation of local failures.

One organization of this movement was the group known as the caifazes, formed in São Paulo around 1886 under the leadership of Antônio Bento, a lawyer and former magistrate who published an abolitionist newspaper, A Redempção. The name, taken from the biblical high priest Caiaphas, was a reference to their secrecy. The caifazes drew members from many social groups, including free Black workers, artisans, railway employees, clerks, students, and some women and clergy. They contacted enslaved people on plantations, arranged escapes, hid fugitives in the city, and moved them by railway to safe places, and they raised funds for these operations. Their activities ranged from publicity to clandestine action that went beyond the law.

Santos, the port at the end of the railway from the plateau, became a refuge. A large community of fugitives gathered at the Jabaquara quilombo on the outskirts of the city, where they were sheltered by abolitionist leaders and supported by local donations and work. Santos's abolitionists, including Black and white activists, defended the community against police attempts to retake its members. The quilombo, which housed thousands at its peak by contemporary estimates, was a visible sign that the legal system could no longer return people to their owners. The railway, which had been built to carry coffee to the sea, now carried people toward freedom.

Police and local authorities faced a dilemma. The law required them to capture fugitives, but in many places the police were reluctant, understaffed, or sympathetic. Some officials faced popular pressure and abolitionist agitation; others feared violence. Planters formed armed groups and demanded the army's help, but the state could not guarantee control. In some districts, owners tried to retain workers by promising wages or freedom after the next harvest, a tactic that blurred the line between slavery and free labor. Abolitionist lawyers brought habeas corpus petitions and freedom suits at a rapid pace. The result was a breakdown in the practical enforcement of slavery that preceded its legal end.

The flight of 1886–1888 should not be read as only the work of outside organizers. The accounts of the caifazes and of police show that plantation communities themselves made decisions about when and how to leave, often communicating through kin networks and with free relatives. Women, men, and children took part, and their departure created a labor crisis at the moment of harvest. For many, the decision meant leaving behind the only home they had known, with uncertain prospects. The collective character of the escapes demonstrates a kind of political action among the enslaved that the older narrative of top-down reform overlooked.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Abolition Primarily a Consequence of State Reform, Organized Abolitionism, or Enslaved People's Resistance?

The question: Which forces explain the end of slavery in Brazil, and how should historians weigh them?

Interpretation A — A reform process led by the state and political elites: Emphasizes the sequence of laws from 1850 to 1888, the role of the Emperor, the cabinets, and parliament, and the aim of managing a controlled transition. On this view, the empire's institutions, responding to British pressure and economic change, set the pace and direction of emancipation.
Interpretation B — Pressure from below, through abolitionism and enslaved resistance: Emphasizes the actions of enslaved people, the lawyers and journalists who supported them, and the mass flight of the late 1880s. On this view, the laws of 1871 and 1885 were attempts to contain a movement that outran them, and the Golden Law recognized a situation that had already broken the system.

Evidence: Both views rely on laws, parliamentary debates, newspapers, police reports, and court records. Historians such as Robert Conrad, whose 1972 study emphasized the interplay of political and social forces, and later scholars working with court and local records have moved toward combining the interpretations, while differing in emphasis. Economic explanations, which stress the shift of planters in São Paulo toward immigrant labor, add a third strand.

Why it matters: If emancipation was a gift of the state, it implies a story of enlightened leadership; if it was won from below, it implies a story of struggle and agency. The latter also suggests why the aftermath left so many demands unmet, since the actors who fought for freedom did not control the terms of its legal implementation. Most current accounts treat the process as the result of overlapping pressures.

Section 66.10

Coffee Planters, Immigration, and the Transition toward Free Labor

Planters did not respond to the crisis uniformly. Their differences reflected regional economies and the age of their estates. In the Paraíba Valley, coffee lands were old and in some cases declining in productivity, and owners' capital was heavily tied to enslaved people, who were collateral for loans. Their ability to adapt was limited, and they were the most determined defenders of slavery. In western São Paulo, new lands were more productive, railway access was good, and planters had capital to invest. Many of them concluded in the 1880s that slavery's days were numbered and that preparing a new labor system was in their interest.

The Paulista response included a campaign to attract European immigrants. Provincial governments subsidized passage, and in 1886 planters and officials founded the Sociedade Promotora de Imigração to organize recruitment, especially of Italians. A large immigrant reception center, the Hospedaria dos Imigrantes, opened in São Paulo in 1887. Arrivals rose from the thousands to tens of thousands, with a sharp increase in 1888 by commonly cited figures. The state subsidies meant that the cost of bringing laborers was borne partly by public funds, a policy that in effect shifted the cost of the labor transition to taxpayers, while planters kept the benefits.

The system that emerged in São Paulo, known as the colonato, combined features of wage labor and family tenancy. Immigrant families signed contracts to tend a fixed number of coffee trees for an annual payment, received a payment for harvesting, and were allowed to grow food on plots. Contracts often tied workers through debts for transport and supplies and restricted their mobility. Disputes over wages, housing, and the conditions of labor were frequent, and immigrants sometimes complained to consuls, protested, or left. Free labor, in short, was not automatically fair. It meant different forms of dependence and of bargaining power.

Immigration did not immediately replace formerly enslaved workers. Many Afro-Brazilians remained in the coffee districts, worked as wage laborers, sharecroppers, or smallholders, and in some places negotiated better terms precisely because the labor market was tight at harvest. In other regions, especially the Northeast and the Paraíba Valley, there was no large immigration, and planters relied on the local population. The assumption that European immigrants simply took the place of the formerly enslaved oversimplifies a complicated process in which owners, workers, and the state each tried to shape the terms. It also reflected racial ideas, since officials and planters often favored immigration partly in the hope of "whitening" the population, a view that cast Afro-Brazilians as less desirable workers and citizens.

Regional differences mattered for politics. Paulista planters, who were prepared for a transition, became more willing to support or at least accept abolition by 1887, and some, like Antônio Prado, a prominent politician and planter, took public positions in favor of ending slavery. In the Paraíba Valley and parts of Minas, planters who were less prepared were more bitter, and after abolition many of them complained of ruin and blamed the monarchy. This division within the planter class is crucial for understanding both the speed of emancipation and the political consequences that followed.

Section 66.11

The Crisis of 1887–1888: Public Opinion, the Army, and Imperial Politics

By 1887 slavery was widely perceived as untenable, though the government of the Baron of Cotegipe resisted further action. The Emperor, in poor health, traveled to Europe for treatment in June 1887, leaving Princess Isabel as regent, and she was sympathetic to abolition. The enslaved population, reduced by deaths, manumissions, and flight, was reported in the 1886–1887 registration at some 720,000, down substantially from the 1.5 million of 1872. Public meetings and press campaigns continued, and in many cities the abolitionist cause had become socially respectable. Slaveholders themselves were divided, and some began freeing people on condition of several years' further service, a strategy to retain labor through the harvest.

The army's attitude became a key factor. In October 1887, the Clube Militar, whose president was Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca, sent a petition to Princess Isabel asking that the army not be used to capture fugitive slaves. The reasons were mixed. Some officers sympathized with abolition; others resented being used as a police force, and many were preoccupied with the army's status and its strained relations with civilian politicians. Whatever the motives, the effect was to signal that the force on which slavery's enforcement ultimately depended would no longer serve that function reliably. The government, which could not rely on the police alone, saw a critical support withdrawn.

Politics shifted as well. Cotegipe's government, defending the planters, was weakened by the army's stance, by popular unrest, and by splits within its party. The Emperor's return to Brazil in 1888 was still pending, and Isabel was in effect the head of state. Cotegipe resigned in March 1888, and Isabel named João Alfredo Correia de Oliveira, a Conservative who had been Cotegipe's colleague, as prime minister on 10 March. João Alfredo, a figure of the party's moderate wing, understood that planters in the new coffee regions were prepared to accept the end of slavery, and he judged that immediate abolition was safer than continued resistance.

Other forces added pressure. In May 1888 the Pope's encyclical against slavery, addressed to the bishops of Brazil, appeared, giving the movement a moral endorsement from the Church. The press, whether liberal or conservative, was increasingly critical of slavery. The princess, who had supported the cause and been associated with antislavery philanthropy, was ready to act. Politicians who had resisted gradual measures now feared that continued resistance would cause social disorder in the countryside, as plantations lost workers and violence broke out between owners and fugitives. Many were prepared to support abolition as a way of regaining control by ending the legal basis of conflict.

This was the moment when abolition became a matter of political survival for the government. The monarchy had delayed, and the delay had cost it the ability to manage the transition. The decision to move toward immediate abolition, made by an unusual coalition of the princess, the new cabinet, abolitionists in parliament, and the pressure of events, was not the culmination of a plan. It reflected a judgment that the alternative, ongoing breakdown of order, was worse. This explains why the final step was both sudden and, once taken, nearly unanimous.

Section 66.12

Princess Isabel and the Lei Áurea, 13 May 1888

The final act was brief. On 3 May 1888, at the opening of the legislative session, Princess Isabel read the speech from the throne herself, announcing that the government would propose the extinction of slavery. On 8 May the Minister of Agriculture, Rodrigo Augusto da Silva, presented the bill to the Chamber of Deputies. Its text was only two articles. The Chamber approved it within days by an overwhelming majority, and the Senate took it up on 13 May. A few senators spoke against it, and some, such as members of the Paraíba Valley elite, protested the absence of compensation, but the Senate also approved it by a large margin, in a rapidity of passage unusual for the empire's slow parliamentary processes.

On the afternoon of 13 May, Isabel, acting as regent in the name of her absent father, signed the law at the Paço Imperial in Rio de Janeiro, before ministers, parliamentarians, and a crowd that filled the surrounding square. The law, numbered 3,353, is known as the Lei Áurea, the Golden Law. Its first article declared slavery extinct from the date of the law, and its second repealed contrary provisions. Nothing else was included: no indemnity for owners, no land for freed people, no provision for education, wages, housing, or the care of the elderly and children. The text left to the future everything that would determine what freedom meant.

The celebrations were widespread. In Rio, crowds gathered in front of the palace and in the streets, with fireworks, music, and church services. Patrocínio, one of the movement's leaders, was reported to have thrown himself at the princess's feet in thanks. Newspapers hailed Isabel as "the Redeemer," a title that circulated in popular and official memory. Celebrations occurred in provincial cities and in plantation districts, where formerly enslaved people held dances and religious ceremonies, and many left their former owners' properties, some to find relatives and some to claim places of their own. These responses were not uniform: some freedpeople stayed on estates to bargain over conditions, and others moved to towns.

The meaning of the law must be understood against this background. It was the end of a legal institution that had existed for over three centuries in Brazil, and it freed several hundred thousand people. It was also the recognition of a situation created by the actions of the enslaved, the abolitionists, the army's withdrawal of support, and the planters' division. The widely repeated story of the princess's generosity attributes to one person a decision that was the result of many. Isabel's personal commitment was real, and her role in signing the law mattered, but she acted when events had left few alternatives. Planters, some of whom later blamed the princess for the monarchy's fall, remembered the law as a betrayal, and a remark traditionally attributed to Cotegipe, that she had redeemed a race but lost her throne, expresses this later view.

The absence of a post-emancipation program was a consequence of the political coalition that produced the law. Planters accepted abolition because compensation would have burdened the treasury and because they expected the freedpeople to remain as laborers. Reformers like Rebouças, who proposed land reform and rural education, could not secure support. The government expected the labor market and the Land Law of 1850, which restricted access to public lands, to channel freedpeople back to estates. In this way, the law that ended slavery also embodied the limits of what its sponsors were prepared to change.

The Golden Law raised questions that structured the following decades. What would freedom look like without land, schools, or political rights? How would former owners and former slaves negotiate work? Who would count as a citizen? These questions had no immediate answers, and they define the content of the next section. The law stands as a landmark of legal emancipation and as a reminder that formal freedom and social equality are different things.

Section 66.13

Freedom without Redistribution: Formerly Enslaved People after 1888

The first weeks after 13 May 1888 revealed how varied freedom could be. In some districts, formerly enslaved people left plantations in large numbers, walking to towns, to relatives on other estates, or to land they hoped to farm. Leaving was itself an act of freedom, a way to test the reality of the law and to reassert family ties broken by sale. In other districts, people stayed on the estates where they had lived, but on new terms: they bargained over wages, over the days they would work, over the size of garden plots, and over the right to leave. Owners who had lost the power of the whip tried to hold workers through advances, debts, and promises, and the first harvests after abolition were a period of intense negotiation.

The central fact was the absence of land. The Golden Law had no provision for redistribution, and the Land Law of 1850 still made public land costly to acquire. A few formerly enslaved communities gained land through inheritance, purchase, or the occupation of unclaimed areas, and some fugitive communities received recognition, but most freedpeople entered the labor market with little capital. In the coffee districts they worked as wage laborers, sharecroppers, or tenants, often alongside immigrants, and the terms were typically worse than those offered to Europeans, who had subsidized passage and in many cases contracts negotiated with consular support. In the Northeast, where sugar estates dominated, freedpeople often remained dependent on the same families that had owned them.

Families and communities were central to the transition. Many freedpeople searched for children, spouses, and parents sold away, and they used churches, brotherhoods, and mutual aid societies to rebuild networks. Women often headed households and worked as domestics, laundresses, cooks, and market vendors, and they faced the dual burdens of racial and gender discrimination. Some ingênuos and young people were kept in dependent labor by former owners, and the legal distinction between freedom and servitude remained blurred in practice for years. Education was scarce: schools were few in the countryside, and the literacy rate of the population remained low.

Political rights were limited. The 1824 Constitution, Article 6, recognized people born in Brazil who had gained freedom as Brazilian citizens; African-born freedpeople were not automatically placed in that category and occupied a more precarious legal position. Citizenship also did not ensure voting rights. The electoral reform of 1881 introduced direct elections but imposed a literacy requirement and generally demanded proof of at least 200 mil réis in annual net income from property, employment, trade, or industry—an income qualification, not a categorical requirement to own land. These provisions excluded much of the poor population, while access to literacy had been severely limited for enslaved and newly emancipated people. Participation was thus restricted regardless of race, but its effects fell hardest on Afro-Brazilians. Some Black leaders, like Patrocínio, remained politically active, and in 1888 he helped organize a Black militia, the Guarda Negra, to defend the monarchy and the princess, which clashed with republican meetings. Its existence shows both the gratitude many felt toward the throne and the way racial politics became entangled with the crisis of the empire.

Racial hierarchy persisted and in some respects hardened. Elites who had justified slavery on paternalist grounds shifted to justifications based on racial science and "whitening," which cast Afro-Brazilians as inferior and encouraged immigration of Europeans. The early republic's penal code of 1890 criminalized vagrancy and capoeira, offenses that police applied disproportionately against the poor and Black. In December 1890, Finance Minister Rui Barbosa ordered the burning of the slave registration records, an act often explained as a way to erase the memory of slavery and prevent indemnity claims by former owners, though historians debate his motives, and its effect was to destroy documents that would have been useful to descendants and researchers.

Formerly enslaved people, nonetheless, shaped the post-emancipation world. They formed communities, brotherhoods, samba and religious associations, and they built households and businesses. Their decisions about where to work, how to organize families, and when to resist labor demands limited the power of former owners and set the terms of the labor market. The story of the years after 1888 is one of freedom won but not secured, in which Afro-Brazilians faced exclusion and coercion and continued to pursue independence and dignity.

What Changed

  • Freedpeople gained the right to move, to bargain over wages, and to form legal families and associations
  • Wage, tenancy, and colonato arrangements replaced ownership in many districts

  • Planter dominance in local and national politics
  • Racial hierarchy, now expressed through scientific racism and policing
  • Restricted suffrage because of literacy and property requirements

Who experienced change most directly: Formerly enslaved people, whose legal and daily lives were transformed. Greater continuity: Large landowners and political elites. Why the pattern matters: It shows why legal emancipation, however momentous, did not by itself alter the distribution of land, power, and opportunity.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Reunions, Contracts, and Communities after Emancipation

For many families the first act of freedom was a journey. Parents set out to find children who had been sold or given away, and spouses separated by the domestic trade tried to reunite. Newspapers carried notices from people seeking relatives, and church records show marriages that were formalized once the owner's permission was no longer required. Households were often built from the remnants of several families, with grandmothers, aunts, and neighbors raising children.

Work was bargained house by house. On coffee estates, freedpeople negotiated payment for each day or each harvest and the size of food plots, and many insisted on Sundays and saints' days free of labor. Contracts, when written, were drafted by planters and enforced by local officials, yet workers' ability to leave limited the owners' demands. In cities, freedwomen worked as domestics and vendors while men found work in ports, construction, and small workshops, often in crowded rented rooms.

Community institutions mattered. Lay brotherhoods, Afro-Brazilian religious practices, mutual aid societies, and musical groups gave shelter and identity. These records are uneven, since police files and newspapers often described the poor through the lens of disorder, and the silences about women's and children's experiences are themselves evidence of whose lives the surviving documents recorded.

Section 66.14

Republicanism, Federalism, and the Political Opposition to Monarchy

Republican ideas were older than the movement to end slavery and followed a separate course. Earlier republican revolts, including the Pernambucan uprising of 1817 and the rebellions of the regency period, had been regional and had failed. Organized republicanism returned in 1870, when Liberal politicians and journalists, disillusioned after the Emperor dismissed the Liberal cabinet in 1868 and as the war ended, founded a Republican Club in Rio de Janeiro and published a manifesto on 3 December 1870 in the newspaper A República. Its authors included Quintino Bocaiúva and Saldanha Marinho, and it presented the monarchy as an obstacle to Brazil's place among the republics of the Americas.

The core arguments were political. The manifesto criticized the concentration of power in the Emperor's moderating authority, the life-tenure Senate, the centralization that subordinated provinces to the Court, and the dependence of elected officials on the cabinet. It called for federalism, giving provinces autonomy over their own affairs, and for broader representation. On slavery, the manifesto had little to say, a silence consistent with the fact that many republicans were themselves slaveholders and that the movement's leaders did not make abolition central. Abolition and republicanism were different causes with different constituencies.

The movement found its strongest base in São Paulo. At a convention in Itu in April 1873, planters and professionals founded the Partido Republicano Paulista (PRP), which combined demands for federalism and fiscal autonomy with the interests of the expanding coffee economy. For Paulista planters, who paid taxes to the central government and felt underrepresented, federalism promised control over provincial revenue and immigration policy. In Rio Grande do Sul, a republican party formed in 1882 under Júlio de Castilhos, shaped by positivist ideas and regional resentments. Elsewhere, republicanism was weaker, with small clubs and newspapers in provincial capitals.

Republicans held differing visions. One current, associated with Bocaiúva and later Rui Barbosa, favored a liberal, constitutional, federal republic reached through persuasion and elections. Another, influenced by positivism, favored a strong executive and a "republican dictatorship" to guide society. Some, like Lopes Trovão, advocated popular agitation, and others, like the Paulista leaders, preferred gradual political change. Many republicans were conservative on social questions, and several were hostile to granting political rights to the poor. The movement was elite, urban, and small in absolute terms: republicans elected few deputies during the empire, and the party had little mass following before 1889.

The relationship to abolition was complex. Some abolitionists, like Luís Gama, were republicans, and some republicans, like Gama's associates in São Paulo, supported abolition. But many leading abolitionists, including Nabuco, Patrocínio at first, and Rebouças, were monarchists who saw in the throne a possible ally for emancipation. Meanwhile, many republican planters opposed abolition, and after 13 May 1888 a group of disaffected slaveholders, mocked as the "republicans of 14 May," joined the movement out of resentment at the lack of compensation. The republican movement thus gained recruits as a consequence of abolition, but it did not arise from abolition.

Section 66.15

The Religious Question and the Crisis of Church–State Relations

The Constitution of 1824 made Catholicism the state religion, and under the system known as the padroado the Emperor exercised extensive authority over the Church. The government paid clergy, nominated bishops, and had to approve papal documents before they could take effect in Brazil, a requirement called the beneplácito or placet. Brazilian Catholicism had a long tradition of lay religious brotherhoods (irmandades), including Black and mixed-race brotherhoods, that were semi-independent organizations. Many elite men were also Freemasons, including prominent politicians, and Masonic lodges and Catholic brotherhoods overlapped in membership.

The tension grew with the Church's increasingly assertive stance under Pope Pius IX, whose 1864 Syllabus of Errors condemned liberalism and Freemasonry. A younger generation of Brazilian bishops, trained in a stricter ultramontane tradition, sought to assert Church authority against state control. In 1872 a priest in Rio de Janeiro was disciplined by his bishop for giving a speech praising the Masonic grand master, who was the Prime Minister, the Viscount of Rio Branco. The incident became a political test of whether the bishop could discipline Masons in the Church.

Two bishops took the lead: Dom Vital de Oliveira of Olinda and Dom Antônio de Macedo Costa of Pará. In 1873 they ordered Masons expelled from the lay brotherhoods in their dioceses, or the brotherhoods suspended. The brotherhoods refused, appealing to the imperial government, which argued that the bishops' orders, based on a papal bull that had not received the placet, were illegal. The bishops refused to yield. The government prosecuted them, and in 1874 the Supreme Court convicted both and sentenced them to four years of imprisonment with labor, a penalty the Emperor commuted to simple imprisonment.

The affair was politically costly. The imprisonment of bishops outraged many Catholics, and the government's insistence on state control seemed to many an overreach. In September 1875 a new ministry, under the Duke of Caxias, granted an amnesty and released the bishops. The government had won on principle and lost in practice, and the Church emerged more critical of a monarchy that had treated bishops as criminals. The monarchy's relationship with the clergy, previously cooperative, became strained, and the issue left a legacy of resentment among sectors of the Catholic hierarchy.

The Religious Question contributed to the monarchy's erosion without being decisive. Many Catholics remained loyal to the throne, and the Church was not a major actor in the events of 1889. But the dispute weakened the idea that the monarchy was a guardian of religion, and it showed that the imperial system of state control over the Church was increasingly hard to sustain. The new republic would separate Church and state in January 1890, a measure that, ironically, many Church leaders came to see as freeing the institution from government control. For Afro-Brazilian communities, the controversy also affected the brotherhoods that were central to social life, though their experiences are less documented than those of bishops and ministers.

Section 66.16

The Military Question, Positivism, and the Crisis of Imperial Succession

The army emerged from the Paraguayan War larger, more experienced, and more conscious of itself as an institution. Yet its officers felt neglected. Pay was low, promotions were slow, and the army's budget was small compared with the navy and the National Guard, the civilian militia dominated by local elites. Many officers resented what they perceived as the contempt of civilian politicians, the "bachelors" who ran the empire. This resentment was reinforced by social differences within the officer corps: officers who rose through combat and seniority tended to be conservative and attached to hierarchy, while younger graduates of the military academies, trained in mathematics and engineering, developed a different outlook.

Positivism shaped that outlook. The philosophy of Auguste Comte, which held that societies evolve through stages toward a scientific order, was taught at the Escola Militar in Rio de Janeiro by Benjamin Constant Botelho de Magalhães, a respected professor who influenced many young cadets. Positivism's emphasis on order, progress, science, and a strong, educated leadership appealed to officers who saw themselves as modernizers. It also encouraged a belief that the military, as a disciplined and rational institution, had a duty to guide the nation. In Rio Grande do Sul and in Rio, positivist groups developed political programs, and a Positivist Church organized by Miguel Lemos and Raimundo Teixeira Mendes promoted the doctrine.

A series of disputes in the mid-1880s, known as the Military Question (Questão Militar), expressed the army's grievances. In 1886 and 1887, officers who had published criticisms of the government in newspapers were disciplined by ministers, and officers such as Antônio de Sena Madureira and Cunha Matos challenged the authority of civilian war ministers to punish them. The conflict raised the question of whether officers had the right to speak publicly on political matters. Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca, a veteran of Paraguay and the most prestigious officer in the army, defended the officers, and in late 1887 he became president of the Clube Militar. The government, which had hoped to discipline the officers, found itself confronted by a united military voice.

The succession compounded the institutional crisis. Pedro II, born in 1825, suffered from illness, including diabetes, and his health worried his advisers; he spent much of 1887–1888 in Europe. The heir was Isabel, whose husband, the French-born Count d'Eu, was unpopular, and many in the political class feared a Third Reign under a woman perceived as devout and under the influence of a foreigner. The Emperor himself reportedly expressed little enthusiasm about a future republic or monarchy, and he took few steps to prepare the succession. The prospect of the throne passing to Isabel, whose abolition of slavery had angered many planters, added to the alienation of important groups.

By 1889, therefore, the monarchy faced hostility from several quarters for different reasons. Republicans wanted federalism, planters resented the lack of compensation, some clergy resented past treatment of the bishops, and military officers resented neglect and civilian control. These groups did not form a coalition, and many wanted different things. Few wanted the same republic. But the monarchy, which depended on the Emperor's prestige and on the cooperation of elites, had lost enthusiastic defenders, and its fate rested on whether any institution would defend it when challenged. That vulnerability, more than any single grievance, explains the speed of its collapse.

Historical Vocabulary
Positivism (positivismo)

Positivism was a philosophy developed by the French thinker Auguste Comte in the nineteenth century, arguing that knowledge should rest on observation and scientific method and that societies progress through defined stages toward a rational order. In Brazil it became a political and cultural current, especially among military academy teachers and students and in Rio Grande do Sul.

It matters because it gave young officers and republican thinkers a vocabulary of order and progress that contrasted with the monarchy's liberal-parliamentary style, and the national motto "Ordem e Progresso" later drew on it. It should not be confused with the everyday sense of being "positive," nor treated as the view of all republicans or all officers.

Section 66.17

The Final Imperial Government and the Conspiracies of 1889

After the Golden Law, the government of João Alfredo remained in office until June 1889, but it faced a Chamber divided by the abolition's aftermath. The Emperor returned from Europe in August 1888, weakened by illness, and he resumed his role while his heir waited. When João Alfredo's cabinet fell in 1889, the Emperor turned to the Liberals and appointed Afonso Celso de Assis Figueiredo, the Viscount of Ouro Preto, who took office on 7 June 1889. Ouro Preto, an energetic Liberal, aimed to save the monarchy through reform.

His program was ambitious. It proposed to expand provincial autonomy by allowing provinces to choose their presidents from lists, to widen the franchise, to end the life tenure of senators, to reform municipal government, and to provide credit to agriculture to help planters adjust after abolition. These measures aimed to answer republican demands for federalism within a monarchical framework. But the Chamber, which was still Conservative from the previous election, rejected the cabinet's program, and the Emperor dissolved it on Ouro Preto's request, and new elections were held in August 1889, with the new Chamber due to convene on 20 November. The republicans interpreted the reforms as too little and too late, and the monarchists feared they went too far.

Tensions with the army increased. Ouro Preto, who regarded the military's political activity as dangerous, took measures that officers saw as provocations, including the transfer of garrisons and the appointment of Gaspar Silveira Martins, a Liberal politician and Deodoro's rival, as president of Rio Grande do Sul, where Deodoro had served. Rumors circulated that Deodoro and Benjamin Constant would be arrested or sent away, and that the army would be reduced in favor of the National Guard and police. Whether such plans existed is uncertain, but the rumors, circulated by republicans, hardened military resentment.

Republicans meanwhile organized. Quintino Bocaiúva, Aristides Lobo, and other civilians contacted officers, especially Benjamin Constant, whose influence over cadets was strong, and sought the support of Deodoro, who was loyal to the Emperor personally and had not been a republican. In early November, republicans and some officers coordinated, and they persuaded Deodoro that his own position and that of the army were threatened. The imperial government held a last grand ball on 9 November on the Ilha Fiscal, in Guanabara Bay, with thousands of guests, an event that later became a symbol of the monarchy's detachment from the gathering crisis.

What is striking about the final months is how limited the conspiracy was. A handful of civilian republicans and a minority of army officers decided to act, while most of the country, including many republicans in the provinces, was unaware. Different participants wanted different outcomes: some a republic, some a change of cabinet, some a reform of the monarchy. The convergence of these discontents, rather than a unified movement, produced the events of 15 November.

Section 66.18

15 November 1889: Deodoro da Fonseca and the Overthrow of the Monarchy

On the night of 14 November, Benjamin Constant and republican leaders, alarmed by rumors that arrests were imminent, urged Deodoro to act. Deodoro, ill and reportedly weak, hesitated. Early on the morning of 15 November, troops assembled at the Campo de Santana in Rio de Janeiro, where Deodoro joined them and led them toward the War Office, where the Ouro Preto cabinet had gathered. Younger officers, including supporters of Benjamin Constant, played an active role in mobilizing units. The size of the force was modest: a few thousand soldiers from several garrisons, a small part of the army and a minute fraction of the population.

The government's response was ineffective. Ouro Preto and his ministers had sought troops loyal to the monarchy, but the commanders, including the Adjutant-General Floriano Peixoto, a veteran officer, did not give orders to fire on the rebels, and in some accounts told the ministers that the troops would not fight fellow soldiers who had been comrades in Paraguay. Floriano's conduct was decisive and has been interpreted in different ways, as loyalty to the army, as reluctance to spill blood, or as tacit sympathy. Deodoro's troops entered the building, and the cabinet was declared deposed. There was almost no resistance and few casualties.

What Deodoro intended is disputed. Accounts suggest that at the Campo de Santana he demanded the removal of the cabinet and cheered the Emperor, and that he had not initially planned to abolish the monarchy. Deodoro was personally loyal to Pedro II and, according to later testimony, said he intended to propose a new cabinet to the Emperor, perhaps led by a Liberal rival of Ouro Preto. Others, like Benjamin Constant and the civilian republicans, aimed at ending the monarchy. The movement thus began as a military action to overthrow a government and became the overthrow of a regime through the interaction of the officers' decision and the republicans' political initiative.

The proclamation of the republic occurred in the afternoon of 15 November, when José do Patrocínio, in the municipal council chamber of Rio, announced the new regime before a small crowd, with the council's endorsement. The Emperor, who had been in Petrópolis, returned to Rio that evening and tried to form a new cabinet, but by then the officers and republicans had convinced Deodoro to accept the republic, and the next day he was persuaded to sign the decrees. The text of the decree establishing the provisional republic is dated 15 November but was formalized in the hours that followed and published on 16 November. The intentions of participants, then, changed during the day.

Popular participation was minimal. A well-known remark attributed to the republican Aristides Lobo describes the public as watching the events without understanding them, and historians often cite this observation, though they debate how to read it, since many people in the capital and provinces simply learned of the change afterward. This was a coup, a seizure of power by military force with civilian republican support, not a mass uprising and not the expression of a national republican majority. Recognizing this does not diminish its significance: a small group acting decisively could change the regime because the monarchy had lost active defenders.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Cumulative Causes of the Monarchy's Collapse

Long-term causes

Centralization that irritated provincial elites; the rise of republican ideas after 1870; Church–State conflict; the army's postwar grievances; the spread of positivism; the Emperor's age and the uncertain succession.

Short-term causes

The loss of planter support after abolition; the Military Question; rumors of arrests and army reductions; the Ouro Preto cabinet's failure to win the Chamber; conspiracy among a few officers and republicans.

Event or transformation

The military action of 15 November 1889 deposed the cabinet and ended the monarchy.

Immediate effects

Exile of the imperial family; a provisional government under Deodoro; transformation of provinces into states.

Long-term consequences

A federal republic with restricted suffrage, strong state oligarchies, and military influence in politics.

Historical complexity

Abolition contributed but did not by itself cause the fall. The coup depended on the convergence of distinct grievances and the absence of defenders.
Section 66.19

The Imperial Family's Exile and the Establishment of the Republic

On 16 November the provisional government informed Pedro II that the monarchy had ended and that the imperial family must leave the country. The Emperor, who was in his sixties and ill, accepted without resistance. In the early hours of 17 November the family boarded a ship for Portugal and then went to France. Among those who accompanied them was André Rebouças, the Black engineer and abolitionist, who remained loyal to the dynasty. Pedro II died in Paris in December 1891. His departure ended a reign of nearly half a century, and for many Brazilians it was a surprise.

The provisional government was led by Deodoro as chief, with a cabinet that combined military and civilian figures: Benjamin Constant at War, Quintino Bocaiúva at Foreign Affairs, Rui Barbosa at Finance, Aristides Lobo at the Interior, Campos Sales at Justice, and others. Decree No. 1 of 15 November proclaimed the federal republic and converted the provinces into states under a federation called the United States of Brazil. The government dissolved the Chamber and the provincial assemblies, appointed governors, and ruled by decree until a constitution could be written. In its first months, it decreed the separation of Church and state in January 1890, civil marriage, and a broad naturalization of foreign residents.

Economic policy under Rui Barbosa encouraged easy credit and the creation of banks and companies, producing a speculative boom known as the Encilhamento, followed by a crash. The government's efforts to modernize the economy ran alongside its attempts to control the former slaves and the urban poor, as in the penal code's vagrancy provisions. Federalism, a central demand of the republicans, transferred power to state governments, which received control over export taxes and the right to contract foreign loans. In practice, this empowered state oligarchies, especially in São Paulo and Minas Gerais, whose planters shaped the republic's politics.

A constituent congress, elected in 1890, drafted the Constitution of 24 February 1891. It established a presidential, federal republic, with three branches, direct election of the president, and extensive state autonomy. It separated Church and state, and it extended the vote to literate adult men. Because the literacy requirement excluded the mass of the population, including most formerly enslaved people and their descendants, the electorate remained a small fraction of the population, a few percent. Women were not explicitly granted the vote and were excluded in practice. The constitution thus created a republic with limited participation, continuity of elite economic power, and a large role for the army.

The new order was unstable. The congress elected Deodoro as president, but he clashed with the legislature and, in November 1891, dissolved it. Faced with revolt, he resigned later that month in favor of his vice president, Floriano Peixoto. The first republican years thus showed that the military continued to shape politics and that the republic's institutions were fragile. For the formerly enslaved and their descendants, the change of regime brought few immediate benefits and some new burdens, a pattern the next chapters will place in comparison with other societies.

Section 66.20

Historical Synthesis: Abolition, Republicanism, and the Unfinished Struggle for Freedom

The chapter began with two dates, 13 May 1888 and 15 November 1889. Their proximity has encouraged a causal story in which abolition destroyed the monarchy by alienating planters. That story contains truth. After 1888, aggrieved slaveholders, especially in the Paraíba Valley, gave the republican movement new recruits and removed a pillar of support. But the story is incomplete. Republican organization began in 1870, the Religious and Military Questions developed independently, federalism animated Paulista planters who were preparing for free labor, and the coup itself was carried out by officers with their own grievances. Abolition was a necessary part of the setting but not a sufficient explanation.

Historians have emphasized different elements. An older view stressed the planters' resentment as the key to the monarchy's fall. Studies of the military have drawn attention to the army's institutional interests and to positivism. Work on political culture has highlighted the republic's limited popular base and the elite character of the transition. Social historians of slavery have shown that enslaved people, lawyers, journalists, and local networks forced the empire's hand on emancipation. Scholars such as Robert Conrad, Emília Viotti da Costa, José Murilo de Carvalho, and others, in different ways, have contributed to this view of overlapping processes rather than a single cause. These interpretations are best read as complements.

The distinction between destroying slavery and transforming labor and racial hierarchy is central. Legal abolition ended ownership of people and the legal power of owners to buy, sell, and punish, and that was a profound change. But land remained concentrated, political participation was restricted, and racial ideas that had justified slavery adapted to the new setting. Freedpeople had to build lives from a position of poverty, exclusion, and surveillance, and their struggles for land, education, dignity, and equal citizenship extended well beyond 1888. The notion that emancipation completed the story of freedom, a view encouraged by celebratory memory of the Golden Law, obscures this continuing struggle.

Comparing monarchy and republic shows both change and continuity. The republic was federal, presidential, secular in its relation to the Church, and open to the army's political role, while the monarchy had been centralized, parliamentary in form, Catholic by establishment, and dependent on the Emperor's moderating authority. But both were governed by elites, both restricted the vote, and both relied on the export economy and on the political weight of large landowners. The republic gave greater autonomy to states, which strengthened regional oligarchies and left Afro-Brazilians, the poor, and women with little formal voice.

Returning to the two dates, the chapter's controlling argument can be stated plainly. The abolition of slavery and the fall of the Brazilian Empire were interconnected but distinct transformations. Together they ended two foundational institutions of nineteenth-century Brazil, while leaving many of its economic hierarchies, racial inequalities, and restrictions on political participation intact. Legal emancipation did not automatically bring landownership, equality, economic security, or political power, just as the establishment of a republic did not establish mass democracy. The struggles of Afro-Brazilian women and men, from the courtroom of Luís Gama to the plantations of São Paulo and the streets of Rio, made the first transformation possible, and their continuing efforts to give freedom substance form the unfinished business that the twentieth century inherited.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced two transformations, the legal abolition of slavery on 13 May 1888 and the military overthrow of the monarchy on 15 November 1889, and argued that they were connected but distinct. Both ended foundational institutions of nineteenth-century Brazil. Neither, by itself, produced equality, land for the formerly enslaved, or mass political participation.

Slavery in the empire was bound to coffee, to the households and markets of cities, and to a political class of slaveholders. The Eusébio de Queirós Law of 1850 ended the Atlantic trade, but not slavery. A domestic trade moved enslaved people toward the coffee provinces of the Southeast, concentrating slavery in the empire's most powerful region, while the Land Law of 1850 protected the large estates and anticipated a future of controlled labor.

The Paraguayan War brought enslaved men into the army through enlistment and purchase, enlarged the military as an institution, and revived discussion of emancipation. The Free Womb Law of 1871 declared children born to enslaved mothers free but allowed owners to use their labor until twenty-one, left their mothers enslaved, and recognized enslaved people's savings and the right of self-purchase. It exposed the contradiction of an empire committed in principle to emancipation but unwilling to carry it out quickly.

Enslaved people undermined the system through flight, quilombos, self-purchase, family strategies, and litigation, and Black lawyers and journalists such as Luís Gama and José do Patrocínio helped give these actions a public voice. In the 1880s an abolitionist movement of newspapers, societies, theaters, and parliamentary speeches transformed public opinion. Abolitionists agreed on the goal but differed on speed, compensation, and the social reforms that should accompany emancipation.

Regional victories, notably Ceará and Amazonas in 1884, showed that abolition could be won locally. The Sexagenarian Law of 1885, a compromise that freed the elderly with conditions, satisfied no side and showed the failure of controlled abolition. In 1886–1888 the caifazes and mass flight in São Paulo destroyed the practical enforcement of slavery, and in October 1887 army officers signaled that they would no longer chase fugitives.

Planters were divided. Western São Paulo planters, with railways and immigrant labor in prospect, accepted the end of slavery; many Paraíba Valley planters felt ruined. The Golden Law of 13 May 1888, signed by Princess Isabel as regent after quick passage in parliament, ended slavery in two articles, with no compensation and no provision for land, schooling, or support. It was the culmination of long struggle, not an act of royal generosity alone.

After 1888 formerly enslaved people left estates, searched for kin, bargained over work, and built communities, but they faced exclusion from land, restricted suffrage, vagrancy laws, and racial ideologies that adapted to the new setting. Freedom had been won legally; its social meaning remained contested.

The monarchy's fall had separate roots. Republicanism, organized from 1870 and strongest in São Paulo and Rio Grande do Sul, was a federalist critique of centralization; the Religious Question strained relations with the Church; the Military Question, positivism, and army grievances alienated officers; and uncertainty about the succession weakened the throne. Abolition cost the monarchy some planter support but did not cause these conflicts.

On 15 November 1889, Deodoro da Fonseca led troops against the cabinet of the Viscount of Ouro Preto. The action was a military coup with a small civilian republican component, not a popular revolution, and Deodoro's initial intentions differed from the regime change that followed. The republic was proclaimed provisionally, the imperial family went into exile, and the Constitution of 1891 created a federal republic with restricted suffrage, strong state oligarchies, and a persistent military role.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how two foundations of the Brazilian Empire fell in quick succession for different reasons. Slavery was dismantled by people who fled, sued, hid fugitives, spoke, and organized, and by a political system that finally judged resistance to be costlier than concession. The monarchy fell because it had lost the active loyalty of the groups on which it depended, and because a small group of officers and republicans acted when no one defended it.

The chapter also shows the difference between legal and social transformation. The Golden Law and the republic were both landmarks, but neither redistributed land or broadened political power. The struggles that followed, over labor, citizenship, education, and racial equality, belong to the twentieth century and link Brazil to other post-emancipation societies, a comparison Chapters 68 and 69 will develop.

The next stages of Volume III turn from Brazil's regime change to the wider export economies and the new hemispheric order. Students should carry forward the view that Afro-Brazilians were historical actors in both emancipation and its aftermath, and that the transitions of 1888–1889 were beginnings of new conflicts, not endings.
