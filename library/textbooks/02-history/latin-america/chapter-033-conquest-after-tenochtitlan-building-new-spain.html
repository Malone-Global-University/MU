Conquest after Tenochtitlan: Building New Spain

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Victory Without a State

When the fighting ended on 13 August 1521, the Spaniards and their Indigenous allies had destroyed a capital but had not created a government. The decade and a half that followed was a struggle over what the victory would mean: who would rule, who would be paid, who would labor, which gods would be worshipped, and which communities would keep their rulers, lands, and privileges.

The city that Cortés and his allies had besieged for roughly three months lay in ruins. Bodies, rubble, and broken canals filled the island of Tenochtitlan and its sister city Tlatelolco, and many of the survivors had fled to the lakeshore towns. The Spanish force, reinforced since the previous year by new arrivals but still numbering only in the hundreds, was surrounded by Indigenous allies who had fought for their own reasons and who expected recognition. No viceroy, high court, or permanent tax system yet existed. The coalition had defeated the principal Mexica political and military center, but the conquest of central Mexico remained incomplete and colonization had barely begun.

This chapter studies the conversion of military victory into colonial rule, and it argues that this conversion was slow, improvised, and dependent on the people it subordinated. Chapter 32 explained how the siege was won. Chapter 33 asks what happened next, using the period from 1521 to 1535 as its frame. It begins with the ruins and ends with the arrival of Antonio de Mendoza as first viceroy of New Spain, a convenient marker of the movement from conqueror government toward a more regularized royal administration. Neither date marks a clean break, and the chapter attends throughout to processes that began earlier and continued later.

Several parties struggled over the postwar settlement, and none controlled it alone. Cortés sought to convert his military prestige into lasting authority and to reward his followers. The Crown, wary of a conqueror turning into an independent lord, sought recognition of its sovereignty and a share of the revenues. Conquerors and settlers wanted tribute and labor. Missionaries, who arrived from 1524, wanted converts and, increasingly, protection for them. Indigenous rulers, nobles, and communities sought to preserve rank, land, and household survival under a new overlord. Former allies, above all the Tlaxcalans, sought to convert wartime service into permanent privilege. The colonial order that emerged was a product of their interaction.

The chapter also insists on a distinction that runs through Part VII between conquest and colonization. Conquest is the use of force to defeat existing political authority. Colonization is the longer creation of institutions that extract resources, regulate labor, assign land, impose a religion, and settle newcomers. In central Mexico the second process depended heavily on Indigenous institutions, especially the altepetl, the local ethnic state described in earlier chapters, and on Indigenous elites who collected tribute, mobilized workers, and spoke for their communities. Spanish rule was imposed on these institutions and also run through them.

Demography shadows every section. Chapter 29 explained epidemic disease and its biological dimensions. Here the question is what population loss did to the making of the colony: how it reshaped labor supply, tribute, settlement patterns, households, and the missionary enterprise. Scholars disagree widely on the size of the preconquest population and the precise pace of decline, and this chapter notes those uncertainties while treating the scale of loss as one of the central facts of the period.

Evidence deserves careful handling. Cortés's Third and Fourth Letters to Charles V, written in 1522 and 1524, present a conqueror's version of rebuilding and governance, shaped by his need to secure royal favor. The Franciscan Toribio de Benavente, known as Motolinía, wrote enthusiastically about conversions, and the Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas wrote critically about Spanish violence. Indigenous voices survive in pictorial documents such as the Huexotzinco Codex of 1531, in the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, in Nahuatl-language records produced as alphabetic writing spread, and in petitions and lawsuits. Archaeology in the historic center of Mexico City adds physical evidence about what was destroyed, reused, and built. No single source provides the whole picture.

The chapter ends at a threshold. By the mid-1530s New Spain had a capital, a viceroy, a tribute system, missionary houses, and a growing Spanish population. It did not yet have control of Mesoamerica. Guatemala, Yucatán, and the frontiers beyond the central valleys were still unconquered or only partly subdued, and Chapter 34 turns to them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the military victory of August 1521 did not automatically produce a functioning colonial government.
  2. Trace the contest among Cortés, municipal officials, royal officers, and the Crown over political authority between 1521 and 1535.
  3. Analyze the decision to rebuild the capital on the site of Tenochtitlan and the continuities of infrastructure and labor within the new city.
  4. Define encomienda precisely and distinguish it from ownership of land or of people.
  5. Evaluate how Spanish rule depended on Indigenous governors, nobles, scribes, and community institutions.
  6. Assess how Tlaxcalans and other allies tried to convert wartime service into colonial rights, and how the Crown and settlers responded.
  7. Interpret the treatment of Cuauhtémoc and other defeated elites as evidence of Spanish insecurity and of the persistence of Indigenous legitimacy.
  8. Describe the beginnings of evangelization and explain why conversion is better understood as a long, negotiated process than as an event.
  9. Connect demographic decline to labor, tribute, settlement, and household life in the early colony.
  10. Compare conqueror claims with Indigenous documentary evidence in a primary-source analysis.
  11. Distinguish the early audiencias and the viceroyalty as developments in this period from the mature institutions treated in Chapter 40.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part VII's argument that conquest was multinational, prolonged, and incomplete.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the destruction of Tenochtitlan create problems of government instead of solving them?
  2. Why did the Crown hesitate to let Cortés become the permanent ruler of the lands he had conquered, and how did that hesitation shape institutions?
  3. Why rebuild on the ruins of the Mexica capital, and what does the reuse of its materials, roads, and labor reveal about continuity?
  4. How did encomienda convert existing Indigenous tribute arrangements into Spanish income, and what tensions did that create?
  5. To what extent was early New Spain governed by Indigenous institutions, and how should historians weigh transformation against survival?
  6. How did Indigenous allies use memory of the conquest as a political resource?
  7. In what ways were resistance and accommodation strategies available to the same people rather than opposing identities?
  8. What can historians say about conversion when the most detailed sources were written by missionaries?
  9. How did population loss alter the possibilities and the brutality of colonial rule?

Historical Context

Chapters 13 and 14 described the Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan as a tribute empire that governed through existing local rulers rather than through direct administration. Subject altepetl typically retained their own dynasties, councils, and territories, and obligations varied: tribute provinces, subject states, and strategic provinces owed differing goods, services, and military support to the Triple Alliance. That arrangement mattered after 1521 because it meant that a tribute machinery already existed, with lists of obligations, collectors, storehouses, and routes. The Spaniards did not need to invent taxation from nothing. They needed to take control of an apparatus that Indigenous officials already knew how to run.

Chapters 30 through 32 narrated the conquest as a multinational war. The Spanish expedition grew into a coalition dominated numerically by Indigenous allies, above all Tlaxcalans, Texcocans, Huexotzincas, and Totonacs, who pursued their own aims against the Mexica. Epidemic smallpox struck the Basin of Mexico in 1520 and killed many people, including leaders. The final siege destroyed much of the island city. The allies' contributions were enormous, and Cortés himself had reason to acknowledge them, since without them he could not hold what he had taken.

The Spanish side of the settlement carried its own inheritance. Cortés had begun his expedition under authority from the governor of Cuba, Diego Velázquez, and had then broken with him by founding the municipality of Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz and having its council name him leader, as Chapter 30 explained. His position in 1521 therefore rested on a legal fiction and on success. The Crown could ratify success, but it was reluctant to reward a man who had defied one royal officer in the name of the king. The Caribbean experience of the previous generation also shaped Spanish expectations. Conquerors expected to be rewarded with Indigenous labor and tribute through encomienda, which had been used in Hispaniola, and Crown lawyers and clergy had already begun to debate its legality and morality.

New Spain is not simply Mexico. The name, which Cortés used in his letters, referred to a growing and ill-defined territory of conquest that would eventually stretch from Central America to what is now the southwestern United States and across the Pacific to the Philippines. In this chapter it means chiefly central Mexico and its adjacent regions, the heartland where the Triple Alliance had been strongest. Even there, as the following sections show, Spanish presence was thin and Indigenous communities held most of the land, most of the population, and most of the knowledge about how to feed and govern the country.

Section 33.1

After 13 August: Ruins, Survivors, and an Unfinished Conquest

The surrender on 13 August 1521 ended organized resistance in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco. Spanish and Nahua accounts agree that the scene was one of devastation. Houses had been pulled down to fill canals, the water supply was disrupted, and starvation, disease, and fighting had killed very large numbers. Survivors were weak, and many fled across the causeways and by canoe to the towns of the lakeshore. Estimates of the dead vary enormously and none is reliable. What can be said is that the island city, which had been among the largest in the world, was physically wrecked and demographically gutted, and that its capture left its conquerors in command of a ruin.

Cuauhtémoc, the last tlatoani, was captured in a canoe during the final collapse and taken to Cortés. Other nobles were seized or surrendered. Spanish accounts describe demands for the treasure of Tenochtitlan, much of which had been lost during the Noche Triste and in the siege, and the disappointment of the conquerors at the modest quantity they recovered. That disappointment is historically important, because it shaped what followed: men who had expected fortune now looked to Indigenous tribute and labor as the substitute for the treasure that was not there.

Cortés and his army moved to Coyoacán, on the southern shore of the lake, while the island was cleared. The choice was practical. The stench and disease of the ruined city made it unlivable, and Coyoacán had space and supplies. From this base Cortés began to act as the ruler of the territory, receiving embassies, assigning tasks, and communicating with Indigenous lords. The first months of the postwar period were less a system of government than a series of improvisations by a victorious commander who needed to feed his army, house his men, reward his allies, and communicate his achievements to a distant king.

The allies who had fought alongside the Spaniards had their own claims. Tlaxcalan and other contingents had borne much of the fighting and the losses, and they left the siege expecting a share of the spoil, recognition of their autonomy, protection from new Spanish tribute demands, rewards for their service, and the preservation of their rulers and privileges. They also looted, and in some cases enslaved, residents of the defeated city. At the same time, the victory removed the old imperial center that had organized tribute across central Mexico. This created a vacuum: communities that had paid tribute within the Triple Alliance system now had to determine to whom, if anyone, they owed it. Spanish officials quickly moved to fill the vacuum, and Indigenous communities tried to shape how it was filled.

The central point of this section is that the end of the war began rather than finished the problem of rule. The Spaniards held the symbolic capital, but they were few, dependent, legally uncertain, and surrounded by a population that had its own institutions and its own view of what had happened. The rest of the chapter traces how they, their allies, and their subjects began to build something out of this situation.

Section 33.2

Who Would Rule? Cortés, Cabildos, and the Crown

The first political problem was Cortés's own authority. He had no royal commission when he sailed, had defied the governor of Cuba, and had justified his actions through the Villa Rica council, which named him chief justice and captain. In 1520 he had written his Second Letter to argue that his conquests deserved royal approval. The Crown had meanwhile received conflicting reports, including complaints from Velázquez and from followers of Pánfilo de Narváez, and a rival investigation was possible. The fall of Tenochtitlan strengthened Cortés enormously, because a conqueror who delivers a great territory is hard to dismiss, but it did not make him secure.

Cortés's response was to build institutions in his own interest and to describe them as the king's. He organized a Spanish municipal government, a cabildo or town council, for the new capital, and he appointed magistrates, councilmen, and a treasury staff. The cabildo was the standard Castilian form of local government, and it gave the conquerors a legal body through which to claim lands, regulate the town, and petition the Crown. For the settlers, who received house lots and, often, Indigenous tribute, the cabildo was also a vehicle for collective interests, which sometimes aligned with Cortés and sometimes did not.

In 1522 Charles V's government formally recognized Cortés as governor and captain general of New Spain. The recognition was significant. It gave him legal authority that he had lacked and it signaled that the Crown, at least for the moment, preferred to work with the man who controlled the ground. It was not, however, a grant of an independent lordship. Royal officials continued to arrive, the Crown demanded its fifth of the proceeds, and Charles's government remained concerned that a conqueror with a loyal army and control over Indigenous tribute could become a rival power. The fear was not fanciful. Within living memory, Castilian monarchs had worked to reduce the power of the great nobility, and Spanish authorities had seen how conquerors in the Caribbean could become difficult to control.

The result was a durable tension between conqueror power and royal sovereignty. Cortés needed royal recognition to secure his position and to protect his men from legal challenge, while the Crown needed the conqueror to maintain order and produce revenue until it could replace him with its own officers. Each side pursued its interest by cooperating and distrusting at once. This pattern, in which the Crown rewarded conquest but then moved to reassert supervision, recurs through the conquest of the Americas, including the Andean case studied in Chapters 35 and 36.

Students should not treat the 1522 appointment as the beginning of a stable colonial state. It was a provisional arrangement, and the following years show how quickly it broke down. Cortés's absences, rivalries among officials, and the spread of encomienda all strained it, as the later sections describe.

Section 33.3

Rebuilding Mexico City on Tenochtitlan

One of the most consequential decisions of the postwar period was to rebuild the capital on the island rather than to abandon it. Cortés could have chosen a lakeshore site such as Coyoacán, which was drier and healthier, or Texcoco, or Tlaxcala. He chose Tenochtitlan. In his Third Letter he gave reasons that combined the city's renown, its position at the center of the lake system and of the networks of tribute and trade, and its symbolic value. Spanish authors also suggested that to build elsewhere would have conceded the continuing prestige of the defeated capital. A new Spanish city on the ruins of the old made a political statement and had a practical logic: the roads, causeways, canals, and supply networks all led there.

The work required a large Indigenous labor force. Debris had to be cleared, canals dredged and restored, causeways repaired, and building materials gathered. Spanish accounts describe thousands of workers, drawn from allied and subject communities, laboring on the project under the direction of Indigenous officials and Spanish supervisors. Stone from the demolished temples and palaces was reused in foundations and walls. The remains of the Templo Mayor precinct, the central ritual complex of Tenochtitlan, were built over, and Spanish churches and government buildings rose in the area around the principal plaza. The reuse of materials has been confirmed by archaeological work in the historic center, which has found Mexica stone in colonial foundations.

Spanish planners, including the surveyor Alonso García Bravo, laid out a gridded central district, the traza, for Spanish residents, with streets on a regular plan. The grid was not a blank-slate imposition. It followed and reused elements of the preconquest layout, including the principal causeways and canals that organized movement and drainage, and archaeological studies have emphasized this continuity. The Spanish core occupied the central area, while Indigenous residents were increasingly settled in surrounding districts, known as parcialidades, which kept names combining a Christian patron saint with a Nahuatl place name, such as San Juan Moyotlan and Santa María Cuepopan. The Tlatelolco district retained a separate identity and became an important mission center.

Historical Vocabulary
Traza

The traza was the planned central district of a Spanish colonial city, laid out on a regular grid around a main plaza and reserved, in principle, for Spanish residents and for the principal institutions of church and government. In Mexico City it was the area first surveyed after 1521.

The term is useful because it names a Spanish intention. It should not be read as meaning that the city was entirely Spanish. Indigenous people lived and worked in and around the traza from the beginning, entered it daily as laborers and sellers, and continued to occupy the surrounding districts. The traza also reused preconquest roads and waterways, so it was less a new city than a Spanish layer on an existing urban structure.

The new capital was also a political symbol. The ruins of Mexica temples, the construction of a church on the sacred precinct, and the placement of Cortés's own residence near the old palace area told residents and visitors that sovereignty had passed to new hands. The same landscape could be read differently by different observers. For the Spaniards it displayed conquest and the triumph of Christianity. For Indigenous residents it was a familiar city whose stones, canals, and neighborhoods still carried older meanings, and whose shrines and markets did not vanish overnight.

Section 33.4

Encomienda: Rewarding Conquerors and Redirecting Tribute

The conquerors expected payment. Many were in debt, most had received little or no pay, and the treasure recovered at Tenochtitlan was far smaller than they had hoped. The standard Spanish solution, borrowed from the Caribbean and from older Iberian practice, was the encomienda. Cortés distributed grants to his captains and soldiers in 1522, assigning Indigenous communities to individual Spaniards, and the system spread quickly across central Mexico.

Historical Vocabulary
Encomienda

An encomienda was a royal grant, given in practice through the conqueror or governor, that entitled a Spaniard, the encomendero, to receive tribute and sometimes labor from a specified Indigenous community. In return the encomendero was supposed to protect the people, defend the territory, and provide for their instruction in Christianity.

It was not a grant of the land itself, and it did not make the encomendero the owner of the people, who were legally free vassals of the Crown. The distinction was real in law, though in practice the abuses of the system could resemble servitude. In central Mexico, the encomienda usually operated by redirecting tribute that the community already owed through its own rulers. The word itself comes from the Spanish verb meaning to entrust.

For Cortés and his followers, encomiendas were compensation, and they also created a settler class with a stake in the colony. Cortés reserved the largest and richest grants, including many communities near the capital, for himself. The system had political uses: it bound captains to him, rewarded loyalty, and gave the new colony a settler and tribute-receiving elite. Because Tenochtitlan had organized a tribute system, the Spaniards could take it over by redirecting existing obligations. An encomendero did not need to establish a new tax. He needed to ensure that the local ruler delivered what the community had once delivered to the imperial center, now to him.

The system raised immediate questions about law and authority. The Crown had been uneasy about encomienda since the Caribbean experience, where it had accompanied catastrophic demographic decline, and royal instructions in 1523 sought to restrict the practice in New Spain. Cortés, arguing that without encomiendas he could not reward his men or keep the colony populated, effectively ignored the prohibition. The Crown eventually accepted encomienda in modified form, while seeking to limit its size, duration, and the labor demands it permitted. This pattern, in which the conqueror acted and the Crown later regulated, repeated across the Americas.

Abuses were common. Encomenderos demanded more than the customary tribute, used the labor of community members for construction, mining, and household service, and sometimes treated those under their grant as personal property. The ruling elites who stood between the encomendero and the community had to meet the demands. Complaints from missionaries, from royal officials, and from Indigenous communities themselves accumulated, as the Huexotzinco case in the primary-source section below shows. Later chapters, especially Chapters 42 and 43, trace the relationship between encomienda, mining, and estate agriculture. Here the key point is that encomienda was the first major mechanism by which conquest income was extracted from Indigenous communities, and that it depended on pre-existing Indigenous institutions of tribute.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Military Victory to Royal Supervision

Long-term causes

Caribbean precedents of encomienda; Castilian traditions of rewarding military service; the Crown's long effort to limit noble power; and a Triple Alliance tribute system that could be redirected.

Short-term causes

Disappointment over the modest treasure recovered in 1521; Cortés's need to reward followers and allies; and the absence of any royal administration in the conquered territory.

Event or transformation

Cortés distributes encomiendas and organizes government on his own authority, while seeking royal recognition.

Immediate effects

A settler class receives tribute and labor; Indigenous rulers must deliver to Spaniards; abuses and complaints multiply; the Crown sends officials to guard its revenues.

Long-term consequences

The Crown creates audiencias and then a viceroyalty to supervise the colony, and in time restricts encomienda, shifting the colony toward royal tribute and other labor systems.

Historical complexity

Royal supervision was not simply a reaction to Cortés. It also reflected the activism of missionaries and Indigenous litigants, rivalries among Spaniards, and Crown interest in revenue.
Section 33.5

Indigenous Government Did Not Disappear

A common error in older accounts is the assumption that the fall of Tenochtitlan swept away Indigenous government and replaced it with Spanish institutions. In fact, Spanish rule in the first generation could not have functioned without Indigenous rulers, nobles, scribes, tax collectors, translators, and community councils. A few hundred Spaniards, even with allies, could not personally collect tribute from hundreds of communities, supervise labor, or provision a city. They needed local officials who knew who owed what, who could mobilize workers, and whose authority was recognized by their own people.

The basic unit remained the altepetl, the Nahua ethnic polity with its ruling house, territory, subdivisions, and obligations. Cortés and later officials generally confirmed local rulers who accepted Spanish authority, and in some cases installed new ones where dynasties had been destroyed or discredited. Spaniards called these rulers caciques, a Caribbean word, or señores, and later gobernadores when the office was formalized. The labels were Spanish approximations of Nahua titles such as tlatoani. In the capital, Cortés worked through Indigenous officials, including a noble named Tlacotzin who held the office of cihuacoatl and was recognized as head of Tenochtitlan's Indigenous government, and later others. These men organized labor, tribute, and the return of residents under Spanish oversight.

Indigenous government was not unchanged. The Spanish presence reordered authority in several ways. Rulers now owed obedience to a new overlord and could be removed if they displeased him. Dynasties were interrupted by deaths in war and epidemic, and succession disputes opened opportunities for rivals. Titles and jurisdictions were reorganized, and Spaniards sometimes favored allies or collaborators over legitimate heirs. Over the following decades, Spanish-style municipal offices, such as alcaldes and regidores, began to be adopted in Indigenous towns, producing hybrid councils that combined older noble rule with Castilian titles. This is a process that Chapter 40 treats more fully under the heading of Indigenous republics.

Indigenous literacy also began to transform administration. Nahua scribes trained by missionaries quickly learned alphabetic writing and used it to write Nahuatl documents, including wills, land records, and petitions, alongside pictorial records. The skills that allowed Indigenous officials to deal with the new regime were therefore both continuous, since they drew on old accounting and record-keeping traditions, and new, since they included Spanish legal forms and literacy. Nobles who mastered these skills could present themselves to Spanish authorities as useful collaborators and protectors of their communities.

Different elites fared very differently. Some Mexica nobles were killed in the siege or in epidemics, some were executed, some were exiled or lost status, and others adapted successfully, marrying into Spanish families or securing grants. Isabel Moctezuma, a daughter of Moctezuma II, became a significant figure: later traditions connected her with marriages to Cuitláhuac and Cuauhtémoc, but the evidence for those unions is disputed. What is securely documented is that after the conquest she married Spaniards and in 1526 received the encomienda of Tacuba (Tlacopan) and its subject communities, which made her descendants part of the colonial elite. Her case shows that dynastic women could carry rights across the conquest, though under conditions that gave them little freedom of choice. Indigenous elites were thus both subordinated and indispensable, and the early colony rested on their continued participation.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Continuity or Rupture after Conquest?

The question: How much of early New Spain consisted of transformed Indigenous institutions, and how much was a new Spanish creation imposed on a defeated society?

Interpretation A — Substantial continuity beneath a Spanish surface: Historians influenced by the study of Nahuatl-language documents, notably James Lockhart, have argued that Nahua communities preserved many of their own institutions, ways of organizing community life, and concepts for decades after the conquest, adopting Spanish elements selectively and on their own terms. Charles Gibson's earlier work on the Aztecs under Spanish rule likewise showed how heavily colonial administration relied on Indigenous governance and tribute structures. On this view, the colony in its first generations was in many respects an Indigenous world under a Spanish roof.
Interpretation B — Profound rupture and cultural transformation: Other scholars emphasize the scale of violence, demographic collapse, forced labor, and religious assault, and argue that continuity can be exaggerated. Serge Gruzinski, for example, has studied how colonial pressures reshaped Indigenous images, memory, and ways of understanding the world, while others stress that survival of forms did not mean survival of the old social order. On this view, even institutions that kept Nahuatl names operated in a radically altered context of subordination, so that continuity of terminology can mask transformation of meaning.

Evidence: Both views draw on Nahuatl notarial documents, pictorial records, tribute records, missionary accounts, and archaeology. Their disagreement is often about emphasis and time frame. Documents from the later sixteenth century show more mixing than those of the 1520s, which are sparse and mostly Spanish, so conclusions about the first years depend on inference.

Why it matters: If continuity is strong, the colonial state looks like a negotiated, layered arrangement in which Indigenous actors exercised real power. If rupture is strong, it looks more like an imposed regime whose use of Indigenous forms was an instrument of domination. Many historians now hold both: that institutions persisted and were thoroughly transformed.

Section 33.6

Allies after Victory: Tlaxcala, Texcoco, and the Politics of Reward

The Indigenous allies had not fought to become Spanish subjects indistinguishable from defeated enemies. They had calculated that cooperation would advance their own interests, as Chapter 31 argued of Tlaxcala, and after the victory they sought to convert their service into lasting rights. They expected exemption from tribute, recognition of their rulers, grants of territory, honors, and protection from Spanish settlers. How much they obtained varied by community and depended on negotiation with Spanish officials who had their own interests.

Tlaxcala was the most successful. Its rulers, who accepted baptism and Spanish alliance, were able to secure royal recognition of their community's special status. By the mid-1530s Tlaxcala had secured significant royal privileges, including direct Crown status and exemption from encomienda; city titles and heraldic privileges also became important symbols of its special position. Its leaders cultivated the memory of their service as the foundation of those rights. Tlaxcala was largely kept out of the encomienda system, remaining under the Crown, and it was granted certain exemptions and honors, though the extent and durability of these privileges were contested and eroded over time. The Tlaxcalans also produced, around 1552, a pictorial history, the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, which depicted their role in the conquest in ways that supported their claims.

Texcoco's situation was different. It had been a partner in the Triple Alliance, but factional conflict after the death of its ruler Nezahualpilli had divided the dynasty, and one faction, associated with Ixtlilxochitl, had joined the Spaniards early. Texcoco's rulers retained considerable standing, and the city became an important Franciscan center. Its nobles, among them Ixtlilxochitl, who took the baptismal name Fernando, worked to preserve their rank, and their descendants later wrote histories emphasizing their ancestors' alliance with the Spaniards. Other allies, including the Huexotzincas, Cholulans, and Totonacs, pursued similar strategies with varying results.

The Crown and colonists recognized allied claims selectively. The Crown found it useful to honor the allies, since they were a counterweight to rebellious settlers and a source of stability, and the need for allied troops continued in later campaigns, including those described in Chapter 34, where Mexica, Tlaxcalan, and other Nahua auxiliaries accompanied Spanish expeditions into Central America. Settlers, however, resented exemptions that removed Indigenous communities from encomienda, and local officials often violated allied privileges. The privileges that survived were those that communities were able to defend through petitions, lawsuits, and the well-timed presentation of their record of service.

The allied claims illustrate a broader point: memory of the conquest was a political resource. Communities produced genealogies, maps, pictorial histories, and testimony that presented their ancestors as loyal participants in a legitimate Christian war. These documents are valuable evidence, but they were written to win legal and political advantages and often idealized the alliance. The same communities that celebrated their conquest service also suffered from tribute, labor demands, epidemic, and loss of autonomy. Alliance did not exempt them from the demographic and economic pressures of colonization.

Section 33.7

Defeated Elites, Cuauhtémoc, and the Problem of Resistance

The defeated rulers remained politically dangerous because defeat had not erased their legitimacy. Cuauhtémoc retained prestige among Nahua communities, and the Spaniards treated him accordingly, as a prisoner whose presence they needed and whose potential as a rallying figure they feared. Spanish accounts report that he and the lord of Tlacopan were tortured, their feet burned, in an effort to make them reveal hidden treasure. The reports derive from Spanish sources, which differ about Cortés's responsibility, and they are consistent with the conquerors' frustration over the small treasure recovered. The episode illustrates how conquerors' greed and insecurity combined.

When Cortés led an expedition to Honduras in 1524, he took Cuauhtémoc and other Indigenous lords with him, apparently to prevent them from raising rebellion in his absence. In February 1525, in the region of Acalan, Cortés ordered Cuauhtémoc and the lord of Tlacopan executed, claiming that they had plotted to kill the Spaniards. Whether such a plot existed is doubtful and has long been debated. The killing removed the most prominent surviving Mexica leader and was both a demonstration of Spanish power and an illustration of how much Spaniards feared the continuing authority of the old elite. The Honduras expedition itself belongs to Chapter 34 and is not narrated here.

Resistance after 1521 was broader than the fate of the elite. It took many forms, some visible to Spanish records and many not. Communities fled to the hills or to other jurisdictions to avoid labor and tribute. Individuals and towns delayed or underdelivered tribute, hid people and goods from counts, and relied on local officials to shield them. Ritual practices continued in private or in forms that outwardly resembled Christian practice. Occasionally resistance became violent: Spanish settlers and officials were attacked in some places, and Spanish punitive responses, including executions and enslavement of captives, escalated the cycle. Many of these actions appear in the records only when Spaniards complained or punished them.

Resistance and accommodation should not be treated as opposing identities. The same noble who collaborated in tribute collection might protect his community by shielding refugees; a town that litigated against its encomendero was using Spanish law to defend itself; a commoner who attended mass might also maintain older rituals. People moved between strategies according to what each situation allowed. The image of an Indigenous population divided into loyal collaborators and defiant rebels fits the evidence poorly. The longer history of such strategies, including litigation, flight, and cultural persistence, belongs to Chapter 47. What matters here is that Spanish rule was contested from its first years.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Building New Spain, 1521–1535

A Spanish encomendero: He had risked his life and expected reward. Tribute and labor were his compensation, and he saw Crown restrictions as ingratitude. His own security and wealth depended on Indigenous intermediaries he could not fully control.

A Franciscan missionary: He saw an enormous opportunity for conversion and often criticized encomenderos whose demands undermined it, while also supporting the destruction of Indigenous temples and images.
A Tlaxcalan ruler: He had made an alliance to secure autonomy and now worked to turn that service into recognized privilege, accepting Christianity and Spanish forms as tools for defending the community.
A surviving Mexica or Tlatelolca noble: He had lost a capital, relatives, and rank, and he needed to decide whether collaboration, flight, or quiet resistance would best preserve his family and community.
An Indigenous commoner supplying labor and tribute: He or she bore the daily burden of clearing, building, carrying, and paying while disease and displacement disrupted the household. Such people rarely appear as authors in the sources, though litigation and pictorial records sometimes preserve their complaints.
Section 33.8

Evangelization Begins: Missionaries, Language, and Indigenous Christianity

Spanish claims to the Americas rested officially on the obligation to spread Christianity, and the Crown encouraged missionary work in the new territory. A few clergy accompanied the conquerors, but systematic evangelization began with the mendicant orders, religious orders that lived from alms and emphasized preaching. Three Flemish Franciscans, including the lay brother Pedro de Gante, arrived around 1523. In 1524 a group known as the Twelve Franciscans, led by Martín de Valencia, reached Mexico, chosen in conscious imitation of the twelve apostles. Dominicans followed in 1526 and Augustinians in 1533. The orders established houses at Mexico, Texcoco, Tlaxcala, Huexotzinco, and elsewhere, often in or near major Indigenous centers.

Language learning was central. Few missionaries spoke Nahuatl at first, and they depended on Indigenous interpreters and assistants. Over time friars studied the language, composed grammars and catechisms, and trained Indigenous youths, usually sons of nobles, as teachers, translators, and assistants. Pedro de Gante's school for Indigenous boys, established first at Texcoco and then in Mexico, taught reading, music, and crafts and prepared students to help in instruction. These Indigenous collaborators were essential to evangelization, and some of them later became important writers and scribes. The later development of mission education belongs to Chapter 46.

Missionary methods combined persuasion, instruction, and coercion. Friars destroyed or repurposed Indigenous temples and images, often building churches on the sites of sacred precincts, and they sought to suppress practices such as human sacrifice and some festivals. They baptized in large numbers. Motolinía and others claimed baptisms in the hundreds of thousands, and such figures are almost certainly inflated and tell us more about missionary enthusiasm than about conviction. Baptism was often performed with limited instruction, and many people accepted it for reasons that included pressure, curiosity, the prestige of the new religion, and the desire to avoid punishment. Real religious change took much longer.

Indigenous people responded in diverse ways. Some nobles embraced baptism quickly, partly because affiliation with the Spaniards could protect rank and property. Some communities incorporated Christian saints into existing patterns of local devotion, so that a patron saint might become identified with the community's identity in the way an older tutelary deity had been. Some people continued older practices secretly, and others combined elements. The surviving sources, written largely by friars, tend to exaggerate the completeness of conversion or, when condemning backsliding, to exaggerate its extent. The interpretive point is that conversion was a long process of coercion, teaching, negotiation, reinterpretation, and cultural creativity rather than an instantaneous replacement of one religion by another in 1521.

Missionaries were not uniformly allies of the conquerors. Many criticized encomenderos, and Bishop Juan de Zumárraga, who arrived in 1528 and was named protector of Indians, clashed bitterly with the First Audiencia. Their criticisms helped shape royal policy. At the same time, missionaries benefited from the colonial order and participated in it, and their criticisms were usually directed at abuses rather than at the legitimacy of Spanish rule. The mission, in short, was both an instrument of colonization and a source of pressure within it.

Section 33.9

Demographic Disruption after the Siege

Chapter 29 explained the epidemiology of Atlantic contact. This section considers what population loss did to the building of the colony. Scholars disagree widely about the size of central Mexico's population before 1519. Older estimates by Sherburne Cook and Woodrow Borah ran above twenty million for the central region, while others have proposed much lower figures, in the range of several million. There is more agreement about the trajectory: central Mexico's Indigenous population fell by a very large proportion over the sixteenth century, with a low point in the early seventeenth century. Smallpox in 1520 was followed by further epidemics, including measles in the early 1530s, and by repeated waves later in the century.

Disease was not the only cause. War deaths, starvation during and after the siege, displacement, the disruption of agriculture, forced labor, enslavement, and the stress of tribute demands interacted with epidemics to raise mortality. Famine followed the siege in some places, since fields went unplanted and food was requisitioned. The simultaneous demands of Spanish construction, tribute, and porterage on a weakened population could be lethal, especially for those who carried heavy loads over long distances. Historians therefore stress that the catastrophe resulted from a combination of biological and human causes.

Population loss altered how the colony worked. The labor supply on which encomienda and urban reconstruction depended shrank, while the demands of Spaniards continued or grew, so that each surviving worker was asked to do more. Tribute assessments based on earlier populations became heavier per person as communities declined. Settlements consolidated or were abandoned, and officials later moved survivors into larger towns, a process that belongs to later decades. Households lost members, leaving widows, orphans, and elderly people without support. Inheritance of land and office became uncertain when heirs died, producing disputes and opportunities for Spaniards and for rival nobles.

Demographic loss also affected the missionary effort. Friars described baptizing the dying and burying large numbers of dead. The loss of elders and specialists weakened the transmission of ritual and craft knowledge, and some missionaries interpreted epidemics as divine judgment, which could reinforce conversion. At the same time, the loss of population weakened the communities that were expected to build churches and support clergy.

Finally, demographic collapse shaped Spanish attitudes. Some officials and clergy saw in it a threat to the colony's economic base and argued for protection of Indigenous workers, while many settlers responded by seeking alternative labor, including enslaved Africans, whose importation expanded in later decades, and enslaved Indigenous people, whom the Crown gradually restricted. Population decline was therefore both a human disaster and a force that pushed the colony toward new labor systems, themes that Chapters 42, 43, and 44 develop.

Section 33.10

Ordinary People: Labor, Tribute, Households, and Reconstruction

The history of the first colonial decade is often told through Cortés, officials, and friars, yet the colony existed because of the daily work of ordinary people. Who cleared the ruins? Who carried the stone? Who rebuilt causeways and canals, homes, churches, markets, and Spanish residences? Who grew and delivered the food that fed a growing capital? The answers are overwhelmingly Indigenous. Teams of workers, drawn from Indigenous communities and mobilized through local officials, encomienda obligations, tribute demands, and adaptations of older community labor practices, did much of the physical work of reconstruction. (The formal rotating labor draft later known as repartimiento or cuatéquitl belongs to the later sixteenth century, though it had Indigenous antecedents.)

Tribute in the early years was paid in goods that Indigenous communities already produced: maize, beans, cotton cloth, turkeys, cacao, and other items, together with labor. Tribute was delivered through Indigenous collectors, and the burden depended on how much was demanded and on how many people remained to pay. The pictorial tribute records produced later in the sixteenth century show the range of goods and the careful accounting that Indigenous officials maintained. In the early years the demands of encomenderos, the royal treasury, and the city's building campaign overlapped, and communities sometimes paid several claimants at once.

Women's work was central and is often invisible in the sources. Women ground maize, prepared tortillas to feed laborers, wove cotton cloth that formed much of the tribute, and sold goods in markets. The market of Tlatelolco, famous before the conquest, revived under Spanish rule, and women remained prominent as sellers of food, cloth, and household goods. Women also bore the strain of war and epidemic: many were widowed, many lost children, and many were taken as servants or sexual partners by Spanish men, sometimes through coerced arrangements that left their children in an ambiguous legal and social position. The first generation of mestizo children was born in these conditions. Malintzin herself bore Cortés a son, Martín, around 1522–1523 and later married the Spaniard Juan Jaramillo, a reminder that her role as interpreter was bound to a personal life shaped by the conquerors.

Spaniards in the capital were few, and their survival depended on Indigenous supply. Chinampa farmers in the southern lakes continued to produce vegetables and flowers for the city. Canoe traffic carried goods across the lake. Indigenous artisans, including stonecutters, carpenters, featherworkers, and painters, adapted their skills to Spanish demands, producing church decoration and household goods. This adaptation is one of the most striking features of the early colony: Indigenous craft traditions were redirected to new patrons and in some cases flourished under them.

Not all labor was free. Enslavement of Indigenous people for war captives or for tribute defaults occurred in the early years, and Cortés branded captives in the early period, a practice the Crown eventually restricted. Enslaved Africans were present in small numbers, as personal servants and workers, even in this early period, a presence that expanded dramatically later. Social life in the capital thus brought together Indigenous commoners, nobles, Spanish soldiers and officials, enslaved people, and the earliest generation of mixed-descent children, in a setting where legal status and treatment varied enormously.

Primary Source Window

Cortés's Account of Rebuilding and the Huexotzinco Codex of 1531

Creators: Hernán Cortés (Third and Fourth Letters); Nahua officials and painters of Huexotzinco (Huexotzinco Codex) · Dates: Cortés wrote in 1522 and 1524; the Huexotzinco documents date to 1531 · Type: A conqueror's report to the Crown and Indigenous pictorial records incorporated into a Spanish-language legal dossier

Context: Cortés wrote to Charles V to justify his governance and to present rebuilding as evidence of orderly conquest. The Huexotzinco documents were produced in the context of a legal complaint by the allied community of Huexotzinco about tribute and labor demands made in the period of the First Audiencia, and Indigenous pictorial sheets recording goods and services became evidence in a Spanish legal dossier. Nahua witnesses testified in Nahuatl through interpreters, and their testimony was recorded in Spanish.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: Cortés reports that the city is being rebuilt, that the Indigenous population is returning, and that Spanish residents are settling, presenting these developments as proof of successful and peaceful governance. The Huexotzinco documents record, in painted form, the quantities of goods, labor, and services that the community delivered to Spanish officials, and they present these demands as a burden that exceeded what was lawful or customary.

Analysis: The two sources were created for opposite purposes. Cortés's letters present a ruler's success and are silent about much of the cost to Indigenous workers. The Huexotzinco documents show Indigenous officials using a Spanish legal process, and their own accounting traditions, to make a claim against Spanish authority. Together they show how the same events looked from the position of a conqueror seeking royal favor and from that of a community seeking protection. The Huexotzinco material also shows that a former ally, a community that had contributed to the conquest, could be exploited under early colonial rule, and that it responded by litigation. The sources do not allow historians to reconstruct the full scale of tribute or the experience of ordinary individuals, and the surviving Huexotzinco record reflects the decisions of officials about what to present.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households among the Ruins

Rebuilding households was as important as rebuilding the capital. Families returned to the island over months and years, rebuilt houses with adobe and salvaged stone, and reestablished neighborhood ties. Many lived with fewer relatives than before, since war and epidemic had killed spouses, children, and elders. Widows took on economic responsibilities, and orphaned children were taken in by relatives, sent to friars' schools, or absorbed into Spanish households as servants.

Tribute and labor demands came on top of this recovery. Households owed cloth, food, and workers to several claimants, and women spun and wove much of the cloth paid as tribute. Religious life changed more slowly than the official accounts imply: baptism and church attendance were added to household and community practices, and local saints were incorporated into neighborhood devotion. Everyday records of this period are sparse, and what survives is largely through the eyes of Spaniards or of later Indigenous writers, so the details of household experience must be inferred cautiously.

Section 33.11

From Conqueror Government to Royal Government

Cortés's personal regime did not become the permanent constitution of New Spain. His absence on the Honduras expedition from 1524 to 1526 exposed its weakness. The royal treasury officials who had arrived in 1524, including Alonso de Estrada, Rodrigo de Albornoz, Gonzalo de Salazar, and Peralmíndez Chirinos, quarreled with one another and with the officials Cortés had left in charge. Rumors that Cortés was dead led to attempts to seize his property and his encomiendas, and factional violence disrupted the capital. The Crown, alerted by complaints, sent Luis Ponce de León in 1526 to conduct a residencia, a formal judicial review of an official's conduct. Ponce de León died soon after arriving, and the confusion continued.

Cortés returned to Mexico in 1526 and went to Spain in 1528. He was received at court, confirmed in important honors, and named Marqués del Valle de Oaxaca, with extensive estates and vassals, but the Crown did not restore him as governor. This outcome shows the Crown's strategy: it honored and enriched the conqueror but moved political authority to its own appointees. Cortés remained a powerful landholder and returned to Mexico in 1530, continuing to dispute with royal officials for years.

Historical Vocabulary
Audiencia

An audiencia was a high court that also exercised governing functions in a Spanish territory. Its judges, oidores, heard appeals, advised the executive, and could investigate officials. The first audiencia of New Spain was created in 1527 and began functioning in 1528. A viceroy was a royal appointee who represented the king and governed a large territory, usually presiding over an audiencia.

These institutions replaced rule by a conqueror with rule by crown officials who were appointed from Spain and could be removed. Their mature form, including the powers of audiencias, viceroys, and cabildos, is the subject of Chapter 40. This chapter treats only their origins.

The Crown created the first audiencia in New Spain in 1527 and it began functioning in 1528 under Nuño de Guzmán, a ruthless official who had governed Pánuco, together with several judges. Its record was disastrous. The audiencia's members enriched themselves, seized encomiendas, persecuted Cortés's supporters, and imposed heavy demands on Indigenous communities. Bishop Zumárraga denounced them and was threatened in return. The Huexotzinco litigation of 1531 arose partly from the demands of this period. Guzmán also led a violent campaign into western Mexico, creating Nueva Galicia, an expedition notorious for enslavement and killing.

The Crown, receiving complaints, replaced the First Audiencia. The Second Audiencia, which arrived in early 1531 under the presidency of Sebastián Ramírez de Fuenleal, included the jurist Vasco de Quiroga, who later founded Indigenous hospital-communities. The Second Audiencia sought to restrain abuses, investigate the First, limit enslavement of Indigenous people, regulate tribute, and encourage the formation of settled Indigenous towns with municipal governments. It was more successful than the first, though conflicts with encomenderos and with Cortés continued, and many reforms were only partly implemented.

In 1535 Antonio de Mendoza arrived as first viceroy of New Spain, bringing with him the authority of the Crown and a mandate to govern the territory and supervise the audiencia. Mendoza's arrival is a convenient endpoint for the chapter because it marks a turn toward more regular administration. It did not end conflict or complete the colony, but it indicated the Crown's determination to govern New Spain through its own officials rather than through a conqueror. The period 1521 to 1535 can thus be seen as one in which Spanish authority passed through three phases: the improvised rule of a victorious commander, the contested transition of rival royal officials, and the first regularized royal institutions.

Section 33.12

New Spain Was Being Built, Not Completed

By the mid-1530s a recognizable colonial order existed in central Mexico. It had a rebuilt capital with Spanish institutions, a viceroy and high court, an encomienda and tribute system, missionary houses and churches, and a growing Spanish settler population. These were real achievements for the colonizers, and they set patterns that endured for centuries.

The order was also incomplete and contested. Indigenous government remained essential and in many places strong. Former allies continued to negotiate privileges, with uneven success. Conversion was uneven and often superficial, and Indigenous religious practice persisted in many forms. Demographic catastrophe continued, undermining the colony's labor base and reshaping its priorities. Spanish control was strongest in the central valleys and weakened sharply with distance from the capital. In the north, west, and south, Indigenous peoples remained independent or only loosely subordinated.

The central claim of this chapter is that the fall of Tenochtitlan did not automatically create New Spain. The colony was made over fifteen years by the interaction of conquerors, Crown officials, missionaries, Indigenous rulers, former allies, and surviving communities, and it depended on the very Indigenous institutions that it subordinated. Spanish authority was negotiated, contested, and incomplete, and Indigenous people were participants in its construction, though under conditions of violence and unequal power.

The final point leads directly to Chapter 34. The existence of a colonial capital in Mexico did not mean that Mesoamerica and Central America had been conquered. Guatemala, Yucatán, and other regions were already undergoing, and would continue to require, prolonged campaigns, Indigenous alliances, settlements, revolts, negotiated accommodations, and frontier warfare, in which Nahua and other auxiliaries from the central valleys would play a conspicuous part.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the fall of Tenochtitlan on 13 August 1521 created problems of government more than it solved them. The Spaniards and their Indigenous allies had destroyed a capital but possessed no functioning colonial state. Between 1521 and the arrival of the first viceroy in 1535, a series of actors struggled to turn military victory into government, tribute, labor, religion, and urban order.

The first political problem was authority. Cortés had begun without secure royal sanction, and he built a municipal government and distributed rewards in his own interest while seeking recognition. In 1522 Charles V recognized him as governor and captain general, but the Crown remained wary of allowing a conqueror to become an independent lord. The tension between conqueror power and royal sovereignty shaped the following decade.

The decision to rebuild the capital on the site of Tenochtitlan reflected both symbolic and practical considerations. The new city reused causeways, canals, stones, and labor, and it was organized into a Spanish traza surrounded by Indigenous districts. Continuity of infrastructure and labor lay beneath the new political and religious landscape.

Encomienda rewarded conquerors by redirecting existing tribute and labor obligations to Spanish beneficiaries. It was a grant of rights to tribute and labor, not of land or ownership of people, but its abuses were frequent. The Crown disliked the system while accepting it in modified form, and Indigenous communities used litigation to resist its excesses.

Spanish rule depended on Indigenous governments. Altepetl institutions, noble rulers, scribes, and tax collectors made colonial administration possible, even as Spanish supremacy reorganized authority and hybrid offices appeared. Different elites fared very differently, and dynastic women such as Isabel Moctezuma carried rights across the conquest under constrained conditions.

Allies such as the Tlaxcalans, Texcocans, and Huexotzincas had sought to turn their service into rights. Tlaxcala was the most successful, securing royal recognition and a special status, though privileges proved contestable. Memory of alliance became a political resource, expressed in pictorial histories, genealogies, and petitions.

Defeated elites remained dangerous because defeat did not erase Indigenous legitimacy. Cuauhtémoc's torture and execution illustrate Spanish insecurity. Resistance took many forms, from flight and underpayment of tribute to ritual persistence and occasional violence, and it coexisted with accommodation as part of a repertoire of strategies.

Evangelization began with the arrival of Franciscans in 1523 and 1524, followed by Dominicans and Augustinians. Language learning, Indigenous assistants, destruction of temples, mass baptism, and the incorporation of saints marked the early process. Conversion was a long negotiation rather than an event, and missionaries both served and criticized the colonial order.

Demographic collapse, driven by epidemics together with war, famine, displacement, and labor demands, shaped every aspect of the new colony. It reduced the labor supply, intensified the burden per worker, disrupted households and inheritance, and pushed the colony toward new labor systems. Ordinary Indigenous people, including women who produced food and cloth and sold goods in markets, bore the daily cost of reconstruction.

Royal government grew out of the failures of conqueror rule. The Honduras expedition, factional conflict, the residencia, the abusive First Audiencia, the reforming Second Audiencia, and finally the arrival of Viceroy Mendoza in 1535 mark a movement toward regular administration. The result was an incomplete colonial order: a capital, a bureaucracy, and a tribute system, built on and dependent upon Indigenous institutions, with weak control beyond the central valleys. This is the situation Chapter 34 inherits as it turns to the conquest of Guatemala, Yucatán, and Central America.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Encomienda
A grant of the right to receive tribute and labor from an Indigenous community, with obligations of protection and Christian instruction; not a grant of land or of ownership of people.
Encomendero
The Spaniard holding an encomienda.
Altepetl
The Nahua ethnic polity with ruling house, territory, and obligations, which survived as the basic unit of local government.
Cabildo
A Castilian municipal council, the basic form of Spanish local government.
Traza
The planned central grid of a Spanish colonial city, reserved for Spanish residents and principal institutions.
Parcialidad
A district or subdivision of an Indigenous community or city, such as those surrounding the traza of Mexico City.
Audiencia
A high court with governing functions, first established in New Spain in 1527–1528.
Viceroy
The king's personal representative governing a large territory; Antonio de Mendoza was the first in New Spain.
Residencia
A formal review of an official's conduct at the end of his term or in response to complaints.
Capitán general
A military and administrative title held by Cortés as governor from 1522.
Royal fifth (quinto real)
The Crown's customary one-fifth share of the proceeds of conquest and mining.
Tribute
Regular payments of goods or labor owed by communities to rulers, redirected after 1521 to Spaniards and the Crown.
Tlatoani
The ruler of an altepetl; Spaniards translated the title as king or cacique.
Cacique
A Caribbean word adopted by Spaniards for Indigenous rulers and local nobles.
Gobernador
A Spanish-created office of Indigenous governor, held by local nobles under Spanish oversight.
Cihuacoatl
A high Mexica official, second to the tlatoani, whose title continued under Spanish supervision in the early years.
Mendicant orders
Religious orders, here Franciscans, Dominicans, and Augustinians, that lived from alms and led early evangelization.
Doctrina
A mission parish or the instruction in Christian doctrine given to Indigenous converts.
Evangelization
The effort to convert Indigenous people to Christianity, involving teaching, coercion, and negotiation.
Tamemes
Indigenous porters who carried loads, a labor burden throughout the early colony.
Nahuatl
The language of the Mexica and many central Mexican peoples, which became a written language in the colonial period.
Huexotzinco Codex
Indigenous pictorial sheets of 1531 recording tribute and labor demands, incorporated into a Spanish-language legal dossier with translated Nahua testimony.
Lienzo de Tlaxcala
A pictorial history made around 1552 presenting Tlaxcala's role in the conquest, used to support its claims.
Mestizo
A person of mixed Indigenous and Spanish descent; the first generation was born in the early postwar years.
Sovereignty
Supreme political authority, contested between Cortés and the Crown.
Legitimacy
The perceived rightful basis of authority, which both conquerors and Indigenous rulers sought to claim.
Accommodation
Strategic adaptation to colonial rule, which could coexist with resistance.
Conquest versus colonization
Related but distinct processes: military defeat of existing authority versus the creation of long-term institutions of extraction and rule.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that conquest and colonization are different things. The Spaniards and their allies won a war in 1521, but the colony they hoped to build had to be assembled over years through negotiation, coercion, and improvisation, and it was assembled largely by the people it subordinated. Indigenous rulers, scribes, laborers, farmers, and market women kept the capital fed and built, and Indigenous institutions carried Spanish tribute and administration. That dependence did not make the colony benign. It made it possible, and it is the reason colonial rule was shaped by Indigenous choices as well as by Spanish intentions.

The chapter also reveals recurring patterns. The Crown rewarded conquerors and then moved to supervise them. Conquerors turned to encomienda to pay their followers. Missionaries became both agents of colonization and critics of its abuses. Indigenous communities turned memory, pictorial records, and legal forms into instruments of survival. These patterns, which appear in Mexico in the 1520s, recur in the Andes, in Central America, and in other regions studied in Volume II.

Chapter 34 turns to the regions where conquest was far from complete. In Guatemala, Yucatán, and the wider Central American frontier, Spaniards and Indigenous auxiliaries, including many from the central valleys, fought prolonged campaigns against Maya and other peoples who resisted, negotiated, and adapted. Students should carry forward the lesson of this chapter: the construction of a colonial capital did not conquer a region, and the history of Spanish power in the Americas is as much a history of limits and negotiation as of military triumph.
