West Africa, Iberia, and the Early Atlantic Slave Trade

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: An Ocean Already in Motion

Long before any Iberian ship dropped anchor off the coast of West Africa, the peoples who lived along that coast, in its river valleys, and across its inland savannas and forests were already merchants, farmers, rulers, scholars, and soldiers embedded in commercial and political networks that stretched across the Sahara to North Africa and the Mediterranean. Portuguese mariners did not sail into an empty or timeless "Africa." They sailed into a densely populated, politically organized, and commercially sophisticated world — and it was the meeting of that world with an expanding Iberian maritime economy that produced, gradually and unevenly across the fifteenth century, the beginnings of what would become the Atlantic slave trade.

Chapter 25 examined Iberia on the eve of American expansion: a peninsula shaped by centuries of the Reconquista, dynastic consolidation, Mediterranean and Atlantic commerce, and the gradual acquisition of navigational and colonial experience in the Canary Islands and Madeira. This chapter turns to the other side of the emerging Atlantic system, a side too often reduced in popular narratives to a passive coastline awaiting European arrival. West Africa in the fifteenth century contained large states with sophisticated administrations, prosperous trading cities linked to trans-Saharan caravan routes, Muslim scholarly communities producing law and literature, gold-producing regions that had drawn North African and European interest for centuries, and diverse systems of slavery, pawnship, and dependency that long predated any Portuguese ship's arrival.

It is tempting, when writing the history of the Atlantic slave trade, to begin the story with Portuguese caravels and to treat everything that follows as something Europeans simply did to Africa. That version of the story is not wrong to emphasize European initiative, capital, shipping, and demand — all of these mattered enormously, and this chapter does not minimize them. But it is incomplete, and its incompleteness matters. African states regulated the terms on which foreign merchants could trade. African rulers and merchants supplied, withheld, negotiated, and profited from commercial relationships with Portuguese factors. African societies had their own long histories of slavery and captivity, shaped by war, debt, and social hierarchy, that differed in important ways from the racialized, hereditary, plantation-based system that would eventually dominate the Americas. Understanding how those two worlds — an already dynamic West Africa and an expanding Iberian Atlantic economy — interacted during the fifteenth century is essential to understanding how the transatlantic slave trade began, and essential to avoiding the common error of treating that trade as though it sprang into being, fully formed, the moment Columbus reached the Caribbean.

This chapter traces that interaction across roughly six decades, from the early Portuguese voyages along the West African coast in the 1430s and 1440s through the founding of the fortified trading post of São Jorge da Mina, or Elmina, in 1482, and the sugar economy of Madeira already discussed in Chapter 25, while also looking forward briefly to the plantation economy that began taking shape on the Atlantic island of São Tomé in the years immediately following Columbus's voyage. It examines how Portuguese sailors, initially inclined toward raiding and captive-taking, increasingly found that regulated commerce with African political authorities produced more reliable and more profitable results. It examines the gold that drew Portuguese attention at least as insistently as captives did, the ivory, textiles, and other goods that moved along African and Atlantic trade routes, and the religious and legal frameworks — Christian, Muslim, and African — that different parties used to make sense of the relationships they were building. It also looks ahead, briefly, to how quickly these already-assembled Atlantic practices could be recombined into a full sugar-and-slavery plantation economy on São Tomé within a few years of Columbus's voyage.

This chapter also asks students to resist two temptations that recur throughout the study of Atlantic slavery. The first temptation is to treat African societies as though slavery there were simply an older, smaller version of the system that would later dominate Brazilian and Caribbean plantations — as though Europeans merely discovered a preexisting institution and exported it westward unchanged. The evidence does not support this. The second temptation is to treat the earliest decades of Portuguese-African contact as though they already exhibited the massive scale, rigid racial ideology, and hereditary chattel status that characterized later centuries. The evidence does not support this either. What this chapter describes is a formative period: a system being assembled, piece by piece, out of African institutions, Iberian ambitions, island plantation experiments, and the gradually hardening commercial and legal habits that later generations would inherit and dramatically expand.

By the chapter's end, students should understand that when Christopher Columbus sailed west in 1492 — the subject of Chapter 27 — he sailed into an Atlantic world already transformed by nearly sixty years of Portuguese-African commerce, already marked by the forced transportation of thousands of enslaved Africans to Iberia and to the sugar plantations of Madeira, and already equipped with the commercial, legal, and institutional tools that, within a few years, would be applied to found a still larger plantation economy on São Tomé and, soon afterward, on a vastly larger scale in the Americas. The history of Latin America's colonial slave systems does not begin with the arrival of the first enslaved Africans in the Caribbean or Brazil. It begins here, on the West African coast and in the counting houses of Lisbon, decades earlier.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political, economic, and religious diversity of West African societies on the eve of sustained Portuguese contact.
  2. Explain the commercial importance of gold, salt, textiles, and trans-Saharan trade routes to West African states and to Portuguese interest in the region.
  3. Distinguish African institutions of slavery, pawnship, and dependency from the racialized, hereditary chattel slavery that later developed in the Americas.
  4. Trace the early Portuguese voyages along the West African coast and their gradual shift from raiding toward negotiated commerce.
  5. Analyze the role of African rulers and merchants in shaping the terms, volume, and conduct of early Atlantic trade.
  6. Explain the significance of São Jorge da Mina (Elmina), founded in 1482, for Portuguese commercial and political strategy.
  7. Describe the role of Atlantic islands, particularly Cape Verde and São Tomé, in connecting African, Iberian, and eventually American commercial networks.
  8. Assess the early relationship between sugar cultivation, coerced African labor, and the plantation systems later transplanted to the Americas.
  9. Evaluate primary source evidence, including Portuguese chronicle accounts, with attention to authorship, purpose, and bias.
  10. Explain why the fifteenth-century Atlantic slave trade should be understood as a formative and comparatively limited system rather than a preview of its later, far larger scale.
  11. Connect the developments examined in this chapter to the Atlantic world Columbus encountered in 1492, examined in Chapter 27.

Historical Context

Chapter 25 established that Iberia entered the Atlantic age already possessing centuries of Mediterranean commercial experience, a tradition of crusading ideology inherited from the Reconquista, an emerging royal interest in overseas expansion, and practical colonial experience gained in the Canary Islands and Madeira. That chapter's central lesson was that 1492 did not mark the beginning of Iberian expansion but rather one further step in a process already well underway. This chapter's central lesson is closely related but faces in a different direction: West Africa, too, did not await European arrival as an undeveloped or static world. It was home to some of the most sophisticated commercial and political systems in the fifteenth-century Atlantic-facing world.

West Africa's geography shaped its political and economic diversity. The Sahara Desert, far from being simply a barrier, functioned as a great commercial highway connecting North Africa and the Mediterranean world to the Sahel — the semi-arid belt of grassland immediately south of the desert — and, through the Sahel, to the savanna and forest zones further south. Camel caravans had carried gold, salt, textiles, enslaved people, and other goods across the Sahara for centuries before Portuguese ships reached the Atlantic coast, producing wealthy trading cities, powerful states, and a commercial infrastructure that Portuguese merchants would eventually seek to divert toward the coast rather than create from nothing.

Students should also understand that "West Africa" in this period was not one society, one government, one religion, or one economic system. It included large imperial states exercising authority over vast territories and diverse subject populations; smaller kingdoms and city-states organized around particular trades, rivers, or ports; decentralized societies without centralized kingship in which political authority rested with lineage heads, councils, or ritual specialists; Muslim merchant and scholarly communities linked to a wider Islamic world; and non-Muslim religious traditions practiced by the majority of the region's population well into and beyond the period this chapter examines. This diversity matters because it shaped how different African communities encountered, negotiated with, resisted, or profited from Portuguese commercial expansion in different ways.

Slavery, too, existed in West Africa before Portuguese contact, but it existed as one part of a much broader set of institutions governing labor, dependency, and social status, institutions that varied considerably across the region's many societies. Understanding these institutions on their own terms, rather than assuming they were simply a smaller-scale preview of Brazilian or Caribbean plantation slavery, is essential to understanding how the Atlantic slave trade actually developed rather than how it is sometimes imagined to have developed.

Section 26.1

West Africa before Portuguese Atlantic Expansion

Just as Chapter 1 warned against treating "Latin America" as a timeless category and Chapter 24 warned against reducing the pre-conquest Indigenous Americas to a single undifferentiated world, this chapter opens with a parallel warning: West Africa did not enter world history when Portuguese ships appeared offshore in the 1430s and 1440s. By that time, the region had already experienced many centuries of state formation, agricultural development, urbanization, long-distance commerce, and religious and intellectual exchange.

West Africa's geography produced enormous internal diversity. Along the southern edge of the Sahara lay the Sahel, a semi-arid belt of grassland and scrubland suited to grain cultivation, pastoralism, and, crucially, to the caravan traffic that connected the region to North Africa. South of the Sahel lay the savanna, a more fertile zone supporting denser agricultural populations and some of the region's largest states and cities. Further south still lay the forest zone, stretching to the Atlantic coast, home to societies organized differently from the Sahelian and savanna states — often smaller in scale, frequently without centralized kingship, and adapted to environments where cavalry, so important to Sahelian warfare and statecraft, was far less effective because of the tsetse fly and dense vegetation.

This ecological diversity translated into political diversity. Some West African peoples lived under powerful, centralized monarchies exercising authority over large territories and diverse subject populations. Others lived in smaller kingdoms oriented around particular trades, ports, or river systems. Still others organized political life without centralized kingship at all, vesting authority in lineage elders, councils of household heads, age-grade associations, or ritual and religious specialists. No single political model describes fifteenth-century West Africa any more than a single political model describes fifteenth-century Europe or the fifteenth-century Andes.

Economically, West Africa in this period was neither isolated nor undeveloped. Agricultural communities cultivated sorghum, millet, rice, yams, and other staple crops adapted to local conditions. Skilled artisans worked iron, textiles, leather, and precious metals. Fishing communities along the coast and major rivers supplied protein and participated in regional exchange networks. Long-distance merchants, some of them organized into influential trading diasporas, moved goods across enormous distances, linking the Atlantic-facing forest zone to the Sahel and, through the Sahel, to North Africa and the wider Mediterranean and Islamic worlds. It was this preexisting commercial infrastructure, not an empty or passive landscape, that Portuguese mariners encountered when their ships began working their way down the West African coast in the second quarter of the fifteenth century.

Section 26.2

States, Cities, and Regional Networks

By the early fifteenth century, the Mali Empire, centered on the upper Niger River and long celebrated for its wealth in gold, had passed the height of its territorial power but continued to exercise substantial regional influence and to anchor important trading cities. Mali's fame had spread well beyond West Africa: the emperor Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 and 1325, during which he reportedly distributed and spent such quantities of gold that its market value in Cairo was affected for years afterward, had made Mali's wealth a subject of discussion across the Mediterranean and Islamic worlds — and, eventually, on European maps, some of which depicted a West African ruler holding a large piece of gold decades before any European ship reached the region.

As Mali's influence receded during the fifteenth century, the Songhai state, centered at Gao on the Niger River, grew increasingly powerful. Under the ruler Sonni ʿAli, Songhai conquered the great trading and scholarly city of Timbuktu in the late 1460s and later brought Djenné under its authority as well, becoming the dominant political force in the region and expanding its territorial reach and administrative sophistication considerably. Timbuktu and Djenné functioned as major commercial and intellectual centers, home to merchants, scholars, and substantial libraries of Arabic-language manuscripts, and their incorporation into the Songhai state gave that empire control over some of the most important nodes in the trans-Saharan trading system.

Along the Atlantic-facing coast and its river systems — the regions that would matter most directly to early Portuguese contact — a different set of political communities predominated. Senegambia, the region around the Senegal and Gambia rivers, was home to Wolof-, Serer-, and Mande-speaking states and communities engaged in agriculture, fishing, and regional commerce. Further south, the region historians generally call Upper Guinea encompassed a mosaic of smaller-scale societies, including Mande-affiliated trading communities that had extended commercial networks toward the coast from the interior. Still further south and east, the forest and coastal societies of the region that would become known to Europeans as the Gold Coast were organized among Akan-speaking peoples whose control of gold-bearing land and forest resources would soon draw sustained Portuguese commercial interest, examined further in Section 26.9.

Students should resist imagining a simple hierarchy in which large Sahelian empires such as Mali and Songhai were "advanced" while smaller coastal and forest societies were not. Political scale does not equal political or economic sophistication. Coastal and forest societies developed their own effective institutions for managing land, labor, trade, and conflict, suited to their particular environments and historical circumstances, and it was these coastal and near-coastal societies, rather than the interior Sahelian empires, that Portuguese mariners encountered most directly and that shaped the earliest decades of Atlantic commerce.

Section 26.3

Gold, Salt, Textiles, Food, and Long-Distance Commerce

Understanding what drew Portuguese mariners to the West African coast requires understanding what West Africa already produced and traded. Gold stood foremost among these goods. West African gold, mined and panned in several regions, including the goldfields associated with Akan territory near the forest-savanna boundary, had supplied significant portions of the gold circulating in North African and, indirectly, Mediterranean and European markets for centuries before any Portuguese ship reached the coast directly. Trans-Saharan caravan routes, worked by camel and organized by experienced Saharan and Sahelian merchants, carried this gold northward in exchange for salt, mined at Saharan sites such as Taghaza, along with cloth, horses, and other goods moving in the opposite direction.

Salt was, in its own right, an enormously valuable commodity in societies where it was not locally available, sometimes said to be worth its weight in gold in certain interior markets far from Saharan salt deposits. Textiles — woven cotton cloth produced in West African workshops — circulated as both practical goods and, in many contexts, as a recognized medium of exchange or store of value. Livestock, grain, kola nuts (a stimulant widely consumed and traded across the region), leather goods, and iron implements moved along regional and long-distance routes alongside gold and salt, sustaining a commercial system considerably older and, in several respects, more extensive than anything Portuguese merchants initially brought to the coast.

This context matters enormously for understanding Portuguese motivation. Portuguese royal and commercial interest in West Africa during the fifteenth century was driven at least as much by the hope of accessing this existing gold trade — diverting some portion of the gold that otherwise flowed north across the Sahara toward Mediterranean markets that Portugal did not fully control — as it was driven by interest in captives. Slave trading developed alongside, and initially on a considerably smaller commercial scale than, the pursuit of gold, a point developed further in Sections 26.6 through 26.9. The Atlantic slave trade did not begin because West African economies lacked other valuable forms of commerce; it began within, and initially as a secondary feature of, a commercial relationship substantially organized around gold, textiles, and other goods.

Section 26.4

Islam and the Religious Diversity of West Africa

Islam had spread into West Africa gradually over several centuries before the fifteenth century, carried initially by North African and Saharan Muslim merchants and subsequently embraced, to varying degrees, by West African rulers, urban populations, and long-distance trading communities. Muslim merchant networks, often organized along lines of shared language, kinship, and religious affiliation, played a central role in trans-Saharan commerce, and cities such as Timbuktu and Djenné became renowned centers of Islamic scholarship, law, and manuscript production, with mosques, schools, and substantial libraries supporting a literate scholarly culture connected to the wider Islamic world stretching from North Africa to Arabia.

It would be a serious error, however, to describe West Africa in this period as simply an Islamic region. Islam's influence was strongest among ruling elites, urban merchant communities, and populations along major trade routes, particularly in the Sahel and savanna zones. Across much of the forest zone and along significant stretches of the Atlantic coast — the regions most directly relevant to early Portuguese contact — the majority of the population practiced a wide range of other religious traditions, involving ancestor veneration, nature and spirit worship, divination, and ritual specialists whose authority operated independently of, and in some regions alongside, Islamic institutions. Many West African political systems, including some with substantial Muslim populations, incorporated religious pluralism as a normal feature of governance rather than enforcing strict religious uniformity.

This religious diversity mattered for how different African communities encountered Portuguese Christians. Some Muslim West African merchants and rulers were already familiar with foreign traders professing a different religion, having conducted business with North African Muslim, and occasionally with non-Muslim, merchants for generations. Non-Muslim coastal and forest societies encountered Portuguese Christian mariners as one more outside commercial and religious tradition among several already present in their world, rather than as an entirely unprecedented phenomenon. Portuguese chroniclers, for their part, frequently invoked crusading and missionary language to describe African societies, sometimes distinguishing "Moors" (a term Portuguese writers used broadly, and often imprecisely, for Muslims) from other African peoples they described in various other, often derogatory, terms — language that students must read critically rather than treat as an accurate ethnographic description, a theme developed further in the Primary Source Window below.

Section 26.5

Slavery and Dependency before the Atlantic Trade

No section of this chapter requires more careful historical distinction than this one. Various forms of slavery, pawnship, and dependency existed across West African societies well before sustained Portuguese contact, and understanding these institutions accurately — neither minimizing them nor collapsing them into the very different system that later developed in the Americas — is essential to understanding how the Atlantic slave trade actually began.

West African societies practiced several distinct, though sometimes overlapping, forms of coerced or dependent labor and status. War captivity produced enslaved people when one polity defeated another, a practice common across many world regions in this period, including Europe. Judicial enslavement could result from certain offenses under some legal systems. Debt-related dependency, often described using the term pawnship, allowed a person — frequently a family member offered as security — to work for a creditor until a debt was repaid, a status that, unlike much later American chattel slavery, was in principle temporary and did not necessarily extend to a pawn's own children. Household slavery incorporated enslaved individuals, in varying degrees across different societies, into kinship and household structures, sometimes permitting eventual social incorporation, marriage, or the improvement of status for an enslaved person's descendants, particularly across the second or third generation.

These institutions varied enormously across West Africa's many societies, and no single description applies uniformly to all of them. In some contexts, enslaved status was rigid and permanent; in others, meaningful pathways toward incorporation, manumission, or improved status for descendants existed. In some societies, enslaved people performed agricultural, domestic, or military labor within relatively small-scale households; in others, they labored in larger productive enterprises, including gold mining and salt production, tied more directly to long-distance commerce.

It is essential that students not conclude from this discussion that African slavery was somehow equivalent to, or a smaller preview of, the plantation chattel slavery that later developed in Brazil, the Caribbean, and elsewhere in the Americas. African systems did include forms of slavery involving sale, property-like status, hereditary transmission in some contexts, and productive labor, and West African slavery should not be minimized or romanticized as uniformly mild. But several differences from the mature Atlantic system deserve emphasis. First, the Atlantic system was overwhelmingly and rigidly hereditary: the enslaved status of a mother generally determined the status of her children in perpetuity, a rule not universally present in West African systems, many of which allowed for eventual incorporation of descendants. Second, the Atlantic system became explicitly and systematically racialized, associating enslaveability with African ancestry as a matter of law and ideology — a development that occurred gradually in the Americas over the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries rather than existing as a preexisting African category. Third, the scale and purpose of American plantation slavery, oriented toward the mass production of export commodities such as sugar for distant markets, differed enormously from the household, military, and smaller-scale commercial contexts in which most African slavery operated before sustained Atlantic contact. The mature, racialized, hereditary Atlantic chattel system was historically produced through the specific circumstances of American and early Atlantic-island colonization, discussed further in Section 26.11 and, at much greater length, in Part X of this volume — it was not simply exported intact from Africa, though it drew on African institutions as one of its raw materials.

At the same time, the existence of preexisting African institutions of captivity and dependency did matter to how the Atlantic trade developed. These institutions provided legal and social categories, and in some cases existing commercial infrastructure, through which African rulers and merchants could supply captives to Portuguese traders, a subject examined further in Section 26.8. Historians must hold both truths together: African institutions of slavery and dependency were real, consequential, and relevant to the trade's origins, while also not being equivalent to the racialized, hereditary plantation system the Atlantic trade later helped produce.

Historical Vocabulary
Pawnship and Dependency

Pawnship refers to a system, documented across many West African societies, in which a person — often a relative of a debtor — worked for a creditor as security against an unpaid debt. Unlike chattel slavery, pawnship was, in principle, a temporary status: the pawn's obligation ended when the debt was repaid, and the pawn's children were not automatically bound by the same arrangement, though practices varied and a pawnship could, in some circumstances, become prolonged or difficult to end.

Historians use "dependency" as a broader umbrella term for the range of West African statuses — including pawnship, various forms of household or war-captive slavery, clientship, and other relationships — that placed an individual under another person's authority or claim on their labor, without assuming that all such relationships were identical to one another or to later American chattel slavery. Precision about which specific institution is under discussion, rather than a single undifferentiated reference to "African slavery," is essential to accurate historical analysis.

Section 26.6

Portugal Reaches the West African Coast

Chapter 25 traced Portuguese maritime development under royal patronage, including the sponsorship associated with Prince Henrique, often called Henry the Navigator, and the technical, navigational, and institutional groundwork that made sustained Atlantic voyaging possible. This chapter picks up that story at the point where Portuguese ships began working their way down the West African coast in a sustained, rather than sporadic, fashion.

For years, Portuguese navigators had regarded Cape Bojador, on the Saharan coast of present-day Western Sahara, as a formidable psychological and practical barrier, associated in sailors' lore with dangerous currents, shoals, and the fear that ships venturing beyond it might never return. In 1434, the Portuguese mariner Gil Eanes successfully rounded Cape Bojador and returned safely, a voyage that, while modest in itself, carried substantial symbolic and practical importance: it demonstrated that ships could sail beyond the cape and return, encouraging further, increasingly ambitious voyages southward along the coast in the years that followed.

These early voyages combined several motives that Portuguese sponsors and mariners did not always clearly distinguish from one another. Exploration and cartographic knowledge were valuable in their own right, expanding Portuguese and, eventually, wider European understanding of African coastal geography. Commerce, particularly the hope of accessing West African gold, motivated crown and mercantile interest alike. Crusading and missionary ideology, discussed in Chapter 25 as a legacy of the Reconquista, framed some Portuguese expansion as a continuation of a religious struggle against non-Christian peoples, a framework Portuguese chroniclers frequently invoked to justify raiding, captive-taking, and eventual conquest or subjugation of non-Christian populations, however imprecisely and inconsistently that framework was actually applied to the diverse peoples Portuguese mariners encountered. Direct raiding for captives and portable wealth constituted a further, and initially significant, motive, examined in the following section.

These motives coexisted uneasily, and Portuguese conduct along the coast during the 1430s and 1440s reflected that lack of a single coherent strategy. Some voyages combined reconnaissance with opportunistic raiding; others attempted, with mixed success, to establish peaceful trading contact. As Portuguese mariners gained more experience with African coastal societies — their military capacities, their commercial interests, and the practical risks and limitations of raiding a well-populated and often well-defended coastline — royal and mercantile strategy gradually, though unevenly, shifted toward the negotiated commercial relationships examined in Section 26.8.

Section 26.7

Raiding, Captivity, and the Beginnings of Atlantic Slave Trading

The documented beginning of Portuguese Atlantic slave trading is generally traced to a series of expeditions in the early 1440s along the Saharan coast, in the vicinity of what Portuguese sources called the Rio de Ouro. Captains including Antão Gonçalves and Nuno Tristão led voyages during this period that resulted in the capture of small numbers of local inhabitants, who were transported back to Portugal. These early captures appear to have combined raiding — direct seizure of individuals encountered along the coast — with the beginnings of exchange, as some captives were reportedly obtained through negotiated ransom or trade with local inhabitants seeking the return of relatives.

A larger and more extensively documented episode occurred in 1444, when a group of several hundred African captives, transported to Portugal by ships associated with Prince Henrique's sponsorship, was sold at the southern Portuguese port of Lagos. This event is described in unusual detail by the Portuguese royal chronicler Gomes Eanes de Zurara, whose account, examined closely in the Primary Source Window below, provides historians with one of the earliest and most extensively analyzed documentary sources for the beginning of Portuguese Atlantic slave trading — while also requiring careful critical reading, since Zurara wrote roughly a decade after the 1444 events he described, as a royal chronicler explicitly celebrating Prince Henrique's patronage, and in language saturated with Christian justificatory rhetoric.

It is essential that students not treat these early 1440s episodes as evidence of an already fully organized transatlantic slave-trading system comparable to the later, vastly larger and more institutionalized trade of the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries. The captives taken and transported during this initial period numbered in the hundreds, not in the hundreds of thousands or millions that later centuries would see. Transportation in this period moved captives to Portugal itself and, somewhat later, to Atlantic island plantations, not yet to the Americas, which had not yet been reached by Columbus. The institutional, legal, and logistical apparatus that would later characterize the mature Atlantic trade — including specialized shipping, established commercial routes, standardized contracts, and eventually direct transatlantic transport on a massive scale — did not yet exist. What existed, in the early 1440s, was a formative, still largely improvised practice of raiding and captive-taking that was, within a relatively short period, substantially reshaped by the practical and commercial considerations examined in the next section.

Direct Portuguese raiding along the coast also encountered real limits and real resistance. African coastal communities were not defenseless, and Portuguese ships attempting to seize captives directly faced organized resistance, including armed defense by local inhabitants who inflicted casualties on Portuguese raiding parties on multiple documented occasions. These practical limits, more than any change of moral conviction, appear to have been central to the gradual Portuguese shift away from raiding and toward the negotiated commercial relationships examined next.

Section 26.8

From Raiding to Trade with African Rulers and Merchants

During the 1440s, 1450s, and 1460s, Portuguese conduct along the West African coast shifted substantially, though unevenly and never completely, away from direct raiding and toward commerce conducted with the cooperation, regulation, and active participation of African political authorities and merchants. This shift reflected practical necessity as much as any change in Portuguese intention: raiding was dangerous, produced relatively small and unpredictable numbers of captives, provoked coastal resistance and retaliation, and damaged the possibility of the more valuable and sustained gold trade that Portuguese sponsors increasingly prioritized.

African rulers along the coasts Portuguese ships increasingly frequented held real and substantial power over the terms of this emerging commerce. They controlled the territory through which trade routes ran and the ports at which ships might anchor. They determined which foreign merchants would be permitted to trade, on what terms, at which locations, and subject to what duties, taxes, or tribute. They supplied, negotiated the price of, and could withhold access to gold, captives, ivory, and other goods sought by Portuguese traders. They conducted diplomacy with Portuguese factors and, on occasion, with the Portuguese crown directly, sending and receiving embassies and exchanging gifts in patterns consistent with established African diplomatic practice. Several African rulers manipulated Portuguese commercial dependence to their own advantage, playing Portuguese traders against rival European or African commercial partners where possible, adjusting the terms of access to gold or captives according to their own political and economic priorities, and, in some cases, converting to Christianity or adopting elements of Portuguese court culture as instruments of diplomacy rather than as evidence of straightforward religious transformation.

This reality should correct a common and misleading textbook simplification: the notion that Portuguese ships, by virtue of possessing oceangoing vessels and firearms, held unrestricted power over African coastal societies from the outset. They did not. Portuguese firearms technology in this period offered real but limited military advantages, particularly at sea, and Portuguese forces along the coast typically operated in small numbers, dependent on African cooperation for provisions, information, and access to interior trade networks they could not penetrate independently. Where negotiation failed or African authorities refused access, Portuguese traders generally could not simply impose their will by force, a reality reflected in the coastal resistance described in Section 26.7 and in the diplomatic, rather than purely coercive, character of most documented fifteenth-century Portuguese-African commercial relationships.

At the same time, this emphasis on African political agency must not be mistaken for an argument that African participation somehow diminishes European responsibility for the resulting trade in enslaved human beings. Agency and coercion coexisted. African rulers who supplied captives to Portuguese traders were, in most documented cases, providing individuals who had themselves been captured through warfare, judicial enslavement, raiding or kidnapping, and other coercive processes within African political and legal systems — including, in some systems, enslavement or seizure associated with unresolved debt, a status distinct from the more limited and in principle temporary institution of pawnship discussed in Section 26.5 — people who had not consented to being enslaved or transported, whatever the negotiated terms between African rulers and Portuguese merchants might have been. The growing European demand for captives, backed by Portuguese capital, shipping, and eventually by expanding American plantation markets, exerted profound pressure on African societies over subsequent centuries, a pressure examined in later chapters as the trade's scale expanded dramatically beyond anything visible in the period this chapter covers.

Section 26.9

Elmina, Gold, and the Institutionalization of Atlantic Commerce

The clearest institutional expression of the shift from raiding toward regulated commerce came in 1482, when Portugal established a fortified trading post, São Jorge da Mina — generally known to history as Elmina — on the Gold Coast, in the territory of local Akan political authorities. Elmina's foundation illustrates several important features of mature fifteenth-century Portuguese-African commerce.

First, Elmina was established with the negotiated consent, and under conditions set in significant part, by the local Akan ruler, rather than through unilateral Portuguese conquest of the surrounding territory. Portuguese sources describe negotiations, an agreed annual payment or tribute to local authorities in exchange for the right to construct and maintain the fort, and an ongoing relationship in which Portuguese access to trade depended on continued African cooperation. Second, Elmina functioned primarily as a feitoria, or fortified trading factory — a secure commercial and storage installation intended to protect goods, personnel, and trade relationships, rather than as the seat of territorial governance over a surrounding African population. Portuguese authority at Elmina extended, in practice, only to the fort itself and its immediate vicinity, not to the broader Akan territories with which Portuguese traders conducted business. Third, Elmina's chief commercial purpose was gold, not primarily captives; the fort's name itself, meaning "the mine," reflects Portuguese priorities. Substantial quantities of Akan gold flowed through Elmina to Portugal over subsequent decades, financing further Portuguese expansion and enriching the crown considerably.

Elmina also became a node in an increasingly complex regional commercial system, one in which Portuguese traders exchanged European and North African goods — cloth, metal goods, and, notably, enslaved people obtained elsewhere along the coast, particularly from the Bight of Benin — for Akan gold, illustrating that the emerging Atlantic trade already, by the late fifteenth century, involved multiple African regions and commodities linked together through Portuguese shipping rather than a single simple bilateral exchange between "Europe" and "Africa." A Portuguese royal monopoly system governed this trade, restricting private commerce with the region in favor of crown-licensed or crown-controlled ventures, an arrangement that generated substantial royal revenue and that later Iberian institutions, examined in Part IX of this volume, would extend and adapt to American colonial administration.

Section 26.10

Cape Verde, Atlantic Islands, and New Commercial Networks

Chapter 25 introduced the Canary Islands and Madeira as sites of early Iberian Atlantic colonization, examined chiefly from the perspective of Iberian expansion itself. This chapter now examines how Atlantic islands functioned as connective tissue binding Europe, Africa, and, eventually, the Americas into a single interacting commercial system.

Portuguese settlement of the Cape Verde islands, beginning in 1462, illustrates this connective function especially clearly. Positioned in the Atlantic several hundred miles off the Senegambian and Upper Guinea coasts, Cape Verde occupied a strategically valuable location: close enough to the African mainland to serve as a base for provisioning, trade, and communication with Senegambian and Upper Guinean commercial partners, yet also positioned along Atlantic wind and current patterns that would later prove important for transatlantic navigation. Cape Verdean settlers, merchants, and, over time, a growing population of mixed African and Portuguese ancestry developed close commercial relationships with mainland African trading partners, in some cases functioning as intermediaries whose linguistic and cultural familiarity with both Portuguese and West African commercial practices facilitated exchange.

Cape Verde's development illustrates an important broader pattern: the fifteenth-century Atlantic was becoming an interconnected commercial geography, linking Iberia, Atlantic islands, and West Africa through regular shipping, established trade goods, and increasingly routine commercial relationships, rather than remaining a set of separate regions connected only by occasional and exceptional voyages. Students should understand this emerging Atlantic geography as the direct predecessor of the still-larger Atlantic system that would develop after 1492, examined in Section 26.12 and throughout the remainder of this volume.

Section 26.11

Sugar, São Tomé, and the Developing Plantation Economy

Chapter 25 traced sugar cultivation's introduction to Madeira, financed by Genoese and Portuguese capital and worked, from an early date, by a combination of free, indentured, and increasingly enslaved labor, including captives brought from the West African coast once Portuguese trading contact made that supply available. This chapter's task is not to repeat that narrative but to look ahead, briefly and deliberately, to how the elements already assembled by 1492 — Atlantic island colonization, sugar cultivation, and a functioning trade in enslaved Africans — were about to be recombined, in the years immediately following Columbus's voyage, into a still more heavily enslaved plantation economy on the Gulf of Guinea island of São Tomé.

Portuguese navigators reached the previously uninhabited island of São Tomé in the 1470s, and a royal charter issued in 1485 formally recognized the island's potential for sugar cultivation. Effective, sustained colonization, however, did not begin until 1493 — the year after Columbus's first voyage — when the donatary captain Álvaro de Caminha took up residence on the island with a substantial body of settlers, many of them transported under coercive or penal circumstances themselves. Enslaved Africans began to be brought to the island in significant numbers from around 1495, drawn from the nearby mainland coast, a proximity that made São Tomé's plantations considerably more dependent on enslaved African labor than Madeira's more mixed labor force had been. Sugarcane fields are securely documented on the island by 1506, and by 1517 two sugar mills were in operation, with plans already underway for the construction of several more. São Tomé's major sugar-and-slavery plantation economy, in other words, developed primarily in the years and decades immediately following 1492, not before it.

Once established, São Tomé's plantation system displayed several features that anticipated the much larger system that would later dominate Brazil and the Caribbean: substantial capital investment in land clearance, milling equipment, and processing infrastructure; a labor force composed predominantly of enslaved Africans rather than free, indentured, or mixed labor; production oriented toward distant European markets rather than local subsistence; and a colonial administrative and legal framework designed to support and protect this commercial arrangement. At the same time, students must not treat early sixteenth-century São Tomé as simply Brazil or Barbados in miniature. The scale of São Tomé's plantations remained far smaller than what would later develop in Brazil alone. The island's population, both enslaved and free, remained modest by later American standards. The elaborate racial ideology that would eventually justify and rationalize American chattel slavery as a matter of law and doctrine had not yet fully developed. And the massive, centuries-long, multi-million-person transatlantic trade that would eventually connect West and Central Africa to plantation economies across the Americas had not yet come into being.

What São Tomé demonstrates, rather, is how rapidly the components already assembled before 1492 — capital, colonial administration, Atlantic shipping, sugar-cultivation expertise from Madeira, and an increasingly regular trade in enslaved Africans along the nearby coast — could be recombined, within a very few years, into a functioning plantation economy considerably closer in character to the later American system than anything that had existed on Atlantic islands before Columbus's voyage. This distinction matters considerably for how students should understand the chronology of Atlantic slavery: São Tomé's plantation complex was not a pre-1492 precedent that Columbus's voyage merely repeated in the Americas; it was itself a product of the same expanding Atlantic system that Columbus's voyage extended, developing on a parallel and only slightly later timeline, discussed further in Section 26.12.

Section 26.12

An Atlantic System before Columbus

By 1492, the Atlantic world that Christopher Columbus was about to enter, examined in detail in Chapter 27, already possessed several defining features that this chapter has traced. West African societies, diverse in political organization, religion, and economic activity, had for centuries participated in extensive regional and trans-Saharan commercial networks well before Portuguese ships arrived, and continued to shape the terms of their engagement with Portuguese traders throughout the fifteenth century. Portuguese ships had established regular, though still evolving, commercial relationships along significant stretches of the West African coast, moving from early raiding toward negotiated trade conducted substantially on terms African rulers and merchants helped establish. Thousands of African men, women, and children had already been forcibly captured, transported, and enslaved — first to Portugal itself, and increasingly to the sugar economy of Madeira — years and, in some cases, decades before any European reached the Caribbean. African and Portuguese merchants participated in a commercial system substantially organized around gold, formalized through institutions such as the fortified trading post at Elmina and the Portuguese royal monopoly system. Cape Verde had already begun functioning as a commercial and demographic bridge linking Iberia to Senegambia and Upper Guinea. São Tomé, by contrast, had been reached by Portuguese navigators and its agricultural potential formally recognized by royal charter in 1485, but its major sugar-and-slavery plantation economy had not yet developed by 1492; that transformation followed rapidly in the years immediately afterward, discussed in Section 26.11, showing how quickly the commercial, legal, and coercive practices already assembled elsewhere in the Atlantic could be recombined into a still more heavily enslaved plantation system once resources and settlers were committed to the task.

Understanding this prior history transforms how students should read Chapter 27's account of Columbus's voyages and the earliest Caribbean encounters. Columbus did not initiate an Atlantic slave trade or an Atlantic plantation economy; he extended an already functioning, though still developing, Atlantic system into a vast new geography, carrying with him institutional, legal, commercial, and coercive practices that Portuguese merchants, sailors, settlers, and the Portuguese crown had spent nearly six decades developing along the West African coast and on Atlantic islands such as Madeira and Cape Verde — practices that were, at almost precisely the same moment, also producing the rapidly developing plantation economy of São Tomé. The history of slavery in colonial Latin America, examined at length in Part IX and, especially, Part X of this volume, has its true origins not in 1492 but in the West African coastal societies, Iberian commercial ambitions, and Atlantic island economies this chapter has examined.

Primary Source Window

Gomes Eanes de Zurara on the Lagos Captives, 1444

Creator: Gomes Eanes de Zurara, Portuguese royal chronicler · Date composed: Zurara's chronicle of Prince Henrique's activities was composed in the 1450s, roughly a decade after the events it describes · Type: Royal chronicle

Context: Zurara was commissioned by the Portuguese crown to chronicle Prince Henrique's sponsorship of Atlantic exploration and to celebrate his patronage in terms consistent with chivalric and crusading ideals. His account of the 1444 sale of several hundred African captives at Lagos is among the most detailed contemporary descriptions of an early Atlantic slave-trading event, describing the captives' arrival, their distribution among purchasers, and the emotional distress of families separated in the process.

Rather than reproducing Zurara's language verbatim, students should understand the chronicle's central claims in summary form: Zurara described the captives' suffering with apparent sympathy while simultaneously presenting their enslavement as ultimately beneficial, on the grounds that it would expose them to Christianity and salvation — a justificatory religious framework entirely characteristic of Portuguese chronicle writing in this period, and one that later Iberian slaveholding societies would continue to invoke in modified forms for centuries.

Analysis: Zurara's account is invaluable precisely because of, not despite, its bias. As a royal chronicler writing to celebrate Prince Henrique's patronage, Zurara had strong reasons to present Portuguese conduct favorably, to minimize African suffering even while describing it, and to frame enslavement within a Christian providential narrative that served Portuguese royal and religious interests. Students must therefore read Zurara's chronicle as evidence of Portuguese self-justification and ideology as much as evidence of the captives' actual experience, which the chronicle filters through a perspective sympathetic to their captors.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a royal chronicler have included details of the captives' suffering even while ultimately justifying their enslavement?
  2. What does Zurara's account reveal about the religious and ideological frameworks Portuguese writers used to justify slave trading in this period?
  3. What aspects of the captives' own perspectives and experiences does this source not, and likely cannot, reveal?
  4. Why must historians distinguish between what a source like this can establish about Portuguese attitudes and what it can establish about the actual lived experience of the enslaved?
Historical Debate

Did Europeans Create the Atlantic Slave Trade, or Enter Existing Systems of Slavery?

The question: Given that various forms of slavery and dependency already existed in West Africa before Portuguese contact, to what extent should historians describe the Atlantic slave trade as a European creation, and to what extent should they describe it as European participation in preexisting African institutions?

Interpretation A — Emphasis on preexisting African institutions: This view stresses that African societies already practiced various forms of slavery, captivity, and dependency, and that African rulers and merchants actively supplied, negotiated, and profited from the early Atlantic trade using existing legal and social categories. Proponents argue that treating Europeans as the trade's sole originators risks erasing African political and economic agency.
Interpretation B — Emphasis on European transformation of scale and character: This view stresses that European capital, shipping, demand, and eventually plantation markets transformed slavery's scale, purpose, and character almost beyond recognition — from small-scale, often non-hereditary household and military institutions into a massive, hereditary, racialized, export-oriented system. Proponents argue that emphasizing African "agency" without equal emphasis on this transformation risks minimizing European responsibility for the trade's eventual scale and brutality.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — Portuguese chronicles, African oral and documentary traditions, and comparative studies of African and American slave systems — but differ in how much causal and moral weight to place on preexisting African institutions versus European commercial and colonial transformation.

Why it matters: This textbook treats both emphases as necessary rather than competing. African institutions of slavery and dependency were real and relevant to how the trade began; European capital, demand, and colonial expansion were what transformed those institutions' scale and character into something categorically different and vastly more destructive. Neither emphasis alone gives an accurate account.

Student task: Using the evidence from Sections 26.5, 26.7, and 26.8, explain why reducing this question to a single cause — either "Africans enslaved Africans" or "Europeans created slavery" — misrepresents the historical evidence, and identify what each interpretation correctly emphasizes.

Multiple Perspectives

Five Vantage Points on the Early Atlantic Trade

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across evidence. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of an Akan ruler negotiating access to Elmina: Portuguese traders offered valuable goods, including cloth and metal wares, in exchange for gold, and permitting a fortified trading post on favorable, negotiated terms could strengthen a ruler's commercial position relative to regional rivals, provided that Portuguese activity remained confined to trade and did not threaten territorial control.
From the perspective of a Senegambian or Upper Guinean merchant: Portuguese ships represented one more commercial partner among existing regional and trans-Saharan trading relationships, offering new goods and new markets, though the growing demand for captives introduced troubling new pressures on regional stability and security.
From the perspective of a captive transported to Lisbon or São Tomé: Forced separation from family, community, and homeland, sale to unfamiliar purchasers, and coerced labor in an unfamiliar environment represented a catastrophic and involuntary rupture, regardless of the commercial or diplomatic arrangements that had produced that person's captivity and sale.
From the perspective of a Portuguese royal factor or merchant: Gold remained the most valuable and desired commodity, but captives, ivory, and other goods supplemented royal and mercantile profit, and maintaining cooperative relationships with African political authorities was essential to sustaining reliable, long-term commercial access.
From the perspective of a sugar planter on São Tomé: Enslaved African labor, transported from the nearby mainland coast, was essential to clearing land, planting and harvesting sugarcane, and operating milling equipment, and expanding the labor force was central to expanding profitable sugar production for export to European markets.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Regional Commerce to Atlantic System

What Changed

  • Portuguese conduct shifted from largely improvised coastal raiding in the 1430s and 1440s toward regulated, negotiated commerce with African rulers and merchants by the 1460s and 1470s
  • Captives were transported first primarily to Portugal and to established Atlantic island economies such as Madeira; São Tomé became an increasingly important destination for enslaved labor only after sustained settlement began in the 1490s
  • Portuguese commercial installations evolved from opportunistic coastal contact into institutionalized trading posts such as Elmina, operating under royal monopoly
  • The scale and organization of coerced labor expanded gradually across the fifteenth century, while São Tomé's heavily enslaved sugar plantation economy developed primarily after 1500, in the years immediately following Columbus's voyage

What Stayed the Same

  • Gold remained a central, and often primary, Portuguese commercial objective throughout the period this chapter examines
  • African political authorities continued to regulate the terms of trade and retained substantial control over their own territories throughout the fifteenth century
  • West African societies' own internal institutions of slavery, dependency, and captivity continued to operate according to their own logics, only some of which fed into Atlantic commerce
  • Portuguese chronicle sources continued to frame slave trading within Christian justificatory and crusading language throughout the period

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the real changes and the underlying continuities helps students avoid two errors: assuming the mature Atlantic slave trade appeared suddenly and completely formed, and assuming that nothing meaningful changed between the 1430s and 1492.

Cause and Effect

Why the Early Atlantic Slave Trade Expanded

Long-term causes

Preexisting West African institutions of slavery, captivity, and dependency; Iberian Mediterranean and Atlantic commercial experience examined in Chapter 25; centuries-old trans-Saharan trade in gold and other goods.

Short-term causes

Portuguese royal sponsorship of coastal voyages after 1434; the practical failure and danger of coastal raiding; growing Portuguese demand for gold and, increasingly, for plantation labor on Atlantic islands.

Effect

A gradually institutionalized commercial system combining gold trade, negotiated African-Portuguese diplomacy, and increasing transportation of enslaved Africans to Portugal and Atlantic island plantations.

Long-term consequences

The commercial, legal, and coercive foundations that would be dramatically expanded after 1492 into the massive transatlantic system connecting West and Central Africa to the plantation economies of Brazil, the Caribbean, and other parts of the Americas, examined in Part X of this volume.

Historical complexity: No single cause — not African institutions alone, not Portuguese ambition alone, not island plantation experimentation alone — adequately explains the trade's origins; each depended on and reinforced the others.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life along the Emerging Atlantic Commercial Frontier

In a Sahelian market town linked to trans-Saharan trade, merchants, camel drivers, scribes, and religious scholars conducted business in a cosmopolitan urban environment shaped by centuries of contact with North Africa and the wider Islamic world. Along the Atlantic coast, fishing and farming communities balanced subsistence activities with growing involvement in regional and, increasingly, Portuguese-linked commerce, as some coastal households found kin or neighbors drawn into the expanding trade in captives. Aboard a Portuguese trading vessel anchored off the coast, factors, interpreters, and sailors negotiated exchanges of cloth, metal goods, and other commodities for gold, ivory, and, increasingly, captives, often relying on African or Cape Verdean intermediaries whose linguistic and commercial knowledge bridged Portuguese and African trading practices. On São Tomé, enslaved laborers cleared forest land, planted and harvested sugarcane, and operated milling equipment under conditions of coerced, often brutal labor, in a small but growing plantation society whose demographic and economic structure anticipated, on a modest scale, the far larger colonial societies examined later in this volume.

Evidence for these varied forms of daily life comes disproportionately from Portuguese sources — chronicles, royal correspondence, and commercial records — rather than from African or enslaved perspectives directly, a limitation historians must acknowledge honestly. Oral traditions, archaeology, and comparative ethnographic and linguistic evidence help partially recover African perspectives that written Portuguese sources rarely convey, but significant gaps in the historical record remain, particularly regarding the direct experience of the earliest generations of enslaved Africans transported across the fifteenth-century Atlantic.

Historical Vocabulary
Feitoria (Trading Factory)

A feitoria was a fortified Portuguese commercial installation, typically combining warehousing, defensive fortification, and residence for royal or mercantile agents (feitores, or factors), established at strategic points along trade routes to secure and regulate commerce. Elmina, founded in 1482, is the most historically significant West African example examined in this chapter, but Portugal established similar installations at other points along the African coast and, later, in Asia.

The feitoria model differed importantly from full territorial colonization: Portuguese authority typically extended only to the fortified installation itself and its immediate surroundings, not to the broader territory or population, which remained under African political control. Understanding this distinction helps students avoid assuming that Portuguese trading posts in Africa functioned the same way that Spanish and Portuguese colonization would later function in the Americas, examined beginning in Chapter 27.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the West African and Iberian foundations of the Atlantic slave trade during the roughly six decades preceding Columbus's first voyage in 1492. It has insisted, as a central organizing principle, that West Africa must be understood as a historical actor in its own right rather than as a passive backdrop to European expansion. Fifteenth-century West Africa contained large imperial states such as Mali and Songhai, smaller kingdoms and city-states, decentralized societies without centralized kingship, Muslim merchant and scholarly communities linked to a wider Islamic world, and diverse non-Muslim religious traditions, all connected through extensive regional and trans-Saharan commercial networks that long predated Portuguese Atlantic contact.

The chapter has examined the commercial goods — gold, salt, textiles, and other commodities — that drew Portuguese attention to the region, emphasizing that gold, not captives, was frequently the primary Portuguese objective. It has carefully distinguished preexisting West African institutions of slavery, pawnship, and dependency from the racialized, hereditary chattel slavery that later developed in the Americas, while also explaining how these African institutions shaped the terms on which the early Atlantic slave trade could develop.

Students have traced the practical history of early Portuguese voyaging along the West African coast, from Gil Eanes's 1434 rounding of Cape Bojador through the documented early 1440s captive-taking expeditions and the 1444 Lagos sale described by the chronicler Gomes Eanes de Zurara. The chapter has emphasized that Portuguese conduct shifted substantially, though unevenly, from raiding toward negotiated commerce with African political authorities and merchants during the following decades, a shift driven by the practical limits and dangers of raiding as much as by any change in Portuguese intention.

The founding of Elmina in 1482 has served as this chapter's clearest illustration of institutionalized, negotiated Atlantic commerce, established with the cooperation of local Akan authorities and oriented primarily toward the gold trade. The chapter has also examined how Atlantic islands, particularly Cape Verde and, from the 1470s, São Tomé, connected African, Iberian, and eventually American commercial networks, and has traced how São Tomé's major sugar-and-slavery plantation economy, though only reached and formally chartered before 1492, developed its full scale rapidly in the years immediately following Columbus's voyage, anticipating important features of the much larger plantation complex that would later develop in Brazil and the Caribbean.

Throughout, the chapter has emphasized that the fifteenth-century Atlantic slave trade should be understood as a formative and still comparatively limited system — one being actively assembled out of African institutions, Portuguese commercial and religious ambitions, and island plantation experimentation — rather than as a preview, on a smaller scale, of the vastly larger and more systematized trade that would develop after 1492. By the time Columbus reached the Caribbean, an interconnected Atlantic commercial world already existed, complete with regularized trade routes, fortified trading posts, plantation labor systems, and thousands of forcibly transported and enslaved Africans, even though the massive scale of later centuries' trade remained still to come.

Chapter 26 has asked how an Atlantic system connecting Africa and Iberia developed before 1492. Chapter 27 turns to the Caribbean encounter itself, examining how Columbus's voyages, Indigenous encounters, and early Spanish settlement extended this already-forming Atlantic system into a new hemisphere.

The West African coastal and near-coastal region south of Senegambia, home to a diverse mosaic of smaller-scale societies engaged in early Atlantic commerce.
Akan
A group of related peoples and states in the forest zone of present-day Ghana and Ivory Coast, controlling significant gold-producing territory central to Portuguese commercial interest.

The European term for the West African coastal region, chiefly Akan territory, associated with gold trade and the site of Elmina.
Pawnship
A West African institution of debt-related dependency in which a person, often a relative of a debtor, worked for a creditor until a debt was repaid; distinct from hereditary chattel slavery.
Household slavery
A form of West African slavery incorporating enslaved individuals into kinship and household structures, sometimes permitting eventual social incorporation or improved status for descendants.
Chattel slavery
A system of slavery treating enslaved people as property, typically hereditary and, in the American context, increasingly racialized; distinct from most preexisting West African institutions of dependency.
Feitoria
A fortified Portuguese trading post or factory, such as Elmina, combining warehousing, defense, and residence for commercial agents.
Cape Bojador
A point on the Saharan Atlantic coast long regarded as a navigational barrier, successfully rounded by Gil Eanes in 1434.
Elmina (São Jorge da Mina)
A fortified Portuguese trading post established in 1482 on the Gold Coast with Akan cooperation, central to Portuguese gold commerce.
Cape Verde
An Atlantic island group settled by Portugal beginning in 1462, strategically positioned between Iberia and Senegambia/Upper Guinea.
São Tomé
A Gulf of Guinea island reached by Portuguese navigators in the 1470s and effectively colonized from 1493; its major sugar plantation economy based on enslaved African labor developed during the early sixteenth century, in the years immediately following Columbus's voyage.
Plantation complex
The combination of capital, land, colonial administration, and coerced labor organized for export commodity production, first assembled on a modest scale on Atlantic islands before its much larger American development.
Royal monopoly
A system in which the Portuguese crown restricted or controlled commercial access to African trade, generating substantial royal revenue.
Chronicle
A narrative historical account, often commissioned by a ruler or institution; Gomes Eanes de Zurara's chronicle is this chapter's primary example.
Racialization
The historical process by which enslaveability became associated with African ancestry as a matter of law and ideology, developing gradually in the Americas rather than existing as a preexisting African category.
Atlantic world
The interconnected commercial, demographic, and cultural system linking Europe, Africa, and, after 1492, the Americas.
Commercial diplomacy
Negotiated relationships between Portuguese traders and African political authorities governing the terms of trade, tribute, and access.
Mansa Musa
A ruler of the Mali Empire whose 1324–1325 pilgrimage to Mecca displayed substantial gold wealth and drew wider Mediterranean attention to West African gold.
Kola nut
A stimulant widely consumed and traded across West Africa, one of many regional commodities alongside gold and salt.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look eastward across the Atlantic before turning westward to the Caribbean, recognizing that the history of colonial Latin American slavery cannot be understood without first understanding the West African societies and Iberian ambitions that produced it. The habits of mind this chapter introduces — taking African political and economic agency seriously, distinguishing African institutions of dependency from American chattel slavery, and tracing gradual institutional change rather than assuming sudden, complete transformation — will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of slavery in Part IX and Part X of this volume.

The Atlantic slave trade did not begin in 1492, and it did not begin as a fully formed system. It began through decades of contact, negotiation, conflict, and gradual institutional development along the West African coast and on Atlantic islands, involving African rulers and merchants as active participants whose power and interests shaped the trade's early terms, alongside Portuguese sailors, settlers, and royal sponsors whose capital, shipping, and demand would, over the following centuries, transform a formative and comparatively limited practice into one of history's largest forced migrations.

Understanding this deep and often overlooked history equips students to read Chapter 27's account of Columbus and the Caribbean encounter with appropriate historical depth: not as the beginning of a new Atlantic world, but as one more chapter, however consequential, in an Atlantic history that had already been unfolding for decades.
