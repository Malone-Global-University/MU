The Conquest of Cusco and the Great Inca Rebellions

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Capital Taken, a Realm Not Conquered

In November 1533 Spanish soldiers entered Cusco as apparent victors. Less than three years later, many of the same men were crowded into the city's central buildings while Inca armies tried to destroy them. In 1572 a Spanish viceroy still judged an autonomous Inca court in the eastern forests dangerous enough to require a full military expedition. The distance between those dates is the subject of this chapter.

Chapter 35 ended with the death of Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1533 and with a question it deliberately left open: did the capture and execution of one claimant to the Inca throne give the invaders control of Tawantinsuyu? This chapter argues that it did not. Spanish entry into Cusco depended on Inca dynastic politics, on the aid of provincial peoples with grievances against the Inca state, and on Indigenous labor and provisioning, at least as much as on Spanish arms. The men who rode into the capital were a few hundred strong. The coalition around them was far larger, and its Andean members had purposes of their own.

The chapter follows one man through most of this story. Manco Inca, a son of Huayna Capac and a member of the Cusco nobility that had backed the defeated Huáscar, at first cooperated with the invaders. He was a ruler seeking to restore his authority after a devastating civil war, and the Spaniards offered military help against his rivals. Spanish demands for gold and labor, the seizure of property and people, the imprisonment of Manco himself, and repeated violence turned that cooperation into war. In 1536 he escaped, summoned allies across a wide region, and launched the great uprising that nearly ended the Spanish presence in the Andes.

The uprising failed to expel the Spaniards, but it did not collapse into defeat either. Indigenous political divisions, the presence of Andean allies inside the Spanish camp, the logistical difficulty of sustaining large armies, and violent quarrels among the Spaniards themselves left neither side able to dominate. Manco withdrew eastward to Vitcos and the Vilcabamba region, where he and his successors maintained a smaller but durable Neo-Inca state. That state fought, negotiated, received missionaries, sheltered Spanish refugees, and preserved Inca royal authority until Viceroy Francisco de Toledo ordered its destruction in 1572. Even then, what ended was one autonomous royal polity. Andean communities, local authorities, and the memory of Inca sovereignty continued.

Words matter in telling this story. Spanish writers called the events of 1536 a rebellion, a term that assumes Spanish rule was already legitimate and established. Inca sources and some modern historians describe them as a war to recover sovereignty. This chapter uses both terms with care and returns to the problem in a Historical Debate box. It also treats the period 1533–1572 as a single, continuing struggle rather than stopping at the siege of 1536, which is where many older accounts leave Manco and where the Inca state seems to vanish from the narrative.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Pedro Pizarro, a cousin of the conqueror, wrote an eyewitness account in 1571. Pedro de Cieza de León gathered testimony from Andean and Spanish informants in the late 1540s and 1550s. Titu Cusi Yupanqui, Manco's son, dictated an account from Vilcabamba in 1570, addressed to Spanish authorities and shaped by his diplomacy. Spanish legal records, letters, and the later testimony of Inca elites add other angles, as do archaeological investigations at Ollantaytambo, Sacsayhuamán, and the Vilcabamba region. Each source had an audience and a purpose. None of them speaks for the thousands of ordinary Andean people who fed, carried, fought, hid, and died in these wars.

The chapter's boundaries are drawn with the outline in mind. It covers 1533 to 1572 and treats the Spanish civil wars only where they changed Manco's options. The mature colonial state belongs to Chapter 40, the mining economy and colonial mit'a to Chapter 42, and the long history of Andean revolt and negotiation to Chapter 47. Toledo appears here only as the destroyer of Vilcabamba. The northern Andes, where conquest took a different course, are the subject of Chapter 37.

  3. Analyze Manco Inca's initial cooperation with the Spaniards as a political strategy rather than as puppetry.

  7. Assess Andean military adaptation at Ollantaytambo and avoid explaining the conquest through European technology alone.
  8. Analyze why the 1536–1537 offensive failed to expel the Spaniards, weighing logistics, Indigenous divisions, and Spanish factional conflict.
  9. Describe the Neo-Inca state of Vitcos and Vilcabamba and explain how negotiation functioned as a form of resistance.
  10. Interpret the sources of Titu Cusi and Spanish participants as evidence shaped by purpose and audience.
  11. Distinguish the destruction of the Neo-Inca state in 1572 from the end of Andean resistance and Inca cultural survival.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part VIII's argument that conquest was prolonged, negotiated, and incomplete.

Historical Context

Chapters 19 and 20 described Tawantinsuyu, the Inca realm of "four parts," governed from Cusco through roads, storehouses, provincial administrators, and labor obligations such as the mit'a, and tied together by kinship, ritual, and the authority of the Sapa Inca. Chapter 35 showed how that state was weakened by the war between Huáscar and Atahualpa, which followed the death of Huayna Capac in the 1520s, and possibly by epidemic disease that may have reached the Andes before sustained Spanish invasion. When Francisco Pizarro's expedition reached Cajamarca in 1532, the realm had a new ruler who had not yet been installed at Cusco and whose commanders, Quizquiz and Chalcuchimac, still held large forces.

By the end of Chapter 35, Atahualpa was dead. Huáscar had been killed while a prisoner of Atahualpa's faction; Spanish accounts attribute the order to Atahualpa, though the evidence is partisan and uncertain. The Spaniards had a ransom and a legal justification of their own making, but they did not have the allegiance of the Andean population, and they did not control the capital. They also did not yet understand the political landscape. Cusco's nobility, whose royal lineages (panacas) had supported Huáscar, were rivals of the northern armies associated with Atahualpa and his commanders. Provincial peoples such as the Cañari, the Chachapoya, and the Huanca had been conquered by the Incas within recent generations and had reasons to welcome a force that opposed the ruling house or its generals.

The invaders were also not a state apparatus. The expedition was a privately organized enterprise under a royal license, led by Francisco Pizarro and financed in a partnership with Diego de Almagro and others. Pizarro's brothers Hernando, Juan, and Gonzalo held key commands. Almagro's followers and the Pizarros had overlapping claims and a growing rivalry. Crown officials were distant and slow, and the Spanish presence in the Andes was always dependent on reinforcements arriving by sea from Panama. That factionalism becomes important in this chapter, but it is not the chapter's main story.

Finally, legitimacy mattered. The Incas ruled through a system in which the Sapa Inca's person and lineage carried sacred and political weight, and in which kurakas, the hereditary local leaders, mediated between communities and the state. A conqueror who killed the ruler without replacing him risked leaving authority in the hands of others. The Spaniards, for their part, were accustomed to ruling through local intermediaries and wanted an Inca who could command obedience and deliver the labor and tribute they sought. That need was the starting point of everything that followed.

Section 36.1

After Atahualpa: Who Would Rule Tawantinsuyu?

The execution of Atahualpa in mid-1533 left the Spaniards in a position that looked stronger than it was. They held a ransom of gold and silver whose distribution among the soldiers was already provoking quarrels, and they had destroyed the ruler who had made the invasion possible by capturing him. But they had not gained the allegiance of a population of millions. The Inca state was built on personal and ritual ties to the Sapa Inca, and its provincial administration, roads, and labor obligations functioned only when the ruler's authority was recognized. The Spaniards needed someone who could be presented as a ruler and who could command the officials and kurakas on whom everything else depended.

Their first answer was Túpac Huallpa, a brother of Atahualpa and Huáscar. Spanish sources, who call him Toparpa, report that Pizarro had him recognized as Inca before the column left Cajamarca for Cusco. The choice shows the logic of Spanish rule at this stage. Pizarro did not abolish the Inca institution. He tried to capture it, by placing a compliant member of the royal family at its head and acting through him. Túpac Huallpa died in 1533 during the march, probably at Jauja. Some accounts blame illness, others suggest he was poisoned by Chalcuchimac. The cause cannot be established, and students should treat the poisoning story as a claim that served those who made it, not as settled fact.

His brief reign matters for what it reveals. The claim to the throne was contested among several candidates, and the Spaniards did not control the choice. His death reopened the succession problem rather than resolving it, and it showed that Spanish sponsorship by itself could not stabilize Inca legitimacy. Túpac Huallpa, Huáscar, Atahualpa, and Manco were all sons of Huayna Capac, so descent alone did not settle the question. What mattered was which claimant could command the support of the Cusco lineages, the provincial leaders, and the armies. Manco, who had survived the civil war, was such a claimant. He had substantial support among the Cusco lineages and political ambitions of his own, and he was already seeking contact with the Spaniards.

Spanish writers sometimes wrote as though the Inca state had been a hollow shell waiting for the first blow. The evidence suggests otherwise. The administrative apparatus, the roads, the storehouses, the corps of messengers and of provincial officials remained, and the invaders would depend on these structures even as they undermined them. Spanish power in 1533 was borrowed power, and the lender could call in the debt.

Section 36.2

The Road to Cusco: Alliance, Resistance, and a Fragile Coalition

The Spanish column left Cajamarca in 1533 and moved south along the Inca road, through Jauja and toward the highlands of Vilcashuamán and the Apurímac. It was accompanied by Túpac Huallpa and by many Andean porters and servants, and it was joined along the way by warriors from peoples such as the Huanca of the Jauja valley, whose grievances against the Inca state and its commanders made them willing partners. The Cañari, who had been bitterly divided by the earlier war and had fought against Atahualpa's forces, also served with the Spaniards, as did Chachapoya contingents.

The motives behind these partnerships varied and are known mainly through Spanish reports. For the Huanca, memories of Inca conquest and tribute demands, together with the damage done in the central highlands by Atahualpa's commanders, made a Spanish-led force a possible counterweight. The Cañari had suffered heavily in the civil war, and some of their leaders had reason to fear the northern armies. Such calculations were local and could change, so that a community that supplied porters in one month might withhold food in the next. The Spaniards, who understood little of this, often wrote of friendly and hostile provinces as though the categories were fixed, but the evidence suggests that Andean groups treated each column as one more armed power crossing their territory, to be accommodated, resisted, or used as circumstances allowed.

Resistance was real. Quizquiz, the veteran general of Atahualpa's army, and other commanders still held forces in the highlands and attempted to block the advance. The Spaniards fought several engagements, among them a hard fight at Vilcaconga, where an advance party suffered serious losses before being reinforced. Spanish sources are concerned with their own heroism and with the number of enemies, and they tell us less about why Andean commanders chose to fight at particular places. What is clear is that the road to Cusco was contested, and that the conquerors benefited from the fact that Atahualpa's forces and the Cusco nobility were enemies.

The fate of Chalcuchimac illustrates the legal and political problems of Spanish sources. He had been Atahualpa's most senior commander in the highlands and had been brought to Cajamarca earlier in the conquest. During the march he was accused of encouraging resistance and was burned at Jaquijahuana, near Cusco, in November 1533. Spanish accounts present the execution as justice for treachery, but the proceedings were improvised and the accusers had every incentive to remove a powerful rival and potential rallying point. The case shows how the Spaniards combined legal language with the removal of Andean leaders, and it left many Andean elites with reason to doubt that obedience would protect them.

By the time the column approached Cusco, it was not the small band of Cajamarca. It was a changing Andean coalition, with Spanish soldiers and horses at its core and Indigenous warriors, porters, guides, and servants forming its bulk. Manco's followers and members of the Cusco nobility now joined it, bringing the political legitimacy the Spaniards lacked. On 15 November 1533 the combined force entered Cusco.

Section 36.3

Cusco Occupied, Manco Inca Recognized

Cusco was the political, religious, and genealogical center of Tawantinsuyu. It held the temples of the sun and other deities, the palaces and mummified bodies of past rulers, the houses of the royal lineages, and the great fortified complex of Sacsayhuamán on the heights above. The city's layout expressed the structure of the empire, with roads and ritual lines radiating toward the four quarters. To enter it was to enter the sacred heart of a realm, and to occupy it was an act of enormous symbolic weight, for both the Spaniards and the Andeans who watched.

The Spaniards plundered what they could find, stripping gold and silver from temples and palaces and taking what the nobility surrendered. But they did not rule alone. Manco joined the Spanish-Andean coalition and received recognition as Inca in late 1533. The formal royal inauguration, in which he was invested with the royal fringe, the mascaypacha, and which was accompanied by a triumphal ceremony in Cusco, took place in March 1534. Andean ritual and Spanish approval were both visible in the process, and the ceremony proved to be the last royal inauguration of an Inca celebrated in the capital. The recognition was an alliance. Manco wanted military assistance to remove the remaining forces of Atahualpa's commanders from the highlands and to restore the Cusco lineages to power. Pizarro wanted a legitimate ruler who could mobilize the realm's institutions and keep the Spaniards supplied.

For these reasons it is misleading to call Manco a puppet. He had his own claim to the throne, a constituency among the Cusco nobility, and a political program. Spanish support mattered to Manco's recognition, but his authority did not derive from Spanish choice alone: he possessed his own dynastic claim and substantial support among Cusco lineages. His cooperation was a calculated policy, and the fighting that followed shows that it paid some dividends: with Spanish and Andean forces, Quizquiz was driven from the region and his army broken up. Other members of the royal house chose differently. Manco's half-brother Paullu allied with the Spaniards more durably and would later accompany Almagro to Chile, and the choices of such figures show that the Inca elite was divided about what the invaders were and how to deal with them.

In March 1534 the Spaniards formally founded a municipality at Cusco, with a town council and a distribution of houses and Andean laborers among the conquerors. The founding created a Spanish city inside the Inca capital. For a time, two authorities coexisted in the same place, an Inca ruler with a court and followers and a Spanish town with its own claims to land, labor, and law. The tension was built into the arrangement, and it would not last.

Historical Vocabulary
Neo-Inca State

The Neo-Inca state is the modern term for the autonomous Inca polity that Manco Inca established after 1537 in the Vitcos and Vilcabamba region on the eastern slopes of the Andes, and that his sons Sayri Túpac, Titu Cusi Yupanqui, and Túpac Amaru I headed until 1572. Spanish writers often called it simply "Vilcabamba."

The term helps students see a continuity that the label "rebels" hides. The state kept a royal court, military forces, religious practice, and diplomatic relations with Spanish authorities, and it claimed to be the legitimate Inca government. It was much smaller than Tawantinsuyu, and "Neo-Inca" should not be read as implying that it was only a revival or an imitation. Its rulers regarded themselves as the Incas.

Section 36.4

From Alliance to Captivity

The partnership deteriorated in 1534 and 1535. The Spaniards wanted gold, silver, and labor, and they pressed Manco and his nobles for more than the royal house could deliver. Spanish soldiers took houses, goods, and servants, and members of the Pizarro faction treated the Cusco elite with growing contempt. Pizarro left Cusco and founded Lima in 1535 on the coast, which put his Andean ally at a distance from the most powerful Spanish commander. Almagro departed for Chile with a large force in the same period, taking Paullu and the high priest Villac Umu, and leaving the highlands in the hands of the younger Pizarro brothers.

Juan and Gonzalo Pizarro, left in command at Cusco, were accused by Manco and by later Spanish writers of abuse. Manco was held in custody and was reportedly mistreated, subjected to threats and humiliation, and pressed to deliver treasure. The royal household also suffered. Spanish men demanded access to elite women, and the Inca family, whose marriages and descent organized the realm's political life, was drawn into relationships over which it had little control. A few such relationships, like that of Pizarro and Inés Huaylas Yupanqui, a daughter of Huayna Capac, produced children who would belong to both worlds, but they also illustrate the coercion and unequal power under which they occurred.

Titu Cusi's account, dictated in 1570, makes Spanish betrayal and abuse the central explanation of his father's rebellion. It is a royal Neo-Inca interpretation composed in a diplomatic context, addressed to Spanish officials in order to establish the legitimacy of the Vilcabamba court and the injustice of the invaders. Students should therefore distinguish between the general pattern, which is supported by Spanish and Andean sources, and the dramatic details and speeches, which belong to a narrative shaped by its purpose. Spanish participants themselves describe the pressure for gold, the seizure of property, and the ill treatment of the Inca, and they differ mainly in how they justify it.

Hernando Pizarro, returning to Cusco from Spain in early 1536 with royal confirmation of his brother's authority, restored Manco to nominal freedom but kept him under close watch. Manco had by then concluded that cooperation had failed. He offered to retrieve a large gold statue from the Yucay valley, using this promise as a means of leaving the city. The Spaniards, anxious for gold, allowed him to go. He did not return.

Section 36.5

Escape and the Great Uprising of 1536

Manco left Cusco in April 1536, according to the usual reconstruction, and the Spaniards soon realized that he did not intend to bring them gold. Juan Pizarro was sent after him and fought Manco's followers in the Yucay valley without capturing him. Within weeks, forces loyal to Manco had gathered in numbers far larger than the Spaniards expected. The scale of the mobilization startled the invaders, and it demonstrates that the Inca state's capacity to organize had not disappeared.

The uprising was possible because of the institutions that Spanish accounts tend to overlook. Roads and messengers carried orders. Kurakas, the local leaders who controlled the labor of their communities, could raise warriors and supplies when they judged that Manco's cause deserved support. The royal household and the lineages of Cusco, whose standing depended on the Inca institution, supplied commanders. Villac Umu, though first associated with Almagro's expedition, was linked to the rising in Spanish accounts, and Manco's captains, among them Quizu Yupanqui, directed operations in distant regions. The royal name still carried authority in the highlands, and Manco spoke as the legitimate Inca.

It would be a mistake to describe the rising as spontaneous or as an ethnic or national uprising of a united Andean people. Communities decided according to their own interests. Some kurakas committed their people to Manco, some stayed neutral, and some, like the Cañari and Chachapoya, remained allies of the Spaniards because of grievances against the Inca state or because of local rivalries. Later chapters show that Andeans repeatedly made such calculations, in colonial courts as well as on battlefields. The 1536 uprising was a political movement with a royal leader and many different followers.

Spanish sources describe armies in the tens of thousands, and some give figures well over a hundred thousand for the forces around Cusco. These numbers should be treated with great caution. Spanish authors had reasons to magnify the danger and their own endurance, and Inca armies were assembled from rotating contingents whose size varied. What the sources do establish is that the force was large, well organized, and sustained over months, and that it was supplied through the same networks that had supplied the Inca state's own armies.

The sequence of mobilization is hard to reconstruct. Manco's first gathering points appear to have been in the Yucay and Urubamba valleys, close to royal estates that held storehouses, terraced fields, and resident retainers. From there, messengers carried summons along the roads to the provinces, and commanders were dispatched to separate regions. The Spaniards, who had relied on Inca roads for their own movement, now found the same roads carrying intelligence against them. A summons from the Inca carried religious as well as political obligations, though the sources say little about that dimension and Spanish writers concentrated on numbers and weapons.

Section 36.6

The Siege of Cusco

Inca forces surrounded Cusco from about the spring of 1536. Within the city were fewer than two hundred Spaniards, among them a body of cavalry, with a larger number of Andean auxiliaries, including Cañari and Chachapoya warriors, Paullu's followers, and Yanacona servants. Spanish writers recorded their own numbers fairly closely but give the besiegers in the tens of thousands. The besiegers cut the roads and watercourses, occupied the heights, and began a series of assaults.

The fighting in the city was brutal and unusual. Inca forces set fire to Cusco, using burning missiles to ignite the thatched roofs, and the flames spread across much of the city, which was largely built of stone walls with thatched coverings. The Spaniards and their allies fought in the plazas and streets, charging with horses when space allowed and holding their positions in buildings when it did not. Spanish accounts later told stories of divine intervention, particularly of apparitions of Saint James and the Virgin, which became part of the colonial memory of the siege and which tell us more about how the Spaniards later explained survival than about the fighting itself.

The fortress of Sacsayhuamán, on the heights, was the key to the city, and the Inca forces held it. The Spaniards decided that they could not survive without taking it. In an assault carried out at night and under constant fire, Juan Pizarro led a charge and was struck in the head by a stone thrown by a defender, an injury from which he died a few days later. His death was a heavy blow to the Spanish command. The fortress was taken after hard fighting in which Andean auxiliaries and Spanish soldiers took part, and Hernando Pizarro assumed command of the defense.

The siege should be understood as a genuine existential danger for the Spaniards. They were short of food and water, outnumbered, exhausted, and cut off from Lima. Their survival depended on cavalry in open ground, on the labor and fighting of Andean allies, and on the failure of the besiegers to press every advantage. Manco's forces held much of the city and the surrounding country for months. Fighting continued, with varying intensity, into the first months of 1537, and the siege was never lifted by a decisive Spanish victory so much as it gradually weakened and then lost its force.

Logistics shaped the siege on both sides. Within the city, the Spaniards depended on stored maize and on what their Andean servants and allies could gather between assaults, and the burning of thatch destroyed shelter and some supplies. The besiegers drew on the surrounding valleys, whose communities had to feed contingents that rotated in and out of the lines. Spanish accounts concentrate on the streets and plazas, but the outcome depended as much on who could keep the countryside producing and moving food. The siege was also not a single continuous assault. Periods of intense fighting alternated with lulls and skirmishes in which horsemen rode out to forage or to break up gathering forces, and those intervals gave the defenders time to recover and the besiegers time to lose contingents.

Everyday Life and Social History

Hunger, Porters, and the Households Behind the Armies

Every army in this chapter ate food that someone grew and carried what someone else bore on their back. Communities in the valleys around Cusco had to supply the Spanish garrison, the Inca besiegers, or both, depending on who controlled their district at a given moment. Porters moved baggage, artillery, and food over mountain roads, and many died of exhaustion and cold. During the siege of Cusco, the city's inhabitants, Spanish and Andean, faced hunger and thirst, and fire destroyed houses and stores. In the countryside, crops were taken or destroyed, and families fled to higher ground or farther valleys.

Women's labor was central. They prepared maize and other food for the households of lords and soldiers, wove cloth for tribute, and managed fields while men served in the armies. Some, especially royal and noble women, were drawn into relationships with Spanish men under conditions of coercion, and others were captured or killed. The sources record these lives unevenly. Spanish writers were interested in battles and gold, and the Neo-Inca account was concerned with royal legitimacy. The silences about ordinary households are themselves evidence of whose experiences the surviving texts preserved.

Section 36.7

A War Larger Than Cusco: Lima and the Central Andes

Manco's strategy was not confined to one city. He understood that the Spaniards in Cusco depended on reinforcements and communications that ran along the road to the coast, and that the Spanish base at Lima was the foundation of the invaders' hold on the country. Inca forces under commanders such as Quizu Yupanqui operated in the central highlands, attacked Spanish detachments, and cut the roads. In the mountain passes, where horses were less effective and ambushes easy, several Spanish relief columns sent from Lima toward Cusco were destroyed or forced to retreat, with heavy losses.

In the second half of 1536, Inca forces advanced toward the coast and threatened Lima itself. Pizarro had only a small force, supported by Andean allies and by a few reinforcements arriving from Panama and elsewhere. The attackers were repulsed on the plains near the city, and their commander was killed, but the danger had been serious. Spanish writers acknowledged that the new capital had come close to being overrun. The defeat of the Lima attack did not end the war, and it left the Spanish position in the Andes dependent on the support of Andean allies and on Spanish ships.

The coastal approach to Lima crossed country very different from the highlands. Inca commanders had to bring highland warriors down into a hot, irrigated environment and sustain them over a long road, while Pizarro sought reinforcements by sea and relied on Andean allies. Some coastal valley communities may have had their own reasons to oppose the highland armies, a point Spanish writers mention only in passing. The failure on the coast cost Manco one of his most capable commanders, but it did not end the highland war.

This broader campaign suggests that Manco's aim was political recovery rather than a localized revolt. Cusco, Lima, and the roads between them formed a system. An attack on the system might remove the Spaniards from the Andes altogether, or at least force a negotiation from a position of strength. The campaign also stretched Inca resources, since the same communities had to supply armies at several places and also keep their own fields cultivated, a problem discussed below.

Students should be careful not to read later outcomes back into the campaign. At the time, the Spaniards' fate was uncertain. Reports reaching Spanish authorities in Panama and Santo Domingo spoke of a general rising, and the colonists' fear was real. The conquest of Peru, which later chroniclers described as swift and complete, looked in 1536 like a venture on the edge of collapse.

Section 36.8

Ollantaytambo and Andean Military Adaptation

While the siege of Cusco continued, Manco established his headquarters at Ollantaytambo, an Inca royal estate and fortress in the Urubamba valley northwest of Cusco, with stone terraces rising steeply above a narrow plain. The site was well adapted for defense. It controlled access along the river, and its terraces gave defenders positions from which they could attack anyone advancing below.

Hernando Pizarro, in command of the Spanish forces at Cusco, decided to attack Manco in his stronghold in early 1537. He took a force that included a few dozen cavalry, some infantry, and a large body of Andean auxiliaries. Spanish accounts report that the Inca defenders had flooded the approach by diverting the water of the river, creating a field in which the horses stumbled and the attackers could not maneuver. Defenders on the terraces threw stones and shot missiles at the Spaniards, and obstacles had been placed in the way of cavalry charges. The Spaniards could neither reach the defenders nor hold the ground, and they withdrew in disorder with significant losses.

Ollantaytambo was also a political statement. The site was a royal estate associated with the Inca elite, and by holding it Manco showed that he was defending Inca territory rather than raiding from its margins. Spanish sources report that Andean fighters used captured horses and weapons, though how effectively cannot be measured. The victory was real but it did not change the strategic situation, because Manco could not take Cusco and the Spaniards could not take Ollantaytambo.

The battle is best read as evidence of Andean adaptation. Manco's forces had by this time had several months of experience fighting horsemen and firearms, and they had learned to use the terrain, to build obstacles, to rely on missile weapons, and to avoid fighting where cavalry could charge freely. Some Inca fighters had also acquired Spanish weapons and horses, and Spanish sources record anxiety about Andeans learning to use them. More recent research has stressed that Andean forces developed deliberate counter-cavalry tactics, rather than simply suffering Spanish superiority.

This episode undermines any explanation of the conquest that relies on European technology alone. Horses, steel, and firearms mattered, but the circumstances determined how much. Where the Spaniards fought in open terrain with room to maneuver, their advantages were great. Where they entered terrain the Andeans controlled and prepared, they were vulnerable. The Spaniards' repeated successes depended less on any single weapon than on allies, on the divisions among their opponents, and on their ability to retreat to ground that favored them.

Section 36.9

Why the 1536–1537 Offensive Did Not Expel the Spaniards

If the Inca forces held so many advantages, why did they fail to expel the Spaniards? Part of the answer is logistics. A large army needed food, and food in the Andes followed the agricultural calendar. Spanish sources, particularly Pedro Pizarro, report that Manco allowed many of his warriors to return home to plant and harvest, because the communities that supplied them could not afford to lose a season's crop. The Inca state's military system depended on rotating labor obligations, and a prolonged siege required that those obligations be sustained far beyond their normal limits. A force that could be summoned quickly was hard to hold together for months.

A second reason was that Spanish forces never fought alone. Andean auxiliaries, among them Cañari, Chachapoya, Huanca, and followers of Paullu, served as fighters, scouts, and carriers, and Yanacona servants supported the Spaniards in the city. For these people the Spaniards were a means of settling older accounts with the Inca state or with individual elites, and Manco's army was in part an enemy to them. The uprising thus faced Andeans on the other side, and the divisions that had made the Spaniards' advance possible in 1533 did not disappear in 1536.

A third reason was that open ground still favored cavalry, and the Spaniards could choose when to engage. They could sally from Cusco, charge, and withdraw, and Inca forces suffered losses each time. Attrition affected both sides, but the Spaniards received small reinforcements, while the besiegers lost fighters to hunger, disease, and the departure of contingents. Hunger and disease may also have affected the armies, but the sources do not allow their contribution to the siege's outcome to be measured confidently.

Finally, the Spaniards began to fight each other. Almagro returned from Chile in 1537, having found neither the wealth nor the settled societies that he had hoped for, and he disputed with the Pizarros over the control of Cusco. In April 1537 his forces took the city and imprisoned Hernando and Gonzalo Pizarro. Manco tried to exploit the quarrel, negotiating with Almagro and at one point attempting to attack. These maneuvers failed, and Almagro's captains soon pursued Manco to Ollantaytambo and drove him from it. The Spanish civil wars that followed, in which Almagro was defeated and executed in 1538 and Francisco Pizarro was killed in 1541, belong to the history of the Spanish settlers. For Manco, their importance lay in changing the options available to him. He had hoped to profit from Spanish division, but he could not turn it into a permanent advantage.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Spanish-Inca Cooperation to the Uprising of 1536

Long-term causes

The Inca civil war; the dependence of Spanish rule on Inca institutions; provincial and elite divisions within Tawantinsuyu; a Spanish culture of conquest organized around plunder and encomienda.

Short-term causes

Demands for gold and labor, seizure of property, imprisonment and abuse of Manco, mistreatment of the royal household, and the dispersal of Spanish forces when Pizarro went to the coast and Almagro to Chile.

Event or transformation

Manco's escape in 1536 and the summoning of an army through Inca roads, lineages, and kurakas.

Immediate effects

The siege of Cusco, attacks on communications and Lima, destruction of Spanish relief parties, and the Spanish defeat at Ollantaytambo.

Long-term consequences

Retreat to Vilcabamba and a Neo-Inca state lasting until 1572; a Spanish settler society more dependent on Andean allies; permanent doubt about the completeness of conquest.

Historical complexity

The uprising was neither inevitable nor an automatic response to Spanish abuse. It required a leader, an institutional base, and the decisions of many communities, and it failed for reasons that included divisions among Andeans.
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Rebellion, Reconquest, or Unresolved Sovereignty?

The question: Was Manco Inca's uprising of 1536 a rebellion against established Spanish rule, a war of reconquest, or a continuation of an unresolved struggle for sovereignty?

Interpretation A — A rebellion: Spanish sources, and much older scholarship that relied on them, described Manco's action as a revolt against a government that had been established in 1533 by conquest and by his own recognition of Spanish authority. The word fits the Spanish legal view that Cajamarca and Cusco had transferred sovereignty to the Crown.
Interpretation B — A war of reconquest or of continuing sovereignty: Other historians emphasize that the Spaniards controlled little beyond Cusco and a few points, that the Inca state's institutions still functioned, and that Manco acted as the legitimate ruler recovering his realm. Some describe the whole struggle from 1536 to 1572 as an Inca war of reconquest. Titu Cusi's account makes a related argument about rightful rule.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same corpus of Spanish chronicles, legal records, and the Neo-Inca account, and each reflects the purposes of its authors. Spanish writers had reason to assert that conquest was complete and lawful, and Titu Cusi had reason to assert the opposite.

Why it matters: The vocabulary decides whether Spanish authority is silently assumed to have been established before it actually was. "Rebellion" treats the Spaniards as the government, while "reconquest" treats the Incas as a sovereign power. A third view, that sovereignty was truly unresolved, fits the long duration of the conflict and avoids deciding the legal question in advance.

Student task: Write a short argument for each label, then explain which additional evidence, such as the extent of Spanish control outside Cusco, might change the balance.

Section 36.10

Retreat to Vitcos and Vilcabamba: A State in Resistance

After 1537 Manco withdrew eastward rather than surrendering. In the later 1530s he moved to Vitcos and, as Spanish pressure continued, deeper into Vilcabamba, a region on the eastern slopes of the Andes where mountain ranges, deep river canyons, and cloud forest made access from Cusco difficult. The area had long been part of the Inca realm and held settlements and agricultural lands, and it lay close enough to Cusco that Inca authority could still be projected into the highlands and the Urubamba valley. The geography protected the court from the cavalry that had been so effective in open country, and it gave access to the lowland forest beyond.

Vitcos lay on the northern approaches of the Vilcabamba region, in a high valley with a palace and shrine complex that archaeologists have usually identified with the site of Rosaspata. As Spanish expeditions pressed in, the court moved deeper into the lower, forested valleys, and later writers used the name Vilcabamba for the court's final stronghold. The Neo-Inca court later shifted deeper into the forested Vilcabamba region. Archaeological and ethnohistorical research now generally identifies Espíritu Pampa with Vilcabamba, the final Neo-Inca capital, although explorers and scholars debated the identification during the twentieth century. The move from Vitcos to the deeper forest reflected a calculation about distance, supply, and defensibility as much as simple flight.

From this base Manco continued to fight. Cieza de León and other writers describe repeated operations, raids on Spanish roads and settlements, and attacks on Andean communities that supported the Spaniards. He maintained a royal court with its officials and ritual life, kept a military force, and learned to use horses and Spanish weapons. The Spaniards sent expeditions against him, and in 1539 Gonzalo Pizarro led a campaign against Vitcos. Manco escaped, but his wife and sister, Cura Ocllo, was captured during that campaign and was later executed on Francisco Pizarro's order. Titu Cusi later made her death a central episode of the story of Spanish cruelty, and Spanish sources record the killing in less detail, so the surviving evidence reflects both grief and strategic purpose.

Manco's end came about 1544 or 1545. After Almagro's defeat in 1538 and the continuing Spanish civil wars, a group of Spanish followers of Almagro, the Almagristas, fled into the mountains and sought refuge with Manco, who sheltered them and employed them, according to later accounts, as military advisers. In a dispute, the refugees killed him, reportedly during a game, and were themselves killed by Inca forces soon afterward. The date and details differ among sources, and students should treat both as approximate.

Women were part of this world. Elite women such as Cura Ocllo stood at the center of royal legitimacy, and the capture, killing, or transfer of such women was a political act that struck at the dynasty itself. Ordinary women worked the fields, prepared food for the court and the soldiers, and carried household goods in the movements between valleys. The Neo-Inca state survived through households as well as through warriors.

Historical Vocabulary
Yanacona

In the Inca state, a yana (plural yanakuna; Spanish yanacona) was a person who served a lord or institution outside the ordinary community-based labor obligation, as a retainer, craftsperson, servant, or agricultural worker. The status varied widely and could carry privilege as well as dependence.

After 1532, Spaniards applied the word to the many Andean servants and auxiliaries who accompanied them and later to a category of detached Indigenous laborers in the colonial economy. The term matters in this chapter because yanaconas fed, guided, carried for, and fought beside the Spanish forces. Students should not equate them with "collaborators" in a modern sense, since their status, motives, and degree of choice varied, and many were taken or attached under pressure.

Section 36.11

The Neo-Inca State: Negotiation as Resistance

Manco's successors were political actors, not declining relics. His son Sayri Túpac, who succeeded as a boy, ruled through advisers, and by the middle of the 1550s Spanish authorities opened negotiations with him. The viceroy sought to bring the Inca out of Vilcabamba, in effect to end its independence by offering honors and property. Sayri Túpac left Vilcabamba in 1557 under negotiated terms and made major public entries into Spanish-controlled territory in 1557–1558. He was received in Lima and Cusco, was baptized, and was granted rich lands in the Yucay valley. He died in 1560 or 1561. Whether his death was natural is unknown, and some Andeans and Spaniards later suspected otherwise.

His half-brother Titu Cusi Yupanqui took power at Vilcabamba and governed through the 1560s until his death in 1571. He alternated between diplomacy and pressure. He negotiated with Spanish officials, and in the settlement of Acobamba, whose principal capitulation dates to 24 August 1566, he formally accepted Spanish suzerainty, in effect vassalage to Philip II, along with other negotiated obligations. He accepted Christian missionaries, and Augustinian friars entered Vilcabamba, where he was baptized, while he kept Inca royal authority and ritual life. Spaniards who traveled to his court reported on its organization, and the court itself conducted diplomacy in the Spanish idiom, including letters and legal petitions. The settlement shows that formal submission did not equal effective control. Vilcabamba retained substantial de facto autonomy, and Titu Cusi kept bargaining over property, succession, political status, and the practical terms of the relationship. His strategy combined engagement with persistence: he accepted formal ties that could strengthen his position and used them to defend the court's independence in practice.

The substance of the negotiations concerned concrete matters of property, succession, and jurisdiction. Spanish officials offered titles, grants, and pardons, and Neo-Inca representatives pressed for recognition of lands and for guarantees about who would rule and on what terms. Each side used the talks for its own ends. The Spaniards hoped to draw the Inca out of Vilcabamba and into the colonial order, while the Neo-Inca court gained time and goods, secured the presence of friars who might testify to its good faith, and sought to press its claims before higher authorities, including the king. Negotiation was thus a political arena in which the Neo-Inca state tried to convert military survival into legal recognition.

His account of the conquest, dictated in 1570 and addressed to the former governor Lope García de Castro, whom Titu Cusi hoped would present his claims to Philip II, is both an Indigenous royal source and a diplomatic text. It was probably dictated in both Quechua and Spanish, translated and put in order by the Augustinian friar Marcos García, and transcribed by the mestizo secretary Martín de Pando, so the process of dictation, translation, and transcription shaped what survives. It makes arguments about Inca legitimacy, Spanish betrayal, and the justice of Titu Cusi's demands, and it was intended to persuade a Spanish audience. The text is extremely valuable precisely because it shows how an Inca ruler used the language of Spanish law and Christianity to defend sovereignty.

The persistence of negotiations for decades demonstrates that the Crown did not treat Vilcabamba as irrelevant. Spanish officials were concerned by the court's existence as a rallying point for Andean grievance, by its role as a refuge for people fleeing Spanish rule, and by the possibility that it might ally with other enemies. Titu Cusi died suddenly in 1571, and members of his household suspected poisoning. Martín de Pando and the friar Diego Ortiz, the Spaniards closest to the court, became targets of suspicion, and both were subsequently killed under different circumstances. The surviving accounts are partisan and uncertain, and the deaths brought the negotiations to a crisis.

Primary Source Window

Titu Cusi's Account and Pedro Pizarro's Relation

Creators: Titu Cusi Yupanqui (Instrucción, dictated at Vilcabamba); Pedro Pizarro (Relación del descubrimiento y conquista de los reinos del Perú) · Dates: 1570 and 1571 · Type: A Neo-Inca royal narrative produced through Quechua-Spanish dictation, translation by a Spanish friar, and transcription by a mestizo secretary; a Spanish eyewitness account written years after the events

Context: Titu Cusi addressed his account to Lope García de Castro, the former governor of Peru, as part of negotiations over the status of Vilcabamba, and sought to have his claims presented before Philip II. The text was probably dictated through both Quechua and Spanish, translated and put in order by the Augustinian Marcos García, and transcribed by the mestizo secretary Martín de Pando, so it is a product of translation and mediation rather than an unmediated written transcript. That production history shows why an Indigenous-sponsored source is not the same as an Indigenous voice heard directly. Pedro Pizarro was a cousin of the conqueror who served at Cusco, and he wrote in Peru, in the 1570s, in part to defend the conquerors' conduct and record his own service.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: Titu Cusi presents Manco as a ruler who welcomed the Spaniards in good faith and offered gifts and hospitality, and who was repaid with seizure of property, humiliation, imprisonment, and violence against his family, so that his flight and war were a rightful response to injustice. Pedro Pizarro describes Manco's flight and the great siege from the viewpoint of the besieged, emphasizing Spanish endurance and the size of the Inca armies, and acknowledges that Spaniards pressed Manco for gold and mistreated him.

Analysis: Titu Cusi's text is valuable because it is an Inca royal argument, composed from within the Neo-Inca state, about legitimacy and betrayal. Its diplomatic purpose shaped its emphasis and its claims. Pedro Pizarro is a participant who knew the events and the men but wrote to justify his companions and himself. Where the two agree, as on the pattern of Spanish extortion and abuse, the evidence is strong. Where they differ, as on motives, numbers, and dramatic details, historians must weigh the interests of each author. Neither source speaks for ordinary Andean households, for the Andean allies of the Spaniards, or for the women whose captivity and deaths the texts mention.

Section 36.12

1572: The Destruction of Vilcabamba and the Limits of "Final Conquest"

Francisco de Toledo, who arrived in Peru as viceroy in 1569, had been charged with strengthening royal authority. He regarded the independent Inca court as an obstacle, and after the death of Titu Cusi and the killing of Spanish envoys and missionaries in Vilcabamba, he abandoned prolonged negotiation and prepared a military expedition. In 1572 a Spanish force, accompanied by Andean allies, advanced into Vilcabamba. The Neo-Inca leaders abandoned and burned their capital and fled into the forest. Túpac Amaru I, a younger son of Manco who had succeeded Titu Cusi, was captured.

Túpac Amaru was brought to Cusco, tried, and executed in the main plaza in September 1572. Many Andeans, including Inca nobles in the city, are reported to have watched in grief. Other members of the Inca royal lineages were removed from the region, and a new colonial order took shape in which the descendants of the Inca elite had a recognized but subordinate place. The man executed in 1572 should not be confused with Túpac Amaru II, who led a very different rebellion in 1780, though the later leader took his name in part to claim the earlier ruler's legacy, as Chapter 49 will show.

The distinction that matters is one of scope. The destruction of Vilcabamba in 1572 ended the last autonomous Inca royal state. It did not end Andean resistance, which continued in many forms, from revolts to litigation to flight. It did not end local authority, since kurakas continued to mediate between communities and the Spanish state. It did not end Andean identities, languages, and religious practices, and it did not end the memory of Inca sovereignty, which became a language of grievance and hope in later centuries. To describe 1572 as the end of Inca resistance would mistake the destruction of one political center for the disappearance of a people.

The history of this chapter also looks forward. The Spaniards who had entered Cusco in 1533 as apparent victors had depended on Andean allies, local rulers, and Andean labor, and so would the colonial state that succeeded them. Chapter 37 turns to the northern Andes, where conquest took a different course, and Chapters 40, 42, and 47 return to the institutions and conflicts that grew out of the events described here.

The destruction of Vilcabamba in 1572 ended the last autonomous Inca royal state, but it did not turn conquest into a completed event. Spanish authority in the Andes would continue to depend on Indigenous communities, local rulers, negotiated cooperation, coercion, and repeated resistance. The conquest of Cusco had taken a city; ruling the Andes would prove to be a much longer project.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Cusco, the Andes, and the Inca State, 1533–1572

What Changed

  • Spanish towns, notably Cusco and Lima, appeared in the Andean landscape
  • The Inca state shrank from a continental realm to the Vilcabamba polity
  • Andeans acquired horses, steel weapons, Spanish literacy, and Christian practice
  • The Spaniards moved from a private expedition to a society with a viceroy

What Stayed the Same

  • Roads, messengers, kurakas, and community labor continued to function
  • Royal legitimacy remained a force, from Cusco to Vilcabamba
  • Spanish dependence on Andean labor, allies, and intermediaries persisted
  • Spanish accounts continued to justify conquest and magnify Spanish danger

Who experienced change most directly: Inca royal lineages, urban Andeans in Cusco, and communities along the roads where armies passed. Greater continuity: Rural communities in many regions, whose local authorities, agriculture, and ritual life continued under new masters. Why the pattern matters: It shows that conquest, in the sense of military seizure, preceded colonization in the sense of long-term institutional control, and that the two processes ran on different timetables.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the capture of Atahualpa and the occupation of Cusco did not give the Spaniards control of Tawantinsuyu. The Spaniards entered the capital in November 1533 as one component of a changing Andean coalition, and their advance depended on the support of provincial peoples, on the rivalries between the Cusco nobility and the commanders of Atahualpa, and on Indigenous labor and provisioning.

After Atahualpa's execution, the Spaniards needed an Inca ruler through whom they could act. Their first choice, Túpac Huallpa, died during the march south, and they turned to Manco Inca, who had his own claim and constituency. Resistance continued under Quizquiz and others, and the execution of Chalcuchimac illustrated how the invaders mixed legal language and political calculation in removing Andean leaders.

The recognition of Manco in Cusco was a mutual calculation. Manco needed military help against his rivals and a chance to restore the Cusco lineages, and the Spaniards needed legitimacy and access to Inca institutions. He should not be described as a puppet. The Spanish municipality founded in 1534 created a second authority in the same city, and the arrangement could not last.

Cooperation collapsed under Spanish demands for gold and labor, the seizure of property, the imprisonment and abuse of Manco, and violence against the royal household. Titu Cusi's later account makes this pattern central, and while its details belong to a diplomatic narrative, the pattern is supported by Spanish testimony. When Manco escaped in 1536, he summoned an army through the roads, messengers, lineages, and kurakas of the old state.

The siege of Cusco, in which Inca forces burned the city and fought for Sacsayhuamán, nearly destroyed the Spanish foothold. The death of Juan Pizarro was a heavy blow, and the Spaniards survived thanks to cavalry in open ground, Andean allies, and the besiegers' own difficulties. The campaign extended beyond Cusco to the roads and to Lima, and several Spanish relief columns were destroyed, which shows that the war was a coordinated effort at political recovery.

At Ollantaytambo, Andean forces used terrain, flooding, obstacles, and missile weapons to defeat a Spanish attack, which demonstrates rapid military adaptation and undermines explanations of the conquest through European technology alone. The offensive nonetheless failed to expel the Spaniards. Logistics, the agricultural calendar, divisions among Andeans, Spanish cavalry, and the quarrel between Almagro and the Pizarros together prevented either side from decisive victory.

Manco withdrew to Vitcos and Vilcabamba, where he and his successors maintained the Neo-Inca state. He continued raids and diplomacy until his death at the hands of Spanish refugees around 1544 or 1545. Sayri Túpac left the region under negotiated terms in the late 1550s, and Titu Cusi pursued a combination of diplomacy, limited accommodation, and Christian engagement while preserving Inca authority, as his 1570 account shows.

In 1572, Viceroy Toledo abandoned negotiation and destroyed Vilcabamba, and Túpac Amaru I was executed in Cusco. This ended the last autonomous Inca royal state, but it did not end Andean resistance, local authority, cultural identity, or the memory of Inca sovereignty. Students should not confuse Túpac Amaru I with Túpac Amaru II, who rebelled in 1780.

The chapter also teaches a method. Spanish participants wrote to defend their conduct, the Neo-Inca account wrote to persuade, and neither speaks for the many Andean households that fed and carried the armies. Reading such sources against one another, and noticing the vocabulary of "rebellion," "conquest," and "reconquest," is a skill that recurs in the chapters that follow.

Key Terms

Tawantinsuyu
The Quechua name for the Inca realm of "four parts," governed from Cusco.

Yanacona
An Andean retainer or servant; applied by Spaniards to the many Indigenous helpers who accompanied them.

The autonomous Inca polity of Vitcos and Vilcabamba, 1537–1572.
Vilcabamba
The eastern Andean region and final seat of the Neo-Inca court.
Vitcos
An earlier seat of Manco's court in the Vilcabamba region.
Sacsayhuamán
The great fortified ceremonial complex above Cusco, a key position in the 1536 siege.
Coya
The principal wife of the Inca, often his sister, whose position was central to royal legitimacy.
Andean allies
Indigenous peoples, such as Cañari, Chachapoya, and Huanca, who served with the Spaniards for their own reasons.
Relief column
A Spanish detachment sent to assist a besieged position; several were destroyed in 1536.
Encomienda
A Crown grant of rights to receive Indigenous tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services, formally paired with obligations of protection and Christian instruction; it was not itself a grant of land or ownership of Indigenous people.
Sovereignty
Supreme political authority over a territory, the central issue between the Spanish Crown and the Inca state.
Legitimacy
The perceived rightfulness of rule, which both Spaniards and Incas worked to claim.
Instrucción
Titu Cusi's 1570 account, dictated at Vilcabamba and addressed to Spanish authorities.
Reconquest
A term some historians apply to the Inca struggle after 1536, treating it as a war to recover sovereignty.
Almagristas
Followers of Diego de Almagro, some of whom took refuge in Vilcabamba after his defeat.
Cavalry adaptation
Andean tactics, such as flooding, obstacles, and use of terrain, developed to counter horsemen.
Acobamba settlement
The 1566 settlement (principal capitulation dated 24 August) in which Titu Cusi formally accepted Spanish suzerainty and vassalage to Philip II while Vilcabamba retained substantial de facto autonomy.
Conquest versus colonization
Related but distinct processes: military seizure versus long-term institutional and economic control.
Puppet ruler
A common label for Manco that this chapter rejects as an oversimplification of his political agency.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that occupying a capital, claiming sovereignty, and ruling a society are three different things. The Spaniards who entered Cusco in 1533 held a city, but their position rested on Inca dynastic politics, on Indigenous allies, and on Indigenous labor and food, and in 1536 they learned how quickly that position could be threatened. Sovereignty remained contested for decades, and colonial rule, in the sense of durable institutions that extracted tribute and labor, was still to be built.

Andean allies appear on both sides of the story. Cañari, Chachapoya, Huanca, and Cusco nobles such as Paullu fought and worked beside the Spaniards for reasons rooted in older grievances, while Manco summoned kurakas and communities who judged that his cause served theirs. The conflict was not a simple struggle between Spaniards and a united Andean people. It was a war in which many Andean communities made calculated choices about whom to feed, follow, or resist, and those choices decided its course as much as horses or steel.

War and diplomacy ran in parallel. Manco's uprising and the raids from Vitcos were matched by the negotiations of Sayri Túpac and Titu Cusi, who used baptism, petitions, and formal vassalage to defend the practical independence of Vilcabamba. The Spanish side likewise combined expeditions with offers of titles and pardons. Reading the period only as a series of battles hides the fact that the decisive struggle was over legitimacy, and that both sides understood it as such.

The destruction of Vilcabamba in 1572 ended an autonomous Inca royal state, and it matters that it ended in this way, with a viceroy's decision and a public execution in the plaza of the old capital. It did not end Andean historical agency. Kurakas, communities, languages, and memories of Inca rule continued, and they would be used in courts, in negotiations over labor and land, and in later rebellions. Chapter 37 turns to the northern Andes, where conquest followed a different course, and the institutions and conflicts that grew from the events of this chapter return in Chapters 40, 42, and 47.
