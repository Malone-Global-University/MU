Central America and the Uneven End of Iberian Rule

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Four Outcomes from One Crisis

Between 1808 and 1840 the collapse of Spanish authority produced a short-lived Central American federation, provinces absorbed by Mexico, a Haitian-ruled Hispaniola and two Caribbean colonies that stayed Spanish.

This chapter closes Part XII by asking why the same Atlantic crisis ended Iberian rule in some places, produced fragmentation in others and left Cuba and Puerto Rico under Spain. Central American independence was not won by a continental army. It emerged from constitutional disputes, provincial rivalries, elite bargaining and earlier popular movements.

Independence came twice: from Spain in September 1821, and from Spain, Mexico and every other power in July 1823. The federation built in 1824 embodied a real effort at union but faced weak finances, provincial loyalties and conflicts over religion and land, and it dissolved between 1838 and 1840.

Cuba and Puerto Rico took another path. Plantation slavery, military garrisons, immigration policy and elite fear of revolution tied many local elites to Spain, though reformers, conspirators and enslaved rebels challenged that order.

Evidence includes constitutions, decrees, municipal records, Indigenous petitions, trial records and travelers’ accounts, each reflecting the interests of its authors. The chapter treats independence, territory, citizenship and effective sovereignty as separate questions.

Chapter 62 continues Central American state formation and Chapter 67 follows Cuba and Puerto Rico to 1898.

Division I — The Crisis of Spanish Authority in Central America, 1808–1821

Section 57.1

The Kingdom of Guatemala: Administration, Geography, and Provincial Identities

The Kingdom of Guatemala, or Captaincy General of Guatemala, ran from Chiapas to Costa Rica; Panama belonged to New Granada. The captain-general, who also presided over the audiencia, governed from Guatemala City, which was rebuilt on a new site in 1776 after the earthquakes of 1773. The archbishopric (from 1743) and the merchant guild, the Consulado de Comercio (1794), made the capital the religious and commercial center as well.

The Bourbon intendancies of 1786 created provincial seats at San Salvador, Comayagua, León and Ciudad Real (Chiapas), while Costa Rica remained a small governorship. The reforms aimed at revenue and efficiency but also created local rivals to the capital. Within provinces, towns competed too: Comayagua with Tegucigalpa, León with Granada, and Cartago with San José, Heredia and Alajuela.

Local government rested on town councils (cabildos), Indigenous town councils and parish priests, and religious orders influenced education and land. Identity was largely provincial: people thought of themselves as belonging to a town or province, and no Central American nation existed. Historians reconstruct this world from tribute registers, censuses, merchant records, council minutes and Indigenous land titles.

Imperial government worked through layers of law and office. Royal law was applied by the audiencia, which also served as the highest appeal court, and by district officials such as alcaldes mayores and corregidores who supervised Indigenous towns. The Crown’s revenue came from tribute, the sales tax (alcabala), the tobacco and liquor monopolies and the Church’s tithe, and officials depended heavily on the cooperation of Indigenous town leaders to collect it.

Geography limited that control. The highlands, where the major cities lay, were separated from the Pacific lowlands and the thinly settled Caribbean coast by mountains and rivers, and trade moved by mule track to ports such as Acajutla, Realejo, Omoa and Puntarenas. Intellectual life was concentrated in the capital, which had the University of San Carlos (founded in 1676), the Economic Society of Friends of the Country (1795) and the Gazeta de Guatemala (1797), where officials and creole professionals debated reform and the Enlightenment. These circles produced figures such as José Cecilio del Valle and Pedro Molina, who would later help shape the independence debates.

Bourbon reform changed the colonial machinery in the late eighteenth century. The 1778 free-trade regulations opened more Spanish ports to Central American commerce, and the intendancies of 1786 replaced many district magistrates who had profited from forced sales to Indigenous towns (repartimientos de mercancías). Reform improved some revenue collection, but wars with Britain in 1796–1802 and 1804–1808 disrupted trade, and the Crown’s demands for war funds drew resentment from local elites.

The capital itself was a source of disagreement. After the earthquakes of 1773 destroyed Santiago de los Caballeros (now Antigua), officials and notables argued for years about whether to move, and the relocation to the Valley of the Hermitage in 1776 was imposed over strong opposition. Each Indigenous town also had its own council with alcaldes and regidores, who collected tribute and organized labor, and the system worked only because these local leaders kept it running.

Defense and frontier policy added another layer of administration. Spanish forts such as San Felipe on Lake Izabal, San Fernando de Omoa and San Carlos on the San Juan River guarded against British raids from the Caribbean, while frontier zones such as the Petén and Talamanca remained only partly under colonial control. The Church’s structure also spread authority across the region, with dioceses in Chiapas, Comayagua and León under the archbishop of Guatemala, so ecclesiastical boundaries and political ones overlapped without matching.

These arrangements gave the captaincy a recognizable shape, but they did not give it a single political identity. Officials, merchants and clergy in each province answered to the capital in some matters and to local interests in others, and the habit of negotiation that resulted would outlast Spanish rule.

Section 57.2

Trade, Indigenous Communities, Social Hierarchies, and Regional Rivalries

Estimates put the region’s population above one million in the early nineteenth century. Highland Guatemala and Chiapas had Maya majorities, including K’iche’, Kaqchikel, Mam, Tzotzil and Tzeltal speakers, while Nawat-speaking Pipil lived in El Salvador and Lenca in Honduras. Ladinos, meaning Hispanicized and mixed rural people, were growing in number, Afro-descendants lived in coastal and Pacific towns, and a small elite of peninsulars and creoles held office and land.

Indigo (añil) was the great export, concentrated in El Salvador, while cochineal cultivation around Antigua was developing and would become a major Guatemalan export in the decades after independence. Honduras had cattle and silver mining around Tegucigalpa, Nicaragua had cattle and trade via the San Juan river, and Costa Rica tobacco and small farms. Guatemala City merchants such as the Aycinena family supplied credit to growers, giving them leverage over provinces, while contraband trade with the British through Belize and the Caribbean coast undermined official monopolies.

Indigenous towns paid tribute, supplied labor, kept communal lands and sustained cofradías (religious brotherhoods), and the Church collected tithes and fees. The Cortes abolished tribute and forced service in principle in 1811–1812, but officials restored tribute after 1814. Women ran households, wove, farmed and sold in markets, and enslaved people were few compared with the Caribbean. Rivalry between Guatemala City and San Salvador, and between León and Granada, linked these economic differences to political conflict.

Indigo shows how a regional economy shaped politics. Salvadoran growers and processors turned the plant into dye in vats and presses, using family labor, hired workers and debt-bound laborers, and sold it to Guatemalan merchants who exported it. Wars and trade disruption during the Napoleonic period depressed prices, and growers resented merchants’ credit terms and the monopoly of the capital’s guild. Many Salvadoran leaders thus linked political autonomy with commercial freedom.

Community institutions mattered as much as plantations. Indigenous towns held communal lands, and religious brotherhoods (cofradías) owned cattle and land that financed festivals and community needs. Villagers litigated over boundaries and labor in colonial courts, and notarial records and land titles show their use of Spanish law. Women organized households, wove cloth and traded in markets, and widows and landholders appear in the records as property managers, although legal and religious structures gave men authority in public office.

Regional social structure was more varied than a simple hierarchy suggests. In Guatemala’s highlands, Indigenous communities were a majority, and in Chiapas, Tzotzil and Tzeltal towns lived under a small Hispanic elite concentrated in Ciudad Real. African-descended people lived on Pacific cacao and sugar estates around Sonsonate and in port towns, and on the Caribbean side, the Miskitu and Garifuna communities, the latter exiled from St. Vincent and landed on Roatán in 1797, lived largely outside Spanish administration.

Labor relations reinforced these differences. On Salvadoran indigo estates, Indigenous and ladino workers labored seasonally, often tied to growers by advances and debts, while in highland Guatemala communities supplied labor drafts for public works and estates. Spanish wars with Britain closed markets and cut incomes, creating the sense of crisis that fed political demands for free trade and for local control of revenues.

Guatemala City’s merchants were a distinct social group. Many belonged to families of Basque and Navarrese origin, such as the Aycinena, Beltranena and Arrivillaga families, who had risen through trade and office, and their control of the merchant guild gave them influence over credit, imports and exports. Salvadoran growers, by contrast, were creole estate owners and smaller producers who saw themselves as the source of the region’s main export but had little say in the guild’s decisions.

Cloth production illustrates the role of Indigenous women. Maya women wove cotton textiles that circulated in regional markets, with highland towns such as Quetzaltenango involved in this trade, and weaving provided income and status within households and communities. These local economies were not marginal to colonial wealth, and they shaped how communities responded to taxation and new trade policies after independence.

Everyday Life and Social History

Labor, Households, Taxes and War

Indigo work in El Salvador involved seasonal labor, advances and debts. Highland Maya households combined maize farming, textile work, communal obligations and religious brotherhoods, and women’s weaving was a source of income. In towns, artisans and market sellers handled goods that British imports threatened.

Taxes and recruitment reached rural families through officials: levies of men, requisitions of food and animals, and tribute or levies under new names. In Cuba, enslaved cane workers faced long harvest hours and mill hazards, and some tried self-purchase, while free people of color served in militias and trades. In Puerto Rico, landless agregados lived on estates for small plots. Church institutions provided schooling, charity and festivals, so reforms touched everyday life. These reconstructions come from records written by officials, merchants and priests, and they should not be read as testimony from the people described.

Section 57.3

The Cádiz Constitution and Central American Movements of 1811–1814

After 1808 Central America recognized Ferdinand VII and elected deputies to the Cádiz Cortes, among them Antonio de Larrazábal, who carried instructions from the Guatemala City council urging reform. The 1812 Constitution created elected town councils and provincial deputations, and it brought Indigenous towns into electoral politics. A brief press freedom allowed newspapers such as Pedro Molina’s El Editor Constitucional (1820) to debate ideas.

Captain-General José de Bustamante (1811–1818) distrusted these reforms. In November 1811 San Salvador rose under the priest Matías Delgado and Manuel José Arce, who deposed the intendant, and the rising was ended within weeks, with leaders imprisoned for extended periods. In December 1811 and 1812 León, Granada and other Nicaraguan towns rose against Spanish officials, and a conspiracy was uncovered at the Belén convent in Guatemala City in December 1813.

Ferdinand VII abolished the constitution in 1814, and it was restored in 1820. That summer K’iche’ leaders Atanasio Tzul and Lucas Aguirre led a revolt in Totonicapán over tribute and church fees, installing themselves as authorities until troops suppressed it. Tzul’s movement used Cádiz language about abolished tribute, showing that Indigenous communities understood and used constitutional politics. Evidence for these events comes largely from prosecution records, which reveal the authorities’ concerns.

The Cádiz Constitution changed the status of Indigenous people. It counted them as citizens, abolished tribute in principle and created elected town councils in towns of sufficient size, including Indigenous towns, so villages gained a new legal tool to dispute land, church fees and obligations. After 1814 officials restored tribute and the old order, and in 1820 renewed constitutionalism produced confusion about what was owed, which helps explain the Totonicapán revolt.

Political camps were also taking shape. In the elections and debates of 1820–1821, conservative families around the Aycinena merchants, who valued order and the Church, faced liberal writers and professionals around Molina’s El Editor Constitucional. Bustamante’s repression drew on fear of Mexico’s insurgency after 1810, and it left a memory of arbitrary rule. The later alignments of 1821–1829 thus had roots in the Cádiz years.

Press and association spread constitutional ideas. The Cortes decreed freedom of the press in 1810, and in 1820 José Cecilio del Valle launched El Amigo de la Patria alongside Molina’s paper, so that arguments over reform, trade and sovereignty reached beyond the capital’s salons. Local councils proliferated under the 1812 Constitution, and more people than before took part in elections and in litigation over local rights.

The risings of 1811–1812 combined elite and popular grievances. In San Salvador, resentments over tobacco and liquor monopolies and over Spanish officials’ powers mingled with demands for local control, and crowds took part alongside creole leaders. In Nicaragua, urban risings against peninsular officials were joined by Indigenous communities in districts such as Matagalpa, whose own demands about land and labor did not always coincide with those of creole leaders. Authorities treated all of these as rebellion, and their repression shows how the Crown understood the challenge.

The 1813 Belén conspiracy in Guatemala City involved clergy, soldiers and professionals who discussed independence, and the authorities answered with arrests and trials. The earlier risings in San Salvador and Nicaragua also lacked coordination and a military base, and creole leaders were divided over how far to go. Repression succeeded partly because many elites feared that unrest would unleash social conflict.

What the movements left behind mattered. They taught local leaders how to organize, argue and negotiate with imperial officials, and they created networks that reappeared in 1821. For Indigenous and rural communities, the same years showed that constitutional language could be a tool, as the Totonicapán revolt would show, and that officials could take it back by force.

Historical Vocabulary
Cabildo

The cabildo (also ayuntamiento) was the town council of a Spanish city or town, made up of councilors (regidores) and magistrates (alcaldes). In the colonial period many seats were bought or held by leading families, so councils spoke for local notables and not for all residents. Indigenous towns had their own councils with alcaldes and regidores that collected tribute and organized labor.

The Cádiz Constitution of 1812 turned the cabildo into an elected body, widening participation and giving communities a legal instrument for land, tax and church disputes. In 1821 councils were consulted about independence and annexation, which made them the main political voice of the provinces. A modern “municipality” is a misleading equivalent, because the colonial cabildo mixed government, justice and elite privilege.

Section 57.4

The Declaration of Independence at Guatemala City, September 15, 1821

After the 1820 restoration Guatemalan politics divided between conservatives around the Aycinena merchants and liberals around Molina and José Francisco Barrundia. Gabino Gaínza, a Spanish officer, took over as captain-general in 1821. News of Mexico’s Plan of Iguala (February 1821) and the Treaty of Córdoba (August 1821), and Chiapas’s break with Spain in late August, forced leaders to decide.

On September 14–15, 1821 the provincial deputation and notables met in Guatemala City while crowds gathered outside. Traditional accounts say Valle favored waiting for a congress, while others urged immediate action. The act, largely drafted by Valle, declared independence from Spain, kept Gaínza and existing officials in place, and called a congress to decide the form of government.

Its limits matter. It was approved by officials, clerics and merchants, not by an elected body, and provinces were informed afterward. September 15 is now a national holiday across Central America, but the date marks a break with Spain, not a constitution, a union or popular sovereignty. Older accounts of ‘independence without war’ overlook both earlier mobilizations and the conflicts that followed.

The meeting of September 15 produced a provisional framework as well as a declaration. The act created a Provisional Consultative Junta with Gaínza as president to govern until the promised congress met, and Valle was among its members. It also stated that independence should be proclaimed from above to prevent the dangers of the people proclaiming it for themselves, which reveals the elite fear of popular action that shaped the decision. Traditional accounts credit liberal speakers such as Barrundia with pressing the assembly while crowds gathered outside, though the extent of the pressure is hard to measure.

Why did Guatemala City’s elites act in 1821? The Spanish liberal regime in Madrid was too weak to protect the colony, Mexico’s settlement offered a model of independence without social revolution, and officials feared that waiting would invite unrest or a separate Mexican move. Historians give different weight to liberal ideals, fear of popular uprising and fear of Mexican intervention, and the surviving evidence of the meeting, the act itself and later memoirs, was produced by participants who had reasons to present their conduct as prudent.

Events in Chiapas increased the pressure. Comitán declared independence on August 28, 1821 and Ciudad Real followed in early September, so a province of the same captaincy had moved before the capital acted. Spanish resistance was minimal because Madrid’s liberal government had few troops to send and its officials in Guatemala City preferred negotiation to confrontation, which is one reason the transition was peaceful.

The clergy, university graduates and officials who attended the meeting also had personal stakes. Many held offices that depended on royal salaries or Church revenues, and they saw independence under existing authorities as the best way to keep both. Their caution explains why the act preserved institutions, officials and a provisional government instead of creating a new order.

The Spanish context also mattered. After the 1820 revolution in Spain restored the constitution, liberal Cortes debated plans in 1821 for autonomous American kingdoms under the Spanish crown, but American deputies judged the proposals inadequate and the plans came too late. For Central American elites, the failure of such compromises strengthened the case for acting before events in Mexico overtook them.

The phrase ‘the will of the people’ also deserves scrutiny. Artisans, market sellers and the urban poor were present in the plaza, and elites feared them because of the unrest of 1811–1813, yet they did not sign the act or help write it. The declaration thus claimed popular authority while keeping decisions in the hands of officials and notables.

Division II — Independence, the Mexican Empire, and the Second Break, 1821–1823

Section 57.5

Different Provincial Responses to Independence

News of the act reached the provinces by courier in September and October 1821, and their answers showed that independence from Spain settled one question while opening several others. Highland Guatemalan towns generally accepted the capital’s lead, but elsewhere the issue was whether to follow Guatemala City, to join Mexico, or to remain autonomous. Honduran towns were divided, and Comayagua and Tegucigalpa, long rivals, did not necessarily choose alike.

In Nicaragua, León’s provincial leaders issued a conditional declaration, often called the Act of the Clouds (Acta de los Nublados), proclaiming independence from Spain while leaving the question of union with Mexico or Guatemala open. Granada’s elites, wary of León’s predominance, took a more assertive line. Nicaraguan politics thus continued the rivalry between the two cities under a new vocabulary.

Costa Rica’s towns learned of the act late. After debate they adopted an interim Pact of Concord in December 1821, which created a provisional government. Annexation to Mexico later divided Cartago and Heredia, who favored it, from San Jose and Alajuela, who opposed it, and the dispute led to fighting at Ochomogo in April 1823.

These provincial answers show that independence did not simply reveal a united Central American will. Merchants, landowners, clergy and officials weighed security, trade, local autonomy and fear of social disorder, and each province calculated differently. Municipal councils spoke for property-owning men, and the sources say little about how most villagers understood the change.

Provincial responses also reflected economic geography. Honduran towns were tied to Guatemala City through mule roads and the port of Omoa, but Chiapas traded with Mexico, and distant Costa Rica had few commercial links to the capital. Provincial elites also worried about the promised congress, because deputies would be apportioned by population and Guatemala, the most populous province, would hold the largest bloc of votes. Fear of domination by the capital was therefore an argument for caution as well as for autonomy.

Town councils spoke for local notables. Some highland councils included Indigenous officials, but their views reach us through Spanish-language minutes written by secretaries and priests, and they cannot be read as direct statements of village opinion. The record of September–December 1821 thus shows elite negotiation clearly, while the views of most Central Americans remain mostly inferred from later events.

The provincial answers can be grouped by the options they favored: prompt independence under Guatemala City’s leadership, delay until a congress could meet, union with Mexico, or local autonomy with no commitment to either. Different groups within each province also disagreed, so provincial positions were often the position of the party in control of the council at the time.

Costa Rica’s Pact of Concord of December 1821 is the clearest example. It created an interim government and left the larger question open, a pragmatic compromise by small towns that could not enforce a choice on one another. Similar improvised arrangements appeared elsewhere, and they show how independence initially produced local provisional authorities instead of a central government.

Provincial deputations created under the Cádiz Constitution, such as those in Guatemala and León, gave local elites a legitimate forum, and they became vehicles for provincial interests in 1821. Information moved slowly by courier, so rumors about Mexican movements, Spanish reactions and neighboring provinces’ decisions influenced local debates and encouraged caution.

These provincial debates also had commercial elements. Provinces with links to Mexico through Chiapas and Soconusco, such as districts in the northwest, saw Mexican union as an economic opportunity, while those tied to Guatemala City’s credit networks worried about trade disruption. Political choice and commercial position were closely linked, which is one reason a single decision proved so hard to reach.

Section 57.6

Agustín de Iturbide and Central America’s Annexation to Mexico

The Plan of Iguala and the Treaty of Córdoba offered a model of independence under a monarchy that preserved Catholicism and property. In late 1821 Iturbide invited Central America to join, and Mexican forces moved toward Chiapas and Soconusco. Gaínza consulted town councils, and the replies were mixed: some favored annexation, some asked to wait for the promised congress, and San Salvador refused.

Arguments for annexation were practical. Guatemala City merchants and clergy hoped that Mexico, with a far larger army, could guarantee order and property and block Spanish reconquest or social revolt. Trade through Chiapas tied some districts to Mexico. Opponents objected that the decision pre-empted the congress, that Mexico City was distant, and that monarchy would restrict local autonomy, and they found support among liberals and in San Salvador.

Despite requests to wait, Gaínza proclaimed annexation on January 5, 1822. After Iturbide became emperor in May 1822, General Vicente Filísola arrived to replace Gaínza and enforce Mexican authority, and the promised Central American congress never met. Liberal critics were watched and some were arrested. Annexation was therefore an elite decision supported by force, not a unanimous provincial verdict.

Iturbide’s invitation carried an implied threat. His government had armies near the border and a stated interest in a single Mexican-led realm, and his letters suggested that delay might bring Mexican troops. Gaínza reported majority support for annexation, but historians question the count: many councils answered conditionally, asked to wait for the congress or did not answer, and the process was managed by officials who already favored annexation.

Liberals such as Barrundia and Molina opposed the decision because it destroyed the chance of a separate republic and placed the region under an emperor. Their criticism had consequences for the next phase. Annexation exposed the divisions among Central Americans, placed Mexican officers in positions of authority and set the stage for the war with San Salvador.

Language reveals how annexation was understood. Contemporaries in Guatemala often spoke of ‘union’ with Mexico, suggesting an association of equals, while Mexican officials spoke of incorporation. The difference mattered because the Guatemalan elite hoped to keep local control, and their disappointment grew once Mexican officers began to give orders.

The decision had social as well as political aims. A monarchy under a Mexican emperor was familiar to creole elites who had lived under Spanish kings, and they saw continuity in religion and law. Opponents, including Salvadoran liberals, saw the same continuity as a betrayal of independence. The junta’s vote, taken with a few dissenters, presented annexation as consensus, but the evidence shows a divided region and a decision reached in the capital.

Supporters saw annexation as a way to avoid the cost and risk of building a new state from scratch. A large Mexican realm promised a bigger market, a stronger army and a monarch whom many already trusted, and a few expected that union would bring prestige and offices for Central American men. Critics replied that the benefits would flow to Mexico City and its officers.

In practice the arrangement lasted barely a year. Mexican officers complained of unpaid troops and requisitions, and local officials resented orders from afar, while liberal critics organized opposition. The brevity of annexation shows how contingent the outcomes of 1821–1823 were, since a different course in Mexico would have produced a very different Central America.

Section 57.7

San Salvador’s Resistance and the Military Struggle over Annexation

San Salvador’s provincial authorities refused to accept the decision, and Matías Delgado and Manuel José Arce organized resistance. A first Guatemalan expedition in 1822 failed to subdue the province, which showed both Salvadoran military capacity and the limits of Guatemalan control. For Salvadorans the war combined opposition to Mexican annexation with older resentment of Guatemala City.

Filísola led a larger force of Mexican and Guatemalan troops in late 1822, and Salvadoran forces were pushed back until the Mexicans occupied San Salvador in February 1823. In December 1822 the Salvadoran assembly even voted to seek annexation to the United States, a move historians interpret as bargaining against Mexican pressure rather than a considered plan for incorporation.

Civilians paid heavily. Recruitment took men from villages, armies requisitioned maize and cattle, and indigo districts suffered disruption. Pipil and ladino communities supplied soldiers and labor, though their own motives are recorded mostly in official correspondence. The war helped to form a Salvadoran republican identity, and Salvadoran leaders later brought it to the federation’s politics.

Occupation did not secure annexation. When Iturbide fell in March 1823, Filísola found his authority undermined, and he called a new assembly rather than continuing the war.

The Salvadoran defense relied on militia, volunteers and Arce’s command, with Delgado providing political leadership and the province’s indigo revenue and forced loans providing funds. Salvadoran forces could hold their own against irregular Guatemalan columns but not against Filísola’s combined Mexican and Guatemalan army, so the strategy shifted to falling back and bargaining. The experience trained officers and political networks that later shaped federal politics, including Arce’s own military career.

Historians interpret the war in two ways. Salvadoran nationalist narratives portray the resistance as an early struggle for national liberty, while more cautious scholars stress that provincial interest, resentment of Guatemala City and liberal ideology were mixed together. The evidence, which includes military correspondence, municipal petitions and Delgado’s own writings, shows leaders defending their authority and their economy, and it shows far less of what soldiers and villagers wanted.

War also altered household and social life. Women ran estates and workshops when men were conscripted, supplied armies with food and cloth, and sheltered fugitives, though the sources record their work mostly in passing. Mexican officers complained of unpaid troops and short supplies, and their army’s own fiscal weakness limited what it could sustain in the province, which helps explain why the occupation proved so fragile.

The consequences outlasted the occupation. Salvadoran liberal lawyers, priests and militia officers formed networks during the resistance that became a core of the federal liberal faction, and the experience gave them military credibility and a sense of grievance against Guatemala City. The war therefore shaped the politics of the federation as much as the annexation itself.

Section 57.8

The Collapse of the Mexican Empire and the Declaration of July 1, 1823

Iturbide abdicated in March 1823, and Mexico’s restored congress accepted that Central America could decide its own future. On March 29, 1823 Filísola called an assembly, which met in Guatemala City in June under the presidency of Matías Delgado. Deputies came mostly from Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras and Nicaragua, and the assembly’s members were elite men who spoke for provincial councils, not for universal suffrage.

On July 1, 1823 the assembly declared absolute independence from Spain, Mexico and any other power, and it adopted the name United Provinces of Central America. It assumed sovereignty itself and placed executive authority in a small committee. This was a different settlement from 1821, because it did not merely replace Spain but defined a sovereign body.

The assembly was conciliatory toward the provinces, and its choices reflect compromises among conservatives and liberals, Guatemala and its rivals. Mexican troops withdrew in the following months, and the assembly proceeded to the abolition of slavery in April 1824 and a federal constitution. The documents record these decisions but say little about the Indigenous and rural majority, who were affected by the recruitment and taxation that followed.

The assembly’s first major act after the declaration was to organize a government. It set up a three-person executive and, in December 1823, adopted constitutional bases (Bases de Constitución) that committed the provinces to representative government. Debates ranged between conservatives who preferred a strong center and Salvadoran and other liberals who preferred a federal system, and federalism emerged as a compromise that protected provincial autonomy while keeping a union.

The assembly also claimed to speak for the nation, while the political nation was small. Its members were elected indirectly by the educated and propertied, Chiapas was absent, Costa Rica joined late, and Indigenous communities had no direct voice. The contrast with 1821 is significant: in 1821 officials issued an act under existing authority, while in 1823 a representative assembly claimed a sovereign power that no longer depended on Spain, Mexico or the old captain-general.

The assembly also adopted symbols of a new political community. In August 1823 it decreed a flag of blue and white stripes, and the name ‘Central America’ gradually replaced older provincial labels in public documents. These measures reflected an effort to create a shared identity among provinces that had known one another mainly through colonial administration.

Historians disagree about how to date independence. Some treat 1821 as the decisive break because it ended Spanish rule, while others argue that 1823 marks the true start of a distinct Central American polity, since only then did a representative body claim sovereignty. Both views are defensible if the questions are kept separate: 1821 answers who ruled, and 1823 answers who had the right to decide the form of government.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty

Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory. In the Hispanic world, sovereignty was traditionally understood as belonging to the king, and the crisis of 1808 raised the question of where it went when the king was captive. Cádiz placed it in the nation, and 1821–1823 asked which body could speak for that nation: the provinces, the capital, a congress or Mexico.

This chapter separates four things that are often confused: independence from a foreign power, control of territory, legal citizenship and effective sovereignty, meaning the ability to enforce decisions. The federation claimed sovereignty in 1823 but lacked revenue and an army to exercise it.

Section 57.9

Chiapas, Soconusco, and the Problem of Territorial Sovereignty

Chiapas had been an intendancy with its seat at Ciudad Real and a large Indigenous population of Tzotzil, Tzeltal, Ch’ol and other communities. In 1821–1822 many of its elites looked toward Mexico, but they were divided among regional centers such as Ciudad Real, Tuxtla and Comitán. After the second independence the province’s position was ambiguous, and neither Mexico nor Central America had settled it.

A provincial consultation resolved the issue. The vote was tallied on September 12, 1824, union with Mexico was proclaimed on September 14, and the Mexican Congress approved incorporation on October 2. The procedure and its representativeness were disputed, because municipal participation was uneven and Indigenous communities were counted indirectly. Chiapas became a Mexican state, and its Indigenous majority came under a new set of obligations.

Soconusco, a cacao-producing coastal district formerly attached to the Chiapas intendancy, followed a different course. Its leaders at Tapachula claimed their own status, and both the Central American federation and Mexico claimed the territory. It remained disputed and largely neutral until Mexico incorporated it on September 11, 1842. Guatemala continued to protest until the boundary treaty of September 27, 1882, in which it renounced claims to Chiapas and Soconusco.

The two cases show that boundaries were produced by local politics, economic ties and external pressure. A colonial jurisdiction did not automatically become a national territory, and Chiapas and Soconusco could follow different paths even though they had been linked under Spain.

The Chiapas case also shows rival principles of territorial claim. Central American leaders could cite the colonial boundaries of the Kingdom of Guatemala, Mexico cited the will of Chiapan authorities and the practical weight of its army, and Chiapas’s own elites divided among regional centers. Because Indigenous communities, a large majority, were consulted only indirectly, the plebiscite expressed the preferences of notables as much as those of the province’s population. Chiapas then organized as a Mexican state.

Soconusco was valuable because it produced cacao and lay on the Pacific route between Chiapas and Guatemala. Negotiations between Mexico and Central American governments failed to resolve its status in the 1820s and 1830s, and local authorities in Tapachula maintained a precarious independence. Mexico’s 1842 incorporation was backed by force, and Guatemala’s later acceptance in 1882 shows that borders were finally settled by treaty long after the independence era.

Chiapas’s economy of cacao, cattle, textiles and Indigenous labor looked toward Oaxaca and Tabasco as much as Guatemala, and incorporation into Mexico changed its relationship to church fees, law and taxation. Mexican law removed colonial caste categories and formally ended tribute, but local elites retained control of land and labor, so continuity in rural life was strong even as the state changed.

Soconusco’s importance was strategic and economic. For Guatemala it was a coastal outlet and cacao district, and for Mexico it was part of Chiapas’s Pacific frontier. Because each side could cite a different principle, the dispute could not be resolved by argument, and the decades of delay show how the lack of an effective authority on the ground allowed a claim to remain open.

Division III — Creating the Federal Republic of Central America, 1823–1826

Section 57.10

The Federal Constitution of 1824 and the Principles of Republican Union

The Federal Constitution of November 22, 1824 created a republic of five states: Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and Costa Rica. Its drafters drew on the Cádiz constitution, the United States Constitution and French models, and they combined national institutions with strong state autonomy. States were to be free and independent in their internal government, and each was to adopt its own constitution, so sovereignty was divided between the union and its members.

The federal government had a congress of deputies apportioned by population, a senate in which each state had equal representation, a president and vice president, and a federal supreme court. Officials were chosen through indirect elections, so a small group of electors and local notables decided national offices. Article 11 made Catholicism the sole recognized religion, reflecting the Church’s social weight.

The system worked through the states. Federal laws needed state officials to carry them out, and the executive had few agents of its own in the provinces. The presidential election of 1825 showed the difficulty: José Cecilio del Valle won a plurality but not a majority, and the federal congress chose Manuel José Arce. The outcome was constitutional but weakened trust in the arrangement, since many supporters of Valle felt that the choice had been made in the legislature, not at the polls.

The constitution was a serious attempt at union, not a mere formality. Its framers wanted to prevent a return to centralized rule from Guatemala City while keeping a single country, and they hoped that representation and equal state votes would balance provincial interests. The structure’s test was whether the states would accept federal decisions that cost them revenue or autonomy.

Procedures show how the system was meant to work. The executive was chosen by indirect election, and if no candidate won a majority Congress selected from the leading names, as happened in 1825. The Senate sanctioned resolutions of Congress and advised and supervised the executive, while state constitutions had to conform to the federal charter. Under Articles 136–137, the Supreme Court heard specified federal cases and played a role in interstate controversies, with arbitration and further review procedures; other constitutional disputes between state authorities followed procedures involving the Senate and Congress under Article 194.

Why federalism? Salvadoran liberals wanted a structure that would restrain Guatemala City, conservatives accepted it as protection for regional elites, and the Cádiz tradition of local councils and the example of the United States provided models. Historians disagree about whether a federal system suited a poor and sparsely populated region or whether it multiplied governments that the region could not afford. The states’ constitutions, adopted between 1824 and 1826, differed in detail, so the union rested on shared principles but unequal institutions.

The constitution also set out guarantees of individual liberty, property and legal process, and it was proclaimed in towns with ceremonies in which officials and priests read it aloud. It was approved by the assembly and not submitted to a popular vote, so its authority rested on the representativeness of the men who drafted it. A process of constitutional reform began in the mid-1830s, which shows that its framers had already recognized weaknesses in the design.

The text mattered even where it was not applied. Municipal officials, litigants and journalists cited it in disputes, and Indigenous towns learned of its provisions through officials and parish priests. The distance between a constitution’s language and its enforcement was therefore a political fact in its own right, and later liberals and conservatives could both claim to defend it.

Section 57.11

Guatemala and El Salvador: Competing Political Centers

Guatemala was the most populous state and the site of the federal capital, which gave its leaders both political and commercial advantages. Within Guatemala, conservatives linked to the Aycinena merchant family and liberals such as Pedro Molina and José Francisco Barrundia competed for control of state and federal offices. Merchant credit, church influence and access to the capital gave conservatives strength, while liberals found support among professionals, journalists and provincial allies.

El Salvador’s leaders had been shaped by the anti-annexation war. Juan Manuel Rodríguez, Delgado and Arce represented a Salvadoran political class that wanted equal weight in the union and resented Guatemalan merchants’ control over indigo credit. In 1822 the Salvadoran assembly named Delgado bishop, which challenged the Guatemalan archbishop’s authority, and the dispute showed how religion and regional politics could combine.

Because the federal capital lay inside Guatemala, Salvadorans suspected that federal officials favored the host state. Guatemalans, for their part, regarded Salvadoran liberalism as radical and Salvadoran resistance as a threat to federal authority. In 1834 the federal capital was moved to San Salvador, which gave El Salvador the symbolic center but also exposed the federation to Guatemalan resentment. The rivalry between the two centers shaped almost every major crisis of the federal period.

Competition also had a commercial side. Guatemala City merchants controlled imports and credit, while Salvadoran growers wanted direct trade through Pacific ports such as Acajutla and La Unión on the Gulf of Fonseca. Federal and state offices in Guatemala City duplicated each other and quarreled over funds, troops and public order, and the capital’s dual role as federal seat and state capital made every dispute personal and urgent.

The religious dispute illustrated the same tensions. Salvadorans insisted on their own bishop in San Salvador, and Archbishop Casaus refused to recognize Delgado’s appointment. Rome did not validate Delgado’s appointment; the separate Diocese of San Salvador was established by papal authority on September 28, 1842. A quarrel that appeared to concern church law thus expressed the larger struggle over whether Guatemala City would remain the center of authority.

The rivalry cannot be reduced to liberals against conservatives. Guatemala had its own liberals, including Molina and Barrundia, and El Salvador had conservative landowners and priests who feared radical reform. Regional interest and local alliance often mattered more than ideology, and political groups reformed when federal policy changed.

Social composition also differed. Guatemala City’s elite combined merchants, officials and old landed families, while the Salvadoran political class included small producers, lawyers and priests drawn from provincial towns. These differences affected how each side judged the federal system, since Guatemalan elites tended to see federalism as a threat to commercial order and Salvadorans as a safeguard against metropolitan dominance.

Section 57.12

Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica within the Federation

Honduras was a poor and sparsely populated state whose economy depended on cattle, small-scale mining and Caribbean trade through ports such as Omoa. Dionisio de Herrera, a liberal lawyer, served as its first head of state (1824–1827), but state authority was weakened by distance and by rivalry between Comayagua and Tegucigalpa. Honduras was often drawn into the quarrels of its larger neighbors.

Nicaragua suffered the most violent internal politics. León, with its bishop and old administrative role, and Granada, with its merchants and trade route through Lake Nicaragua and the San Juan river, competed for control. Armed conflict between factions and militia leaders lasted from 1824 to 1827, and the federal government intervened under Arce, an action that pulled it into a provincial war.

Costa Rica followed a quieter path. After clashes between Cartago and the towns of the central valley in 1823, the state’s center shifted toward San José. Juan Mora Fernández, head of state from 1825 to 1833, strengthened local institutions and promoted coffee through incentives such as land grants. Costa Rica’s small, distant, mostly ladino and mixed population and its limited elite conflict made it less dependent on federal arbitration, though it still participated in federal debates.

The three states show that the federation was not one experience but several. Each had its own economy, elites and internal quarrels, and shared republican language did not remove those differences.

Honduras illustrates the weakness of a small state in a federal system. Its population was small and dispersed among mining towns, cattle districts and Caribbean ports such as Omoa and Trujillo, and its revenues were meager. Yet it supplied the soldiers and the leader, Morazán, who later reshaped the federation. Garifuna settlements on the northern coast lay largely outside state control, and the Honduran constitution of 1825 governed a territory that officials could hardly reach.

Nicaragua’s conflicts had commercial roots. León and Granada controlled different trade routes, and the route through Lake Nicaragua and the San Juan River attracted British and later American interest, which linked local rivalry to foreign power. The labels of liberal León and conservative Granada took shape in these years. Costa Rica, by contrast, expanded small-scale farming and coffee, introduced late in the colonial period, and its elites, who had fought at Ochomogo in 1823, came to value stable local government more than federal arbitration.

Why did these states join the federation at all? After Mexico’s empire collapsed, independence on a provincial scale would have left each state exposed to larger neighbors and foreign pressure, and union offered legitimacy, a larger market and a shared defense. Elites in smaller states, such as Costa Rica, valued union partly as protection against domination by larger rivals, though they did not wish to pay for it.

Local conditions also affected how federal rule was received. Costa Rica’s Fundamental Law of 1825 and its early assemblies gave the state a stable constitutional order, while Nicaragua’s Mosquito Coast frontier and Honduras’s timber and Garifuna coast exposed both to British activity that the federation could not control. The three states thus faced different external pressures that shaped their politics as much as federal law did.

Section 57.13

Federal Institutions, Taxation, Military Power, and the Catholic Church

The federation’s finances were its most serious institutional weakness. States retained customs revenue, the tobacco and liquor monopolies and most other taxes, and federal revenue depended on contributions apportioned among them. The states often withheld payments because they needed money for their own governments and militias, so the federal treasury was chronically short.

To address the shortfall the federation turned to London. A loan was authorized in December 1824 and offered in 1825 through Barclay, Herring, Richardson & Co. The nominal amount was much larger than the proceeds that reached the government, because of discounts, commissions and the way the bonds were sold. The federation nevertheless owed the full obligation, and the loan became a long-term debt after the bond market and the bank’s fortunes deteriorated in the financial crisis of those years.

Military power reflected the same problem. The federal army was small and poorly paid, and states retained militias that obeyed their own governments and leaders. Authority therefore passed to commanders who could raise troops, which explains why civil war, not legislative bargaining, became the decisive mechanism in 1826–1829.

The Catholic Church complicated these tensions. It owned land, collected tithes and shaped public life, and Archbishop Ramón Casaus y Torres, a conservative, resisted liberal measures. Liberals saw clerical privileges as a barrier to a modern republic, while many priests and rural communities defended them. The Church thus became both an institution of the old order and a political party in the making.

The federal quota system explains the shortfall. Congress assigned each state a share of federal expenses, but states paid irregularly, and the wars of 1826–1829 made collection harder. Records of the London loan’s use are incomplete, and historians agree mainly that little of the borrowed sum became durable public investment. Payments stopped within a few years, and the unpaid debt lingered as a grievance in later relations with British creditors.

Military recruitment worked through levies on villages and towns. Officers such as Manuel Arzú commanded federal forces, but soldiers were often paid late and fed by requisition, and both Indigenous and ladino communities bore the burden. The Church, meanwhile, collected tithes through state officials, owned land through convents and brotherhoods, and supplied educated men for public office, so its position was inseparable from the fiscal and social order that liberals wanted to reform.

Trade policy added another strain. British goods entered through Belize and Caribbean ports and competed with local textiles, and artisans and small producers complained that new commerce undermined their livelihoods. The federation had no common tariff policy strong enough to answer these complaints, so regional merchants and artisans each judged union by their own interests.

Conscription also had social costs. Villages were required to supply men for federal and state forces, and desertion was common because soldiers were poorly fed and paid. The Church’s role in schools and charity meant that disputes over its property also touched education and poor relief, so questions of finance, religion and military service were intertwined in ordinary life.

Section 57.14

Citizenship, Indigenous Communities, Slavery, and the Social Limits of Republican Equality

Article 13 declared that people in the republic were free and that slave traders could not be citizens, and the Constituent Assembly had decreed abolition of slavery in April 1824. Slavery was a small institution compared with Cuba or Brazil, which made abolition less costly, but it still mattered legally and symbolically. The contrast with the Spanish Caribbean, where slavery grew after independence, is one of the chapter’s central comparisons.

Article 14 extended citizenship to native-born or naturalized inhabitants who were married or over eighteen and had a useful occupation or recognized means of subsistence. These were constitutional conditions, not a literacy or property test, but actual political participation was narrower. Indirect elections, local notables and unequal access to schooling and office meant that voting and public service were largely confined to men of some means, and women did not take part in them.

Indigenous communities faced a double change. The colonial legal category of the Indian republic and its protections were dismantled in the name of equality, while communal lands, local office and customary labor obligations came under pressure. Several states kept direct levies under new names, and rural people could still be compelled to work. Garifuna and Afro-descendant communities on the Caribbean coast, and the Miskitu farther east, lay largely outside effective state control, and their history is poorly documented.

The result was legal equality without social equality. Republican language promised citizenship, but wealth, region, ethnicity, gender and literacy shaped who could use it, and the later liberal reforms of the 1830s would deepen the conflict over land and community rights.

The abolition of slavery in 1824 can be compared with other cases. Mexico abolished it in 1829, Brazil not until 1888 and Cuba in 1886, so Central America acted early, in part because enslaved people were few. This does not make the decision trivial: it set a legal principle and marked a break with the colonial order, but it said nothing about the labor demands placed on free workers or about the economic power of landholders.

Women appear in the records as property holders, litigants, market sellers, household heads and managers of estates when widowed, yet they were absent from voting and office. Indigenous communities lost the special courts and protections of the colonial Indian republic in the name of equality, so disputes over land and labor now fell under general law that favored those with property, schooling and local influence. This legal change helps explain why rural people later viewed liberal projects with suspicion.

Rural labor regulation shows how formal freedom coexisted with coercion. Debts and advances tied workers to indigo and other estates, and vagrancy regulations in several states required people without land to show employment, so landless men could be compelled to work for estate owners. These rules rarely appeared in constitutional debates but shaped daily life more than the language of rights.

Identity categories were changing too. The colonial label of Indian was losing legal force while the category of ladino grew, and Indigenous town leaders adjusted by using the new language of equality and by allying with local officials or rival factions. The record shows people of African descent serving in militias and working in ports and on estates, and they fell within the formal rights of citizens while facing informal discrimination.

Division IV — Federal Conflict and the Beginnings of Fragmentation, 1826–1840

Section 57.15

Manuel José Arce and the First Major Federal Political Crisis

Manuel José Arce, a Salvadoran who had fought against annexation, became federal president in 1825 after Congress chose him over Valle. He began with liberal support but came to depend on conservative Guatemalan merchants and officials, and his shifting alliances alienated those who had elected him. Many liberals concluded that the president was turning the federal executive against the principles of 1824.

The first major crisis grew from a question the constitution had left open: when could federal authority override a state? In 1826 Arce used federal troops against state authorities in Guatemala, where the liberal head of state José Francisco Barrundia was removed and conservative influence strengthened. Federal forces also intervened in Honduras and Nicaragua, treating the disputes of those states as threats to the union.

These actions were defended as protecting order and punished by opponents as violations of state sovereignty. El Salvador, under a head of state who broke with Arce, became the center of resistance, and Honduran liberals mobilized in defense of their own state’s government. Federal power thus appeared to Salvadorans and Hondurans as Guatemalan power under a Salvadoran president.

The crisis showed the federation’s central dilemma. A weak federal executive could not enforce decisions, while a strong one could look like a conqueror of the states. Because the federal army was small and loyalties were personal, the dispute turned quickly from constitutional argument into war.

Arce’s conflicts included quarrels with Congress over appointments and executive power and with state governments such as El Salvador, where head of state Mariano Prado opposed him. His supporters presented the use of troops as necessary to defend the union, while critics called it a coup against state sovereignty. The balance of constitutional argument is mixed because the 1824 text did not clearly settle when federal force could intervene.

Historians have judged him differently. Older liberal narratives treated him as a traitor to his own party, while more recent accounts stress that he governed in a fiscal crisis with hostile states and few resources, and that he sought a stronger center. Evidence comes from Arce’s later justification, from the accounts of liberal opponents and from official correspondence, so every version was produced by a participant with reasons to defend his own role.

The question of the federal capital sharpened the crisis. Because Guatemala City was both the seat of the union and the capital of the largest state, Arce’s use of troops there raised the issue of who controlled public order in the federal capital, and a separate federal district had not yet been created. His reliance on loans and goodwill from Guatemalan merchants made it harder for him to present himself as a neutral arbiter.

The controversy also shows the difficulty of defining federal authority in a young system. Defenders argued that a union could not survive if states could defy it, while opponents argued that a federation could not exist if the president could overthrow state governments. Neither argument was frivolous, and both are found in the surviving pamphlets and official letters of 1826–1827.

Section 57.16

Civil War, Francisco Morazán, and the Defense of Federal Union

The civil war of 1826–1829 pitted the federal government and Guatemalan conservatives against a coalition of liberal states. Francisco Morazán, a Honduran liberal who had served in state government, emerged as the coalition’s military leader. His victory at La Trinidad in November 1827 defeated a federal force and gave him control of Honduras, and he then led Honduran and Salvadoran troops against Guatemala.

In April 1829 Morazán’s army entered Guatemala City. The conservative leadership was removed, Arce was captured and forced out, and many conservative officials and clerics were expelled or jailed. The victory was won by an army made up of Honduran and Salvadoran soldiers, mestizo and ladino officers and provincial volunteers, and it reshaped the balance of power among the states and between liberals and conservatives.

Morazán became federal president in 1830 and was reelected in 1834, serving until 1839. He defended federal union, encouraged trade and education, and tried to build a reliable federal administration. Yet his authority rested on armed force and on cooperation with provincial liberals, and his victory left a defeated conservative minority with local influence, Church support and a grievance.

Historians differ on how to judge him. Admirers see a principled union builder, while critics note that he relied on military power and that liberal reforms provoked resistance. Both views agree that the war of 1826–1829 weakened the idea that disputes within the union could be settled by constitutional means.

Morazán’s army, called the Allied Army Protecting the Law (Ejército Aliado Protector de la Ley), was a coalition of Honduran and Salvadoran forces and volunteers, and it won through mobility and local support more than through superior equipment. After taking Guatemala City in April 1829, the victors jailed or expelled prominent conservatives and members of the Aycinena family and seized property, which turned a political victory into a lasting family and religious grievance.

As federal president, Morazán promoted ports, roads and commerce to reduce dependence on the Guatemalan trading elite, and in 1834 he moved the capital to San Salvador. Yet his control was never complete. Guatemala’s conservatives remained influential, state governments resisted federal direction, and Costa Rican leaders increasingly governed on their own terms. His career shows both the strengths of a charismatic soldier-politician and the fragility of authority that depended on him.

Morazán’s background shaped his politics. Born in Tegucigalpa in 1792, he came from a provincial family, trained as a clerk and administrator and rose through state government, so he saw federation as a protection for provinces against Guatemala City. His liberalism combined church-state separation, jury trials, commercial opening and public education with a belief that these reforms would create a modern republic, a program that many provincial elites shared and many rural people did not.

His historical reputation has also been shaped by memory. Honduras and El Salvador honor him as a founding hero, and a Honduran department bears his name, while conservative historians in Guatemala long portrayed him as an invader. These images are useful reminders that national traditions were built after the federation broke up and that the same events could support opposing stories.

Section 57.17

Liberal Reform, Religious Conflict, Rafael Carrera, and Rural Mobilization

After 1829 liberal governments, especially in Guatemala under head of state Mariano Gálvez (1831–1838), pursued reforms that changed religion, law and economy. They expelled Archbishop Casaus y Torres and the religious orders, ended the compulsory collection of the tithe, introduced civil marriage and divorce, and adopted jury trials and codes inspired by Edward Livingston’s Louisiana codes. Supporters saw modernization, while many priests and villagers saw an attack on the moral order.

The reforms also affected taxes and land. Gálvez’s government imposed a direct tax in 1836 and encouraged colonization contracts with foreign companies, and rural communities feared that their land and labor were at risk. Indigenous towns, ladino peasants and parish priests each had reasons to resent officials who seemed to side with distant elites against local custom and the Church.

In 1837 a cholera epidemic struck Guatemala. Rumors that officials or foreign doctors had poisoned the water spread among rural people and were encouraged by priests who opposed the government. The uprising that followed in the eastern region of Mita was led by Rafael Carrera, a ladino with ties to peasants and priests who emerged as a military leader and presented himself as defender of religion and community.

Carrera’s movement combined religious conviction, resistance to taxation, defense of land and local grievances, and it later allied with conservative merchants and clergy. It was neither a simple restoration of colonial rule nor a purely personal ambition. It showed that liberal reform without rural consent could create an armed popular opposition, and that Central American politics was not only a contest among elites.

The reforms collided with daily life. Jury trials and new legal codes, adopted in Guatemala in 1836–1837, were unfamiliar to villagers who had few lawyers and relied on interpreters, and civil marriage and new cemetery rules intruded on practices managed by priests. Health measures during the cholera epidemic, including burial and quarantine rules, interfered with funeral customs, so rumors that outsiders were poisoning communities fell on prepared ground.

Carrera’s uprising began in Mita in 1837, and government troops answered with burned villages that widened the revolt. By early 1838 he forced negotiations in Guatemala City, Gálvez resigned, and Mariano Rivera Paz became head of state under Carrera’s influence. Once in power, the Carrera government restored religious orders and some colonial-style protections for Indigenous communities, which helps explain why many villagers supported him even though he was neither a village leader nor a social revolutionary.

The government’s own policies fed the rumors. A colonization contract of 1836 with a British-backed company promised land on the Caribbean coast, and villagers feared that foreigners and liberals were taking their land, while cholera, which reached Guatemala in 1837, killed thousands. Priests and rural leaders linked the epidemic to the reforms, and the explanation made sense in a society where religion and community life were closely tied.

Historians have changed their view of Carrera. Older liberal narratives portrayed him as an illiterate barbarian, while later studies, such as Ralph Lee Woodward’s biography, emphasize rural grievances and the protection he offered to villagers against liberal land and tax policies. A fair judgment recognizes both elements: his government protected some communities and the Church, and it also sustained a regime that excluded liberal critics and relied on military power.

Section 57.18

The Disintegration of the Federation, 1838–1840

The federation did not collapse in a day. By 1837–1838 Morazán’s government was fighting Carrera’s rural forces in Guatemala, facing political opposition in the states, and losing control of federal finances. On April 30, 1838 Nicaragua declared its separation, and on May 30 the federal congress authorized states to organize themselves as they saw fit, which in practice removed the legal basis for a single government.

Honduras declared its separation in October 1838 and Costa Rica in November. Guatemala, where Carrera’s forces had pressed the capital, separated in April 1839 under a conservative-led assembly. El Salvador remained the most committed to union, and Morazán defended it there. In March 1840 Carrera’s army defeated Morazán in Guatemala City, and Morazán went into exile.

Disintegration involved several causes working together: fiscal weakness, the militarization of politics, regional rivalry, religious conflict and rural revolt. No single cause was decisive, and contemporaries differed over whether the union had failed or been destroyed. Efforts to restore it continued, and Morazán himself returned in 1842, but the five states increasingly developed separate political lives, a story continued in Chapter 62.

Morazán’s final campaign shows the combination of local and national pressures. He defended the union from El Salvador while Carrera, allied with conservatives and with hostile forces in neighboring states, pressed Guatemala City, and in March 1840 Morazán was defeated there. He went into exile, returned to Costa Rica in 1842 and was executed in San José in September of that year, an episode that belongs to the later phase of the story (Chapter 62).

The end of the federation did not end the idea of union. Short-lived attempts to restore it were made in the 1840s, and later generations remembered Morazán as a symbol of Central American unity. Yet the separate states had developed their own institutions, armies and elites, and the break-up shows how quickly a political community can come to be defined by its parts. Historians therefore treat the federation neither as a doomed experiment nor as an unrealized nation, but as a real project whose chance of success depended on choices made between 1826 and 1840.

The separations were declared by state assemblies that argued the federation had failed to protect them or to provide order. Their declarations asserted sovereignty but often left open the possibility of future union, which explains why short-lived confederation efforts followed in the 1840s. Most boundaries between the new states followed the colonial provincial lines, so the breakup tended to restore the units that had existed under Spanish rule.

The legacy was ambiguous. Central Americans continued to speak of a shared nation and attempted unions in the 1840s and again in the 1890s, yet each republic developed its own elites, armies and tax systems. The federation’s story therefore asks students to avoid two mistakes: reading the five republics back into 1823 as inevitable nations, and treating union as a lost certainty that only bad leaders prevented.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Central American Federation Disintegrate?

The question: What explains the separation of the states in 1838–1840, and how should historians weigh long-term conditions against decisions made at the time?

Structural explanations: Mountains, poor communications, small markets and separate regional economies gave each province its own interests, and the federal government lacked reliable revenue. The London loan left debt, and states withheld contributions.
Institutional explanations: The 1824 constitution left unclear when federal force could override a state, relied on indirect elections and gave the federal executive few agents of its own. Because the capital lay inside Guatemala, federal and state offices competed.
Political and contingent explanations: Arce’s shift toward conservatives, the civil war of 1826–1829, Morazán’s reliance on armed force and Gálvez’s rapid reforms turned grievances into open conflict. Different choices might have produced different outcomes.
Religious, social and regional explanations: Liberal measures against the Church and the 1837 cholera epidemic mobilized rural communities behind Carrera, while rivalries between Guatemala and El Salvador and between León and Granada made compromise difficult.

Evidence and how to assess it: Historians use the federal and state constitutions, fiscal and loan records, official correspondence, pamphlets and the decrees of 1838–1839, and they compare the timing of events. One test is whether structural conditions present in 1824 also existed when union worked; another is whether the same political choices would have mattered without the fiscal and institutional weaknesses. Sources written by participants, such as Arce’s defense or liberal and conservative pamphlets, must be read as arguments.

Why it matters: The strongest accounts treat conditions and decisions as interacting. Structure limited what leaders could do, and leaders’ choices determined which limits became decisive.

Section 57.19

Cuba as an Imperial Stronghold: Havana, Trade, and Spanish Military Authority

Havana was Spain’s great Caribbean fortress, naval arsenal and commercial port. After the British captured the city for eleven months in 1762, the Crown invested in fortifications such as La Cabaña, expanded the garrison and organized militias that included battalions of free men of color. Military institutions thus became part of Cuban social life, and they gave the colonial state both defenders and a stake in its survival.

The collapse of Spain’s mainland empire raised Cuba’s importance. Royalist officials, soldiers and families from Venezuela, Mexico and elsewhere sought refuge in Havana and Puerto Rico, and the end of the situado, the subsidy from New Spain, forced Cuba to rely on its own revenue. Customs duties and the sugar economy made the island increasingly valuable to Spain, so its defense became a central imperial priority.

Captain-General Francisco Dionisio Vives (1823–1832) governed through the extraordinary powers granted in 1825 and a standing military commission that handled political crimes. The system rested on speed and fear: suspects could be tried without ordinary judicial procedures, and dissent was treated as a security matter. Colonial government was therefore military in form, whatever its constitutional language.

Local elites supported this order for reasons of their own. Creole planters and merchants, led by figures such as Francisco de Arango y Parreño, had lobbied for free trade and slave imports, and the 1818 opening of Cuban trade, pushed by intendant Alejandro Ramírez, increased their wealth. Cuban loyalty was thus a bargain: Spain protected property, slavery and trade, and planters accepted military authority and limited political voice.

Havana’s shipyards had built great warships such as the Santísima Trinidad (launched 1769), and its arsenal, garrison and customs house made it a state within the colony. In 1832 the Crown replaced the old merchant guild with a Junta de Fomento that promoted roads, railways and commerce, which linked state policy to planter interests. Free battalions of color served in the city’s militias, giving many families a stake in the imperial order while keeping them under military discipline.

Foreign powers also helped keep Cuba Spanish. In 1823 John Quincy Adams, then U.S. secretary of state, described Cuba as a fruit that would eventually fall to the United States, and both the United States and Britain preferred Spanish control to a Bolivarian republic or to a rival power. Cuban elites knew this, and many reasoned that independence would probably invite either foreign domination or slave revolution.

Regional variation within Cuba mattered. The west, around Havana and Matanzas, concentrated sugar and slavery, while eastern Cuba, centered on Santiago de Cuba, had coffee, cattle and a larger share of free people of color, and its leaders often felt distant from Havana’s decisions. Spanish authorities also encouraged white immigration, including Canary Islanders who became tobacco farmers, in the hope of balancing the island’s racial composition.

This pattern helps explain political caution. Elites in the west depended on military order and imperial markets, while eastern towns had fewer ties to the sugar economy and more reasons to resent Havana’s control. These differences created fault lines that would surface in later wars (Chapter 67), but in the 1820s and 1830s they were obscured by the constant presence of the garrison.

Section 57.20

Sugar, the Atlantic Slave Trade, the Haitian Revolution’s Legacy, and Cuban Society

The Haitian Revolution reshaped the Cuban economy. After Saint-Domingue’s plantations were destroyed in the 1790s, Cuban planters expanded sugar and coffee production to supply markets that Haiti had left. The 1789 liberalization of the slave trade, together with new capital and technical knowledge from French refugees, helped mills multiply around Havana and Matanzas. By the 1830s Cuba was one of the world’s leading sugar producers.

The labor of enslaved Africans made this growth possible. The Anglo-Spanish treaty of 1817 banned the slave trade from 1820, but the traffic continued illegally with the connivance of officials, and tens of thousands were brought to the island. Enslaved people came from many regions of Africa, including Yoruba (Lucumí), Kongo, Carabalí and Mandinga groups, and in Havana they formed mutual-aid associations called cabildos de nación. Plantation work involved long harvest hours, mill accidents and harsh discipline, and resistance included flight, sabotage and revolt.

Technology and transport changed the countryside too. Steam engines were introduced in the 1820s, and the first Cuban railway opened in 1837, linking sugar districts to Havana. These changes tied planters to British and American markets and to credit, while the growing enslaved majority in sugar districts made them fear rebellion.

Haiti’s example therefore cut two ways. It opened markets and capital, but it also taught planters that a political break with Spain could become a slave revolution. Free people of color, many of them artisans, militiamen and small property owners, occupied a precarious middle position, benefiting from military service and trades yet vulnerable to suspicion. This social structure explains why independence found fewer allies among Cuban elites than on the mainland.

Sugar estates, called ingenios, grew in size with steam mills and new equipment, and their labor force was heavily male. High mortality and an unbalanced sex ratio meant that the enslaved population did not reproduce itself, so planters depended on new arrivals from Africa. Contemporaries accused officials of profiting from the illegal traffic, which made enforcement of the 1817 treaty a matter of imperial politics.

Enslaved people resisted in many ways. Plantation revolts occurred in the 1820s and 1830s, and runaways formed communities (palenques) in the eastern mountains. A substantial minority of the population were free people of color, and many occupied skilled trades. Their position helped keep Cuban society stratified and made racial fear a constant element in elite politics, since planters often connected free Black organization with rebellion.

Enslaved people had legal and religious avenues that shaped their lives. Spanish law allowed self-purchase by installments (coartación), and many tried to save money for freedom through skilled work, market sales and urban labor, though the path was open to few. In the cabildos de nación and Catholic brotherhoods, people of different African origins preserved languages and religious practices, including Yoruba-derived traditions that fed into later Afro-Cuban religion.

Labor was also gendered. Women worked in cane fields, in households and as laundresses and market sellers, and their reproductive labor was affected by plantation demands. Planters relied on credit from Havana merchants and foreign lenders, so the plantation economy tied the island’s social order to financial networks that reached London and the United States.

Section 57.21

Cuban Reformism, Resistance, Conspiracies, and the Limits of Independence Movements

Resistance took several forms. José Antonio Aponte, a free Black carpenter and former militia corporal in Havana, helped lead the conspiracy of 1812, which connected risings in several districts including Puerto Príncipe, Holguín and Bayamo. Authorities executed Aponte and others in April 1812 and used his paintings and papers as evidence, so we know his aims largely through the prosecution.

Other opposition came from white creole circles. The Soles y Rayos de Bolívar conspiracy (1821–1823), led by José Francisco Lemus, aimed at a republic, and Bolivarian governments in Colombia and Mexico considered expeditions to Cuba in the mid-1820s. The United States and Britain opposed such expeditions, and many Cuban planters feared that Bolivarian liberation would threaten slavery.

Reform was a third current. Félix Varela, a priest and philosopher, represented Cuba in the Cortes of 1822–1823 and later wrote from exile in the United States. José Antonio Saco, a leading intellectual, criticized Spanish policy and the slave trade, and was exiled in 1834 by Captain-General Miguel Tacón, who ruled strictly from 1834 to 1838. In 1836 General Manuel Lorenzo proclaimed the 1812 constitution in Santiago de Cuba, and Tacón expelled him.

These movements did not share a program. Aponte’s circle sought liberty and change for free and enslaved Black people, Lemus sought a republic, Varela and Saco sought reform within the empire, and many planters sought only security. Cuba under Spain was therefore not politically passive, but opposition was divided by race, slavery and fear of social upheaval.

Aponte’s papers, as reported by the prosecution, included images of Africa and of Black rulers and references to Haiti, which suggests that his movement was shaped by Atlantic as well as local ideas. Because the case is known through trial records, historians must read around the authorities’ interests, and they continue to debate how large the conspiracy was and how much of it the court invented or exaggerated.

The 1830s brought a more literary and reformist climate. Domingo del Monte’s circle encouraged writers and critics, and the enslaved poet Juan Francisco Manzano, freed in 1836, wrote an autobiography in 1835 at del Monte’s request that became an important testimony of slavery. Planters, reformers and Black Cubans thus held different ideas of Cuba’s future, and all faced a government that treated political dissent as a security matter.

Reformers faced hard limits. Varela’s writings criticized slavery and urged gradual emancipation, but few planters accepted such ideas. Saco opposed the slave trade yet favored white immigration to change the island’s racial balance, a position that shows how reform could combine criticism of Spanish policy with fear of a Black majority.

Fear of rebellion shaped every later movement. Authorities and planters suspected free people of color of conspiracy whenever unrest appeared, and repression culminated in the harsh response to the so-called Escalera conspiracy in 1843–1844, which lies beyond this chapter. For the period to 1840, the key point is that Spanish rule survived partly through division among its opponents and partly through the shared fear of social upheaval.

Section 57.22

Puerto Rico’s Changing Economy: The 1815 Royal Decree of Graces, Migration, and Slavery

The Royal Decree of Graces (Real Cédula de Gracias) of August 10, 1815 offered land grants, trade concessions and tax relief to foreign Catholic settlers from nations friendly to Spain. It was part of a program of intendant Alejandro Ramírez, who served in Puerto Rico from 1813 to 1816, to promote commerce after the mainland wars. Settlers came from Spain, Venezuela, Santo Domingo, France and elsewhere, and royalist refugees added to the island’s conservative character.

Economic change followed. Sugar plantations spread along the southern and eastern coasts, especially around Ponce, while coffee expanded in the highlands, and enslaved Africans were imported to work estates. Puerto Rico’s plantation system was smaller than Cuba’s and slavery was never as dominant, because free peasants, small farmers and day laborers made up much of the population. Landless workers faced pressure to work for estates, a pattern later formalized in labor regulations (see Chapter 67).

The island remained a military outpost under governors with wide powers. Miguel de la Torre (1822–1837) governed through the discretionary authority extended after the 1823 return to absolutism. Bolivarian agents and Puerto Rican sympathizers conspired in the 1820s but never launched an effective rising, and the growing economy tied many landowners to Spain.

Evidence about ordinary Puerto Ricans is thinner than for Cuba’s dramatic conspiracies. Municipal records, parish registers and plantation papers show a society of free and enslaved workers and small farmers, but the voices of the poor appear mostly through officials and priests.

Trade changed along with immigration. The island sold sugar, molasses and coffee to merchants from the United States and shipped goods through the nearby Danish port of St. Thomas, which served as a transshipment point and a channel for contraband. Newcomers brought capital and contacts, and the Crown hoped that commerce would bind the island to Spain, while the arrival of Venezuelan royalist refugees added families who opposed the mainland revolutions.

Slavery grew in this setting, and regulations issued in the 1820s tried to control enslaved workers and discipline free laborers. Highland farmers and landless day laborers were pressed to work for estates, and a society of free people of color, small proprietors and estate owners developed. The extent of the plantation economy was smaller than Cuba’s, but its social effects were still large, because it tied growth to coerced labor and to the political security that Spanish rule seemed to provide.

Fiscal change also shaped the island. After Mexican independence ended the situado that had financed the colonial government, Puerto Rico had to rely on local customs duties and taxes, which encouraged officials to promote exports and commerce. Ponce became a center of sugar and trade, Mayagüez and highland towns grew with coffee, and landless agregados lived on estates in exchange for labor or small plots.

This rural society produced a different politics from the plantation west of Cuba. Small farmers, estate owners, merchants and officials depended on one another, and the economy rewarded loyalty to the colonial state, while the absence of the huge slave majority that existed in Cuban sugar districts shaped how people thought about reform and abolition.

Section 57.23

Puerto Rico, the Cádiz Constitution, Ramón Power y Giralt, and Colonial Reform

Ramón Power y Giralt, a naval officer from San Juan, was elected to the Cádiz Cortes and chosen vice president on September 25, 1810. He pressed for reforms in trade and local government and for equal treatment of American subjects, and he became a symbol of the possibility of representation within the empire. He died in Spain in June 1813.

Cádiz’s elected councils and provincial deputations gave Puerto Ricans experience in electoral politics. It also limited citizenship for people of African descent, who could petition for it only through merit, a restriction that bore heavily on island societies. Representation for American territories remained unequal in the Cortes, so equality was promised more than delivered.

Constitutional rule was suspended in 1814, restored in 1820 and ended again in 1823, so each reform could be reversed. Despite this, reformers used petitions, newspapers, municipal councils and their allies in Madrid to seek local autonomy and fair taxation. Representation was a real alternative to separation, and it persisted as a political language even when the Crown ignored it.

The Puerto Rican path was shaped by this mixture of reform and constraint. The island’s elites gained from trade and immigration, feared social disorder, and lacked the large regional armies that had made independence possible elsewhere. The question of whether reform could succeed within the empire would remain open until the later nineteenth century (Chapter 67).

Puerto Rico’s experience of constitutional politics included elections for local councils and deputies in 1812–1814 and again in 1820–1823, and these elections involved a narrow electorate of property holders and officials. Reformers pressed for fair taxation, local appointments and freer trade, and they used the language of rights created at Cádiz to criticize abuses by governors and the intendancy. Many were creole landowners and merchants who also wanted order and feared slave rebellion.

Why did independence find little support? The island was small, strongly garrisoned and tied to Spain by trade and by new immigrants, and it lacked the large regional armies that sustained independence movements elsewhere. Venezuelan exiles and Bolivarian agents plotted against Spanish rule, but they never mounted an effective invasion. This did not mean that residents were passive: the persistence of reform petitions shows a political culture that sought change within the empire.

Governors used censorship and discretionary powers after 1823, so reformers turned to petitions, municipal councils and private correspondence. These methods were slower but kept alive a language of rights and local interest, and they created networks that would later support more open political movements (Chapter 67).

Some historians argue that the smaller scale of slavery in Puerto Rico made reformers there less fearful of discussing abolition than their Cuban counterparts, though this was never uniform. Because the island’s elites depended on trade, immigration and Spanish protection, they tended to seek concessions inside the empire, and the Crown’s selective grants of commerce and land reinforced that choice.

Colonial Order and New States

  • Colonial institutions and republican government: Offices, titles and constitutions changed, but councils, clergy and merchant families persisted, so local elites kept power.
  • Indigenous rights and legal equality: The Indian republic’s protections disappeared in favor of equality before the law, while communal land and labor demands remained under pressure.
  • Slavery and emancipation: The federation abolished slavery in 1824 and barred slave traders from citizenship, while Cuba and Puerto Rico expanded slavery under Spanish rule.
  • Provincial autonomy and central authority: The captaincy’s capital gave way to a federation that could not enforce its decisions, and separate states emerged on colonial provincial lines.
  • Imperial trade and national economies: Indigo and export dependence continued, British goods entered through new channels, and Cuba’s sugar economy tied it to Atlantic markets.

Who changed most: Urban elites, officials and the formerly enslaved in Central America. Who faced continuity: rural Indigenous people, the enslaved in Cuba and landless workers in every region. Why it matters: Political change did not remove the underlying hierarchies.

Section 57.24

The Spanish Constitution of 1837 and the Special Colonial Status of Cuba and Puerto Rico

The Spanish Constitution of 1837 was approved after a military mutiny at La Granja in 1836 forced the Crown to restore constitutional government. Its Additional Article 2 provided that the overseas provinces would be governed by special laws, and the Cortes had already refused to seat deputies elected from Cuba and Puerto Rico, among them José Antonio Saco. The constitution thus turned a long Spanish debate into a formal legal difference between the peninsula and its remaining colonies.

The contrast with 1812 is important. The Cádiz Constitution had treated American territories as parts of the Spanish nation and given them representation, though unequally and with exclusions for people of African descent. The 1837 arrangement removed even that promise of shared constitutional status, and it left the content of the ‘special laws’ undefined for years, so colonial governors continued to use broad executive authority.

Cuban and Puerto Rican elites interpreted the change in different ways. Planters who prized security and slavery could accept special laws that protected their property, while reformers saw the exclusion of their deputies as proof that Spain would not share power. Free people of color and the enslaved had little voice in the discussion, and the decision confirmed their dependence on the colonial executive.

The long-term significance was political as well as legal. Reformist hopes within the empire did not disappear, but they now rested on a constitutional order that had written colonial difference into law. The conflicts that followed, including the Ten Years’ War, abolition and the movements of Martí, are the subject of Chapter 67.

The constitution was written during Spain’s own civil war with the Carlists, and metropolitan liberals were more concerned with domestic politics than with colonial questions. When deputies from Cuba and Puerto Rico arrived in 1836, the Cortes refused to seat them, arguing that conditions overseas, including slavery and racial composition, required different laws. Saco published a critique in 1837, but the exclusion stood.

The promised special laws remained largely unwritten for decades, so the colonies were governed by royal decree and by the discretion of captains-general. This left both reformers and planters dependent on the colonial executive. The decision therefore did more than postpone the question of representation: it created a legal category of permanent colonial difference that later movements would challenge.

The 1837 settlement was part of a wider pattern. The French Charter of 1830 also provided that France’s colonies would be governed by special laws, and historians of liberal empires have noted that several European states combined constitutional rule at home with separate legal regimes overseas. Spain’s decision was therefore not unique, but its consequences were severe because Cuba and Puerto Rico were among the few American territories left to it.

The evidence for the debate includes the records of the Cortes sessions, colonial petitions and pamphlets such as Saco’s, which show that the exclusion was argued openly and not simply decreed. They also show who spoke: deputies and writers from the colonial elite and Spanish officials, while enslaved people and most free people of color left no direct record in this forum.

Section 57.25

Santo Domingo, the Independence Movement of 1821, and the Union with Haiti

The eastern part of Hispaniola, Santo Domingo, was ceded to France in 1795, reconquered for Spain in 1809 and then governed as a poor, thinly populated colony in a period often called ‘the foolish Spain’ (España Boba). Its economy depended on cattle, timber and small farming, and slavery existed but on a smaller scale than in Saint-Domingue. The 1821 crisis grew from this neglected situation and from the independence of Spanish colonies around it.

On December 1, 1821 José Núñez de Cáceres proclaimed an independent state, often called ‘Spanish Haiti’, and sought union with Gran Colombia. The movement was led by local officials and landowners, and it had little military base. It expected protection from Bolívar’s government, which was too far away and too occupied elsewhere to respond.

Haitian president Jean-Pierre Boyer entered Santo Domingo on February 9, 1822 and unified the island under the Haitian government. Many easterners welcomed the abolition of slavery, but others resented new taxes, conscription, the heavy taxation that followed the indemnity France imposed on Haiti in 1825, after unification, changes in landholding and the weight of a French-speaking, Haitian-dominated administration. Haitians presented unification as completing the island’s liberation, while many landowners and clergy saw occupation.

The union lasted until 1844, when a movement led by Juan Pablo Duarte and others proclaimed Dominican independence on February 27. Historians differ over how to describe 1822–1844, and Haitian and Dominican historiographies have often framed it differently. The case shows that a declaration of independence did not guarantee self-rule: within months the new state was absorbed by a neighbor, and its later nationhood emerged from resistance to that union.

Haitian rule changed eastern society in ways that explain its mixed reception. Boyer’s government abolished slavery, but it also applied Haitian land laws that clashed with eastern customs of communal ranching, confiscated church property, closed the old university and drew conscripts and taxes from a poorer population. Many elite families emigrated to Cuba, Puerto Rico and Venezuela, while others, including free people of color, supported the new order.

Haiti’s own position helps explain the policy. France’s 1825 ordinance recognized Haitian independence in return for an indemnity of 150 million francs, later reduced, and Boyer’s government feared reconquest and needed revenue. Dominican nationalists who founded the secret society La Trinitaria in 1838 later described these years as occupation, and the 1844 declaration was therefore as much a rejection of Haitian rule as a revival of the 1821 project.

Núñez de Cáceres was a jurist and official who led a movement of the local elite, and his government did not abolish slavery, which helps explain why many enslaved people and free people of color welcomed the Haitian arrival. Haitian leaders had long claimed the whole island as one territory, so Boyer could present unification as completing independence rather than as conquest, and he found some supporters in the east.

The case shows the limits of a purely Spanish-American frame. Santo Domingo’s history ran through the Haitian Revolution, French diplomacy and the commercial pressures of the Atlantic economy, so a chapter on the end of Iberian rule must include Haiti as an actor and not only as a background event.

Section 57.26

Panama, British Honduras, the Mosquito Coast, and the Limits of Territorial Claims

Panama’s path differed from Central America’s. The independence movement began at Villa de Los Santos on November 10, 1821, and Panama City declared independence from Spain on November 28, choosing association with Gran Colombia. It had belonged to New Granada, not the Kingdom of Guatemala, and its commercial route across the isthmus tied it to Colombian and Atlantic networks (see Chapter 53). It therefore never became one of the federation’s five states.

On the Caribbean coast, British settlers known as Baymen had cut logwood in the Belize River area since the seventeenth century. Spain allowed logging under eighteenth-century treaties but claimed sovereignty, and the settlers repelled a Spanish attack at St. George’s Caye in 1798. After Spanish rule ended, Britain’s presence continued without any acceptance of Central American claims, and the settlement formally became the colony of British Honduras in 1862, with Crown Colony government following in 1871.

Farther south, the Miskitu kingdom on the Mosquito Coast had long maintained trade and political ties with Britain and Jamaica. Its rulers, who had their own succession, diplomacy and military power, negotiated with outsiders and resisted incorporation into Spanish and later Central American states. Britain agreed to evacuate its settlements in the Convention of London of July 14, 1786 and completed the evacuation in 1787, but kept influence, and in the 1830s and 1840s it asserted new claims linked to the Miskitu king (see Chapter 62).

These cases show that the lines on Spanish maps were not equivalent to control on the ground. The federation claimed the Caribbean coast in law, but it lacked roads, ports, revenue and an army to occupy it. Indigenous polities, Afro-descendant communities and foreign settlers shaped the region’s politics, and sovereignty remained shared, disputed and negotiated.

Panama’s relationship with Bogotá was never settled. Separatist movements appeared in 1830–1831 and again in 1840–1841, when an Isthmus state briefly asserted independence, which shows that the commercial importance of the transit route gave its elites bargaining power. The territory’s trade, and its separate administrative history, kept it from joining the Central American federation even though geography linked it to the isthmus.

The British settlements and the Mosquito Coast complicate the picture further. Logwood and mahogany cutting in Belize relied on enslaved African labor until British abolition in 1834, and Garifuna migrants from Honduras arrived in 1832 and became a lasting community. On the Mosquito Coast, the Miskitu king, crowned with British support in the 1820s, allied selectively with outsiders, and the speculative Poyais scheme of 1822–1823, in which a Scottish adventurer sold land in a fictional colony to British investors, shows how foreign promoters treated the coast as empty land. Neither Spanish maps nor the federation’s claims reflected the political structure that existed there.

Foreign officials sought to exploit the situation. Frederick Chatfield, who arrived in 1834 as British consul general in Central America, defended British commercial and territorial interests and often intervened in local disputes, and his presence shows how Britain’s influence worked through diplomacy as well as through settlements. The federation’s weakness made it hard to answer such pressure.

Other Indigenous societies also kept autonomy. On Panama’s Caribbean coast, the Guna lived largely outside the control of the state and traded with outsiders, a reminder that the Miskitu were not unique. The isthmus’s importance as a transit route attracted foreign interest, yet the political structure remained one in which local peoples and elites bargained with every state that claimed them.

Section 57.27

Independence without Uniform Sovereignty: Central America and the Caribbean in Comparative Perspective

By 1840 the former Kingdom of Guatemala and the Spanish Caribbean showed several different outcomes. Five states had formed a federation and then separated, Chiapas had joined Mexico and Soconusco remained disputed, Panama had joined Gran Colombia, Santo Domingo had been absorbed by Haiti, Cuba and Puerto Rico remained Spanish, and Britain held settlements and influence on the Caribbean coast.

These outcomes illustrate four distinct problems. Independence meant the end of Spanish authority, territory meant control of land and people within borders, citizenship meant legal membership in a polity, and effective sovereignty meant the ability to enforce decisions. The federation held independence but not effective sovereignty, Cuba and Puerto Rico held neither independence nor full citizenship, and Soconusco and the Mosquito Coast show territory under dispute.

Comparison suggests several causes. Where slavery and export agriculture bound elites to imperial protection, as in Cuba and Puerto Rico, separation had fewer supporters. Where regional economies were small and rival capitals strong, as in Central America, independence led to provincial competition. Where a strong army or a dynasty with real legitimacy held territory together, as in Brazil (Chapter 56), unity was easier; Iturbide’s short-lived Mexican empire (Chapter 52) collapsed within two years, and Central America’s separation from it shows the limits of the monarchical solution. External powers, including Spain, Mexico, Britain, Haiti and the United States, shaped the results.

Part XII therefore ends with a reminder that Latin American independence was not one movement with one outcome. It was a set of different wars, negotiations, constitutional crises and refusals, with consequences that persisted through the nineteenth century. Chapter 58 turns to the general problems that new states faced in building republics.

Citizenship was also uneven. A Salvadoran landowner, a Guatemalan Maya villager, an enslaved sugar worker in Cuba, a free Black militiaman in Havana and a Miskitu leader stood in very different relations to the states that claimed them. Formal independence did not alter this immediately, and in several cases it removed legal protections that had once existed, so the same event could enlarge rights for some and reduce security for others.

Periodization matters too. Independence is often dated to 1821, but the questions it raised, including borders, federalism, the status of the Church and the position of Indigenous and enslaved people, were still being fought over in the 1840s and later. This is why Chapter 58 treats republican nation-building as a general problem, Chapter 62 follows the later history of the Central American states, and Chapter 67 returns to the two islands that remained Spanish.

The historiography has shifted. Older national histories told the story of each country’s independence separately, while newer work connects the cases through the imperial crisis of 1808 and the Atlantic revolutions, including Haiti. That approach shows how events on one island or province affected decisions elsewhere, for example how fear of another Haiti shaped Cuban loyalty and how Mexico’s collapse shaped Central American choices.

British abolitionism also mattered. Pressure to end the slave trade pushed Spain to sign the 1817 treaty, and its weak enforcement showed how imperial priorities and planter interests could override diplomatic commitments. The comparison across the region therefore shows that independence, sovereignty and freedom followed different paths, and that each depended on power, economy and the choices of people at every level of society.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined why the collapse of Spanish authority produced different outcomes: an independent but short-lived Central American federation, territories absorbed by neighbors, and Cuba and Puerto Rico under continuing Spanish rule. Its argument is that independence, territorial control, citizenship and effective sovereignty were separate questions, and that each depended on local economies, social structures and political choices.

In the Kingdom of Guatemala, Spanish rule worked through the capital’s officials, merchants and clergy and through Indigenous town councils that collected tribute. Provincial economies, from Salvadoran indigo to Honduran mining, and rivalries such as Guatemala City against San Salvador or León against Granada, meant that independence would raise the question of who governed. The Cádiz years of 1808–1814 introduced elections, councils and a free press, and the risings of 1811–1813 and the Totonicapán revolt of 1820 showed wider mobilization than a purely elite account suggests.

Independence in 1821 came from officials and notables who acted to prevent unrest and to keep order, and it left existing authorities in place. Provinces responded differently, so the settlement was a break with Spain without union. The annexation to Mexico in 1822 was an elite decision backed by force, and San Salvador’s resistance led to occupation by Filísola’s army.

After Iturbide’s fall, the assembly of 1823 declared absolute independence, claiming a sovereignty that depended on no foreign power. The federal constitution of 1824 created five states, a congress, a senate and a president, and it barred slave traders from citizenship. Its authority rested on indirect elections and on states that kept most revenue and military power, so the federal government depended on cooperation it often could not command.

Federal politics divided along regional and ideological lines. Arce’s presidency and the war of 1826–1829 showed the unresolved question of when the union could override a state. Morazán’s liberal victory led to reforms against the Church and the old order, and these provoked rural opposition that culminated in the cholera-year revolt under Rafael Carrera. The federation separated in 1838–1840 in stages, and attempts to revive it continued without success.

Social groups experienced these changes differently. Indigenous communities used constitutional language but lost colonial protections, ladinos and elites held offices, and the formerly enslaved gained freedom in the federation while slavery expanded in Cuba. Women appear as workers, property managers and community members but not as voters. The evidence for most people comes through officials, priests and merchants.

In Cuba, sugar, the Atlantic slave trade, garrisons and fear of another Haiti tied planters to Spain, and Captain-General Vives’s powers of 1825 added military authority. Aponte’s 1812 conspiracy, republican plots and reformers such as Varela and Saco show that opposition existed but was divided by race, slavery and goals. In Puerto Rico, the 1815 Decree of Graces, immigration and coffee and sugar expansion built a loyal elite, while Power y Giralt’s Cádiz career showed the possibilities and limits of representation.

The Spanish Constitution of 1837 wrote colonial difference into law by promising special laws for the overseas provinces. Santo Domingo’s declaration of 1821 ended in absorption by Haiti, Panama chose Gran Colombia, and British settlements and Miskitu and Guna authority show that the Spanish map did not match control on the ground.

The chapter concludes Part XII by showing that independence was not one movement with one result. Union, fragmentation, absorption and continued colonial rule all grew from the same imperial crisis, and each case shows how power, economy and social structure shaped outcomes. Chapter 58 turns to how new states built republics, and Chapter 62 and Chapter 67 follow Central America and the Spanish Caribbean later in the century.

Spain’s captaincy general from Chiapas to Costa Rica, governed from Guatemala City by a captain-general who also presided over the audiencia. It did not include Panama and cannot be equated with modern Guatemala.

A royal high court that also advised and sometimes governed. In Guatemala City it heard appeals and shared authority with the captain-general, tying provincial disputes to the capital.

A province created by Bourbon reform in 1786 under an intendant responsible for revenue and administration. San Salvador, Comayagua, León and Ciudad Real became seats, sharpening local rivalry with the capital.

The senior military and political governor of a colonial territory. In Cuba the captain-general held extraordinary powers after 1825, making colonial government military in form.

A town council, sometimes elected after 1812, that spoke for local notables and became the main provincial voice in 1821. Indigenous towns had their own councils collecting tribute and organizing labor.

The Spanish parliament of 1810–1814 that drafted the 1812 Constitution with American deputies. It created elected councils and abolished Indigenous tribute in principle, but was dissolved in 1814 and revived in 1820.

Indigo dye, the leading Central American export, centered in El Salvador and sold through Guatemala City merchants. Price swings and credit terms shaped Salvadoran politics.

In Guatemala, a Hispanicized or mixed rural person not counted as Indigenous. The category grew after independence as the colonial label “Indian” lost legal force.
Situado
A subsidy sent from New Spain to pay for defense in Caribbean colonies. Its end after Mexican independence forced Cuba and Puerto Rico to rely on local revenue.

Agustín de Iturbide’s monarchy of 1822–1823, to which Central America was annexed from January 1822. It collapsed in March 1823 and should not be confused with the Mexica (Aztec) empire.

A system dividing power between national and state governments. The 1824 constitution made the five states largely self-governing, which left the federation dependent on state cooperation.

The union of Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and Costa Rica created in 1824 and effectively dissolved by 1840. It was also called the United Provinces of Central America in 1823.

A political outlook favoring constitutions, secular reform, commerce and legal equality. In Central America it meant limiting the Church and corporate privileges, which provoked rural resistance.
Conservatism
A political outlook defending the Church, established elites and local order. Guatemalan conservatives around the Aycinena merchants favored a strong center and later allied with Carrera.

A regional leader whose power rested on armed followers and personal loyalty. Carrera and, in a different way, Morazán illustrate how military leaders shaped the federation’s fate.

The colonial capital of Honduras, rival of mining Tegucigalpa. Its role shows that provincial capitals themselves contained rival interests.
Cartago
The colonial capital of Costa Rica, which lost leadership of the state to San José after clashes in 1823.

The 1815 decree that offered land and trade concessions to foreign Catholic settlers in Puerto Rico, spurring immigration and the sugar, coffee and slavery economy.
Facultades omnímodas
The extraordinary powers granted to Cuba’s captain-general in 1825, similar to those of a governor of a besieged city, which concentrated executive authority in one officer.

A labor system producing export crops through enslaved workers. It expanded in Cuba and Puerto Rico after 1820 while the Central American federation abolished slavery in 1824.
Special laws
The principle in Additional Article 2 of the 1837 Spanish Constitution that overseas provinces would be governed by separate laws, which turned colonial difference into constitutional doctrine.

Supreme authority within a territory. The chapter distinguishes it from independence, territorial control and citizenship, since a state could claim it without being able to enforce it.
Miskitu
The people of the Mosquito Coast whose kingdom traded and allied with Britain and kept political autonomy. They show that Spanish maps did not equal control.
Gran Colombia
The union of Venezuela, New Granada and Ecuador that Panama joined in 1821. Panama’s choice kept it outside the Central American federation.

Chapter Reflection

The chapter’s central lesson is that political independence did not guarantee effective sovereignty. Central American leaders declared independence twice, built a constitution and still lacked revenue, an army and consensus, while Cuba and Puerto Rico remained under a sovereign Spain that governed them by special laws. In each case, authority depended on money, force and legitimacy, not only on a declaration.

Territories followed different paths because their social structures differed. Where regional elites competed and the central state was weak, as in Central America, independence produced fragmentation. Where slavery and imperial protection bound elites to Spain, as in the islands, separation lost support, and where neighbors could absorb a small state, as in Santo Domingo, independence was brief. These outcomes were not inevitable, and the sources show contemporaries weighing real alternatives.

The histories of Indigenous communities and enslaved people expose the limits of independence. Maya villagers lost colonial protections without gaining equal power, and enslaved Cubans lived under a regime that grew after the mainland empire fell, while in the federation abolition arrived but was accompanied by new forms of labor control. For them, political change was measured by land, labor and legal standing, not by flags.

The chapter concludes Part XII by showing that independence was a set of different processes, from Mexico’s insurgencies to Brazil’s monarchy to Central America’s federation and the Caribbean’s continuing colonies. It also prepares Chapter 58, which asks how new states built constitutions, citizenship and legitimacy after empire, and why so many struggled to do so.
