Commodities, Railroads, and the Export Boom

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Sack of Coffee at Santos

Around 1900, a sack of coffee picked in the interior of São Paulo could be on a steamship in the harbor of Santos within a day or two and in a European or North American warehouse within weeks. That journey had been impossible to imagine a half-century earlier, and every stage of it, from the planter’s estate to the foreign roaster, was a link in a system that tied Latin America to the industrial world.

Consider the stages of that journey. The coffee was grown on an estate in the western plateau of São Paulo, harvested and processed by a workforce that after 1888 was no longer enslaved and that included many immigrants from Italy and other countries alongside Brazilian-born workers. It was carried by railway down the steep escarpment to the port of Santos, where laborers and, increasingly, mechanical equipment moved it onto quays and into ships. Commission agents and export houses financed and sold it, banks extended credit, insurers covered the voyage, and steamship companies carried it across the Atlantic. At several stages taxes and charges affected the transaction, but taxes on coffee exports generally accrued to the São Paulo state government after the federal constitution of 1891, while federal revenue depended heavily on import duties. Railway and dock concessions could impose public subsidies or guarantees as well as provide services and regulatory leverage; they did not automatically generate public income. Seen as a whole, a single shipment reveals the chapter’s subject: the production, transportation, finance, and public revenue that together made up the export economy.

Between roughly 1870 and 1914, economies across Latin America were reorganized around the sale of primary commodities to industrializing countries. Coffee from Brazil and Colombia, beef, wool, and grain from the Río de la Plata, sugar from Cuba and Peru, nitrates from the Atacama, silver and copper from Mexico, tin from Bolivia, cacao from Ecuador, bananas from Central America, and rubber from the Amazon moved in growing volume to Europe and the United States. The growth was real and measurable. It built railways, ports, cities, schools, and fiscal systems, and in some countries it supported early manufacturing. But it was also uneven among countries and regions, concentrated infrastructure along profitable corridors, and left economies exposed to prices and decisions made far away.

The chapter’s central question is therefore how expanding global demand, railroads, steamships, foreign capital, and government policy transformed Latin American economies, and why those changes produced both growth and new vulnerabilities. It rejects three simplifications. The first is that Latin America was economically uniform; the export economies of the pampas, the Brazilian coffee frontier, the Chilean desert, and the Central American coast differed profoundly. The second is that foreign investors controlled everything; foreign capital was often essential, but local planters, merchants, bankers, and governments built and owned much of the system and shaped its terms. The third is that the region was incapable of industrial development; several countries developed manufacturing during this very period, often as a by-product of export prosperity.

Economic historians such as Victor Bulmer-Thomas, Luis Bértola, and José Antonio Ocampo have shown that the boom resulted from an interaction among world demand, cheaper transport, capital flows, and domestic institutions, not from any one of these alone. Where governments could raise revenue, secure property, and cooperate with investors, export growth tended to be faster and more durable. Where they could not, or where geography made transport prohibitively expensive, the boom passed by whole regions. The chapter therefore pays close attention to the state, to institutions, and to geography as well as to markets.

Evidence for this subject is largely quantitative and institutional. Historians use customs returns and trade statistics, company reports and prospectuses, railway concession contracts, government budgets, consular and commercial reports, and maps. Each has limits. Export statistics often record the volume or value of goods at the port without showing who profited; prospectuses were written to attract investors; and national figures can hide enormous regional differences. Seven Primary Source Windows in this chapter introduce these materials and the habits of reading them critically. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes export volume from export value, export earnings from government revenue, and foreign investment from foreign ownership of the whole economy.

The chapter opens Part XV by building on the territorial, constitutional, and abolitionist transformations of Chapters 63 through 67. The wars and reforms of those chapters left states with settled borders, in many cases greater political stability, and a labor question that abolition had reshaped. Chapter 68 explains how those states entered the world economy. It does not retell the politics of land, labor, immigration, and Indigenous dispossession, which Chapter 69 examines in full, nor the diplomacy of Panama, the Roosevelt Corollary, and the new hemispheric order, which belong to Chapter 70. It mentions Cuba and Puerto Rico for their economic significance without retelling the wars covered in Chapter 67, and it touches Mexico’s economy under Porfirio Díaz without anticipating the political analysis of Chapter 71.

Historical Context

In the half-century after independence, Latin American economies had been shaped by the legacies of colonial trade, by the disruption of the independence wars, and by political instability that Chapters 58 through 62 described. Exports continued, including silver from Mexico and Peru, hides and salted beef from the Río de la Plata, sugar from Cuba and Brazil, and coffee from Brazil, but transport was slow and costly. Mule trains and ox carts moved most goods overland, rivers were seasonal highways, and the cost of carrying a bulky good a few hundred kilometers could exceed its value. Governments were often poor, dependent on customs duties, and burdened by debts and civil conflict. Some economies had already found specialized niches, such as Peru’s guano, whose fertilizer exports before the 1870s supplied the state with large revenues and then, as deposits declined and debts accumulated, left it in crisis. That experience offered an early lesson about the risks of dependence on a single export.

The decades after 1850 changed the external environment. Industrialization in Britain spread to continental Europe and the United States, a process often called the Second Industrial Revolution, with steel, chemicals, electricity, and new machinery. Industrial cities grew rapidly and needed food, raw materials, and fuel. At the same time, railways, steamships, and telegraphs lowered the cost and uncertainty of moving goods and information. For the first time, bulk commodities from distant tropical and temperate lands could be sold profitably to mass markets in Europe and North America. Capital, especially British capital, sought opportunities abroad, and Latin America, with its mineral and agricultural potential, drew a substantial share.

Domestic political conditions also changed. By the 1870s and 1880s many Latin American states had reached a degree of stability that earlier decades had denied them. Chile after the War of the Pacific, Argentina after its internal conflicts and the consolidation of national authority, Brazil’s empire and then republic, and Mexico under Díaz all offered investors more predictable conditions, and governing elites largely accepted a liberal program that stressed trade, private property, and foreign investment. Chapter 66 and Chapter 67 showed how abolition and imperial collapse had transformed labor and sovereignty in Brazil, Cuba, and Puerto Rico. This chapter takes up the economic consequences.

A caution on terms and data is necessary. Historians of the period rely on national trade statistics that differ in quality, units, and method. Prices were volatile, and the value of exports could rise or fall independently of volume. The period 1870–1914 contained both booms and downturns, and later developments, such as synthetic fertilizer competition, petroleum, and the Great Depression, belong to other chapters. The chapter therefore avoids describing the whole era with conditions that arose only later and states clearly when figures are approximate.

The World Economy and the Foundations of Export-Led Growth

Why did Latin America’s export economies expand so rapidly, and why did participation in world trade depend on domestic politics and infrastructure?

Section 68.1

The Second Industrial Revolution and the Expansion of World Commodity Markets

The export boom began outside Latin America, in the industrial economies whose growth created demand. Britain had industrialized first, but from the mid-nineteenth century Germany, France, Belgium, and above all the United States built large industrial sectors of their own. The newer industries of the period, steel, chemicals, electrical equipment, and machinery, relied on raw materials and fuels, while the growing industrial workforce lived in cities and had to be fed. Urban population in Europe and North America rose steadily, and rising wages in many industrial countries allowed more households to buy imported foods and stimulants such as sugar, coffee, cacao, and beef. Demand was therefore both industrial, for metals, fertilizers, and rubber, and consumer, for foods and beverages.

The categories of demand mattered because each opened a different opportunity. Industrial metals such as copper, tin, lead, and zinc were needed for wiring, machinery, and packaging. Nitrates supplied fertilizer and an ingredient in explosives, and demand grew as European agriculture intensified. Wool fed textile mills, and later refrigerated beef and mutton supplied urban consumers. Grain, especially wheat and maize, was needed as European population outgrew domestic supply. Natural rubber became indispensable for belts, hoses, insulation, and, from the 1890s, pneumatic tires. Tropical goods such as coffee and sugar, once luxuries, became staples of mass consumption. Each good had its own technology, its own price behavior, and its own competitors, a point that deserves emphasis because it explains why export economies diverged.

Historians distinguish long-term global demand from short-term price changes. Over the decades after 1870, the volume of world trade expanded dramatically, and Latin American exports grew with it. But prices for individual commodities fell, rose, and fell again, sometimes sharply, and a country could export more in volume while earning less in value. Falling prices were not merely a misfortune: they were partly the consequence of the same transport and production improvements that opened new supply. This duality, expanding demand with unstable prices, runs through the entire chapter.

Commercial finance and communications completed the picture. London, the center of the international financial system, provided short-term trade credit, marine insurance, shipping services, and long-term capital. The gold standard, adopted by Britain and gradually by other major economies, provided a common monetary framework, although several Latin American countries operated with silver or inconvertible paper currencies, which created exchange-rate risks that later sections examine. Commercial houses in European and North American ports bought and sold commodities and extended credit to producers, so that a planter in Brazil or a rancher in Argentina was tied to a network of merchants and bankers reaching to Liverpool, Le Havre, Hamburg, and New York.

Historical Vocabulary
Export-led growth

Export-led growth describes an economic pattern in which the expansion of overseas sales of primary products drives the growth of income, government revenue, transport, and commerce. Historians use the phrase to describe Latin America from roughly 1870 to 1914, when exports of coffee, grain, meat, minerals, and other goods increased strongly and stimulated railways, ports, banking, and in some countries manufacturing.

The concept does not imply that every export boom produced balanced development, nor that growth was automatic. Export-led growth depended on world demand, falling transport costs, access to capital, and domestic institutions that secured property and raised revenue. It should also be distinguished from export dependence, the vulnerability that arose when an economy relied heavily on a few products, a topic treated in Section 68.19.

Section 68.2

Latin America’s Export Geography: Different Commodities, Different Regional Economies

Latin America supplied the industrial world with a remarkable range of goods, but no single country supplied all of them. Climate, soil, mineral endowment, population, and above all distance from a port determined what each region could sell. Brazil became the world’s dominant coffee producer, with its production concentrated in the highlands of São Paulo, Minas Gerais, and Rio de Janeiro states, where well-drained upland soils and a suitable climate supported the crop. Colombia developed coffee in the mountain valleys of Santander, Cundinamarca, and the western districts, in a pattern of smaller holdings. Central American highlands in Guatemala, El Salvador, and Costa Rica also produced coffee, while the Caribbean lowlands of Costa Rica, Honduras, and Guatemala suited bananas.

The temperate grasslands of the Río de la Plata, the pampas of Argentina and the campos of Uruguay, were suited to pastoral and then arable farming. Wool, hides, and tallow came first, followed by refrigerated mutton and beef, and wheat and maize as railways opened the interior to cultivation. In the Caribbean, sugar remained the principal export of Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Dominican Republic, while on the arid Peruvian coast, irrigated estates grew sugar and cotton. Ecuador’s coastal lowlands around Guayaquil produced cacao, which became the country’s leading export for several decades.

Minerals gave other regions their economic identity. The Atacama Desert of northern Chile held the world’s principal natural nitrate deposits. Mexico’s mountain ranges contained silver, and later copper, lead, and zinc, while the Bolivian altiplano and the Peruvian highlands held silver, tin, and copper. The Amazon basin, hot, wet, and remote, yielded wild rubber from trees scattered through the forest, a geography that shaped a labor and trade system very unlike plantation agriculture. Mexico also developed agricultural exports, notably henequen, the fiber used in binder twine, produced in Yucatán for the world’s grain harvesters.

Specialization was not simply a matter of nature. Distance from ports, the availability of labor, the cost of transport, the existence of markets, and government policy determined whether a region’s potential was realized. Nitrates were valuable because deposits lay near a coast, so that even a desert location could be commercially exploited with short railways. Rubber trees were widespread, but the cost of collecting from dispersed trees required a labor system that rested on advances of credit. Colombian coffee could be grown in the mountains but its export depended on moving the crop down rugged valleys to the Magdalena River and the coast, a handicap that kept Colombia’s growth slower than Brazil’s until railways improved. The export map of Latin America was thus a map of resources, transport, and politics together.

Section 68.3

Economic Liberalism, Commercial Policy, and the State’s Role in Export Development

Latin American elites of the late nineteenth century generally professed economic liberalism, a doctrine that favored open trade, private property, limited government interference, and the attraction of foreign capital and immigrants. In practice, governments were active. The distinction between liberal rhetoric and real policy is essential, because the export boom depended heavily on what states did: they built or subsidized transport, sold or granted land, passed mining codes, set tariffs, and borrowed abroad. Export-led growth was therefore not a retreat of the state but a particular form of state activism, one directed at creating conditions for export production and at capturing a share of its proceeds.

Customs duties were the cornerstone of public finance. Most governments lacked the administrative capacity for broad income or property taxation and relied heavily on import duties, with export duties in some cases adding substantially, as in Chile’s nitrate tax. This arrangement tied government revenue to the volume and value of foreign trade. When trade expanded, revenue grew and governments could borrow and spend more; when trade fell, revenue shrank and debt burdens weighed. Tariff policy was therefore not only commercial but fiscal. Import duties also gave incidental protection to local manufacturers, and some governments adjusted them to favor domestic industries, such as textiles and food processing, while remaining rhetorically committed to free trade.

Governments promoted investment in many ways. Railway concessions granted companies the right to build and operate lines, often with land grants, tax exemptions, and subsidies per kilometer built or guarantees of a minimum profit on invested capital. Mining codes defined ownership of subsoil resources and the terms of exploitation; reforms in Mexico in the 1880s and 1890s, for example, made it easier for private and foreign investors to acquire and hold mining property. Land laws and the sale of public lands opened frontier regions to ranchers and farmers, with consequences for Indigenous communities that Chapter 69 examines. Governments also issued bonds in European markets, using customs revenues as security, to finance ports, railways, and public works.

Monetary arrangements shaped these relationships. Countries on the gold standard or committed to convertibility offered investors currency stability, while those with inconvertible paper currencies or silver standards faced exchange-rate fluctuations that could help or hurt exporters. A depreciating currency raised the local-currency price of exports and could stimulate production, as in Brazil’s coffee sector, but it raised the burden of debts payable in gold or sterling. These tradeoffs surfaced in political disputes between exporters, importers, and bondholders, and Section 68.16 returns to them.

Historical Vocabulary
Railway concession and foreign investment

A railway concession was a legal grant from a government to a company, domestic or foreign, giving it the right to build and operate a railway on specified terms. Concessions commonly provided land along the route, exemptions from duties on imported materials, and sometimes a guaranteed return, meaning that the government promised to pay the company if profits fell below a stated percentage of invested capital. Governments also granted subsidies per kilometer of track.

The term should be distinguished from foreign investment itself, which took several forms: direct ownership of an enterprise by foreign investors, portfolio lending through the purchase of government or company bonds, and joint enterprises with local partners. A railway might be built by a foreign company under a concession, financed by bonds sold in London, and partly owned by local investors, so that a single project could combine all three. Distinguishing these forms is necessary to avoid the mistaken assumption that foreigners owned everything.

Section 68.4

Steamships, Refrigeration, Telegraphs, and the Integration of International Markets

Technological change in transportation and communication lowered the cost of distance. Steamships, especially after the introduction of more efficient compound engines and iron and then steel hulls, made ocean freight faster, more reliable, and cheaper than sail. Regular steamship schedules replaced the uncertainty of winds, which meant that exporters could plan shipments, importers could count on delivery, and merchants held less inventory. The opening of canals and improved port facilities supported the trend. By the late nineteenth century, freight rates for bulk goods had fallen enough that grain from the Argentine pampas could compete in European markets with grain from nearer sources.

Communications changed commercial behavior. Submarine telegraph cables linked Europe with Brazil in the mid-1870s and soon extended along the coasts of South America and to other regions. Merchants could learn prices in London or New York within hours rather than weeks, adjust offers, and arrange credit. Planters and exporters were better informed, though information remained unevenly distributed, and large commercial houses with their own telegraph connections often held advantages over small producers. Faster information also meant that price changes in distant markets were transmitted quickly to Latin American producers, accelerating both booms and downturns.

Refrigeration opened the market for perishable goods. Experimental shipments of frozen meat from the Río de la Plata to France in the late 1870s demonstrated the possibility, and in the following decades frozen mutton and then chilled beef became important exports, shipped in ships equipped with mechanical cooling. Refrigerated shipping transformed pastoral economies by making meat, not only hides and tallow, the main product, and it later helped bananas and other fruit reach distant markets. Insurance and commercial credit developed alongside, spreading risk across large international firms.

The cumulative effect was to shorten the interval between production and sale and to make previously uncompetitive regions and products commercially viable. A good that could not previously be sold abroad because it spoiled, cost too much to carry, or arrived at an unpredictable time could now find a market. But the new technology did not benefit all regions equally. It rewarded places with ports and railway links and left others behind, a pattern examined in Sections 68.13 and 68.18. And it deepened dependence on distant markets, because integrated markets transmitted shocks as well as opportunities.

Section 68.5

The Coffee Economies of Brazil and Colombia

Coffee was the largest single export of Latin America and the foundation of two very different economies. In Brazil, coffee cultivation had expanded in the first half of the nineteenth century in the Paraíba Valley between Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo, on estates worked by enslaved people. By the 1870s and 1880s the valley’s soils were declining, and production moved westward onto the plateau of São Paulo, where the fertile reddish soils, often called terra roxa, supported higher yields. The westward movement, connected to railway construction, created a new coffee frontier around Campinas, Ribeirão Preto, and beyond. Coffee’s share of Brazilian exports was very large, and Brazil supplied a majority of the world’s coffee.

Railways and coffee grew together. The São Paulo Railway, a British-financed line opened in 1867, climbed the steep escarpment between Santos and the plateau and handled the sea-bound coffee. Other lines, such as the Paulista and the Mogiana, were largely financed by coffee planters and local capital, extending the network into the interior. The railways allowed planters to plant farther from the coast, lowered the cost of moving the crop, and raised land values along their routes. The port of Santos, modernized by a private dock company that began operating in the 1890s, became the world’s leading coffee port. This was not simply a case of foreign capital building infrastructure: local planters were investors and managers, and the São Paulo state government supported rail and port development.

Labor changed with abolition in 1888, discussed in Chapter 66. Even before abolition, São Paulo planters had begun to look to European immigrants, and the provincial and later state government subsidized the passage of workers, especially from Italy. Many immigrant families worked as colonos under contracts that combined wages for tending and harvesting coffee trees with the right to grow food crops. This arrangement gave planters a stable workforce while giving workers some prospects, though conditions were often harsh and disputes frequent. Brazilian-born workers, including freedpeople and their descendants, also labored in the coffee zone, and the experience of different groups varied widely. Chapter 69 examines these labor systems and immigration in detail.

Commercial organization involved intermediaries between planters and consumers. The comissários, commission agents in Santos and Rio, sold the crop, advanced credit to planters, and supplied goods. Export houses, many foreign-owned, purchased the coffee for shipment. Banks, both domestic and foreign, financed the harvest. Because coffee was a perennial crop that took years to bear fruit, planting decisions responded to prices years earlier, and periodic overproduction depressed prices. In 1906, state governments in the main coffee-growing regions reached the Taubaté agreement, buying surplus coffee with the help of foreign loans to hold prices up, a program known as valorization. It illustrates the state’s role in managing a dominant export and the interdependence of coffee, credit, and politics.

Colombia’s coffee economy developed differently. Coffee production began in the northeastern department of Santander and spread to Cundinamarca and, in the early twentieth century, to western regions including Antioquia and the territories settled by Antioqueño migrants. Colombian coffee was grown more often on small and medium holdings, though larger estates existed, especially in Cundinamarca and Tolima. Transport was a severe obstacle. The mountainous terrain, the dependence on mule trains and the Magdalena River, and the lack of rail connections to the coast raised costs, and the civil conflict known as the War of a Thousand Days (1899–1902) disrupted production. Railways, often built with state support, gradually improved access, and by the early twentieth century coffee had become Colombia’s principal export. Compared with Brazil, Colombian growth was more dispersed among smallholders and slower in its early phase, which later contributed to a distinct social structure in coffee regions.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Commodity’s Journey: From a Coffee Estate to a European Cup

Follow a shipment of Brazilian coffee through the stages at which people worked and institutions took a share. On the estate, families picked cherries by hand during the harvest, dried them on open terraces, and removed the husk with machinery; women and children worked alongside men. The coffee was bagged and carried to the nearest railway station by ox cart or later by estate rail spurs, where railway workers loaded it onto wagons. On the descent to Santos, the line used cable-hauled inclines on the steep escarpment, a feat of engineering that required skilled labor and constant maintenance.

At the port, dockworkers, many of them immigrants and Afro-Brazilians, carried sacks to warehouses where coffee was sampled, graded, and stored by commission agents and exporters. Weighers, clerks, customs officials, and stevedores each performed a role, and the state collected export duties. Ships carried the coffee to Europe or the United States, where importers and roasters sold it to wholesalers and grocers. By the time it reached a household cup, the price included freight, insurance, commissions, interest, and taxes, and the planter received only part. At every stage, wages, risks, and profits were unequally distributed: the dockworker and the harvester at the bottom, the exporter and banker in the middle, and the consumer at the end, often unaware of the chain.

These lives are recorded unevenly. Company reports and consular dispatches describe tonnage and prices, while the voices of workers appear in police records, immigrant letters, newspapers of labor organizations, and oral histories. Reading the shipment as a chain of human labor helps correct the impression, easy to gain from trade tables, that commodities moved by themselves.

Section 68.6

Argentina, Uruguay, and the Export Economy of the Río de la Plata

The Río de la Plata region, comprising Argentina, Uruguay, and the Atlantic ports of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, followed a trajectory shaped by its grasslands. In the mid-nineteenth century the principal exports were hides, tallow, salted meat, and increasingly wool, produced by cattle and sheep ranching on large estates. The wool boom of the 1860s and 1870s expanded sheep raising, often through partnership between landowners and immigrant shepherds. This was an extensive, low-labor economy, supported by an abundance of land that the Argentine government acquired through military campaigns against Indigenous peoples of the pampas and Patagonia in the 1870s and 1880s, a process treated in Chapter 69.

The railway transformed the region. Argentina’s network expanded from a few thousand kilometers in the early 1880s to more than 30,000 kilometers by 1914, radiating from Buenos Aires into the pampas and linking producers to the port. Most lines were built and owned by British companies, often with government concessions and guarantees, though the state also owned lines. Railways allowed farmers to cultivate land far from the coast, and the grain frontier moved outward. Wheat and maize exports, which had been negligible, grew rapidly, and Argentina became a leading world exporter of grain. Immigrants, largely from Italy and Spain, worked as tenant farmers and seasonal laborers, many of whom returned home after the harvest, a pattern that gave them the name golondrinas, swallows.

Refrigeration added a second transformation. Frozen mutton shipments began in the 1880s, and in the first decade of the twentieth century chilled beef, which required better cattle and faster shipping, became the key export. Plants known as frigoríficos slaughtered and prepared meat for export, first under British ownership and, from the early twentieth century, increasingly with American packers entering the field. Ranchers improved their herds with imported breeds and sowed alfalfa pastures, and many landowners combined ranching and renting land to farmers. The relationships among producers, who often depended on frigorífico buyers, and the packers, who held market power, were a source of conflict.

Buenos Aires and Montevideo were the commercial centers of the system. Buenos Aires concentrated export houses, banks, insurers, shipping offices, and the offices of railway companies, and the port was modernized in the 1880s and 1890s. Uruguay, a smaller economy, specialized in wool, meat, and hides, with Montevideo as the main port, and developed in the early twentieth century a notable program of state-led reform. Both countries experienced the heavy inflow of foreign capital and immigrants, and both had economies in which urban commerce, finance, and services were closely tied to the countryside. The region’s growth was among the fastest in Latin America before 1914, and its dependence on British markets and capital was correspondingly deep, as the crisis of 1890 would show.

Section 68.7

Sugar, Cotton, and the Caribbean and Peruvian Coastal Economies

Sugar remained a central Latin American export, but its organization changed profoundly in the nineteenth century. In Cuba, the leading producer, sugar had expanded with slave labor in the first half of the century. After the Ten Years’ War (1868–1878) and the gradual abolition of slavery, completed in 1886, planters and mill owners turned to wage labor, tenant cane growers known as colonos, and technological modernization. Large steam-powered mills, called centrales, concentrated processing and drew cane from surrounding farms by rail. Capital requirements grew, and many smaller mills disappeared or were absorbed, while U.S. investors and refiners became increasingly involved. The war of 1895–1898, discussed in Chapter 67, damaged the sugar economy, and recovery after 1898 occurred under U.S. occupation and, after 1902, under a Cuban republic whose sugar economy was tied to the U.S. market, notably through the 1903 reciprocity treaty. Cuban production before 1898 and after must be distinguished: the later period saw far greater U.S. capital, larger mills, and further expansion of land under cane.

Puerto Rico followed a different course. Sugar, coffee, and tobacco were its principal exports in the late nineteenth century, with coffee particularly important in the highlands. After 1898, and the transition to U.S. rule examined in Chapter 67, sugar expanded substantially under U.S. corporate investment and access to the U.S. market, while coffee lost its former markets and suffered from a devastating hurricane in 1899. The island’s agricultural structure thus shifted toward large sugar enterprises, changing the position of highland coffee farmers and plantation workers. In the Dominican Republic, sugar production revived from the 1870s, aided in part by Cuban planters who had left the island during the Ten Years’ War and brought capital and experience, with modern mills and foreign investment expanding in the following decades.

On the arid coast of Peru, irrigated estates grew sugar and cotton. Cotton boomed briefly during the U.S. Civil War, when Southern supplies to European mills were interrupted, and sugar estates relied on indentured Chinese labor in the mid-century and then on other recruited workers. The Peruvian phase of the War of the Pacific (1879–1883), within the broader 1879–1884 conflict involving Bolivia, devastated sections of the coastal economy, and estates were damaged and in debt. Recovery in the 1890s and after drew on new investment, mechanization, and a consolidation of estate ownership. Sugar and cotton became again important exports, and Peruvian coastal agriculture, alongside mining, formed the basis of a gradual economic revival. These economies, in the Caribbean and on the Peruvian coast, shared a plantation form, large landholdings, heavy capital in mills, and a dependence on cheap labor, but they differed in their politics, their sources of capital, and their relations to the markets they served.

World sugar markets were difficult. European beet sugar, subsidized in several countries, competed with cane sugar and drove prices down in the later nineteenth century, a pressure that encouraged cane producers to cut costs through technology and scale. Producers who could modernize and gain secure access to a large market, notably Cuba in the U.S. market, prospered; those who could not were squeezed. The sugar story therefore shows how the same commodity could yield different outcomes depending on capital, technology, labor arrangements, and political relationships.

Section 68.8

Cacao, Bananas, and the Commercial Transformation of Tropical Agriculture

Ecuador’s cacao boom illustrates an export economy built on coastal plantations and a single port. Cacao, grown in the humid lowlands of the Guayas basin and shipped from Guayaquil, expanded strongly in the late nineteenth century as chocolate consumption rose in Europe and North America. By the early twentieth century Ecuador was among the world’s leading cacao producers. Large estates dominated production, worked by tenant and debt-bound laborers, many of them migrants from the highlands, and landowners and the merchant-bankers of Guayaquil became an economic and political elite. The boom financed Guayaquil’s growth and the government’s revenue, and it supported the construction of the railway between Guayaquil and Quito, completed in 1908 under the government of Eloy Alfaro with U.S. financing and engineering. But Ecuador’s dependence on one export, and on a few merchants and banks, left it vulnerable when cacao prices fell and plant disease spread after the First World War, developments that lie beyond this chapter.

Central America combined coffee and bananas. In Guatemala, El Salvador, and Costa Rica, coffee became the leading export from the 1860s onward, with governments adopting land and labor policies that favored growers. Coffee shaped political power and social structure in each country, but in different ways, and Chapter 69 treats the labor and land dimensions. The Caribbean lowlands, by contrast, became the region of the banana trade. In Costa Rica, Minor Cooper Keith, an American entrepreneur, built a railway from the highlands toward the Caribbean coast under government concession; to finance construction and to give the railway freight, he planted bananas along the line. Keith’s railway and banana business, supported by contracts and land grants, became a model of the linkage between transport and fruit.

The transformation of commercial organization was profound. Early banana trading was done by independent importers and shippers, such as the Boston-based firm that became the Boston Fruit Company, who bought from local growers and shipped fruit to U.S. ports. Bananas are perishable, and shipping them required speed, refrigeration or carefully timed ripening, and reliable markets. In 1899 the United Fruit Company was incorporated through the merger of Boston Fruit and Keith’s interests. It owned plantations, railways, ships, ports, and communications, and it marketed the fruit in the United States. Competitors, such as the firm that became Standard Fruit, also operated in Honduras and elsewhere. This model, known as vertical integration, meant that one corporation controlled production, transport, shipping, and sale, a very different structure from the Brazilian coffee chain, in which independent planters, railway companies, merchants, and exporters were separate.

Distinctions matter. Local growers sold fruit to the companies, often on terms the companies dictated; merchants handled local trade; large corporations controlled the export chain. In some regions, small growers continued to supply fruit under contract, while in others the companies cultivated their own vast estates. The companies negotiated concessions with weak governments, gained land and tax exemptions, and in places became dominant economic and political actors, a position that critics later described as enclave control. Chapter 70 touches on the political consequences of U.S. corporate power in the Caribbean basin; here the point is the commercial transformation, the movement from a trade in commodities by independent merchants to integrated corporate systems.

Section 68.9

Chile’s Nitrate Economy and the Transformation of Public Finance

The nitrate fields of the Atacama Desert gave Chile one of the most distinctive export economies of the era. Caliche was the nitrate-bearing desert ore or deposit; processing it yielded marketable sodium nitrate, used as fertilizer and in the manufacture of explosives. Rich fields lay in Tarapacá, formerly Peruvian, and the Antofagasta region, formerly Bolivian. The legal arrangements after the War of the Pacific were not identical: Peru ceded Tarapacá under the 1883 Treaty of Ancón, whereas the 1884 Chile–Bolivia truce maintained Chilean administration of the occupied Bolivian coast without a final cession. The 1904 Chile–Bolivia peace treaty later recognized Chilean dominion over that territory. Chile's government consequently taxed nitrate production from fields it controlled, but it is inaccurate to cite the 1884 truce alone as final recognition of sovereignty. Earlier Peruvian expropriations in the 1870s had compensated holders with certificates; under Chilean administration, certificate ownership became an important route to controlling works. British entrepreneur John Thomas North acquired major interests and became known as the nitrate king, though local investors and other foreign interests remained significant.

The industry combined desert labor, industrial processing, and railways. Workers excavated the caliche, and processing plants, known as oficinas, dissolved and crystallized the nitrate. Short railways carried it to ports such as Iquique and Antofagasta, from which steamers took it to Europe and elsewhere. The work was hard and the environment severe, and the labor force, recruited largely from central Chile and also from Peru and Bolivia, lived in company-controlled communities where wages were often paid in company tokens redeemable at company stores. Chapter 69 examines the labor movement that emerged from these conditions. Here the point is the structure: nitrate was a capital-intensive, geographically concentrated industry in a desert far from the centers of Chilean population.

Ownership was largely private, and a substantial share of the industry came to be controlled by British interests, although Chilean and other investors also held important positions. The Chilean state did not own the nitrate works but taxed their exports. This distinction is crucial. Export taxes on nitrate produced a very large share of Chile’s ordinary government revenue, in some years roughly half or more, allowing the government to reduce other taxes, fund public works, expand education, and build railways. The state captured a major part of the industry’s proceeds without running it, an arrangement that gave Chile unusual fiscal strength among Latin American states but also tied public finance closely to one product.

The wealth transformed Chilean public life. Spending on railways, port works, schools, military modernization, and the capital’s urban improvement drew on nitrate revenue. The government of José Manuel Balmaceda (1886–1891) pursued an ambitious program of public works financed largely by nitrate taxes, and the political conflicts of that period, ending in civil war in 1891, involved disputes over executive power and the relations among the state, the Congress, and nitrate interests. Historians debate how far nitrate wealth was channeled into productive investment rather than consumption and how far it benefited the broader population. What is clear is that the economy was exposed to risk: the industry depended on nitrate prices and demand, and the eventual development of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, whose commercial effects belong to the period after 1914, would erode the market. That later history should not be read back into the boom, when nitrate seemed to promise indefinite prosperity.

Section 68.10

Mexico’s Mining Revival: Silver, Copper, and the Porfirian Economy

Mexico had long been the world’s leading silver producer, but mining had been disrupted by the wars and instability of the early republic and by the costs of draining and transporting ore from mountain districts. Revival came in the last quarter of the century, tied to railways, new technology, legal change, and investment. Railway construction, which expanded from a few hundred kilometers in the mid-1870s to roughly twenty thousand by 1910, linked northern mining districts to the U.S. border and to ports. Lines built by U.S. and other foreign investors, with substantial federal subsidies, connected Mexico City to Ciudad Juárez and other border crossings, creating direct routes to the U.S. market.

Mining legislation changed the legal environment. The mining codes of 1884 and 1892 clarified rights to subsoil resources and made it easier for private owners, including foreigners, to acquire and hold mining property. Technological changes, including improved smelting, cyanide and other chemical processing, and electrical power, allowed exploitation of lower-grade ores. Foreign companies, notably from the United States, invested heavily in smelters and mines, and Mexican investors participated as partners and owners. The northern states, Chihuahua, Sonora, Coahuila, and Nuevo León, became centers of mining and processing, and a steel works and brewery at Monterrey indicated how mining and railway growth could stimulate related industry.

Silver remained important, but the world price fell after the 1870s as major economies adopted the gold standard and as new supply came onto markets. For a country on a silver standard, Mexico, the falling price had mixed effects: it made exports cheaper abroad and helped producers while raising the cost of foreign debts payable in gold. The silver decline also encouraged diversification. Copper became a major product, particularly at Cananea in Sonora, developed by U.S. interests after 1899, and the growth of electrical industries raised demand. Lead and zinc also expanded. In 1905 Mexico moved to a gold-exchange standard, a monetary reform that aimed to stabilize the currency for foreign investors and trade.

The mining boom was part of the broader economic program of the Díaz government, which sought to attract foreign capital, build railways, and open the economy. Export growth, mining revenue, and railway expansion strengthened federal finances, and Mexico’s overall foreign trade grew substantially. Evaluating the political dimensions, including repression, land concentration, and labor unrest that culminated in revolution, belongs to Chapter 71, and Chapter 69 covers labor and land. Here the point is economic: mining and railways reinforced each other, northern Mexico became closely tied to U.S. markets and capital, and the economy displayed both real growth and the risk of dependence on foreign finance and a few export sectors.

Section 68.11

Bolivia, Peru, and the Changing Andean Mineral Economies

Bolivia’s economy had been built on silver since the colonial period, centered on Potosí, whose mines Chapter 42 described. In the later nineteenth century, silver mining revived with new investment and technology, especially in the south, but the decline of silver prices and the high cost of transport undermined profitability. Tin, long found alongside silver and previously of little value, became commercially attractive as demand for tinplate, used in food cans, and for other industrial uses grew. Beginning in the 1890s and expanding in the following decades, tin overtook silver as Bolivia’s leading export, concentrated in the mining districts around Oruro and in the northern Potosí department.

Transportation determined which deposits could be worked. Bolivian tin lay high in the Andes, hundreds of kilometers from the sea, and was valuable only if it could be carried economically to ports. Railways were essential. A line from the Chilean port of Antofagasta reached Oruro in the early 1890s, followed by connections to La Paz and other centers, and the lines, financed largely by foreign capital, opened the altiplano to export. Processing and transport costs also mattered: much Bolivian tin was exported as concentrate and smelted abroad, so that part of the value added went to refiners in Europe. The geography of the industry therefore tied Bolivia to Pacific ports and foreign smelters.

The structure of ownership changed as the industry grew. Initially mines were held by Bolivian entrepreneurs and foreign companies of different sizes; consolidation later produced powerful groups. Simón I. Patiño's business career is associated with the extraordinarily rich La Salvadora tin lode, whose breakthrough discovery is generally dated to 1900, not the late 1890s. The precise sequence of acquisition, exploitation, and discovery needs to be distinguished rather than compressed into a single date. During the early twentieth century he expanded mining and processing interests into one of the world's important tin enterprises. Chapter 69 treats the labor and political consequences of the mining economy and Chapter 78 addresses the later revolution of 1952; here the central changes are the rise of tin, reliance on railways and foreign smelting, and concentration of capital.

Peru recovered gradually from the War of the Pacific and the loss of Tarapacá, but the state faced a severe foreign-debt burden. The debt settlement commonly known as the Grace Contract was negotiated and approved in stages in 1889–1890, with congressional approval in 1889 and later modifications. The Peruvian Corporation was registered in London on 20 March 1890 to implement arrangements under which foreign bondholders gained extensive railway and other rights in return for relieving Peru of much external debt. The complex settlement should not be represented simply as a single contract first concluded in 1890. It illustrates how financial restructuring could shift the control and revenues of transport infrastructure. Meanwhile silver and then copper, lead, and other highland minerals revived. An American-led syndicate acquired the Cerro de Pasco mining interests early in the twentieth century and developed copper production; railway links and processing technology made commercialization possible. The Central Railway toward Callao demonstrated the exceptional engineering required to carry highland output to the sea. As in Bolivia, mineral value at the pithead meant relatively little if freight, energy and refining expenses made sale abroad unprofitable.

Section 68.12

The Amazon Rubber Boom and the Extraction of Tropical Forest Resources

The rubber boom took place in a very different setting from the plantation and mining economies described above. Natural rubber, obtained from the latex of trees native to the Amazon basin, became essential to industry with the growth of machinery, electrical insulation, and, from the 1890s, bicycle and automobile tires. Amazonian rubber, harvested from wild trees dispersed through the forest, supplied the world’s demand for decades. Rubber exports rose strongly from the 1870s, and prices climbed in the early twentieth century, bringing prosperity to the commercial centers of Belém, at the mouth of the Amazon, and Manaus, far up the river.

The extraction system relied on credit and river transport. Rubber tappers, called seringueiros in Brazil, worked individual trails through the forest, collecting latex from scattered trees and smoking it into balls. They lived in isolation and received supplies, tools, food, and goods on credit from a trader or the owner of the trading post, to whom they had to deliver rubber. This arrangement, known as aviamento, tied tappers to patrons through debt and gave the patron control over prices and supplies. Steamers carried the rubber down the rivers to Belém and Manaus, where export houses, many of them foreign, purchased and shipped it. The commercial chain thus involved tappers, local traders, large merchant houses, and foreign buyers, and each link extracted value from the one below.

Workers came from varied backgrounds. Many tappers in the Brazilian Amazon were migrants from the drought-stricken northeast, recruited by labor agents and traveling to the forest in hope of earnings; others were Indigenous people and people of mixed ancestry already living in the region. The conditions were often harsh: indebtedness, disease, isolation, and the lack of legal protection. In parts of the western Amazon, in Peru, Bolivia, and Colombia, the system took particularly brutal forms. An investigation in the Putumayo region, publicized in 1912 by the British consul Roger Casement, documented the enslavement, torture, and killing of Indigenous people by agents of a rubber company operating there, and the report caused an international scandal. Such abuses were not universal, but they show how remote extraction zones, weak states, and demand for a valuable commodity could combine in extreme exploitation. Chapter 69 considers coerced labor and Indigenous dispossession in more detail.

The boom brought the construction of impressive urban buildings in Belém and Manaus, including the opera house in Manaus completed in 1896, and attracted merchants, immigrants, and investors. But this prosperity was concentrated in the commercial cities and among traders and merchants, and it did not transform the forest economy. Borders were also at stake: the Acre region, rich in rubber and populated by Brazilian tappers though Bolivian territory, was contested in a conflict that ended with the Treaty of Petrópolis (1903), under which Brazil acquired the territory. The treaty committed Brazil to pay £2 million sterling and to construct the Madeira–Mamoré railway to connect Bolivian transport with Amazonian navigation while bypassing difficult rapids. Construction in the early twentieth century revealed how costly it was to make even a valuable forest export accessible. The financial payment and railway obligation show that an international boundary settlement could also be an infrastructure bargain.

The boom ended abruptly. Seeds taken from Brazil in the 1870s had been propagated in British botanical gardens and then in plantations in Southeast Asia. Plantation rubber, grown in orderly rows, could be tapped more cheaply and reliably than wild rubber, and Asian output increased steadily in the early twentieth century, overtaking Amazonian production around 1912 and driving prices down. The Amazonian system, based on dispersed trees and costly labor, could not compete. The collapse impoverished traders and tappers and left the commercial cities in decline. It stands as a warning about commodities dependent on a single region when technology and organization allow competitors elsewhere. As Barbara Weinstein’s study shows, the rubber boom must be understood through its labor systems and commercial structures, not only through its spectacular urban monuments.

Section 68.13

Railroad Construction and the Creation of Export Corridors

Railroads were the central infrastructure of the export boom. Where they were built, they cut the cost of carrying bulk goods dramatically, opened new land, raised the value of property, and tied interior producers to ports. Where they were not built, goods remained expensive to move and regions remained outside the export economy. Construction expanded sharply after about 1870. Argentina’s network grew from a few thousand kilometers to more than thirty thousand by 1914, Brazil’s to roughly twenty-five thousand, and Mexico’s to approximately twenty thousand by 1910, while Chile, Peru, Cuba, and Central American countries built smaller but important systems. These are rounded figures drawn from standard compilations and should be verified against the scholarly sources before research use.

The layout of the networks reflected the purposes of their builders. In Argentina, lines fanned out from Buenos Aires across the pampas, a radial pattern that carried grain and livestock to the port but provided little east-west connection. In Brazil, lines ran from coffee districts to Santos and from other producing regions to Rio de Janeiro and northeastern ports, with different companies using different gauges, which hindered integration. In Mexico, trunk lines connected Mexico City to the U.S. border and to Veracruz, and branches served mining districts, so that the network was oriented toward the north and the sea. In Chile, a long north-south line linked the central valley with the south, while short lines in the northern desert served nitrate fields. In Peru and Bolivia, lines climbed from Pacific ports to mining districts in the highlands, among the highest railways in the world. In Central America, lines connected coffee and banana zones to ports and were often owned by fruit companies.

Engineering challenges were often formidable. Lines climbing the Andes, the escarpment behind Santos, and the Mexican sierras required tunnels, bridges, switchbacks, rack sections, and cable-hauled inclines, and construction costs were high. Financing came from several sources: private foreign companies, often British, building under concession; domestic companies, such as those financed by São Paulo planters; and state-owned lines built with public funds. Concessions often included subsidies per kilometer or guaranteed returns, which reduced investors’ risk but burdened state budgets. Some critics have argued that guarantees encouraged overbuilding or inflated costs; others emphasize that without them many lines would not have been built.

The economics of railroads explain why networks were uneven. A railway was profitable where traffic was heavy: a productive agricultural or mining region with a nearby port. Where population was sparse, production low, or terrain extreme, building was unprofitable without large subsidies. Hence regions with major export products received extensive networks, while others, such as much of the Amazon basin, the Colombian interior, and parts of the Andes and Central America, remained poorly connected or isolated. This result is a principal reason that export growth was geographically uneven, a theme returning in Section 68.18. Economic historians have also stressed the role of state fiscal capacity: governments with reliable revenue could afford subsidies and guarantees and so attracted railway investment, while fiscally weak governments struggled to do so.

Continuity and Change: Production and Commerce before and after the Railway

What Changed

  • Freight costs for bulk goods fell sharply, making distant land and low-value products commercially viable
  • Land along railway lines rose in value, and production shifted toward new frontiers
  • Ports and railway junctions grew into large commercial and urban centers
  • State revenue, tied to the volume of trade, expanded

  • Exports remained primary products, and elites who held land or mines remained powerful
  • Regions off the railway continued to rely on mule trains, carts, and rivers
  • Governments continued to depend on customs duties and foreign credit

Who experienced change most directly: Producers and merchants along new lines, and workers recruited to build and operate them. Greater continuity: Communities beyond the lines and many subsistence farmers. Why the pattern matters: Railways reorganized economic geography without erasing older patterns, so that modernization and persistence coexisted.

Section 68.14

Ports, Steamship Lines, Warehouses, and the New Infrastructure of Commerce

Railways delivered goods to ports, and ports had to be able to receive them. Many Latin American harbors in the mid-nineteenth century were shallow, exposed, or poorly equipped, and ships often anchored offshore while lighters carried cargo to land. As trade grew, governments and private companies invested in docks, breakwaters, cranes, warehouses, railway connections to the quays, customs sheds, and, increasingly, cold storage. These works were expensive and often depended on foreign capital and engineers, and they changed the cities in which they were built.

Buenos Aires, once handicapped by shallow waters, built a new port in the 1880s and 1890s with basins and quays that handled the grain and meat flowing from the pampas, becoming the principal commercial center of the south. Santos acquired its modern docks from the 1890s, a private company managing the quays and warehouses. Rio de Janeiro underwent port modernization in the early twentieth century. Valparaíso served central Chile, while Iquique and Antofagasta handled nitrates and, in Antofagasta’s case, Bolivian minerals. Veracruz, long a difficult harbor, was modernized with foreign contractors in the early 1900s, and Guayaquil developed as the point of export for Ecuadorian cacao. In the Caribbean and Central America, ports such as Havana, Limón, and others gained facilities, often tied to railways and fruit companies.

Shipping lines tied ports to world markets. British, German, French, Italian, and later American companies operated scheduled steamship services, and the line serving a port helped determine which markets and suppliers it connected with. Telegraph connections linked offices at the port with merchants and brokers abroad. Warehouses stored and graded goods, and facilities such as refrigerated holds and cold storage enabled the meat and fruit trades. The infrastructure concentrated commercial power in port cities, whose merchants, shippers, and bankers organized the flow of goods and credit.

The effects on cities were considerable. Port modernization created employment for dockworkers, warehouse staff, clerks, and builders, many of them immigrants, and drew migrants from the countryside. It also brought labor organization and conflict, as stevedores and sailors formed unions and struck, a theme taken up in Chapter 69. It enriched merchants and landowners with port connections and widened the gap between port cities and interior regions. In some places the concentration of port facilities in a single city, as in Buenos Aires, reinforced a pattern in which one metropolis dominated national commerce and finance.

Section 68.15

British, European, and American Capital in Latin America’s Export Economy

Foreign capital financed a large part of Latin American infrastructure, but how it participated mattered. Investment took the form of direct ownership of enterprises such as railways, mines, utilities, and plantations; loans to governments through bonds; shares in banks and companies; and shipping and commercial services. These forms differed in their risks, returns, and political consequences, and a historian who calls all of them “foreign investment” without distinguishing them will misread the period.

Britain was the leading supplier of overseas capital to much of South America. Estimating its aggregate investment requires care: one broad reconstruction places total British capital invested in Latin America at about £1.2 billion by 1913, whereas narrower tallies of particular securities or portfolio categories give smaller totals. Such numbers are not directly interchangeable because researchers differ in how they count government debt, locally registered companies, private placements, direct equity, and valuations. The figure here is a broad estimate of capital holdings at a date, not an annual flow of money and not proof of British majority ownership in each Latin American economy. Investments were concentrated especially in Argentina and Brazil, with major interests in Chile, Mexico and Uruguay, and heavily represented in railways and sovereign debt. Merchant bankers including Baring Brothers and Rothschild handled loans; British-registered companies held railway, utility, banking and commercial interests. Continental European capital from France, Germany, Belgium and elsewhere also financed loans, banks and electrical enterprises. The scale of British participation must be weighed against domestic investment, state ownership and the distinct sectoral pattern of U.S. capital.

The United States became increasingly important, especially in Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean. U.S. investors built railways and developed mines in Mexico, financed fruit and sugar enterprises in the Caribbean basin, and acquired utilities. U.S. investment was more often direct, owners managing enterprises themselves, than the portfolio lending typical of British investors. By 1914 the United States was a major investor in Mexico and Cuba, while Britain remained dominant in South’s southern economies. Chapter 70 examines the political consequences; the point here is the differing pattern of capital.

Local economic elites were not passive. Many projects were joint enterprises: São Paulo planters financed railways, Argentine landowners invested in banks and meatpacking, Chilean investors held nitrate properties, and Mexican elites partnered with foreign mining firms. Governments owned railways in several countries, financed through bonds. Foreign capital often arrived in partnership with local intermediaries who knew markets, laws, and politics. Foreign investment thus did not simply replace local capital; it combined with it in varying proportions. Yet foreign capital was often decisive for large, risky projects that domestic savings could not support, and its terms, including guarantees, tariffs, and repatriation of profits, shaped national development.

Section 68.16

Banks, Public Debt, Customs Revenue, and the Political Economy of Export Development

The financial system connected exports, government, and investment. Export earnings flowed into domestic banks and merchant houses, which financed harvests, advanced credit, and held deposits. Governments collected customs duties on trade and, where these were insufficient, borrowed, usually through bonds sold in London, Paris, or other markets and secured against customs revenue. The precise tax recipient varied: under Brazil’s 1891 federal constitution, import duties formed a central federal source while states, including coffee-rich São Paulo, gained the power to tax exports. Bond proceeds financed railways, ports, and public works, and interest and principal were repaid in gold or sterling. The system worked while exports and revenue grew, and it could unravel when they did not.

Exchange rates mattered. Exporters earned foreign currency, usually gold-linked, and paid costs in local currency, so a falling local currency raised their local earnings. Importers and governments with foreign-currency debts bore higher costs. Politicians and interest groups therefore fought over monetary policy: coffee planters in Brazil, who benefited from a weak currency, often favored it, while importers and bondholders favored stability. Several countries attempted to stabilize through convertibility, Argentina establishing a conversion office in 1899, Brazil in 1906, and Mexico moving to a gold-exchange standard in 1905, aiming to reassure foreign investors, at the price of constraining domestic monetary flexibility.

Domestic banks and merchants played roles often overlooked in accounts focused on foreign capital. Local banks financed trade and agriculture, and merchant-bankers in Guayaquil, Buenos Aires, and São Paulo accumulated significant wealth and political influence. Some governments established national banks to support exporters and manage public finance. Relations with foreign lenders varied: governments cooperated when they needed credit, bargained over terms, and sometimes defaulted or negotiated restructurings when revenues failed, as Peru did in the 1870s and 1880s and as other countries did in later crises. Foreign bondholders could press for settlements, as in Peru’s arrangement with its creditors, and lenders sometimes demanded control of revenues.

The central tension was between investment-led growth and fiscal risk. Borrowing financed infrastructure that raised exports and revenue, a virtuous circle, but it also committed governments to fixed payments in foreign currency, linked to volatile export earnings. When export prices fell or credit tightened, governments faced pressure to cut spending, raise taxes, or default, and their ability to respond was limited by their dependence on customs revenue. This is why rising export revenue could coexist with growing external debt and vulnerability: the same system that created growth also created exposure.

Section 68.17

Export Revenue, State Expansion, Urban Growth, and Early Industrialization

Export prosperity financed the growth of governments. Customs duties and, in some cases, export taxes gave states revenue to expand bureaucracies, armies, police forces, courts, schools, and public works. Chile’s nitrate revenue funded railways, education, and urban improvements, Argentine governments invested in ports, schools, and the capital’s transformation, and Brazilian states, notably São Paulo, built institutions and infrastructure supported by coffee revenue. Municipal governments installed water, sewers, paving, lighting, and tramways, and public health campaigns, such as the sanitation of Rio de Janeiro in the early twentieth century, depended on public funds. State expansion was uneven, and many rural areas saw little of it, but it marked a change from the fiscally weak states of earlier decades.

Cities grew rapidly. Buenos Aires, São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, Santiago, Mexico City, Havana, Montevideo, and others expanded through migration from the countryside and from abroad. Their growth reflected commerce, finance, services, and administration tied to exports, and they developed new neighborhoods, theaters, newspapers, and an urban middle class of clerks, professionals, and shopkeepers alongside a growing working class. Urban conditions varied: elite districts were modernized while working-class housing was often crowded and unsanitary, a contrast examined in Chapter 69.

Manufacturing also emerged, and its relationship to exports was complementary rather than opposed. Export earnings created demand for goods and capital for investment, railways created national markets, and tariffs provided some protection. Food processing, brewing, flour milling, textiles, cement, and small metalworking firms developed in Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Chile, and Colombia. São Paulo and Rio developed textile industries; Monterrey in Mexico built a brewery, glassworks, and steel plant; Argentine industry processed agricultural goods and made consumer goods; and Medellín in Colombia, enriched by coffee commerce, began textile manufacturing in the early twentieth century. Often the entrepreneurs were immigrants or merchants who had profited from trade, and the capital came from export-related wealth. Industrial growth and export specialization therefore occurred simultaneously, though industry remained small relative to the export sector and depended on imported machinery and fuel.

The fiscal arrangements differed sharply among countries, and an aggregate revenue figure can obscure the institutions that spent it. Chile could tax the export of nitrates at northern ports while leaving most enterprises in private hands; this financed central government projects but exposed the national treasury to nitrate prices. Brazil's First Republic, by contrast, strengthened state governments: coffee-rich São Paulo could collect export duties, issue debt and sponsor infrastructure, while the national government depended heavily on import duties. When coffee expanded, São Paulo acquired a fiscal advantage over regions with smaller internationally saleable surpluses. In Argentina, prosperity strengthened the national government's customs receipts at Buenos Aires while provinces negotiated over railways, credit and public works. The result was not a single fiscal 'export model' but several ways of translating the same commercial boom into public authority.

Schools, water systems and modernized streets were among the most visible results, yet their financial basis was often less secure than the finished buildings suggested. Public borrowing frequently anticipated future customs receipts, and railway companies sometimes received guaranteed earnings when traffic fell below expectations. A government could therefore inaugurate an impressive line while committing future taxpayers to payments independent of actual freight carried. Urban sanitation also reflected public-health crises and political struggles over who should pay for services. In Rio de Janeiro, major early-twentieth-century public works coincided with disruptive rebuilding and forceful campaigns that treated poor households differently from affluent districts. Economic modernization was a choice among investments with unequal beneficiaries, not merely an automatic dividend from foreign sales.

The emergence of factories needs equally precise treatment. Industrial activity tended to cluster where transport, energy, credit and consumer demand already existed. Food processing near meat-export centers and flour mills in wheat districts linked manufacturing directly to agricultural growth, while textile plants and breweries served expanding urban populations. Factory owners often bought machinery, chemicals or metal inputs from abroad, so growing local industry could raise imports even as it substituted for finished consumer goods. In São Paulo, the export economy created both the investment resources and the market for manufacturing, but manufacturing did not erase the economy's dependence on coffee. An economic history that labels the region 'only agricultural' misses these links; one that treats these early factories as fully diversified national industry exaggerates their reach.

The boom was a product of world demand, technology, finance, and domestic institutions together. It generated growth and vulnerability from the same causes, and its outcomes varied by country.
Section 68.18

Regional Inequality, Economic Enclaves, and the Uneven Distribution of Growth

The export boom was geographically selective. Regions with exportable products and transport links grew; others were left outside. Within countries, the contrast between prosperous export zones and neglected interiors was stark. In Argentina, the pampas and Buenos Aires flourished while the northwest and parts of the interior stagnated. In Brazil, São Paulo and the south of the country surged ahead while the northeast, once the center of sugar, declined in relative importance. In Mexico, the north and the capital region benefited disproportionately from railways and mining, while parts of the south were less connected. In Colombia, mountain barriers kept regions apart. Such patterns meant that national averages hid large regional differences.

Historians use the term enclave economy for export zones, often mining or plantation areas controlled by foreign or large companies, that were tightly linked to overseas markets but weakly integrated into the national economy. Nitrate fields, banana plantations, and some mining districts fit the description: they were developed by companies, depended on imported equipment, employed labor in company communities, and shipped products abroad, with limited purchases from or links to local producers. Other export systems were more nationally integrated. Argentina’s pampas economy involved many local farmers, merchants, and industries, and Brazilian coffee created a dense network of railways, towns, and services in São Paulo. The difference between enclave and integrated systems affected how widely the benefits of exports spread, how much of the revenue stayed in the country, and what linkages to other sectors developed. The distinction is a matter of degree, and historians debate which economies were closer to either pole.

Concentration characterized finance and commerce. Port cities and a few commercial centers held banks, export houses, insurance, and shipping offices, and capital flowed to areas with profitable opportunities. Access to infrastructure was unequal, and small producers away from the railway paid more to move goods and had less access to credit. Land ownership was highly concentrated in many regions, a theme developed in Chapter 69, and workers, especially in remote or coerced labor systems, shared little of the gains. The introduction of these inequalities here is intended to show how they interacted with the geography of export growth; their social history is reserved for the next chapter.

The contrast can be measured in travel cost as well as in export totals. An inland producer without a railway often relied on pack animals and carts, paid several intermediaries and faced long delays before reaching a port; a comparable producer near a main line could ship larger quantities, receive more current market information and borrow against predictable deliveries. These cost differences encouraged further planting and investment next to existing track, which made the commercially favored corridor still more attractive. The railway network therefore did not merely record regional differences; it reinforced them. Routes were usually selected where freight revenue or a government guarantee justified construction, not through a comprehensive effort to provide equal access to all communities.

The distinction between enclave and integrated production also requires care. A nitrate oficina could be foreign-financed and oriented toward overseas buyers but still purchase local food, use Chilean labor and contribute large tax receipts to the national treasury. A banana company might own docks and steamships yet rely on independent smallholders for part of its fruit. Conversely, a locally owned estate or mining enterprise could be highly concentrated and offer few benefits beyond the export corridor. It is therefore misleading to label every foreign-invested project an enclave or to assume that domestic ownership ensured broad development. The relevant tests are supplier linkages, local processing, labor earnings, tax retention and the circulation of profits within the region.

Export geography also shaped political bargaining. Provinces through which railways and customs revenues passed could demand favorable fiscal treatment; towns excluded from a trunk line pressed for branches or road funds; and port merchants often had interests different from those of hinterland growers. Economic statistics usually aggregate these competing communities into one national export figure, which is why historians compare provincial budgets, railway freight returns, census occupations and municipal accounts. These records can reveal why two areas exporting similar quantities of goods might have very different levels of local employment, manufacturing activity or public services. Chapter 69 examines the deeper land and labor inequalities that underlay these choices.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Export-Led Modernization or Structural Dependency?

The question: Did the export boom lay a foundation for sustained development, or did it entrench a pattern of dependence that limited it?

Interpretation A — Export-led modernization: Many economic historians emphasize that export growth was real and substantial, that it financed infrastructure, institutions, urban services, and early manufacturing, and that Latin America’s leading export economies grew rapidly relative to much of the world before 1914. On this view, specialization according to comparative advantage was a rational response to world demand, and later difficulties stemmed from later events, such as the Great Depression and wartime disruptions, or from domestic policy failures, rather than from the boom itself.
Interpretation B — Structural dependency: Dependency theorists, including Latin American scholars such as Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto, argued that specialization in primary exports tied economies to external markets and capital, concentrated benefits among exporters and foreign investors, and limited the development of domestic industry and internal markets. Related arguments about the terms of trade, associated with Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer, held that primary-product prices tended to decline relative to manufactures, though later research found that terms-of-trade trends varied by period and product and did not show a uniform decline before 1914.

Evidence: Both sides cite the same data: growth rates, export concentration, foreign ownership, fiscal revenue, price movements, and regional inequality. Scholars such as Bulmer-Thomas, Bértola, and Ocampo emphasize variation among countries and the role of institutions, which complicates a single verdict. Comparative evidence, such as the difference between Argentina’s integrated pampas economy and the enclave character of some nitrate and fruit zones, suggests that outcomes depended on how exports were organized.

Why it matters: The question shapes how twentieth-century policy, including import-substituting industrialization examined in Chapter 75, is understood: as a rejection of a failed model or as a response to specific vulnerabilities. Most current scholarship rejects a simple choice and instead asks under what conditions export growth produced broader development.

Section 68.19

Commodity Price Cycles, Financial Crises, and the Vulnerabilities of Export Dependence

Export economies faced risks from price movements, credit conditions, and competition. Prices of primary commodities were volatile, driven by weather, harvests, demand cycles, and the expansion of supply elsewhere. Peru’s guano, the early example, showed what could happen: revenues that had financed the state in the mid-century dwindled as deposits were exhausted and competing fertilizers appeared, leaving the government in default by 1876. Later commodities faced analogous threats from new producers, as with rubber from Asian plantations, or from overproduction, as with Brazilian coffee, where large harvests depressed prices and led to state intervention.

Argentina’s crisis of 1890 shows how finance and exports interacted. During the 1880s, Argentina received heavy inflows of foreign capital, much from British investors, and the government and provincial banks expanded credit and borrowed abroad, fueling speculation, land and construction booms, and inflation. A political uprising in Buenos Aires in July 1890, followed by the resignation of President Miguel Juárez Celman, coincided with a loss of investor confidence. Baring Brothers, which had underwritten large Argentine issues, found itself unable to meet obligations, and in November 1890 the Bank of England organized a rescue involving other banks to prevent a wider panic. In Argentina, the crisis brought currency depreciation, bank failures, and a sharp contraction, and the government had to restructure its debts. The episode shows how heavy borrowing in good times could produce vulnerability, and how a financial shock in one country could affect London. Recovery came in the 1890s as exports of grain and meat grew, illustrating also the underlying strength of the pampas economy.

Later episodes tested other economies. The international financial disturbance of 1907, beginning in the United States, tightened credit and lowered commodity prices, affecting exporters of copper, coffee, and other goods and showing how closely Latin American economies were tied to global finance. Competition changed fortunes: Asian rubber, new nitrogen sources in the longer term, and expanding supplies of other goods altered markets. The outbreak of war in 1914 disrupted shipping, insurance and credit, made access to some European destinations difficult, and led some governments to suspend convertibility; it did not close European trade uniformly. Credit contraction and wartime maritime risk abruptly altered the system of integrated international finance in which the export boom had flourished. The effects varied: some exporters lost markets, while others, such as suppliers of food and metals for the war effort, would later gain. These developments belong in part to the chapters that follow and should not be read back into the earlier decades.

A commodity downturn affected different groups through separate channels. A fall in the world price of coffee immediately reduced exporters' foreign-currency receipts, but its effect on planters depended on exchange rates, stored inventories, debts and freight charges. Merchants carrying unsold stock could face margin calls, while governments whose budgets relied on duties might lose revenue even when output remained high. The São Paulo coffee-valorization agreement reached at Taubaté in 1906 attempted to stabilize prices by arranging the purchase and financing of surplus coffee. Such action showed both the market power of a dominant producer and the risks of tying public credit to the maintenance of a particular export price. Students must therefore avoid mistaking increased production, export value and fiscal revenue for interchangeable measures.

The Baring crisis provides a second mechanism, centered on capital rather than a single harvest. The immediate difficulty was not that the pampas had ceased producing wheat or meat, but that creditors doubted whether heavily indebted Argentine public authorities and related enterprises could meet obligations. When underwriters in London could not place or refinance securities, a local borrowing problem became an international banking problem. The Bank of England coordinated a guarantee for Baring Brothers in November 1890, but that intervention did not remove Argentina's need for adjustment. The crisis demonstrates why an otherwise productive export economy might suffer a financial breakdown when liabilities matured faster than capital or tax receipts arrived.

The disruption after the outbreak of war in 1914 similarly varied by product and destination. Wartime shipping shortages, higher insurance premiums, blocked finance and changing European consumption reduced some sales and delayed payments; foodstuffs and metals useful to belligerent economies could eventually find stronger demand. Countries reliant on imported machinery or manufactured supplies faced difficulties even when their exports continued. For historical comparison the decisive questions are which market closed or remained open, how contract prices and freight rates changed, and whether the government could substitute alternative tax receipts. These questions prepare the reader for the social and political tensions of the following chapters without suggesting that one global crisis produced identical national results.

Historical Vocabulary
Commodity dependence and terms of trade

Commodity dependence describes a situation in which an economy relies heavily on the export of a small number of primary products, so that its income, government revenue, and employment depend on prices and demand in international markets. Terms of trade are the ratio between the prices a country receives for its exports and the prices it pays for its imports; when export prices fall relative to import prices, a given volume of exports buys fewer imports.

These concepts help explain why export growth could coexist with vulnerability. They should not be applied mechanically: before 1914, terms of trade for Latin American exporters varied by country and period, and some experienced improvements as well as declines. Dependence also differed in degree: Cuba’s reliance on sugar was far greater than Argentina’s reliance on a mix of meat, wool, and grain.

Historical Synthesis: Growth, Dependence, and Latin America’s Place in the World Economy

Returning to the coffee sack at Santos, one can now see the system behind it. A planter, supported by railway and port, a commission agent and a bank, a steamship company and an insurer, and a state collecting taxes and borrowing abroad, all participated in a chain whose product was consumed far away. The same chain, repeated with variations for beef, nitrates, sugar, tin, and rubber, defined the export economies of the period. Comparing them reveals both common features and sharp contrasts.

The benefits were real. Exports grew rapidly, railways and ports were built, governments gained revenue and capacity, cities expanded, and in several countries early industry emerged. Integration into world markets raised incomes in the most dynamic regions and created new opportunities for entrepreneurs, merchants, professionals, and workers. The leading export economies, notably Argentina, Uruguay, Chile, Brazil’s south, and Mexico’s north, achieved significant growth by the standards of their time. To deny these achievements would be to ignore the evidence.

The limits were equally real. Economies depended on a few commodities whose prices they did not control, on foreign credit that could be withdrawn, and on fiscal systems tied to trade. Infrastructure was built along profitable export corridors rather than to knit national economies together, leaving interior regions isolated. In enclave and frontier zones, as in nitrates, bananas, and rubber, the benefits were narrowly distributed and labor was often harshly treated. Foreign capital was important but not all-powerful; local elites and governments shaped the terms. And the gains were distributed unequally among countries, regions, and social groups.

The opportunities available to different actors differed. Governments could capture revenue and build institutions, but at the price of dependence on trade taxes and debt. Domestic entrepreneurs could prosper in export commerce, finance, and, in some countries, industry, but often depended on foreign markets and capital. Foreign investors earned returns, sometimes handsome and sometimes disappointing, and gained influence over infrastructure and finance. Workers could find employment and, in cities, rising wages, but many faced coerced or precarious conditions. A single evaluative label, success or failure, does not fit these varied experiences.

The answer to the chapter’s central question is therefore not an either-or. The export boom was a powerful and measurable transformation that established infrastructure, institutions, and commercial networks, and it also created new vulnerabilities and uneven development. How durable the foundation proved depended on institutions, infrastructure, commodity mix, market conditions, and social organization, and these differed by country. The consequences were not only economic. Export growth intensified struggles over land, labor, immigration, and Indigenous territory, examined in Chapter 69, and it deepened the ties between Latin American economies and the United States and Europe that shaped the political realignment of Chapter 70. The export boom thus formed the economic setting for the social and political conflicts of the early twentieth century.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the export boom that transformed Latin American economies between about 1870 and 1914. Its central argument is that the boom was a major transformation in production, transportation, finance, and state revenue that connected the region to international markets and created real opportunities for growth, while reinforcing dependence on fluctuating commodity markets, concentrating infrastructure along profitable corridors, and distributing benefits unevenly.

The boom began with world demand. Industrialization and urban growth in Europe and the United States created demand for foods, fertilizers, industrial metals, rubber, and tropical goods, while steamships, telegraphs, and refrigeration lowered the cost and uncertainty of distance. Demand alone did not produce growth: it required domestic political stability, state policies, infrastructure, and capital. Governments professed liberalism but acted vigorously, granting concessions, guaranteeing returns, passing mining and land laws, and depending on customs revenue.

Agricultural exports followed varied patterns. Brazilian coffee and the railway grew together, shifting to western São Paulo, drawing on immigrant labor after abolition, and supported by local planter capital and a British-built line to Santos. Colombian coffee developed more slowly among smaller holdings and was hampered by transport. The Río de la Plata combined wool, grain, and refrigerated meat in an economy tied to British capital and railways. Sugar economies in Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, and coastal Peru industrialized under changing labor and capital conditions, and cacao and bananas showed a movement from independent trading toward vertically integrated corporations such as the United Fruit Company.

Mineral and forest exports differed in capital needs and geography. Chile’s nitrates, privately owned but taxed by the state, financed public finance while tying it to one export. Mexico’s mining revival depended on railways, legal change, and investment, and Bolivia and Peru shifted toward tin and copper where transport permitted. The Amazon rubber boom, based on credit-bound tapping and merchant networks, produced prosperity in Belém and Manaus and severe exploitation in the forest before Asian plantations destroyed its markets.

Railways, ports, and finance made the system work. Railway networks, shaped by profitability and geography, created export corridors and left other regions isolated. Ports modernized and concentrated commercial power. Foreign capital, led by Britain and increasingly joined by the United States, took the forms of ownership, lending, and concessions, but local investors and governments were active partners. Banks, bonds, and customs revenue connected exports to public finance, and the same system that fostered growth exposed governments to fiscal risk.

The boom financed state expansion, urban growth, and early industrialization, with manufacturing developing alongside exports in Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, and elsewhere. But growth was geographically uneven, producing prosperous export zones and neglected interiors, and in some regions, enclave economies weakly integrated into national life. The historical debate between export-led modernization and structural dependency reflects both facets, and current scholarship emphasizes variation and institutions.

Crises tested the model. Peru’s guano decline offered an early warning, Argentina’s Baring crisis of 1890 showed the risks of heavy borrowing, the financial disturbance of 1907 transmitted shocks internationally, competition undermined rubber, and the outbreak of war in 1914 disrupted trade and credit. These episodes showed both the vulnerability of export economies and their capacity to recover.

Regional variation was a constant theme. The pampas, the Brazilian coffee frontier, Chile’s desert, Mexico’s mines, Central America’s banana coast, and the Amazon rubber frontier followed different paths, and no single country represented the region. Spanish and Portuguese America, and the Caribbean, were all integrated, in different ways, into the world economy.

The chapter therefore rejects both triumphalist and purely pessimistic accounts. The boom built infrastructure, institutions, and wealth, and it created vulnerabilities and inequalities. Its consequences for land, labor, immigration, and Indigenous peoples are taken up in Chapter 69, and its deepening of links with the United States and Europe frames Chapter 70. Students should also take away a method: economic evidence, whether trade tables, prospectuses, concession contracts, or press reports, reflects the purposes of its creators and must be read critically, with attention to units, dates, and whose experience it omits.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Export-led growth
Economic expansion driven by overseas sales of primary products, which stimulated transport, commerce, and state revenue.
Commodity
A standardized primary product, such as coffee, wheat, nitrate, or copper, traded in international markets at prices set largely by supply and demand.
Terms of trade
The ratio of export prices to import prices, indicating how much imports a given quantity of exports can buy.
Customs revenue
Government income from duties on imports and, in some countries, exports; the main source of public finance in the period.
Economic liberalism
A doctrine favoring open trade, private property, and foreign investment, which in practice coexisted with extensive state support.
Concession
A government grant to a company of the right to build or operate a railway, mine, or utility on stated terms.
Guaranteed return
A government promise to make up a company’s earnings if they fell below a stated percentage of invested capital.
Gold standard
A monetary system in which currency is convertible into gold at a fixed rate, adopted by major economies and, in various forms, by several Latin American states.
Sovereign bond
A government debt security sold to investors, often in London or Paris, and repaid in gold or sterling.
Foreign direct investment
Ownership and management of an enterprise by investors from another country, as distinguished from portfolio lending.
Enclave economy
An export zone, often controlled by large or foreign companies, tightly linked to overseas markets and weakly connected to the national economy.
Export corridor
A route of railways and ports connecting a producing region to overseas markets.
Frigorífico
A meatpacking plant that slaughtered and prepared meat, often refrigerated, for export.
Comissário (commission agent)
A merchant who sold producers’ goods, advanced credit, and supplied goods, especially in Brazilian coffee ports.
Colono
A term for a contract farm worker or tenant, applied to immigrant coffee workers in São Paulo and to tenant cane growers in Cuba.
Oficina
A nitrate processing plant and the company community surrounding it in northern Chile.
Aviamento
The credit system of the Amazon rubber trade in which patrons advanced goods to tappers in exchange for rubber.
Seringueiro
A rubber tapper of the Brazilian Amazon.
Valorization
Government purchase of surplus commodity stocks to support prices, as in the Brazilian coffee program of 1906.
Vertical integration
Control by one firm of production, transport, shipping, and marketing, characteristic of the United Fruit Company.
Baring crisis
The 1890 financial emergency triggered by Baring Brothers’ exposure to Argentine debt and resolved by a Bank of England-led rescue.
Treaty of Ancón
The 1883 treaty ending the Chilean-Peruvian phase of the War of the Pacific and ceding Tarapacá to Chile.
Terra roxa
The fertile reddish soil of western São Paulo that supported high-yield coffee.
Centrales
Large steam-powered sugar mills that concentrated processing in Cuba and drew cane from surrounding farms.
Dependency theory
A body of twentieth-century scholarship arguing that peripheral economies were shaped and constrained by their position in the world economy.
Import-substituting manufacturing
Domestic production of goods previously imported, which began on a small scale before 1914 and became state policy later.
Golondrinas
Seasonal immigrant harvest workers in Argentina, called “swallows” because they migrated back and forth.
Guano
Bird droppings used as fertilizer, whose export financed Peru’s state until declining deposits and debt led to default.

Chapter Reflection

The export boom matters because it created the economic setting in which twentieth-century Latin America took shape. Railways, ports, fiscal systems, and commercial networks built between 1870 and 1914 outlasted the boom, and the patterns of regional inequality, commodity specialization, and external linkage that emerged continued to influence policy debates for generations.

The chapter also shows how much depended on institutions and geography. The same global demand produced very different results in the pampas, the Chilean desert, the Brazilian plateau, and the Amazon, because states, property systems, labor arrangements, and transport differed. Learning to read economic evidence critically, as statistics, contracts, prospectuses, and reports shaped by their authors, is a skill that continues through the volumes that follow.

Chapter 69 takes up the social foundations and consequences of this transformation: the oligarchies that controlled land and exports, the immigrants and workers who supplied labor, the forced labor and dispossession that accompanied frontier expansion, and the emerging labor movements. Chapter 70 then turns to the growing role of the United States, Panama, and the changing hemispheric order. Students should carry forward the view that Latin American economies were neither passive objects of foreign power nor uniformly successful modernizers, but active participants in a changing world economy whose gains and risks were unevenly shared.
