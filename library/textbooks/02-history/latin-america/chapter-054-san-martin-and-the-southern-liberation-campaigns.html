San Martín and the Southern Liberation Campaigns

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Declaring Independence Is Not Winning It

Between 1816 and 1822 an army assembled in the dry foothills of Cuyo crossed the Andes, liberated Santiago, sailed up the Pacific, and entered Lima. It still did not defeat Spanish power in Peru.

This chapter follows the southern independence campaigns from the Río de la Plata to Chile and Peru. Its central problem is how a regionally divided revolution that began in Buenos Aires in 1810 became a continental campaign that liberated Chile and established an independent government in Lima, yet failed to defeat royalist power across Peru.

José de San Martín stands at the center of the story, but the chapter does not reduce independence to his achievements. He returned to a revolution already two years old. His army depended on the civilians of Cuyo, on the government of Juan Martín de Pueyrredón, on Chilean exiles led by Bernardo O’Higgins, on a Chilean navy commanded by Thomas Cochrane, and on thousands of soldiers, many of them enslaved or free men of African descent, whose names the records seldom preserve. Manuel Belgrano, Martín Miguel de Güemes, Juana Azurduy, and Peruvian patriots and royalists also shaped the war.

Three settings must be kept distinct. The Río de la Plata was a contested political space, not yet an Argentina. Chile was a captaincy general divided among its own patriot factions. Peru was the richest and most conservative stronghold of the crown, where many elites feared social upheaval more than they resented Spanish rule. Indigenous communities appear throughout as diplomats, soldiers, laborers, tribute-payers, and political actors.

The evidence includes San Martín’s correspondence, official proclamations, congressional records, military dispatches, newspapers, and memoirs, many written to defend reputations or to build national memory. The chapter therefore asks of each source who wrote it, for whom, and what it leaves out.

The chapter belongs to Volume III and Part XII. Chapter 53 covers Bolívar’s northern campaigns and Chapter 55 covers the final wars in the Andes, including Junín, Ayacucho, and the creation of Bolivia. This chapter ends with San Martín’s withdrawal in September 1822 and does not treat the proclamation of 1821 as the end of the war.

Historical Context

The Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata was created in 1776 and joined Buenos Aires, Cuyo, the northwest, Paraguay, the Banda Oriental, and Upper Peru. After Napoleon’s invasion of Spain (Chapter 51), the crown’s legitimacy collapsed, and juntas appeared from Buenos Aires to Santiago. The Wars of Independence that followed were also civil wars, in which royalists included creoles, Indigenous communities, and Afro-descended people.

Chile was a captaincy general with its own elite and a southern frontier with the Mapuche. Peru was a viceroyalty with a wealthy capital, highland Indigenous majorities, and a royalist army drawing on Upper Peru. Chapter 53 explains how Bolívar’s northern campaigns developed in parallel, and Chapter 49 treats the earlier Andean rebellions whose memory shaped elite fears.

Distinguishing declaration from control is essential. A government could proclaim independence without governing the territory it claimed, and a victorious army could hold a capital without defeating the enemy’s main force. The chapter returns to these distinctions repeatedly.

Section 54.1

The Río de la Plata on the Eve of Revolution

The Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata was created in 1776, a product of the Bourbon reforms examined in Chapter 48. It joined territories that had previously looked in different directions: the port of Buenos Aires on the Atlantic estuary, the silver district of Upper Peru centered on Potosí, the agricultural and artisan towns of Tucumán, Salta, and Cuyo, the Guaraní mission lands and yerba mate economy of Paraguay, and the Banda Oriental across the river, where Montevideo served as the Spanish naval base. The viceroyalty was an administrative arrangement, not a nation. Its regions had different economies, different Indigenous and African-descended populations, and different relationships with Lima, Santiago, and Madrid.

Buenos Aires had risen on commerce. The 1778 decree of "free trade" within the empire legalized direct commerce with Spanish ports, and the city's merchants profited from hides, tallow, and salted meat from the pampas and from the legal and contraband movement of silver coming down from Upper Peru. Its population is usually estimated at roughly forty thousand around 1810, though such figures are approximate. In the interior, Tucumán and Salta raised and sold mules for the Andean mines, Córdoba and the northwest produced textiles and foodstuffs, and Cuyo, on the eastern flank of the Andes, sent wine and brandy to the Pampas and traded cattle across the cordillera to Chile. Mendoza and San Juan would later become the base of San Martín's army.

The population was varied. In the Andean north and in Upper Peru, Quechua and Aymara communities formed the majority and carried obligations of tribute and labor. Guaraní communities lived in the old Jesuit mission towns, whose institutions had been disrupted after the Jesuits' expulsion in 1767. On the southern and western frontiers, Pampa, Ranquel, Pehuenche, and Mapuche peoples traded with, raided, and negotiated with the Spanish. Enslaved Africans and their descendants worked in Buenos Aires, Montevideo, Córdoba, and the interior as domestic servants, artisans, laborers, and rural workers, and free people of African descent served in the militias. Historians note that census categories of the period are imprecise and politically shaped, so the sizes of these groups are matters of estimate.

The event that changed local politics before the Napoleonic crisis was foreign invasion. In June 1806 a small British force under William Carr Beresford captured Buenos Aires after the viceroy, the Marqués de Sobremonte, withdrew toward Córdoba. The occupation lasted only weeks. A force organized by Santiago de Liniers, a French-born officer in Spanish service, retook the city in August 1806 with the help of militia units and volunteers. A second and larger British attack in 1807 reached Montevideo and then Buenos Aires, where it was defeated in street fighting in July.

The consequences were large and lasting. The city had been defended by locally raised units, divided by origin, including Patricios and other corps, and by companies of free Black and pardo men. The cabildo of Buenos Aires had acted when the viceroy had not. Creole officers, merchants, and officials came to see armed local power as legitimate. Historians have debated how directly the invasions caused the revolution of 1810, but there is broad agreement that they created militarized urban society, weakened confidence in the viceroy, and trained men such as Cornelio Saavedra who would later hold power. The evidence for these years includes cabildo minutes, militia rolls, official dispatches, and British accounts, each reflecting the interests of its authors.

Section 54.2

The May Revolution of 1810 and the Struggle for Sovereignty

The crisis of the Spanish monarchy described in Chapter 51 reached Buenos Aires in the autumn of 1810. News that the Spanish Junta Central, which had governed in Ferdinand VII's name, had been dissolved and that most of southern Spain had fallen to French forces reached the city in mid-May. The viceroy, Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros, had been appointed by that Junta, and many in the city argued that his authority had lapsed with it. The question of who now held legitimate power was a constitutional problem before it was a military one.

Pressure from militia officers and creole leaders forced the cabildo to convene an open assembly on 22 May. Several hundred invited residents attended, and after debate, the assembly voted to remove the viceroy. Efforts to preserve a form of continuity with Cisneros at the head of a new body collapsed in the following days, and on 25 May 1810 a Primera Junta took office with Cornelio Saavedra as president and Mariano Moreno and Juan José Paso as secretaries. The Junta governed in the name of Ferdinand VII. That formula allowed leaders to claim loyalty to the king while exercising independent authority, and historians still argue over how sincerely it was meant and how far it concealed a deliberate move toward independence.

Historical Vocabulary
Cabildo abierto

A cabildo abierto was an "open" meeting of a town council, to which leading residents were invited, used in emergencies. In 1810 these assemblies in Buenos Aires and other cities became the means by which royal officials were replaced by local juntas.

They were not democratic assemblies. Participation was limited to invited men of standing, and the claim on which they acted, that sovereignty reverted to the people (pueblos) when the monarch was absent, was understood differently in different cities. That difference of interpretation is the source of much of the conflict described in the next section.

That theory of sovereignty created a difficulty from the beginning. If power had returned to the pueblos, which pueblos? Buenos Aires acted as the heir to the viceroy and expected the interior cities to follow. Many interior cities accepted the new government, but others did not. Córdoba rose against the Junta under Liniers, who had been a hero of 1806 and was captured and executed in August 1810. Montevideo, Paraguay, and the royalist-held regions of Upper Peru and Chile each took their own course. The Junta responded in December by enlarging itself with provincial deputies as the Junta Grande, and later governments took the forms of Triumvirates (1811–1814) and, from 1814, a Supreme Directorship.

Within Buenos Aires, the revolution brought a split between Moreno's radical, centralizing approach and Saavedra's more cautious one. Moreno, who died at sea in 1811, wrote and edited the Gazeta de Buenos Ayres, which spread revolutionary ideas. A document called the "Plan of Operations," long attributed to him, advocated ruthless measures, but its authenticity has been questioned. This is an example of a source whose later use as evidence of revolutionary intent must be treated with care.

Section 54.3

Buenos Aires, Provincial Rivalries, and War on Multiple Fronts

From 1810, the revolutionary government fought on several fronts, each with its own politics. To the north, an expedition under Juan José Castelli entered Upper Peru and won the battle of Suipacha in November 1810, but was crushed at Huaqui in June 1811. Royalist armies, supplied from Lima by Viceroy José Fernando de Abascal, reoccupied the region. Over the next years, patriot armies under Manuel Belgrano won victories at Tucumán in September 1812 and Salta in February 1813, but lost at Vilcapugio and Ayohuma in late 1813. Upper Peru remained a royalist base, and the northern passage to Lima, which patriot leaders at first assumed would be the natural route, stayed closed.

To the northeast, Belgrano led a small force into Paraguay in late 1810 and early 1811. He was defeated at Paraguarí and Tacuarí, but the outcome was not a royalist restoration. Paraguayan officers deposed the Spanish governor in May 1811 and established their own governing junta, and Paraguay moved toward a separate independence rather than joining Buenos Aires. This is a useful example of the difference between opposing Spanish rule and accepting Buenos Aires's leadership.

In the Banda Oriental, José Gervasio Artigas, a former militia officer, joined the revolution in 1811 and helped win the battle of Las Piedras in May. In the winter of 1811, after Buenos Aires made an armistice with the royalists of Montevideo, thousands of rural people, many of them with their families and livestock, followed Artigas in a mass migration across the Uruguay River known as the Exodus of the Orientals. Artigas became the leader of a movement that favored a confederation of equal provinces and also pursued social measures that favored small rural producers. His "Instructions of 1813" expressed these views, and his League of Free Peoples challenged Buenos Aires's claim to lead. Buenos Aires forces took Montevideo in June 1814, but the dispute over the nature of the union continued.

The Assembly of the Year XIII, convened in Buenos Aires in 1813, did not declare independence, but it passed notable reforms: it abolished the Indigenous tribute and personal service, limited titles of nobility, ended the Inquisition, and decreed that children born to enslaved mothers on or after 31 January 1813 (the decree itself dates from 2 February 1813) would be free. The "free womb" law did not end slavery, and enslaved people in the region continued to work in bondage for decades, but it shows that the revolution raised questions about status and legal equality. For the southern campaigns, the main significance of these years is that revolution in the Río de la Plata was a series of struggles over sovereignty. It was not a single national war, and every later request for troops and money depended on provincial consent that could not be assumed.

Section 54.4

San Martín's Return, the Granaderos, and the Early Revolutionary Army

José de San Martín arrived in Buenos Aires in March 1812, two years after the revolution began. He had been born in 1778 in Yapeyú, a former Jesuit mission town in the Misiones region, and had been taken to Spain as a child. He entered the Spanish army as a young cadet, fought in North Africa and in the war against revolutionary France, and served in the Peninsular War, where he took part in the Spanish victory over a French army at Bailén in 1808. In 1811 he resigned his commission and traveled via London to Buenos Aires, in the company of other American-born officers, including Carlos de Alvear. His motives for leaving Spain are not fully documented, and later accounts that describe his journey as a planned mission rest on reminiscence as much as on contemporary evidence.

He returned to a revolution in progress, not to a blank slate. The government of the First Triumvirate received him and commissioned him to form a cavalry unit. In the following months, he helped organize the Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers (Granaderos a Caballo), trained on European lines and led by officers chosen for discipline. San Martín was also involved in political networks that sought to direct the revolution. Historians refer to the secret "Lautaro Lodge" as a vehicle for this, though the nature, size, and influence of the lodge are debated, and much about it comes from later recollections. In October 1812, a movement in which San Martín took part removed the First Triumvirate, and a new government took power.

In February 1813, the Granaderos fought their first action at San Lorenzo, on the Paraná River, repelling a Spanish landing party from the river fleet that was raiding the coast for supplies. The clash was small but valuable: it gave the new unit a victory and a tradition, and it stands as one of the foundational episodes of the revolutionary army. The account of the rescue of San Martín by a soldier, usually identified as Juan Bautista Cabral, comes from later retellings and should be read as part of the memory of the battle.

The unit and its commander show features of the army that would later cross the Andes. The Granaderos recruited many men from the provinces and from the lower classes, including free people of African descent, and San Martín valued discipline, training, and engineering as much as courage. His experience in Europe made him skeptical of improvised campaigns, and the defeats in Upper Peru gave him evidence for his skepticism. On 29 January 1814 he formally took command of the Army of the North after Belgrano's defeats, and what he saw there would lead him to propose a different strategy, which is the subject of the next division.

Section 54.5

The Northern Front, Belgrano, Güemes, and the Strategic Impasse

The northern front was the first and longest test of revolutionary strategy. Upper Peru held the silver of Potosí and the Indigenous labor that had sustained the viceroyalty, and royalist commanders there drew supplies and troops from Lima. Patriot armies entered it four times between 1810 and 1815 and each time were forced back. After Belgrano's victories at Tucumán and Salta in 1812–1813 and his defeats at Vilcapugio and Ayohuma, the army under José Rondeau was crushed at Sipe Sipe in November 1815. The pattern was consistent: long supply lines through mountainous country, hostile or exhausted local populations, and a royalist army that could retreat toward its own bases.

San Martín took command of this demoralized army on 29 January 1814 and spent only months with it. He concluded that a frontal advance through Upper Peru was not feasible, and he asked to be reassigned. The government of Gervasio Posadas appointed him governor-intendant of Cuyo in August 1814. His reasons, as later told by himself and his associates, combined strategic judgment with ill health, and historians treat the retrospective accounts cautiously. What is established is the result: the main army no longer sought a breakthrough in the north, and a different plan began to form in the west.

The northern front nevertheless mattered, and not only as a failure. In Salta and Jujuy, Martín Miguel de Güemes organized rural militias of gauchos, tenant farmers, Indigenous men, and Afro-descended people, who harassed royalist columns and defended the frontier in what is often called the "gaucho war." Güemes's forces held back royalist invasions, notably in 1817, and kept large royalist forces occupied while San Martín prepared to cross the Andes. Güemes was mortally wounded in a royalist attack on Salta in June 1821 and died shortly afterward. His movement relied on the support of local elites and the grant of legal privileges (fuero) to the militiamen, which also made him a political rival to the elite of Salta.

Inside Upper Peru itself, regional guerrilla bands called republiquetas resisted royalist rule for years. Their leaders included Manuel Ascencio Padilla and his wife, Juana Azurduy, who fought as a commander and was wounded in the campaign. The republiquetas were rooted in Indigenous and mestizo communities and relied on local support, which the patriots in Buenos Aires could not supply from outside. They tied down royalist troops but could not drive them out. The strategic impasse, therefore, was a result of terrain, supplies, and the strength of royalist institutions in a region that had been the center of colonial wealth.

Section 54.6

Chile's Early Revolution, Rancagua, and the Royalist Reconquest

Chile's revolution began on 18 September 1810 with a junta in Santiago that, like the one in Buenos Aires, claimed to govern in the name of Ferdinand VII. Chilean elites were divided between moderates, radicals, and royalists, and also by family and regional rivalries. José Miguel Carrera, a young officer from a prominent family, seized power in 1811 and led a government that promoted reform, the press, and a flag of its own, but he also faced rivals in Concepción and in the province's southern elite. Bernardo O'Higgins, born the illegitimate son of the Irish-born viceroy Ambrosio O'Higgins and a Chilean woman, became a military commander and rival of Carrera.

Royalist Peru did not wait. Viceroy Abascal sent expeditions from Lima in 1813, led successively by Antonio Pareja, Gabino Gaínza, and Mariano Osorio, and the war divided Chileans and their communities. A truce, the Treaty of Lircay in 1814, failed to hold. On 1–2 October 1814, Osorio defeated O'Higgins at Rancagua, after Carrera's forces failed to come to his aid, a failure that deepened the rivalry between the two leaders. Patriot survivors, perhaps several thousand with their families, fled across the Andes to Mendoza.

The royalist restoration that followed, usually called the Reconquista, lasted until early 1817. Governor Casimiro Marcó del Pont imposed repression, confiscations, and surveillance; some patriot leaders were exiled to the Juan Fernández Islands, and a special tribunal prosecuted suspected rebels. This harshness alienated many who had been neutral. Guerrilla bands, including those associated with Manuel Rodríguez, carried out raids and spread information, and rural communities supplied the patriots with intelligence. Mapuche communities south of the Biobío followed no single policy: some leaders, remembering past conflicts and relations with Spanish officials, were suspicious of the patriots, and others made their own deals. The Chilean exiles in Cuyo thus brought military experience, political grievances, and bitter factional memories.

Section 54.7

San Martín in Cuyo and the Development of the Continental Plan

As governor of Cuyo, San Martín combined civil administration with military preparation. He maintained order, imposed new taxes and forced contributions, regulated trade, and promoted local production. The region's towns, Mendoza, San Juan, and San Luis, had small populations compared with Buenos Aires or Lima, so the scale of mobilization placed a heavy burden on them. He cultivated local notables, who supplied resources, and he used his authority to build an administrative base for the army.

His strategic concept, formed in 1814 and refined over the following two years, rested on three ideas: that the royalist stronghold in Lima was the key to the war; that the northern road through Upper Peru was blocked; and that an army could cross the Andes to Chile, liberate it, and then use the Pacific for a seaborne attack on Peru. In letters to associates, he outlined this plan in terms that historians have paraphrased in different ways, and the surviving texts should be read with attention to their audience. The plan also required naval power, which would be assembled later. Its originality lies in joining the western and Pacific theaters into one design, though other leaders had speculated about similar routes.

Intelligence and deception were part of the preparation. San Martín spread information, including false rumors, to mislead royalist officials about the route of the coming expedition, and he held a parlamento with Pehuenche leaders at Fortín San Carlos in 1816, partly to secure goodwill and partly to encourage the idea that the army would cross by a southern pass. Historians debate how far the Pehuenche understood or accepted this role, and the account of the meeting comes largely from patriot sources. It nonetheless shows that Indigenous diplomacy on the frontier was part of the campaign. Relations with Chilean exiles were a second challenge: O'Higgins joined San Martín, while Carrera and his supporters opposed the plan's leadership, producing quarrels that would continue after the crossing.

Section 54.8

The Congress of Tucumán, Independence, and Political Support for the Campaign

The government in Buenos Aires needed political legitimacy and unity. After the fall of Napoleon in 1814 and Ferdinand VII's return to the throne in 1814, the revolutionary cause faced the prospect of Spanish reconquest. A congress of deputies from many provinces opened in the city of Tucumán on 24 March 1816. Deputies from Upper Peru, Cuyo, Córdoba, Salta, and elsewhere attended, but the provinces of the Littoral under Artigas's influence did not, and the Banda Oriental was absent. The congress was therefore a gathering of part of the former viceroyalty, not of all of it.

The congress elected Juan Martín de Pueyrredón Supreme Director in May 1816, and on 9 July 1816 it declared independence. A phrase adding independence from "any other foreign domination" was approved on 19 July. The declaration was printed in Spanish, and also in Quechua and Aymara, an indication that deputies hoped to reach audiences in the Andean north. Deputies also discussed forms of government, including proposals for a constitutional monarchy headed by an Inca descendant, an idea that reflected both the Andean base of the revolution and its elite uncertainty about republican government. No such monarchy was established.

Primary Source Window

The Act of Independence of Tucumán, 1816

Creators: Deputies of the Congress of Tucumán · Date: 9 July 1816, with an addition of 19 July · Type: Official declaration of sovereignty

Context: Issued by deputies of provinces of the former viceroyalty meeting under pressure from royalist armies in the north and the restored Spanish monarchy.

Paraphrase: The deputies, in the name of the provinces they represent, declare their will to break the bonds that tied them to the kings of Spain and to take their place among the nations of the world as independent. The July addition extends this to independence from any other foreign power.

Analysis: The text is a legal assertion of sovereignty, not a description of a functioning state. It reflects the views of deputies who were elite men, and it does not record the opinions of most Indigenous, enslaved, or rural people, though its translation into Quechua and Aymara shows awareness of them. It was also incomplete in its representation: the Littoral provinces and the Banda Oriental did not take part, and no constitution accompanied it. It provided political cover for the military expedition.

Pueyrredón's role was practical. He met San Martín in Córdoba in July 1816, after the declaration, and promised support. As Director, he provided funds, arms, and transport and managed relations with the provinces, which were increasingly resentful of Buenos Aires's demands. The expedition therefore depended on a political decision made in the interior and on the cooperation of a central government that was itself under pressure from federalist rivals.

Section 54.9

Building the Army of the Andes: Recruitment, Labor, Finance, and Supplies

The Army of the Andes was formed in Mendoza between 1815 and 1816 and was based at a camp known as El Plumerillo. Estimates of its size vary, but most sources place the combatants at roughly 4,000 to 5,000, supported by several thousand muleteers, laborers, and auxiliaries. It included infantry, cavalry (including the Mounted Grenadiers), and artillery, and it was organized in units with trained officers. The force came from Cuyo, from the Río de la Plata, and from Chilean exiles who formed their own units under O'Higgins's and others' command.

Recruitment drew heavily on people at the bottom of colonial society. The government purchased or requisitioned enslaved men, promising freedom in exchange for service, and enlisted free people of African descent, who served in the infantry in large numbers. Estimates often put Afro-descended soldiers at a third or more of the infantry, though the figures are uncertain and recruitment records are incomplete. Many were enslaved when they entered, and their owners' interests were weighed against the army's needs. Indigenous and mestizo men also served, some willingly and some under compulsion.

Production and finance were as important as recruitment. A workshop at El Plumerillo, associated with the friar Luis Beltrán, produced and repaired cannon, gunpowder, bayonets, and horseshoes. Cuyo's towns supplied cattle, grain, wine, mules, and clothing, while the government raised funds through special taxes, forced loans, and donations, and received subsidies from Buenos Aires. Women of Mendoza sewed uniforms and a flag and donated jewelry, according to patriot accounts, and women also ran households while men served. Chilean exiles supplied knowledge of terrain and politics.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Muleteers, and the Cost of an Army

Preparing the army fell on ordinary households. Families surrendered sons, animals, and grain, paid extra taxes, and sewed and mended clothing. Muleteers (arrieros) who knew the mountain trails were essential, as were the owners of the thousands of mules and horses that would cross the Andes. Enslaved men who joined the army gained a path to freedom but left families behind and faced the hazards of war, while owners received compensation or lost labor. These burdens are recorded unevenly, because commanders and officials wrote about numbers and supplies, not about the lives of those who paid.

Section 54.10

Crossing the Andes: Strategy, Geography, and Military Logistics

In mid-January 1817, the army began its march from Mendoza and San Juan. San Martín divided it into columns that used several different passes across the cordillera. The principal columns took the two main routes, through Los Patos and Uspallata, while smaller detachments used passes to the north and south to confuse royalist defenders and to seize passes on the Chilean side. The columns were timed to converge in the central valley, and communication among them depended on couriers.

The terrain imposed severe constraints. The passes rose above 3,000 meters, with the highest approaching 4,000, and the route crossed rivers, narrow trails, and high desert. The army carried cannon, ammunition, food, and fodder on mules, and many animals died from cold, altitude, and exhaustion. Soldiers suffered from altitude sickness and exposure, and the army depended on the expertise of local guides and muleteers. Estimates of losses of men and animals vary and should be treated cautiously. The crossing took roughly two to three weeks, and royalist defenders in Chile, under Marcó del Pont and his officers, had divided their forces to guard multiple possible routes. They were thus unable to concentrate against the invaders when the columns emerged.

Section 54.11

Chacabuco and the Recovery of Santiago

When the columns emerged from the mountains in early February 1817, royalist defenders had been divided among several possible routes and could not stop them. Marcó del Pont's commanders gathered forces north of Santiago and chose to make a stand on the heights of Chacabuco, in the pass that led to the capital. The royalist force, under Rafael Maroto, numbered roughly 1,500 to 2,000 men, according to commonly cited estimates; the patriot army that confronted them was larger, though precise figures vary by source and by the political aims of those who wrote them.

On 12 February 1817, patriot columns attacked from different directions. The plan called for converging movements, but coordination was imperfect, and the battle was decided when O'Higgins's infantry charged without waiting for the full plan to unfold. The royalist line broke, and the cavalry pursued the fleeing troops. Casualties among the royalists were heavy, and many were taken prisoner. The patriot army, which had suffered losses in the crossing, now held the road to Santiago. Marcó del Pont fled toward the coast and was captured while trying to escape by sea.

Santiago was entered on 14 February. Much of the royalist administration had left, and the cabildo, composed of local notables, met to choose a new leader. They offered the post of Supreme Director to San Martín, who declined, and chose O'Higgins, who took office on 16 February 1817. San Martín's refusal has often been read as a sign of his unwillingness to rule Chile, but it also fit a strategy that required him to keep command of the army and to preserve Chilean legitimacy. Historians note that his decision also avoided a direct conflict with Chilean factions who disliked rule by an Argentine officer. The sources for these events are dispatches, cabildo records, and later memoirs, many written by participants who had reason to emphasize their own roles.

The victory brought immediate political consequences. Prisoners were held, royalist officials were arrested, and property was seized or reviewed. Many Chileans who had been neutral or reluctant now shifted toward the patriots, partly from resentment of the Reconquista's repression and partly from calculation. But Chacabuco was a battle for the capital and the central valley. It did not end the war, and royalist forces remained in the south and in Peru.

Section 54.12

O'Higgins, the New Patriot Government, and Political Rivalries

Bernardo O'Higgins governed Chile as Supreme Director from 1817 until 1823. He was a military officer and a landowner's son, who had fought at Rancagua and crossed the Andes in exile, and he took office in a state that had to be rebuilt after the Reconquista. His government faced the demands of the war, the needs of the army, the cost of the planned expedition to Peru, and the task of creating institutions in a society divided by class, region, and family.

The government abolished hereditary titles of nobility, regulated entailed estates (mayorazgos), and promoted education and public works, including the revival of the Instituto Nacional and the creation of new schools. It also built a new army and navy, which required heavy taxes, forced loans, and the use of confiscated royalist property. These policies met resistance from landowners and merchants, who were asked to pay for a war whose gains they could not be sure of, and from parts of the Church, which was wary of reforms. O'Higgins's authoritarian style, his reliance on the army, and his links with the secret political networks associated with San Martín produced criticism that would grow in later years.

Rivalries among Chilean patriots were especially bitter. The Carrera family had led Chile's early revolution, and its supporters saw O'Higgins and San Martín as outsiders who had displaced them. José Miguel Carrera, who had gone into exile in the Río de la Plata, continued to oppose them, and his brothers Juan José and Luis, arrested in Mendoza, were executed in April 1818. José Miguel Carrera himself, after leading a rebellion in the Río de la Plata provinces, was captured and executed in Mendoza in 1821. Manuel Rodríguez, the guerrilla leader popular with many Chileans and distrusted by the new government, was killed in May 1818, and responsibility for his death remains debated. These cases show that the patriot movement was not a unified bloc, and that the consolidation of power involved violence among former allies.

Relations between San Martín and O'Higgins were close, though they were not simple. San Martín retained command of the army and kept political influence, while O'Higgins controlled the state. Their cooperation depended on a shared aim, the expedition to Peru, but it also depended on the willingness of Chilean elites to pay for it, a condition that was never fully secure. Evidence for these relationships comes from correspondence and memoirs, and much of it was shaped later by the political interests of Chileans and Argentines who wanted to claim or distance themselves from the liberators.

Section 54.13

Royalist Resistance, Talcahuano, and the Struggle for Southern Chile

South of the capital, the royalist cause remained strong. The region around Concepción, the port of Talcahuano, and the fortified town of Valdivia, as well as the island of Chiloé, remained in royalist hands or open to them. Royalist officers could draw on local support, supplies from Peru by sea, and alliances with some Mapuche and Pehuenche communities, whose leaders had their own histories of negotiation with Spanish officials and their own reasons to distrust patriot governments. The south was therefore not simply a territory waiting to be liberated but a region with its own political alignments.

In late 1817, O'Higgins led patriot forces to besiege the royalist garrison at Talcahuano, a fortified position on a peninsula that was difficult to attack. The assault failed in December 1817, and O'Higgins withdrew, losing men and prestige. The setback came as a new royalist expedition from Peru, under Mariano Osorio, prepared to land. It arrived in January 1818 with several thousand troops, and the royalists resumed the offensive. The patriot government, newly created and short of resources, faced the prospect of losing the gains made at Chacabuco.

The conflict in the south also took the form of guerrilla war. Royalist bands, some led by local caudillos such as Vicente Benavides, fought a harsh irregular war in the following years, sometimes called the "war to the death" (guerra a muerte), which targeted civilians and supporters of the enemy and which lasted into the early 1820s. Such violence was fed by local rivalries, by banditry, and by the interests of Indigenous communities, and it undermined the patriots' claim to have pacified the country. The distinction between the defeat of an army and the control of a territory, which is a central theme of this chapter, is clear here.

Section 54.14

The Declaration of Chilean Independence and the Crisis at Cancha Rayada

The Chilean government formally proclaimed independence on 12 February 1818, the first anniversary of Chacabuco. Chile had been governed since 1810 by juntas acting in the name of the king, and by patriot governments that had not formally broken with him. O'Higgins judged that a declaration would strengthen the cause politically and diplomatically, giving the patriots a clearer claim to sovereignty and a stronger basis for seeking support abroad. The act was signed and promulgated in a ceremony that combined civil and military symbolism.

Section 54.15

Maipú and the Consolidation—and Limits—of Patriot Victory

The patriots reorganized within days. Units that had escaped regrouped, reinforcements arrived, and the army took up positions south of Santiago. On 5 April 1818, on the plain of Maipú, it met Osorio's army in a battle fought by forces of several thousand on each side. The patriot artillery and a series of infantry and cavalry actions decided the battle, and the royalist army broke. Osorio escaped with a small party, and many of his soldiers were killed or captured. The victory was won by a force that included Chilean units under O'Higgins's command and units of the Army of the Andes under San Martín, and it is conventionally remembered as a joint achievement.

The meeting of San Martín and O'Higgins after the battle, known as the "embrace of Maipú," has become a symbol of cooperation between the two peoples. Historians treat the scene as part of commemorative memory, and its details come from later accounts. What is clear is that the victory removed the main royalist army from central Chile, secured the capital, and made it impossible for Peru's viceroy to reconquer the country by a similar expedition. It also strengthened the standing of O'Higgins and San Martín, and confirmed the declaration of independence made two months earlier.

The limits of the victory were equally important. Royalist forces remained in the south, where guerrilla war and the stronghold of Chiloé persisted, and Valdivia was taken only in 1820 by a naval force. Chiloé remained in royalist hands until 1826. Chile's finances were exhausted, its army needed to be maintained, and its leaders were divided. Most important, the royalist base in Peru was untouched, and the viceroy could send new forces by sea. Independence could not be secured while Spain controlled the Pacific coast, and this recognition led to the naval strategy described in Division IV. The distinction between a military victory, effective control of territory, and the construction of a stable state is the central theme of the next stage of the campaign.

Section 54.16

The Chilean Navy, Cochrane, and the Pacific Strategy

Maipú secured central Chile, but Spain still controlled the sea. As long as royalist ships could move troops from Callao to the Chilean coast, any patriot gain was reversible, and an attack on Peru required ships to carry an army. The government of O'Higgins therefore set out to create a navy where none existed. Chile bought and chartered merchant vessels, obtained warships abroad, recruited sailors from Chile and from the foreign merchant communities of Valparaíso, and fitted out a squadron in 1818. In October of that year, the squadron under Manuel Blanco Encalada captured the Spanish frigate María Isabel near Talcahuano, an act that gave the young navy a flagship and an early victory.

Thomas Cochrane, a Scottish officer who had served with distinction in the British navy before his dismissal in 1814 following a conviction connected to a stock-exchange fraud, arrived in Valparaíso in November 1818 and took command of the Chilean squadron. His record of daring operations made him an effective commander, and his leadership produced notable actions, including the capture of the fortress of Valdivia in February 1820 and the seizure of the frigate Esmeralda in the harbor of Callao in November 1820. His relations with Chilean and Argentine leaders were difficult. He disputed pay and prize money, the command of the expedition, and the authority of San Martín, and his quarrels grew sharper as the campaign continued. Foreign officers and sailors in the squadron made it a multinational force, whose motives combined belief in the cause with pay and prize-taking.

Financing was a major obstacle. Chile bore most of the expense of the navy and the expedition through taxes, forced loans, confiscations, and donations, and its elites resented the burden. The political situation in the Río de la Plata made the problem worse. In 1819 the Buenos Aires government asked San Martín to return with the Army of the Andes to fight provincial opponents in a civil war, and after the government's collapse early in 1820, the authority that had supported the expedition disappeared. San Martín refused to abandon the campaign. In April 1820, at Rancagua, his officers expressed their confidence in his leadership, which in effect made the expedition an enterprise of the Chilean and Argentine officers in the field and of the Chilean state, and no longer an operation of the Argentine government. The capacity to project power across the sea was thus a Chilean achievement as much as an Argentine one.

Section 54.17

Peru as the Great Royalist Stronghold

Peru differed from the Río de la Plata and Chile. It was the old center of Spanish power in South America, with a viceregal court in Lima, a wealthy merchant community linked to the silver of Cerro de Pasco and Upper Peru, and a population in which Indigenous people were the majority of the highlands, while Lima had a large Afro-descended population, both enslaved and free. The viceroyalty had provided troops and money for royalist campaigns in Upper Peru and Chile, and Viceroy José Fernando de Abascal, in office until 1816, had built an effective military and political machine. Joaquín de la Pezuela succeeded him, and in January 1821, after the royalist officers' coup at Aznapuquio, José de la Serna became viceroy.

Lima's elites were cautious. Many creole merchants and landowners disliked Spanish commercial restrictions but feared the social consequences of revolution, particularly the potential for rebellion among Indigenous and enslaved people. Memories of the great rebellion of Túpac Amaru II (Chapter 49) and of the Cuzco uprising of 1814–1815, which had been led by the Indigenous noble Mateo Pumacahua and crushed by royalist forces, helped sustain this fear. Spain's own liberal revolution of 1820, which restored the Constitution of Cádiz, changed the context: it brought new liberties, such as freedom of the press, and divided royalists between constitutionalists and absolutists.

Indigenous communities took varied positions. Many highland communities, bearing tribute and labor demands, had no reason to favor a new elite that promised continued obligations, while others supported patriot guerrillas, and many fought in royalist armies. Estimates of the royalist forces vary, but historians agree that the army in Peru was composed largely of American-born soldiers, including many Indigenous recruits, and that this fact complicates the picture of a war between "Spaniards" and "Americans." Enslaved and free people of African descent also took part in military and political life, and the promise of freedom could draw them to either side. The strength of royalism in Peru was therefore rooted in social structure, in the army, and in elite fears.

Section 54.18

The Liberation Expedition of 1820 and the Campaigns from Pisco

The Liberating Expedition of Peru sailed from Valparaíso on 20 August 1820 with commonly cited figures of about 4,500 troops, carried on transports and escorted by the Chilean squadron. It was composed of Argentine and Chilean soldiers, including many Afro-descended infantrymen, under San Martín's command. The fleet landed at Paracas, near Pisco, in early September 1820. The aim was not an immediate assault on Lima, which was defended by a large royalist army, but a combination of pressure, negotiation, and political agitation.

Section 54.19

Negotiation, Internal Resistance, and the Royalist Withdrawal from Lima

The months at Huaura combined diplomacy with pressure. San Martín spread proclamations, corresponded with Peruvian notables, and encouraged desertion, while Cochrane's squadron blockaded Callao and raided the coast. Patriot sympathizers in Lima organized networks, and rural guerrilla bands, often called montoneras, harassed royalist communications in the sierra. The campaign also had costs. The blockade and the movement of troops disrupted trade and food supplies, and disease spread in the occupied coastal areas.

The royalist command was divided. In January 1821, officers at Aznapuquio, north of Lima, forced Pezuela to resign and named La Serna viceroy. The new viceroy opened a second round of negotiations at Punchauca in May and June 1821, and an armistice was declared, but no settlement was reached. Proposals discussed in these talks, including the idea of a constitutional monarchy under a Spanish prince, show that San Martín was willing to consider forms of government other than a republic, a theme that returns in the Protectorate. The viceroy concluded that Lima could not be held under blockade and with a hostile population, and he judged that the army would be more secure in the highlands, where supplies and manpower were reliable.

On 6 July 1821, La Serna withdrew royalist troops from Lima, moving toward the sierra and eventually establishing a government at Cuzco. The garrison of the fortress at Callao remained under royalist command for a time, and it surrendered in September 1821. The withdrawal was a strategic choice, not a defeat in battle, and it underlines the point of this division: the capture of a capital did not equal the destruction of the army that had held it. The royalist forces preserved their strength and their bases, and the war would continue.

Section 54.20

The Occupation of Lima and the Proclamation of Independence, July 1821

San Martín entered Lima on 12 July 1821. The city's leading residents, who had remained, took part in a series of public acts: on 15 July, an assembly of notables signed an act of independence, and on 28 July, in the Plaza Mayor, San Martín proclaimed Peru's independence in a public ceremony. The ceremonies served to demonstrate legitimacy before the city's population and before the outside world, and they placed Lima's elites in a position of having committed themselves publicly to the new order. The public support they displayed was real for some and prudent for others.

Section 54.21

San Martín's Protectorate: Government, Reform, and Constitutional Debate

On 3 August 1821, days after the proclamation, San Martín assumed supreme civil and military authority in Peru with the title of Protector. He justified the concentration of power as temporary, a necessity of war in a country whose institutions had to be created and whose territory was not secure. The Protectorate was staffed by a small group of ministers, among them Juan García del Río, Hipólito Unanue, a Peruvian physician and scholar who directed finance, and Bernardo Monteagudo, a lawyer born in Tucumán and educated in Upper Peru, who oversaw war and the navy and became the regime's most powerful and unpopular figure.

Historical Vocabulary
Protectorado

The Protectorado was San Martín's provisional government of Peru, created in August 1821. The title "Protector" signaled a temporary, guiding authority that claimed to hold power until Peruvians could organize their own government. The Protector held executive, military, and in practice many legislative powers.

The word should not be confused with the later use of "protectorate" for a state under foreign control. Its meaning here is closer to a wartime regency, but critics, including many Peruvian elites, saw it as rule by foreign officers.

The government issued reforms that reflected both liberal ideals and political calculation. In August 1821, it declared that children born to enslaved mothers after the proclamation of independence would be free, and it prohibited the slave trade. These measures did not end slavery, and many enslaved people remained in bondage for decades, in part because the government needed the support of slaveholding elites. It also abolished the Indigenous tribute and the forced labor service known as the mita, and declared that Indigenous people should be called Peruvians. Implementation was uneven, and the loss of tribute revenue worsened the treasury's problems. Press freedom was proclaimed, and a new honorific order, the Order of the Sun, was created to reward service. The government also confiscated property of royalists and expelled or penalized Spaniards, which brought it support from some patriots and hostility from many elites, since merchants and landowners had family and commercial ties with Spaniards.

The question of the form of government was unresolved. San Martín was skeptical that a republic could survive in a society as hierarchical and divided as Peru's, and he and some of his advisers favored a constitutional monarchy under a European prince. He sent envoys to Europe in 1821 to explore the possibility. The idea was debated in the Patriotic Society of Lima in 1822, where supporters of monarchy and republic argued publicly, and republican views gained strength. The sources for these debates are newspapers, pamphlets, and official papers, which reflect the views of an educated minority.

Section 54.22

The Unfinished War: Royalist Power, Social Conflict, and Financial Constraints

Outside Lima, the royalists remained strong. La Serna held Cuzco, the old Inca capital, and General José de Canterac commanded the army in the central highlands around Jauja. The royalist forces, drawn largely from American-born soldiers, were seasoned and could be supplied from the interior and from Upper Peru. In September 1821, Canterac marched down toward the coast to relieve the garrison of Callao and then withdrew without fighting a major battle, a sign of the cautious strategy of both sides and of San Martín's reluctance to risk his army in battle. In 1822, a patriot division was defeated at Ica, and the war in the south and in the highlands continued without a decisive result.

The Protectorate's finances were precarious. Customs revenue was disrupted by the war, the mines had been damaged, tribute income had been abolished, and the government relied on forced loans, confiscations, and the issuing of paper money. The army and navy were expensive, and disputes over pay contributed to Cochrane's break with San Martín and his departure from Peru in 1822 after seizing funds for his squadron. The troops themselves suffered from disease and desertion, and reinforcements from Chile and the Río de la Plata were insufficient.

Social conflict made governance harder. Lima's elites resented confiscations, forced loans, and the harsh measures against Spaniards, and many hedged their loyalties. Indigenous communities in the interior experienced the war chiefly as requisition and recruitment by both armies, and the abolition of tribute did not by itself alter their position. Enslaved people used the war to seek freedom, by enlisting, fleeing, or negotiating, but the government's measures offered limited relief. The reasons why liberation in Chile was followed by continuing war in Peru, summarized below, lie in these structural conditions.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Liberation in Chile Was Followed by Continuing War in Peru

Long-term causes

Peru's role as the center of Spanish power, the strength of its elites' fear of social upheaval, and its links to the military and economic resources of Upper Peru.

Short-term causes

The small size of the expedition, shortages of money, San Martín's preference for negotiation and defections, and the collapse of support from Buenos Aires.

Event or transformation

The occupation of Lima and proclamation of independence in 1821 while the royalist army withdrew intact to the highlands.

Immediate effects

Royalists retained the sierra and Upper Peru; the Protectorate faced fiscal crisis, social tension, and unresolved military threats.

Long-term consequences

The need for Colombian forces and Bolívar's leadership, and the further wars treated in Chapter 55.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the outcome. Geography, elite politics, finance, and the royalist army's strength all contributed.
Section 54.23

The Guayaquil Meeting: San Martín, Bolívar, and Competing Visions

By 1822, the northern and southern campaigns were converging. Bolívar's forces, with Antonio José de Sucre, had won the battle of Pichincha in May 1822 and taken Quito (see Chapter 53). The port of Guayaquil, which had declared independence in 1820, was claimed by Colombia and by Peru, and was effectively placed under Bolívar's authority in July. San Martín, who needed reinforcements and wanted to settle the political question, traveled there by sea, and the two leaders met on 26–27 July 1822.

What was said is uncertain. The conversations were private, and no minutes were kept. Historians rely on letters written by both men and their associates at the time and afterward, on the testimony of aides who were not in the room, and on later recollections. Most accounts agree that the discussion covered military aid for Peru, the status of Guayaquil, and the form of government Peru should adopt, and that San Martín was disappointed by the help offered. A letter from San Martín to Bolívar in August 1822, written after his return, shows that he had decided to withdraw, but the reasons he gave and the reasons later attributed to him differ.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was San Martín's Continental Strategy Decisive?

The question: Did San Martín's strategy of crossing the Andes and using sea power produce independence in the south, or did success depend mainly on wider coalitions and changing conditions?

Interpretation A — A decisive strategy: Some historians stress that the plan combined the Andean and Pacific theaters in a way earlier leaders had not, and that its execution turned scattered regional struggles into a coordinated campaign. Without it, they argue, Chile would not have been liberated so quickly and Lima would not have been reached.
Interpretation B — A contingent success: Others emphasize that the plan depended on Cuyo's resources, Chilean funding and naval construction, the weakness of royalist forces distracted by events in Spain, local uprisings, and the contributions of many leaders. On this view, no single plan was decisive, and the eventual victory in Peru required Bolívar's forces.

Evidence: Correspondence, military records, memoirs, and official papers, many written or published by participants and nationalist historians who wished to credit or diminish particular leaders. Why it matters: The question affects whether independence is understood as the achievement of individual leaders or of collective, multinational effort. Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Southern Campaigns

Note: these are reconstructed analytical perspectives, not quotations, and the groups did not have equal power.

Río de la Plata revolutionaries: Saw the expedition as defending their revolution, though the provinces bore its costs and resented Buenos Aires's demands.
Chilean patriots: Welcomed liberation but were divided between supporters of O'Higgins and of the Carreras, and paid heavily for the army and navy.
Peruvian patriots and royalists: Patriots hoped for a republic or a monarchy of their own design; royalists, including many Peruvian-born soldiers, defended the crown and feared social upheaval.
Indigenous communities: Experienced requisition and recruitment by both sides, and gained the abolition of tribute on paper, with uncertain effect.
Afro-descended soldiers and enslaved people: Gained freedom by service in many cases, but bore heavy casualties, and saw slavery continue despite limited reforms.
Section 54.24

San Martín's Resignation and the Legacy of the Southern Campaigns

San Martín returned to Lima to find that opposition to Monteagudo had grown into a popular movement that forced his removal in July 1822. The Protector was weakened politically and facing a stalled war. When the first Peruvian Congress was installed on 20 September 1822, he resigned the Protectorate and handed power to the new body. He left Peru soon afterward, returning to Chile and Mendoza, and in 1824 he went to Europe, where he lived in exile and died in France in 1850. His departure was the end of his role in the campaigns, but not of the war.

The southern campaigns achieved significant results. They liberated Chile, created the first Chilean navy, took Lima, and established a political framework for Peruvian independence. They also demonstrated that independence required more than battlefield victories: the proclamation of sovereignty, the control of territory, political legitimacy, and the creation of stable states were distinct achievements, accomplished at different times and in different ways. The campaigns' limitations were equally clear. The royalist army in the Peruvian highlands remained undefeated, the new governments were poor and divided, and Chile and Peru's elites disagreed about the costs and goals of the war. These unresolved problems led to the arrival of Bolívar's forces, and the final wars of independence in Peru, which are the subject of Chapter 55.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Colonial Hierarchy to Independent Governments

What Changed

  • Royal authority was replaced in Chile and coastal Peru by governments claiming independent sovereignty
  • Indigenous tribute and the mita were abolished by law, and children of enslaved mothers were declared free
  • New armies and a navy, built by American states, replaced reliance on the Spanish crown's forces

What Stayed the Same

  • Slavery continued, and landowning elites kept their economic power
  • Indigenous communities remained under pressure for labor, goods, and recruits
  • Regional rivalries and fiscal weakness persisted

Who experienced change most directly: soldiers, freed people, and urban elites in the liberated cities. Greater continuity: enslaved people not covered by the free-womb decrees and Indigenous villagers of the interior. Why it matters: It shows that political independence did not automatically transform social structures.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that San Martín’s southern strategy transformed a regional revolution into a coordinated campaign, but that military victories, territorial control, political legitimacy, and the construction of states were different achievements that occurred at different times.

The Río de la Plata revolution began in 1810 as a struggle over sovereignty. Buenos Aires claimed to inherit the viceroy’s authority, but Paraguay, the Banda Oriental, and other regions pursued their own paths, and later demands for troops and money depended on provincial consent.

On the northern front, repeated expeditions into Upper Peru failed. Güemes’s militias and the republiquetas held royalist forces in place, but the road to Lima through Upper Peru stayed closed. San Martín concluded that a different route was needed.

Chile’s early revolution ended at Rancagua in 1814, and the royalist Reconquista produced repression that alienated many Chileans. In Cuyo, San Martín used the region’s resources, Pueyrredón’s support, the Congress of Tucumán’s declaration of 1816, and Indigenous diplomacy to assemble the Army of the Andes.

The army drew heavily on Afro-descended soldiers and on civilians who gave animals, money, labor, and sons. Its crossing in 1817, by several passes, was a logistical achievement that depended on guides, deception, and royalist miscalculation.

Chacabuco recovered Santiago, and O’Higgins became Supreme Director. Chilean independence was proclaimed in 1818, followed by the crisis at Cancha Rayada and the decisive victory at Maipú. Yet royalist resistance continued in the south, where guerrilla war and the stronghold of Chiloé persisted.

Control of the sea became essential. Chile created a navy, and Cochrane’s leadership brought notable successes, though disputes over pay and command strained the alliance. The collapse of the Buenos Aires government left Chile carrying most of the cost.

Peru was the stronghold of royalism, with elites that feared upheaval and a royalist army composed largely of Americans. San Martín’s expedition of 1820 relied on defections, blockade, and negotiation rather than battle. The royalist army withdrew from Lima intact, and independence was proclaimed on 28 July 1821.

The Protectorate abolished tribute and the mita and declared the free womb, but slavery continued, finances were precarious, and elites resented confiscations. The war in the highlands remained unresolved.

San Martín and Bolívar met at Guayaquil in July 1822, and the evidence for their conversation is limited and contested. San Martín resigned in September 1822. His departure prepared the way for Bolívar’s forces and the final wars treated in Chapter 55.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that independence was a sequence of achievements: declaration, victory, recognition, and state-building. San Martín’s plan depended on communities in Cuyo, Chile, and Peru whose interests shaped the outcome, and its success at Chacabuco and Maipú coexisted with unresolved wars in the south of Chile and in the Peruvian highlands.

The campaigns also reveal how much of the revolution was fought by people whose names seldom survive. Muleteers, enslaved men who enlisted, women who sewed and donated, and Indigenous communities who negotiated or were requisitioned all appear in the sources unevenly, and the silences are themselves evidence of whose lives the surviving documents recorded.

The limits of the Protectorate’s reforms foreshadow problems of republican state formation treated in Chapters 58 and 62: fiscal weakness, regional rivalry, and the persistence of slavery and Indigenous obligations under new constitutions.

Chapter 55 takes up the consequences. Bolívar’s forces and Sucre’s army would enter Peru, and the final battles at Junín and Ayacucho, and the creation of Bolivia, would complete what the southern campaigns had begun.
