Portuguese Brazil, the Río de la Plata, and South American Frontiers

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Conquest without a Capital

In Mexico and the Andes, invaders could seize a ruler and claim an empire. In eastern and southern South America there was no such prize. Europeans settled on coasts and riverbanks, but the interior remained in the hands of Indigenous communities who negotiated, resisted, and sometimes defeated them.

Chapters 31 through 38 followed Iberian invaders into states with capitals, tribute systems, and rulers who could be captured, killed, or manipulated. This chapter turns to a different part of the continent. Along the Atlantic coast of what is now Brazil, and along the great river system that drains into the Río de la Plata, the Indigenous peoples encountered by Portuguese and Spanish newcomers were organized chiefly in village-based societies without a single imperial center. There was no Tenochtitlan to besiege and no Cajamarca at which a sovereign could be seized. Conquest therefore proceeded, when it proceeded at all, community by community, alliance by alliance, and river by river. It took generations, and it was never completed.

The chapter has two principal narratives that gradually converge. The first follows the Portuguese from the brazilwood trade of the early sixteenth century to the hereditary captaincies, the sugar coast, the wars against the French and the Dutch, and the movement of Luso-Brazilian expeditions into the interior from São Paulo, the São Francisco valley, Maranhão, and the Amazon. The second follows the Spaniards from the failed first settlement at Buenos Aires in 1536 to the founding of Asunción, the creation of a river-based colony in Guaraní country, the permanent refounding of Buenos Aires in 1580, and the establishment of the Jesuit mission towns known as reductions. The two narratives meet in the borderlands of Guairá and the Uruguay River in the 1620s through 1640s, where Portuguese-speaking raiders and Guaraní communities collided, and again at Colônia do Sacramento, founded by the Portuguese in 1680 on the north bank of the Río de la Plata opposite Buenos Aires.

Three arguments run through the chapter. First, Indigenous political decisions shaped every stage of European expansion. Tupiniquim chiefs allied with Portuguese settlers when it served their interests, and Tupinambá groups allied with the French when it served theirs. Guaraní leaders entered into kinship and labor arrangements with Spaniards in Asunción, and their descendants later chose, in very different circumstances, to live in mission towns, to flee them, or to fight in them. Second, the boundaries of colonial claims and the boundaries of colonial control were not the same. The Treaty of Tordesillas, discussed in Chapter 28, drew a line on paper; neither crown could enforce it in the forests, grasslands, and rivers that mattered. Third, Brazil and the Río de la Plata were not separate stories. Their economies, labor markets, religious orders, merchants, and military forces interacted constantly, and the Iberian Union of 1580 to 1640, which placed the Portuguese and Spanish crowns under one monarch, made that interaction easier even as it blurred the legal line between the two empires.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. The Portuguese and Spanish sources are numerous but partisan. Pero Vaz de Caminha’s letter of 1500, the Jesuit letters of Manuel da Nóbrega and José de Anchieta, and the treatises of Pero de Magalhães Gândavo and Gabriel Soares de Sousa describe Tupi-speaking societies through the eyes of people who wished to trade with, convert, or enslave them. The German soldier Hans Staden, who was held captive by the Tupinambá in the 1550s, and the French Calvinist Jean de Léry, who lived in the short-lived French colony in Guanabara Bay, wrote vivid ethnographic accounts shaped by religious and commercial purposes. For the Río de la Plata, the chronicles of Ulrich Schmidl, a German soldier who took part in the first Spanish expeditions, and the letters and reports of Asunción’s governors and clergy supply much of the narrative. For the missions, the central source is the Jesuit Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s Conquista espiritual of 1639, a work of advocacy as well as history. Archaeology, linguistics, and the study of Indigenous-language documents increasingly supplement these texts, but almost no source preserves the voices of Tupi or Guaraní people in their own words during this period.

The chapter also tests a vocabulary. “Conquest,” “settlement,” “colonization,” “mission,” and “frontier” are not synonyms. A coastal fort could exist for decades without controlling the surrounding countryside. A mission town could shelter its inhabitants from slave raiders while also subjecting them to new religious and labor demands. A frontier could be a zone of exchange and intermarriage, a theater of war, or a market for captives, sometimes all three in the same decade. Students should watch how these terms shift in meaning as the chapter moves between regions and decades.

The chapter’s chronological boundaries run from about 1500 to 1680. It treats the Portuguese captaincies and the Spanish river settlements only as instruments of settlement and expansion; the full administrative history of Portuguese Brazil belongs to Chapter 41, the plantation economy and colonial cities to Chapter 43, Atlantic slavery and Afro-Brazilian society to Chapter 44, and the wider religious history of missions to Chapter 46. This chapter also completes Part VIII. After the sieges and campaigns of Mexico and the Andes, it shows that the conquest of South America was not a single event but a family of different processes, many of which ended not in victory but in a standoff between colonial claims and Indigenous autonomy. Part IX will turn to the institutions through which the Iberian crowns tried to govern what they claimed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the Indigenous societies of the Brazilian coast and the Paraná–Paraguay–Uruguay basin, distinguishing Tupi-speaking, Guaraní, Jê-speaking, and other communities rather than treating them as a single bloc.
  2. Explain why the brazilwood trade, the captaincy system, and sugar cultivation shaped Portuguese settlement differently from Spanish conquest in Mexico and the Andes.
  3. Analyze the role of Indigenous alliances, rivalries, and military participation in coastal warfare, including the Tamoio Confederation and the wars against the French.
  4. Assess how the demand for labor led to Indigenous enslavement and then to the growing use of enslaved Africans, without reducing either process to a single cause.
  5. Evaluate competing interpretations of the bandeirantes, distinguishing later heroic memory from documented patterns of captive-taking.
  6. Trace the failure of the first Buenos Aires settlement and the founding of Asunción, and explain why Asunción, not Buenos Aires, became the center of early Spanish colonization in the region.
  7. Interpret the Spanish–Guaraní relationship in Asunción, including kinship, women’s labor, and coercion, without romanticizing unequal power.
  8. Explain the origins and functioning of the Jesuit reductions and the reasons Guaraní communities entered, left, or fought for them.
  9. Connect the Brazilian and Río de la Plata narratives through the raids on Guairá, the exodus of missions, and the Guaraní victory at Mbororé in 1641.
  10. Analyze the effects of the Iberian Union and the founding of Colônia do Sacramento on the disputed southern frontier.
  11. Interpret Antonio Ruiz de Montoya’s Conquista espiritual as missionary testimony, identifying its purposes, strengths, and limitations.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part VIII’s argument that South American conquest was a set of prolonged, uneven processes rather than a single Spanish victory.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the absence of centralized Indigenous states change the character of European expansion in eastern and southern South America?
  2. How did Indigenous alliances and rivalries determine which colonial settlements survived and which failed?
  3. To what extent was the Portuguese captaincy system an instrument of conquest, and to what extent was it a legal claim without occupation?
  4. Why did the demand for labor produce such different arrangements in Pernambuco, São Paulo, and Asunción, and what did those arrangements have in common?
  5. How should historians interpret the bandeirantes when later regional memory and surviving documents point in different directions?
  6. Why did the Guaraní missions attract some Indigenous communities, repel others, and become targets for slave raiders?
  7. How did events in Brazil and events in the Río de la Plata affect one another before 1680?
  8. In what sense had Spain and Portugal “conquered” the regions they claimed by 1680, and what evidence supports a more limited description?

Historical Context

Chapter 24 introduced the Indigenous societies of southern and eastern South America on the eve of the Iberian arrival, including Guaraní, Charrúa, and Tupi-speaking communities, and Chapter 23 described the long Indigenous transformation of Amazonian landscapes. Chapter 28 explained how the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 divided the Atlantic world between Castile and Portugal along a line whose location on the American continent neither crown could fix with precision, and how Pedro Álvares Cabral’s landfall in 1500 gave Portugal a claim to the eastern bulge of South America. Chapter 29 described the epidemics that followed Atlantic contact. Those epidemics were part of the background to every episode in this chapter, though their timing and severity varied by region and are often poorly documented.

Chapters 35 through 38 introduced a contrast that this chapter develops. The conquests of Tenochtitlan and Cusco were campaigns against complex states, and even there, as earlier chapters showed, conquest was incomplete and prolonged. Chile and the Mapuche frontier, treated in Chapter 38, already showed that decentralized societies could resist for generations. Eastern South America presents a still broader version of that pattern. Tupi-speaking peoples of the coast, Guaraní peoples of the river basin, and many neighbors lived in autonomous communities linked by language, kinship, trade, and warfare, but not by a tribute empire. The colonial powers confronted a mosaic rather than a monarchy.

The two Iberian crowns also arrived with very different priorities. For Portugal, whose economy and fleets were oriented toward Africa and the Indian Ocean after Vasco da Gama’s voyage of 1497–1499, Brazil was initially a source of dyewood and a strategic coast to be denied to rivals. For Castile, the discovery of silver in the Andes, especially at Potosí in 1545, made Peru the center of imperial interest, and the Río de la Plata was valued first as a possible route to Andean wealth and only later as a port and cattle-raising region. Neither crown had the manpower to occupy a continent. Both relied on private entrepreneurs, religious orders, and Indigenous intermediaries, which is why this chapter spends so much time on actors who were not royal officials.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a world of European rivalry. French merchants traded with the Tupinambá from the early sixteenth century, French Huguenots tried to plant a colony in Guanabara Bay in the 1550s, and the Dutch West India Company seized parts of northeastern Brazil in the 1620s through the 1650s. Each of those rivals found Indigenous partners and each affected the coastal balance of power. Modern national borders, including those of Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, and Bolivia, did not exist in this period. The chapter uses the names of historical regions, such as the Estado do Brasil, the Estado do Maranhão, the Río de la Plata, Paraguay, and Guairá, and gives modern geographic orientation where it helps.

Section 39.1

The Atlantic Coast and the Indigenous Worlds of Eastern South America

The Portuguese entered a long, narrow, and ecologically varied coastline. From the northeastern bulge near Pernambuco southward to São Vicente and beyond, a humid coastal strip of Atlantic forest, mangroves, estuaries, and fertile soils lay between the sea and a rising escarpment. Behind that strip lay the highlands and, farther inland and to the north, the drier sertão, the Portuguese word for the backlands. To the south, the Paraná, Paraguay, and Uruguay rivers gather the waters of a vast interior and flow into the great estuary named the Río de la Plata. These waterways were the roads of the region. The Tietê, which rises near the coast but flows inland toward the Paraná, would later become an inland highway for expeditions leaving São Paulo.

Much of the coast and the river basins were occupied by speakers of languages in the Tupi-Guaraní family. Along the Atlantic shore lived Tupinambá, Tupiniquim, Potiguara, Caeté, Temiminó, and related groups, often hostile to one another and sometimes closely related through language and custom. Guaraní peoples occupied large parts of what is now Paraguay, northeastern Argentina, southern Brazil, and Uruguay’s river margins. These communities practiced slash-and-burn agriculture with manioc, maize, beans, squash, and sweet potatoes, supplemented by fishing, hunting, and gathering. Villages typically consisted of several large communal houses, and men and women divided labor: men cleared fields, hunted, and made war, while women planted, harvested, processed manioc, and prepared food and drink. Leaders gained authority through eloquence, generosity, and success in war rather than through hereditary office, and spiritual leaders, whom the sources call pajés or karaí, could attract wide followings. Estimates of the Indigenous population of the territory that is now Brazil in 1500 vary widely, from roughly one million to several million, and they should be treated as uncertain.

Warfare was a central institution among many Tupi-speaking groups. Wars were fought to avenge earlier deaths and to take captives, some of whom were held for a time and then executed in ceremonies in which the captor’s group consumed part of the body. European observers such as Hans Staden and Jean de Léry described these practices at length. Modern anthropologists generally interpret them within systems of vengeance, honor, and relations among enemy groups rather than as signs of savagery, but Europeans of the sixteenth century used the practice to justify the enslavement and conquest of the peoples they called cannibals. Peoples whom the Tupi called tapuia, many of them Jê-speaking groups of the interior, were treated as enemies by many Tupi groups, and Europeans adopted the label, which hides great linguistic and cultural diversity.

The central point for what follows is political. These societies were autonomous, and there was no emperor to defeat. A Portuguese captain who allied with a Tupiniquim village gained a local partner but not a kingdom, and an alliance with one group often meant war with its neighbors. European claims of sovereignty therefore meant little until Indigenous communities accepted them or were forced to. Across the region, the colonial story would be a long sequence of negotiations, betrayals, and wars with many separate peoples, in contrast to the campaigns that toppled the Triple Alliance and Tawantinsuyu. Chapter 38’s Mapuche frontier offers the closest comparison within Part VIII.

Section 39.2

From Portuguese Arrival to the Brazilwood Frontier, 1500–1530

Cabral’s fleet, bound for India, made landfall on the coast of present-day Bahia in April 1500. Pero Vaz de Caminha’s letter to King Manuel I describes a friendly first encounter, exchange of gifts, and curiosity on both sides, and it suggests that the land might support settlement and conversion. The letter, like all early accounts, reflects its author’s purposes. Two convicts, known as degredados, were left behind, and Cabral’s fleet sailed on. For Portugal, the main prize remained the spice trade of the Indian Ocean, and the new coast received little royal investment for three decades.

What drew Europeans to the coast was brazilwood, a tree whose red heartwood produced a valuable dye for European textiles. The wood gave the territory its name. Portuguese and French merchants, the latter particularly from Normandy, sailed to the coast to load it. They did not usually fell the trees themselves. Instead they established fortified trading posts, feitorias, and bartered with Tupi-speaking communities, a practice called escambo. Iron axes, knives, mirrors, cloth, and beads were exchanged for the labor of Indigenous men, who cut and carried the heavy logs to the shore. A surviving account book of the ship Bretoa, dated 1511, records brazilwood together with exotic animals and a small number of enslaved people, showing the trade’s early commercial range.

The trade worked because it suited both sides, at least for a time. Tupi communities gained metal tools that transformed agricultural clearing and woodworking, and leaders who controlled access to European goods increased their prestige. Europeans gained cargoes without paying the costs of settlement. Some individuals, convicts, castaways, and sailors, lived among the Tupi and acted as interpreters and intermediaries. Tupi custom incorporated outsiders by marriage: a stranger who received a wife entered a network of kin obligations. The anthropologist Darcy Ribeiro called this pattern cunhadismo, from the Tupi-derived word for brother-in-law. Later tradition preserved names such as Diogo Álvares, called Caramuru, in Bahia and João Ramalho near the plateau of Piratininga, though the details of their lives are reconstructed from late and sometimes legendary sources.

Settlement developed slowly because the trade did not require it and because Portugal’s resources were committed elsewhere. The French competed openly, and Indigenous trading partners had little reason to favor one European group over another. By the late 1520s and early 1530s the Portuguese crown, alarmed by French activity and by the possibility that the coast might be lost, decided that merely visiting it was no longer enough. Chapter 28 described the diplomatic claims; what changed in the 1530s was the decision to turn a claim into occupation.

Section 39.3

São Vicente, the Hereditary Captaincies, and the Beginnings of Settlement

In 1530 King João III sent Martim Afonso de Sousa with a fleet to patrol the coast, expel French traders, explore as far as the Río de la Plata, and begin settlement. His expedition founded São Vicente in 1532, in the region of present-day Santos, where Portuguese, Indigenous allies, and the men who already lived on the coast established a small town. It was the first permanent Portuguese settlement in Brazil. Sources associate it with early sugar cultivation, and a mill associated with the Schetz family of Antwerp, later known as the Engenho of the Governors or São Jorge dos Erasmos, operated nearby. São Vicente’s importance lay both in its early sugar and in its access, by a steep trail up the escarpment, to the plateau of Piratininga, which became the site of São Paulo.

The crown then adopted a method it had already used in the Atlantic islands. Between about 1534 and 1536, João III divided the coast into hereditary captaincies and granted them to private individuals, who were expected to settle them at their own expense in return for substantial rights.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitania and Donatário

A capitania (captaincy) was a territorial grant, typically a coastal strip extending inland, given by the Portuguese crown. The grantee, the donatário, received rights to found towns, distribute land through sesmarias, collect certain revenues, and exercise justice, while the crown retained sovereignty and certain monopolies. The grants were hereditary.

The word is easily misread. A captaincy was a legal claim and a framework of obligations, not a functioning province. Many grants were never settled, and the Indigenous peoples who lived within them were not parties to the grant. The system matters here because it shows how Portugal tried to claim a coast it could not afford to occupy directly. The administrative history of the captaincies and their later replacement by royal government belong to Chapter 41.

About fifteen lots were granted to roughly a dozen donatários. They included nobles, officials, and veterans of Asia. Results varied widely. Pernambuco, under Duarte Coelho, and São Vicente prospered, thanks to capital, settlers, sugar, and Indigenous alliances. Duarte Coelho founded Olinda and secured the support of local Indigenous leaders, notably groups of Tabajara, against others, such as the Caeté. Bahia’s donatário, Francisco Pereira Coutinho, was driven out and then killed by Tupinambá in the mid-1540s. Other captaincies, such as Ilhéus, Porto Seguro, and Espírito Santo, survived in reduced form. Grants in the north, including those assigned to the historian João de Barros and his partners, failed. The reasons were consistent: too little capital, settlers who could not hold their ground, Indigenous resistance provoked by abuses, and distance from the sea lanes.

The captaincies illustrate a distinction that recurs throughout this chapter: the difference between a territorial claim and actual occupation. On paper, the coast was parceled out. On the ground, settlements were scattered footholds whose survival depended on local Indigenous alliances and the arrival of ships. In 1549 the crown, concerned by the failures, sent Tomé de Sousa as the first governor-general and founded Salvador in Bahia, and the first Jesuits arrived with him, including Manuel da Nóbrega. These were not the end of the captaincies but a layering of royal authority on top of them, a process Chapter 41 examines.

Section 39.4

Indigenous Alliances, Coastal Warfare, and Colonial Consolidation

Portuguese settlement survived because some Indigenous groups chose to support it. In São Vicente, the Tupiniquim leader Tibiriçá, baptized with the Portuguese name Martim Afonso, allied with the settlers and with the Jesuits who founded the school at Piratininga in 1554. In 1562, according to Jesuit accounts, Tupi-speaking enemies attacked the little community at Piratininga, and Tibiriçá’s warriors helped defend it. In Pernambuco, Duarte Coelho’s allies fought groups who resisted settlement. Near Rio de Janeiro, the Temiminó leader Araribóia and his people supported the Portuguese against the French and the Tamoio, and they received land in return. These alliances were not simple loyalty. Leaders sought tools, protection against rivals, access to captives, and recognition. They also bore heavy costs.

The mirror image was the alliance between many Tupinambá groups and the French. Rouen and Norman merchants had traded with them for decades, and when a French expedition under Nicolas Durand de Villegaignon established a colony in Guanabara Bay in 1555, it found willing partners. From about the mid-1550s, Tupinambá communities along the coast between present-day Rio de Janeiro and São Vicente, whom the Portuguese called Tamoio, formed a loose military alliance, often called the Tamoio Confederation, against Portuguese settlements. Leaders such as Cunhambebe organized attacks and raids on São Vicente and Piratininga. The word “confederation” should not suggest a state. It was an alliance of communities held together by common enemies and by French support, and it lasted only as long as that support and their shared interests did.

In 1563 the Jesuits Nóbrega and Anchieta traveled to Iperoig, near present-day Ubatuba, to negotiate a truce with Tamoio leaders. Anchieta remained for months as a hostage or guest while Nóbrega returned to arrange terms, an episode that shows Indigenous diplomacy at work. The fullest accounts come from Jesuit letters and later retellings, so embellished details should be treated cautiously. The truce did not end the war. Under Governor-General Mem de Sá, the Portuguese attacked the French fort in 1560 and, with reinforcements and Temiminó allies, finally expelled the French from Guanabara Bay in 1567 after the founding of Rio de Janeiro by Mem de Sá’s nephew, Estácio de Sá, in 1565. Many Tamoio were killed, enslaved, or moved into mission villages, and others fled inland.

Epidemics accompanied the wars. Smallpox and measles struck coastal communities in the early 1560s, especially in Bahia, where Jesuit villages were hit hard, and the sources record population collapse and a surge of people entering Portuguese households as dependents or captives in the aftermath. The same decade saw Mem de Sá proclaim “just wars” against groups such as the Caeté, who had killed the first bishop of Brazil, Pedro Fernandes Sardinha, after a shipwreck in 1556. Students should avoid treating the Indigenous peoples of the coast as a bloc. They fought one another as well as Europeans, and the Portuguese success in the 1560s depended as much on Temiminó and Tupiniquim allies as on Portuguese arms.

Section 39.5

Sugar, Settlement, and the Struggle for Indigenous Labor

Sugar made the coast worth holding. The crop grew well in the rich soils of Pernambuco and Bahia, the European market was expanding, and the Portuguese had experience with sugar in Madeira and São Tomé. A sugar estate, an engenho, combined cane fields with a mill and boiling house and required land, capital, and a large labor force. Land was obtained through sesmarias, grants that Portuguese law had developed for unused land. In Brazil they were applied to territory that Indigenous communities had long used, so the appropriation of land and the pressure for labor were linked from the outset. This chapter treats sugar as a driver of conquest and settlement; Chapter 43 examines the plantation economy itself.

Barter had worked for brazilwood but not for sugar, which demanded constant, heavy, year-round labor. Colonists turned to coercion. Some Indigenous captives were obtained through resgate, a term meaning rescue or ransom that was used to describe the purchase of prisoners held by Indigenous captors who would otherwise have killed them. Others were taken in wars that colonists and authorities labeled guerra justa, just war. Still others were “brought down” from the interior through descimentos to live in villages near Portuguese settlements. The Crown issued laws, including one in 1570, that prohibited most enslavement of Indigenous people while allowing it in cases of just war, and the exceptions swallowed the rule. The Jesuits opposed the worst abuses of settlers, argued for the protection of converts, and organized Indigenous aldeias, mission villages, under their own authority. Those villages provided labor to colonists and the crown as well as spiritual instruction, which complicates any simple picture of the Jesuits as protectors.

Indigenous people did not accept these pressures passively. They fled, resisted, and sometimes created their own movements. In the 1580s, in the interior of Bahia, a movement known as the Santidade combined Indigenous and Christian elements and drew followers who left plantations and mission villages, before Portuguese forces suppressed it. Disease, flight, and violence reduced the Indigenous workforce near the sugar coast, and the planters’ demand did not decline.

Enslaved Africans therefore entered the picture. They were present in Brazil from the mid-sixteenth century and arrived in larger numbers after the Portuguese established Luanda in 1576 and expanded their presence in Angola. They were more expensive than Indigenous captives but available through a well-developed Atlantic trade and less able to escape into familiar country. The transition was gradual and uneven, and for decades Indigenous and African workers labored together on the same estates. By the early seventeenth century, Africans made up the bulk of the workforce on the large sugar estates of the northeast, while Indigenous captive labor remained important in other regions, especially São Paulo. Indigenous women and enslaved African women were also subject to sexual exploitation, and the children of such unions, many of whom were not recognized by their fathers, contributed to the formation of a mixed population. Chapter 44 examines African experience and the making of Afro-Brazilian society in depth.

Section 39.6

French and Dutch Challenges to Portuguese Brazil

The French challenge had two phases. The first was commercial, as noted above. The second was the attempt at colonization in Guanabara Bay, France Antarctique, led by Villegaignon from 1555. The colony was torn by religious disputes between Catholics and Calvinists, and the arrival of Protestant pastors, including Jean de Léry, led to conflicts that weakened it. Léry’s Histoire d’un voyage, published in 1578, became one of the major sources on the Tupinambá. A second French attempt, France Équinoxiale, established São Luís in Maranhão in 1612, and was ended in 1615 by a Portuguese force from Pernambuco, which defeated the French at Guaxenduba in late 1614 before the final surrender. In both cases the French found support among Tupinambá groups and lost it as Portuguese power, epidemics, and Indigenous divisions took their toll.

The Dutch challenge was larger. The Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621, aimed to seize the sugar-producing regions from a Portugal then ruled by the Spanish crown, with which the Dutch were at war. Dutch forces captured Salvador in May 1624, and a combined Spanish and Portuguese fleet recovered it in 1625. In 1630 the Dutch took Olinda and Recife and gradually extended their control over much of the northeast. Under the governor Johan Maurits of Nassau, who served from 1637 to 1644, Dutch Brazil developed a sugar economy with Dutch capital, the Atlantic slave trade, and a notable intellectual and artistic circle. The Dutch also seized Portuguese outposts in West Central Africa, including Luanda in 1641, and the Portuguese expedition that retook it in 1648 sailed from Rio de Janeiro, a reminder that Brazil and Angola were bound together.

Indigenous and African people fought on both sides. The Potiguara leader Pedro Poti allied with the Dutch, while another Potiguara leader, Felipe Camarão, fought for the Portuguese and was honored by the crown. Henrique Dias, a free Black man, led a militia unit on the Portuguese side. Enslaved Africans fled from plantations in the confusion of war, and the settlement of Palmares, in the highlands of the northeast, grew during this period; Chapter 47 treats it. The Portuguese defeated the Dutch at the battles of Guararapes in 1648 and 1649, and the Dutch surrendered Recife in 1654. The wars cost Portugal heavily and demonstrated that the colony had become valuable enough to defend, but also that its defenders depended on Indigenous and Afro-Brazilian soldiers as much as on Europeans.

Section 39.7

São Paulo, the Bandeirantes, and the Expansion of the Frontier

São Paulo de Piratininga was founded in 1554 by Jesuits, including Nóbrega and Anchieta, on a plateau above São Vicente, in territory of Tupiniquim and Guaianá communities. It grew slowly into a town of settlers, many of them of mixed Portuguese and Indigenous ancestry, known as mamelucos. The language of daily life in the town in the early seventeenth century was largely a Tupi-based língua geral, not Portuguese. The town lacked a large export crop such as the sugar of the northeast, and its settlers sought wealth through agriculture on small estates, trade, and above all through the labor of Indigenous captives, whom they sold to the coast and used on their own farms.

Historical Vocabulary
Bandeira, Bandeirante, and Sertão

A bandeira (literally “banner” or “flag”) was an armed expedition, usually organized privately from São Paulo, that traveled into the interior to capture Indigenous people, search for minerals, or both. A participant is a bandeirante, though the noun appears to have become common only later and contemporaries more often spoke of entradas and sertanistas. The sertão is the Portuguese word for the backlands, the vast interior beyond the coast.

The terms carry modern connotations. In twentieth-century São Paulo, “bandeirante” became a symbol of regional pride and national expansion, and that memory colors both the word and the evidence. It matters in this chapter because the bandeiras were not state armies. They were private ventures that included Indigenous participants in large numbers and that, in practice, supplied a labor market.

The bandeiras drew on Indigenous knowledge and Indigenous manpower. Most participants were Tupi-speaking auxiliaries, who knew the routes, spoke the languages, and fought in the expeditions. The Paulista leaders were often themselves of mixed ancestry. The expeditions followed rivers, especially the Tietê, and traveled for months or years. In the 1620s and 1630s, leaders such as Manuel Preto and Antônio Raposo Tavares directed large expeditions against Guaraní communities in the Spanish province of Guairá, discussed in Section 39.14, and later bandeiras reached far into the continent. Raposo Tavares’s journey between about 1648 and 1651 took him from São Paulo across the interior to the Amazon. Contemporary accounts and later estimates of the numbers of captives taken in these raids vary widely and are difficult to verify, but the practice of captive-taking was extensive and documented.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Should Historians Interpret the Bandeirantes?

The question: Were the bandeirantes pioneers who extended the territory of Brazil, or slave raiders whose expeditions were driven by the demand for Indigenous labor?

Interpretation A — Pioneers of territorial expansion: Historians in the early twentieth century, notably Afonso de Taunay in his multivolume history of the bandeiras, portrayed the Paulistas as explorers and nation-builders whose expeditions pushed Brazil beyond the Tordesillas line and laid the basis for its modern territory. This reading was influential in São Paulo’s regional identity and in public monuments.
Interpretation B — Slave raiders in a labor economy: Later scholarship, notably John Monteiro’s Negros da terra (1994), emphasized that the central activity of the bandeiras was the capture and sale of Indigenous people, and that São Paulo’s economy depended on that labor. On this view, territorial expansion was a by-product of a slave trade, and the heroic image obscures the human cost.

Evidence: Both readings use the same documents: Jesuit and Spanish complaints, municipal records, wills and inventories listing Indigenous workers, and accounts of individual expeditions. Interpretation A relies on the geographic reach of the expeditions and on later claims of sovereignty. Interpretation B relies on estate inventories and legal records showing the prevalence of Indigenous captives.

Why it matters: The question affects how Brazilian territory, regional memory, and Indigenous history are understood. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive: many scholars accept that the expeditions had territorial consequences while emphasizing that captive-taking was central. Statues and monuments to bandeirantes in São Paulo have become sites of public debate in recent years.

Section 39.8

The Sertão, the São Francisco Valley, Maranhão, and Amazonian Frontiers

While the Paulistas moved west and south, other expansions moved north and inland. In the northeast, the demands of the sugar coast created a cattle-raising frontier. Cattle supplied meat, hides, and draft animals to the mills, and ranchers moved up river valleys, especially the São Francisco, which became known as the river of the corrals. Prominent landholders, such as the Ávila family of Bahia, built vast estates. The advance displaced and killed Indigenous peoples of the sertão, many of them speakers of non-Tupi languages whom the Portuguese called tapuia, and it sparked prolonged wars later in the century, outside this chapter’s scope. Settlement was thin and discontinuous: a ranch here, a fort there, with large spaces in between.

On the northern coast the Portuguese moved against Potiguara and French traders. The founding of Paraíba in 1585 and of the fort of the Reis Magos at Natal in the late 1590s extended Portuguese control, sometimes through alliance with groups opposed to the Potiguara and sometimes through war. After the defeat of the French in Maranhão, the Portuguese founded Belém in 1616 at the mouth of the Amazon, building the Forte do Presépio. The crown organized the northern territories as a separate Estado do Maranhão in 1621, a distinction explained in Chapter 41. The Amazon was an Indigenous world of dense riverine populations, as Chapter 23 showed, and the Portuguese presence was a fort and a few settlements at its mouth.

From Belém, expeditions moved up the river. Pedro Teixeira led a large expedition upriver to Quito and back between 1637 and 1639, asserting Portuguese claims and demonstrating that the Amazon could be traveled from the Atlantic to the Andes. English and Dutch trading posts on the lower Amazon were attacked in the 1620s and 1630s. Slave-raiding expeditions, including “ransom troops” that purchased or seized captives, supplied labor to the colonists of Maranhão and Pará, where the sugar and tobacco economies were smaller and Africans were fewer. Jesuits, including Antônio Vieira in the 1650s, campaigned against abuses. Earlier royal legislation had alternated between declaring Indigenous freedom and permitting captivity under categories such as just war and ransom. A law of 1 April 1680 for the Estado do Maranhão went further: it prohibited Indigenous enslavement, including enslavement through ransom (resgate) expeditions and other exceptions that earlier laws had permitted. The law also provided that captives taken in authorized wars were to remain prisoners, to be placed in villages of free Christian Indigenous people, and to keep their legal freedom rather than become enslaved. Its text is reproduced in the Anais da Biblioteca Nacional do Rio de Janeiro, volume 66, pages 57–59. A companion provision issued the same day divided the labor of the working-age men in the mission villages, assigning a share to colonists for paid service, and other orders favored the Jesuits’ role in the villages. The law’s reach was limited. The historian José Ribamar Bessa Freire has argued, in a 2007 interview, that the law forbade new captivity without freeing people already held, and that it expressed a struggle between settlers and Jesuits over Indigenous labor as much as concern for freedom. These are his interpretations and not express clauses of the law. Settlers resisted it, and the revolt in São Luís in 1684 is associated with colonial hostility to the new regime. The crown then revised the system. The Regimento das Missões of 21 December 1686 regulated missionary authority over Indigenous villages and the distribution of their labor, and a royal decree of 28 April 1688 revoked the general prohibition of 1680, again permitting specified forms of enslavement through authorized warfare and resgate. The administrative details belong to Chapter 41. Formal legal freedom and actual labor therefore diverged: people who were legally free could still be assigned, pressed, or ransomed into work for colonists. Missions and forts occupied key river points, but the interior remained in the hands of Indigenous peoples. Occupation of the vast northern territory remained discontinuous, a pattern of forts, mission villages, and cattle ranches separated by long stretches where Indigenous peoples decided who could pass.

Section 39.9

The Río de la Plata Basin and Its Indigenous Societies

The Río de la Plata is not a single river but an estuary, fed by the Paraná and Uruguay, with the Paraguay joining the Paraná from the north. Together these rivers drain a basin that reaches from the Brazilian highlands to the edge of the Andes, and they link grasslands, forests, wetlands, and the Chaco. For the Indigenous peoples who lived there, this was not an empty frontier but a connected landscape of villages, fields, fishing places, and paths.

Guaraní communities were the principal agricultural people of the upper rivers. They lived in villages governed by local leaders and linked by kinship and alliance, cultivated maize and manioc, and moved periodically to new lands as soils were exhausted. Some Guaraní groups had expanded over centuries, and Guaraní warriors also raided to the west, toward the Andean borderlands of the Inca empire, a link Chapter 24 introduced. South and east of the Guaraní, peoples such as the Charrúa, Chaná, and Minuane lived by hunting, fishing, and gathering in the lands along the Uruguay and the northern shore of the estuary, and the Querandí hunted on the pampas to the south and west. In the Chaco and along the Paraguay River lived the canoe-using Payaguá and equestrian groups such as the Guaycurú, who adopted the horse after Europeans introduced it and became formidable opponents. The Timbú and other river peoples lived along the lower Paraná. These societies traded, intermarried, and fought, and none of them was subject to a distant capital.

The region’s name came from an early rumor of silver. In the 1520s the Portuguese-born Alejo García, who had been shipwrecked after the Solís expedition, traveled inland with Guaraní companions and reached the borderlands of the Andes, bringing back, or at least sending word of, silver objects before he was killed. The sources are fragmentary and late, and the details of the journey are uncertain. Rumors of such wealth drew later expeditions. The silver in question came through Indigenous trade networks from the Andes, not from a mine in the Plata basin, a fact that explains both the optimism of the first Spanish expeditions and their disappointment.

Section 39.10

Spanish Exploration and the First Failure of Buenos Aires, 1516–1541

Juan Díaz de Solís entered the estuary in 1516 and was killed in a clash with Indigenous people on the northern shore, an event often attributed to the Charrúa. Sebastian Cabot, a Venetian-born navigator in Spanish service, explored the rivers between 1526 and 1530, built a fort called Sancti Spiritus on the Carcarañá River, and obtained silver ornaments from Indigenous traders. The fort was destroyed by Timbú in 1529. Cabot’s reports, and the name he gave the river, fed expectations of wealth.

In 1536 Pedro de Mendoza arrived with a large expedition, traditionally estimated at over a thousand men, and founded a settlement called Santa María del Buen Ayre on the southern shore of the estuary, on the site of the future Buenos Aires. The expedition lacked adequate provisions and depended on food from nearby Indigenous groups, notably the Querandí. When relations broke down, the Querandí besieged the settlement, according to Spanish accounts, and the settlers suffered from hunger and disease. Ulrich Schmidl, a German soldier who took part, described the suffering in his later narrative, which must be read with caution since it was written for a European audience. Mendoza, ill, left the region and died at sea in 1537.

The lesson was that a founding act did not establish control. The settlement had a charter, a name, and a few hundred hungry men, but it had no fields, no allies, and no supply line. Its leaders, including Juan de Ayolas and then Domingo Martínez de Irala, moved upriver, where Guaraní villages offered food and the possibility of alliance. Buenos Aires was abandoned in 1541 and its settlers moved to Asunción. For nearly four decades, the estuary had no permanent Spanish town. That outcome says as much about Indigenous decisions, on the part of the Querandí to resist and the Guaraní to engage, as it does about Spanish planning.

Section 39.11

Asunción, Spanish–Guaraní Alliances, and the Formation of Colonial Paraguay

Asunción was founded in August 1537 on the Paraguay River by Juan de Salazar, in the territory of Carios Guaraní, who had their own reasons to welcome allies against Chaco peoples, including the Guaycurú. The Spaniards were few, the Guaraní numerous, and the relationship that emerged combined alliance, kinship, and coercion. Guaraní leaders gave women to Spanish captains, creating ties of kinship in which Spaniards became brothers-in-law, a pattern later called cuñadazgo. These ties brought obligations of labor and military support. They also created a mixed population, known in Spanish sources as mancebos de la tierra, “young men of the land,” who would play a decisive role in later settlement.

The arrangement was unequal. Spaniards, in practice, took Guaraní women as concubines and servants in large numbers, and sources describe households in which a Spanish man held many Guaraní women. The women were embedded in Guaraní kin networks that sought to turn alliances to their own advantage, and their own experience can be seen only through the sources’ distortions. Domingo Martínez de Irala, who became the dominant figure after the colony’s early crisis, consolidated this order. After a period under the adelantado Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, who tried to restrict abuses and was deposed by colonists in 1544, Irala governed until his death in 1556. Near the end of his rule, he distributed Guaraní communities in encomienda to Spanish settlers. The institution gave encomenderos claims to Guaraní labor and tribute, and in Paraguay it was woven into the existing kin ties. Guaraní resistance was recurrent. Revolts are recorded in 1539, in 1546, in the late 1550s and early 1560s, and repeatedly afterward, and shamans who preached a return to older customs inspired several of them. The best known is the movement of Overá (also written Oberá), a shaman from Guarambaré, near Asunción. With his son he traveled among encomienda villages in the Ypané and Jejuí region in 1578–1579, proclaiming a return to Guaraní traditions and rejecting the labor, religion, and customs the Spaniards imposed, and he drew on Christian ideas as well as Guaraní ones. Bartomeu Melià, whose research on Guaraní resistance informs this account, places the movement in 1578–1579. In 1579 Spanish militia under Juan de Garay defeated Overá’s followers near the Ypané River. Some fled with their leader to the hills and others were returned to encomienda service. Much of the narrative rests on Spanish sources and on the pro-Spanish verse of Martín del Barco Centenera (1602), so the movement is known mainly through its suppression rather than in its own voice. Historians read it as a religious revitalization movement that was also a revolt against encomienda labor.

Spanish women began to arrive. A group led by Mencía Calderón reached Asunción in the mid-1550s, and the settler population grew. The colony remained small, isolated, and poor relative to Peru. It also remained dependent on Guaraní labor and on the descendants of Spaniards and Guaraní women, who spoke Guaraní as a first language. That linguistic fact, which endured, is one of the clearest markers of Paraguay’s distinct colonial formation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Guaraní Households, Women’s Work, and Colonial Family Formation

In Guaraní villages women planted and harvested crops, processed manioc and maize, made pottery and textiles, and prepared the fermented drinks that accompanied ceremonies. When Spaniards settled in Asunción they relied on this knowledge for food. Guaraní women’s agricultural and domestic labor underpinned the colony from its first years, and the transfer of women to Spaniards was in practice also a transfer of the capacity to feed them. Women moved into households in which they had little control, often combining forced labor, sexual exploitation, and obligations to kin.

Children of these unions grew up speaking Guaraní with mothers and relatives and Spanish with fathers and officials. As mancebos de la tierra they served as soldiers, interpreters, and settlers, and some of them led later expeditions, such as the party that founded Santa Fe and refounded Buenos Aires. Men’s labor was drawn into encomienda service, which removed them from fields, and epidemics, which struck repeatedly, disrupted household life. The sources, written by Spanish officials and clergy, record these experiences unevenly and often by their complaints. The persistence of the Guaraní language in colonial Paraguay is evidence that Guaraní women and families shaped colonial society, not just served it.

Section 39.12

Santa Fe, Buenos Aires, and the Colonial River Network

As Asunción’s population grew, its leaders looked for outlets. In 1573 Juan de Garay, leading a party of settlers from Asunción, many of them mancebos de la tierra, founded Santa Fe on the Paraná River. In 1580 Garay refounded Buenos Aires, which he named the City of the Trinity and Port of Santa María del Buen Ayre, with a small group of settlers, again many born in the Asunción region. Corrientes was founded in 1588. These towns, with Asunción, formed a river-based chain along which people, cattle, and goods moved. The refounded Buenos Aires differed from Mendoza’s settlement in a crucial respect: it was supplied by a nearby colony with food-producing capacity, and its settlers were familiar with the land and its peoples.

Relations with Indigenous communities remained tense. Querandí, Charrúa, and other groups attacked settlements and livestock, and the river network was vulnerable to Payaguá canoe raids. Cattle and horses brought by Europeans multiplied on the pampas and became the basis of a new economy, as hunters of both Indigenous and mixed ancestry organized hunts for hides and meat. These animals also changed Indigenous societies, as groups on the pampas and in the Chaco adopted horses and became more mobile, a transformation that strengthened their resistance to settlement in later centuries.

The two principal cities occupied different positions. Asunción was an inland center whose strength lay in its Guaraní population and its role as a source of settlers and a base for expeditions. Buenos Aires was a port on the Atlantic, at first poor and marginal because Spanish trade regulations restricted its commerce, but well placed for smuggling. Silver from Potosí flowed down to it, and goods, including enslaved Africans, came in, often in violation of the rules. Portuguese merchants, many of them New Christians, were prominent in this commerce, especially during the Iberian Union. In 1617 the crown divided the region into separate governorships of Paraguay and Río de la Plata (Buenos Aires), which recognized the growing weight of the port. The long-term significance of Buenos Aires lies beyond this chapter.

Section 39.13

Jesuit Missions, Guaraní Communities, and the Reduction Frontier

The mission towns of the Guaraní were not the first attempt to concentrate Indigenous people in new settlements. Franciscans, notably Luis de Bolaños, founded reductions near Asunción in the late sixteenth century. The Jesuits, who arrived in the Paraguay region in the late 1580s and became a separate province in 1604, built on this precedent and on royal and governors’ support. Governor Hernando Arias de Saavedra, known as Hernandarias, a criollo born in Asunción, backed the Jesuits, and the first Jesuit reductions among the Guaraní were established in 1609.

Historical Vocabulary
Redução / Reducción

A reducción (Portuguese redução) was a town in which dispersed Indigenous people were gathered, converted, and governed according to a Spanish or Portuguese model. The Spanish word comes from reducir, to bring back or bring into order. Reductions typically had a church, a central plaza, and a municipal council, the cabildo, with Indigenous officeholders.

The word obscures a range of experiences. For missionaries and officials, a reduction was a civilizing and religious project. For Indigenous residents, it could be a refuge from raiders and encomenderos, a place of new religious and political opportunities, or a place of coercion and loss. This chapter treats reductions as frontier institutions; Chapter 46 examines the religious and cultural history of missions more broadly.

Guaraní communities joined the reductions for varied reasons. Some sought protection from slave raiders and encomenderos, since mission residents were generally exempt from the personal service demanded under encomienda and owed the crown a modest tribute. Some followed leaders, caciques, who negotiated with the Jesuits and retained authority within the new towns. Some were attracted by metal tools, food, and new forms of prestige. Others rejected the missions. In late 1628 the Jesuit Roque González and two companions were killed by followers of the cacique Ñezú, an episode that mission accounts commemorate as martyrdom and that also shows Indigenous opposition to the new order. Many people entered and left the towns, and epidemics in the 1610s and 1630s killed large numbers of residents.

The mission towns reorganized Guaraní life. Residents lived in houses grouped around a plaza, attended church, and worked fields partly cultivated in common and partly cultivated for families. Indigenous officials sat on the cabildo, and craftsmen learned European skills, such as masonry, carpentry, and music. The Jesuits taught in Guaraní, and Montoya compiled a Guaraní grammar and dictionary in 1639 and 1640. The language of the missions was Guaraní, which ensured its survival. By the early eighteenth century there were about thirty mission towns, a development beyond this chapter’s scope. Missions were also defensive communities. After a conditional royal decree of 21 May 1640 allowed firearms for the mission Guaraní under Jesuit supervision, and the Jesuits had already begun arming and training militias, mission militias became a significant military force, as Mbororé would show.

Section 39.14

Guairá, the Bandeirante Raids, and the Battle of Mbororé

The meeting of the two narratives came in Guairá, a region east of the Paraná in the borderland between Paraguay and the Paulista frontier. Spanish settlers had founded Ciudad Real del Guairá in 1556 and Villa Rica del Espíritu Santo in 1570, and Spanish encomenderos exploited Guaraní labor there. In 1610 Jesuits founded reductions in the region. These villages concentrated large Guaraní populations in one place, which made them attractive targets for Paulista raiders, who sought captives. From the late 1620s, expeditions led by Antônio Raposo Tavares and Manuel Preto attacked the missions. A major raid in 1628–1629 is recorded in Jesuit and Spanish sources as involving a force of several hundred Luso-Brazilians and well over a thousand Tupi auxiliaries, and thousands of Guaraní were seized. Further raids followed.

Facing destruction, the Jesuit Antonio Ruiz de Montoya organized the evacuation of the Guairá missions. In 1631 and 1632, thousands of Guaraní, with Jesuits, left their towns and traveled down the Paraná on rafts and on foot, around the falls and into the lands to the south. Melià reports that about twelve thousand people set out and about four thousand reached the new settlements, since hunger, illness, and attacks took many lives; other estimates differ and should be treated as approximate. The Spanish towns of Guairá, whose encomenderos had also exploited Guaraní labor, were destroyed or abandoned in the same years. The raiders then turned south, attacking the Tape region and the Uruguay River missions in the mid-1630s, and the missions relocated westward.

Primary Source Overview (Paraphrase): in these chapters Montoya narrates the invasion of the reductions by the people of São Paulo and the destruction of a Spanish settlement and Indigenous towns, followed by the departure of the residents of Loreto and San Ignacio, the only two of thirteen Guairá towns that remained. Melià’s account of the same events reports that about twelve thousand people set out on the journey and about four thousand arrived, and that eleven of the thirteen reductions had been destroyed.

Analysis: Montoya was an eyewitness who spoke Guaraní, and his narrative is a major source for the raids, the evacuation, and the Jesuit view of mission life. He also wrote to win support in Madrid. Melià notes that the captive totals Montoya gave in his memorials are very large and that the book itself is more cautious on some counts, so students should treat such totals as advocacy and not as measured counts. The book also criticizes the Spanish encomenderos and officials of Guairá, which shows that the Guaraní faced pressure from more than one direction. The Guaraní appear mainly through the filter of a missionary who needed to present them as worthy of protection. Their own reasons for entering, leaving, or resisting the missions, and the views of the Paulistas and their Tupi auxiliaries, must be sought in other sources, such as Spanish administrative records and Paulista municipal documents. Students are encouraged to read Chapters XXXV and XXXVIII in the digitized edition and compare them with this overview.

The turning point came on the Uruguay River. On 11 March 1641, at Mbororé, near the hill of that name in what is now Misiones, Argentina, a mission army fought a Paulista expedition that had come down the river in a large flotilla of canoes. Guaraní caciques, including Ignacio Abiarú and Nicolás Ñeenguirú, commanded the mission forces, and Jesuits, including lay brothers who had been soldiers, such as Domingo de Torres, trained and helped organize them. The fighting combined river and land action, and it is reported to have lasted several days, up to about a week, ending with the flight and pursuit of the raiders. Reported numbers differ and should be treated as approximate: about four thousand Guaraní warriors, of whom only a few hundred had firearms, together with improvised artillery; against a Paulista force put at some three to four hundred and fifty Luso-Brazilians and from about 2,700 to several thousand Tupi auxiliaries, in anywhere from a hundred and thirty to several hundred canoes. The victory did not end slave raiding in the wider region, but it halted large Paulista attacks on the Uruguay missions and protected the mission towns for decades. It shows that Indigenous military organization, firearms, and knowledge of the river shaped the limits of Portuguese expansion, and not the Jesuits alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Missions, the Raids, and Mbororé

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom.

Guaraní mission residents: Many entered the towns to escape raiders and encomienda service and to gain tools and standing, and they accepted new religious, labor, and political obligations in return. They organized the militia that won at Mbororé. Their own accounts of these choices are largely missing.
Guaraní outside the missions: Some communities rejected the Jesuits, as the killing of Roque González shows. Others lived under encomenderos or remained in their own villages. They bore the pressures of both slave raiding and Spanish demands without the missions’ protection.
Paulista raiders and their Tupi auxiliaries: Settlers of a poor town sought labor, and their auxiliaries pursued their own goals, including captives and plunder. Their view survives mainly in Paulista records and in Spanish and Jesuit denunciations.
Jesuits: They sought to protect and convert the Guaraní, and they also governed them, relied on their labor, and competed with settlers for control of that labor. Their writings are the principal source and were composed for audiences whose support they needed.
Spanish encomenderos and officials: Settlers in Guairá exploited Guaraní labor and resented the missions’ exemptions, while officials in Asunción and Madrid balanced the defense of the frontier against settler interests and the cost of war.
Section 39.15

Spanish–Portuguese Rivalry and the Southern Frontier

In 1580 Philip II of Spain claimed the Portuguese throne after the death of King Henrique, and for sixty years, until the Portuguese revolt of 1640 brought João IV to the throne, one monarch ruled both empires. The Iberian Union had mixed effects. The Tordesillas line lost much of its practical meaning, which allowed the Portuguese to push beyond it without protest from a king who ruled both sides. Portuguese merchants entered Spanish markets, including Buenos Aires, and Paulista expeditions moved into territory that Spain claimed. At the same time the union drew Brazil into Spain’s wars, and the Dutch attacks on the northeast were a direct consequence. Portugal remained a separate kingdom with separate institutions, and the legal distinction between the two empires persisted.

After 1640 the border mattered again. Portugal, at war with Spain, had no wish to leave its American claims undefended, and the Portuguese crown, newly independent, sought revenue and strategic advantage. In January 1680 Manuel Lobo, governor of Rio de Janeiro, founded Colônia do Sacramento on the north bank of the Río de la Plata, directly across the estuary from Buenos Aires. The site gave Portugal a base for trade with the silver economy and a claim to the northern shore. The Spanish governor of Buenos Aires, José de Garro, attacked and captured it in August 1680, using a force that included thousands of Guaraní militia from the mission towns, a reminder that the missions had become an instrument of Spanish frontier defense. The two crowns negotiated, the settlement was provisionally restored, and it changed hands repeatedly in later decades, a history beyond this chapter.

Colônia is evidence that the boundary remained disputed. Neither empire could dictate the outcome by decree. Indigenous peoples who lived near the estuary, such as the Charrúa, continued to control much of the countryside. Smugglers ignored both crowns. The mission Guaraní, Portuguese Brazilian settlers, Spanish officials, and Indigenous peoples of the plains each had their own aims, and the border was the unstable result of their interaction.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Territorial Competition Intensified in the Southern Frontier

Long-term causes

The vague Tordesillas line, the attraction of Andean silver reaching the Atlantic through the Plata, the Portuguese demand for labor, and the weakness of both crowns on distant frontiers.

Short-term causes

The end of the Iberian Union in 1640, the Portuguese need for revenue after the Dutch war, the growth of Buenos Aires as a smuggling port, and the consolidation of the Guaraní missions as a barrier.

Event or transformation

The Portuguese founding of Colônia do Sacramento in 1680 and the Spanish attack in the same year.

Immediate effects

Diplomatic negotiation, a provisional restoration of the settlement, increased use of mission militias, and a new center of contraband commerce.

Long-term consequences

A standing dispute over the northern shore of the estuary that shaped Spanish and Portuguese policy in the following century.

Historical complexity

The competition was not a simple contest between two crowns. Indigenous peoples, mission communities, merchants, and smugglers all influenced its course.
Section 39.16

Conquest without Complete Control: Brazil, Paraguay, and the Río de la Plata

Taken together, the histories of Brazil, Paraguay, and the Río de la Plata show a pattern of conquest by negotiation, attrition, and partial occupation. Portuguese settlers held the coast and a few river mouths and had pushed cattle and expeditions into the interior, but they depended on Indigenous allies and on African labor, and most of the interior remained outside their control. Spaniards held Asunción, the river towns, and the port of Buenos Aires, but the Chaco, the pampas, and the plains of the Banda Oriental remained largely in Indigenous hands. Missions sheltered thousands of Guaraní and also changed their lives. Labor extraction took different forms: sugar plantations and enslaved Africans in the northeast, Indigenous captives in São Paulo and Maranhão, encomienda and kinship in Paraguay, and mission labor in the reductions.

The comparison with the conquests of Mexico and Peru is instructive, and so is the comparison with Chile. There was no single blow that brought down a state. Conquest was a series of local arrangements, each fragile. Some Indigenous groups became close allies and suffered for it, others resisted and were enslaved or displaced, and others retreated. A territorial claim on a map did not imply political control. Even where a settlement existed, as at Colônia, its survival depended on its ability to supply itself, to secure alliances, and to survive the opposition of both Indigenous people and rival Europeans.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Indigenous Autonomy, Colonial Settlement, and Shifting Frontiers, 1500–1680

What Changed

  • The coast moved from a barter frontier to a colonial economy of sugar, towns, and forced labor
  • Epidemics and enslavement transformed Indigenous populations, especially on the coast
  • Enslaved Africans replaced Indigenous workers on the large estates of the northeast
  • The Guaraní missions created a new institution and a new military force
  • Buenos Aires grew from a failed camp into a port with an Atlantic commerce

What Stayed the Same

  • Large parts of the interior remained under Indigenous control
  • Settlers depended on Indigenous allies, knowledge, and labor
  • The crowns relied on private entrepreneurs and religious orders rather than regular armies
  • Rivers remained the principal routes of movement
  • Guaraní language and culture persisted in Paraguay and the missions

Who experienced change most directly: Coastal Tupi peoples, who faced epidemics, war, and enslavement within a few decades, and Africans brought to the plantations. Greater continuity: Peoples of the Chaco, the pampas, and the deep interior, whose autonomy persisted. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the colonial map of 1680 was a map of claims and footholds, and that Indigenous autonomy remained a fact of life long afterward.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the expansion of Portuguese and Spanish colonization into eastern and southern South America and argued that, in these regions, claims of conquest outran actual control. Without Indigenous states to defeat, Europeans advanced through trade, alliance, coercion, and war with many separate communities, and the result was a pattern of footholds rather than domination.

The Indigenous societies of the Atlantic coast and the river basin were village-based and autonomous. Tupi-speaking peoples of the coast, Guaraní communities of the rivers, and neighbors such as the Charrúa, Querandí, Payaguá, and Guaycurú had their own agricultural, military, and political systems. The Portuguese and Spaniards were few and relied on Indigenous food, labor, and knowledge.

The Portuguese began with the brazilwood trade, in which Indigenous labor was exchanged for metal tools, and the crown turned to hereditary captaincies in the 1530s after the founding of São Vicente. The grants showed the difference between a legal claim and occupation: a few succeeded and many failed. The arrival of a governor-general and the Jesuits in 1549 added a royal and religious layer.

Coastal warfare involved Indigenous allies on both sides. Tupiniquim and Temiminó leaders supported the Portuguese, many Tupinambá groups allied with the French, and the Tamoio war ended in the expulsion of the French in 1567 and the founding of Rio de Janeiro. Epidemics and the demand for labor shaped the outcome. Sugar required constant labor, led to Indigenous enslavement under the cover of just war and ransom, and then to the growing use of enslaved Africans.

French and Dutch challenges involved Indigenous and African participants on all sides. The Dutch occupations of Salvador and the northeast, and their eventual defeat, depended in part on Indigenous and Afro-Brazilian soldiers. Meanwhile São Paulo became a base of expeditions that moved into the interior. The bandeiras drew heavily on Tupi auxiliaries, fed a market in Indigenous captives, and had territorial consequences that later memory celebrated. Historians continue to debate how to balance these aspects.

In the Río de la Plata, the first Buenos Aires failed in 1541 because it lacked food, allies, and a supply line. Asunción, founded in 1537, succeeded through alliance with Guaraní communities, kinship ties, women’s labor, and encomienda, and it produced a mixed society in which Guaraní persisted. Settlers from Asunción founded Santa Fe, refounded Buenos Aires in 1580, and established Corrientes, building a river network.

The Jesuit reductions were frontier institutions that sheltered and reorganized Guaraní communities. Guaraní joined for reasons of protection, leadership, and opportunity, and some rejected the missions. The raids on Guairá and Tape by Paulista expeditions in the 1620s and 1630s destroyed missions, led to the exodus organized by Montoya, and ended with the Guaraní victory at Mbororé in 1641.

The Iberian Union weakened the Tordesillas line, and the Portuguese restoration of 1640 revived the border. The founding of Colônia do Sacramento in 1680 and the Spanish response showed the boundary remained disputed. Mission militias had become an instrument of Spanish frontier defense, and smugglers and Indigenous peoples further complicated control.

Students should take away both a conclusion and a method. The conclusion is that colonial expansion in these regions depended on Indigenous decisions and left Indigenous autonomy in large areas. The method is source criticism: nearly all of the evidence was written by Europeans with purposes of their own, from Caminha to Montoya, and the Indigenous voices that shaped events survive only in fragments and through later sources.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the conquest of South America was not one event. In Mexico and the Andes, invaders exploited the structures of powerful states. In Brazil, Paraguay, and the Río de la Plata they met societies without such structures and had to bargain, fight, and settle with many communities, none of which could deliver the rest. The result was a map of claims overlaid on a landscape that remained largely Indigenous, and a set of colonial societies that took shape through alliance, kinship, coercion, and mission life.

The chapter also links regions that are often studied separately. The sugar coast, São Paulo, Maranhão, Asunción, the Guaraní missions, and Buenos Aires were connected by rivers, merchants, religious orders, slave raids, and the shared pressure of imperial rivalry. The raids on Guairá and the victory at Mbororé show that Indigenous political and military organization could define the limits of European expansion. The turn to enslaved Africans in the northeast, and the persistence of Indigenous captive labor elsewhere, show that labor systems depended on local conditions as well as imperial policy.

Several developments will recur. Indigenous autonomy in the interior survived long past 1680. The Guaraní language and mission legacy shaped later Paraguay. The dispute over the northern shore of the Río de la Plata remained a flashpoint, and the bandeirante legacy remains contested in Brazilian memory. Students should carry forward the habit of distinguishing a claim from control, and a founding from a settlement.

Part IX turns from the expansion of colonial frontiers to the institutions that governed them. Chapter 40 examines the viceroyalties, audiencias, and cabildos of Spanish America, and Chapter 41 returns to Portuguese Brazil to trace how captaincies gave way to royal government. The question that this chapter leaves open is how crowns that controlled so little of what they claimed managed to rule at all.
