Amazonia and the Tropical Lowlands

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Rethinking the "Pristine Amazon"

For much of the twentieth century, the Amazon rainforest was described in scholarship, travel writing, and popular imagination as one of the last great untouched wildernesses on Earth — a green ocean of trees that had absorbed and erased whatever small, scattered bands of human beings had once passed through it. That picture is no longer sustainable. Beneath the canopy, along the rivers, and in the soil itself lie the material remains of societies that farmed, built, traded, and reshaped their environment on a scale archaeologists are still working to measure.

In the early 1540s, a Spanish expedition under Francisco de Orellana descended an enormous river system east of the Andes in search of cinnamon and, eventually, a way back to the Atlantic. A Dominican friar traveling with the expedition, Gaspar de Carvajal, kept a chronicle describing what the exhausted, half-starved Spaniards saw from their boats: villages following one after another along the riverbank for great distances, broad roads leading inland from the water's edge, and, in some stretches, what appeared to be substantial and densely inhabited settlements. For centuries afterward, most historians dismissed these descriptions as the exaggerations of hungry, frightened men desperate to justify their expedition or to inflate the wonders of a hostile land. Twentieth-century archaeologists, working from the assumption that tropical forest soils were too poor to support large populations or complex societies, tended to agree: Carvajal, they concluded, must have been describing something far smaller and more modest than what he claimed to see.

Beginning especially in the final decades of the twentieth century and accelerating through the early twenty-first, a growing body of archaeological, botanical, and soil-scientific evidence has forced a substantial revision of that dismissal. Extensive fields of unusually fertile black soil, created and enriched over centuries by human settlement, have been identified across wide stretches of the Amazon basin. Geometric earthworks — ditches, causeways, and mounds — have been mapped beneath forest canopy in Acre, in the Bolivian lowlands, and elsewhere, some visible only through satellite imagery or after recent deforestation exposed their outlines. Raised agricultural fields, artificial fish weirs, planned villages linked by roads, and enormous shell-and-earth mounds along the lower Amazon all point toward societies considerably larger, more settled, and more capable of transforming their environment than earlier generations of scholars believed possible in a tropical lowland setting.

This chapter takes up the historical and scientific problem these discoveries have created. It does not claim that Amazonia was, before 1492, a single unified civilization comparable to the Mexica or Inca states examined in earlier chapters, nor does it claim that the entire basin was densely populated in a uniform way. Instead, it asks students to hold a more complicated and, in the end, more historically accurate picture in view: Amazonia was a mosaic of Indigenous landscapes, in which some regions, especially along major rivers and productive floodplains, supported substantial, long-lived, and highly organized communities capable of reshaping soils, forests, and waterways, while other regions supported smaller, more mobile populations whose material traces are far less visible to archaeologists. Both kinds of society were full participants in Amazonian history, and neither should stand in for the whole.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about the kinds of evidence available for lowland tropical history and about why that evidence has been slow to accumulate and easy to overlook. Organic materials decay quickly in humid tropical environments; dense forest cover and, until recently, limited archaeological investment across much of the basin meant that many sites simply had not been surveyed; and long-standing scholarly assumptions about tropical environments as inherently limiting discouraged researchers from even looking for the kind of large-scale landscape modification that has since been documented. Learning to read soils, earthworks, plant distributions, river networks, and colonial-era testimony together, critically, is itself one of the central historical skills this chapter aims to develop.

Finally, this chapter situates Amazonian history within the wider argument of Part V of this volume: that the great empires of Mesoamerica and the Andes, examined in Parts II through IV, must not be allowed to stand in for the whole of Indigenous Latin America. Amazonian peoples did not build an empire on the scale of the Mexica Triple Alliance or Tawantinsuyu, and this chapter does not attempt to force them into that mold. What it does argue is that Amazonian societies developed their own distinctive and historically significant forms of political organization, agricultural innovation, and environmental transformation — forms that a narrow definition of "civilization," borrowed uncritically from Old World or highland American contexts, has often failed to recognize.

By the chapter's end, students should be equipped to explain why archaeologists and historians now describe pre-Columbian Amazonia as an anthropogenic, or human-shaped, landscape rather than a pristine wilderness — while also understanding precisely what that claim does and does not establish, and why population figures, settlement density, and the scale of environmental transformation remain subjects of genuine, ongoing scholarly debate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why earlier scholarship often described Amazonia as an untouched wilderness incapable of supporting large or complex societies, and why archaeologists and historical ecologists have substantially revised that view.
  2. Distinguish várzea floodplain environments from terra firme upland environments and explain why this distinction mattered for settlement, agriculture, and social organization.
  3. Trace the long-term development of lowland subsistence systems, from early fishing, hunting, and gathering through the domestication and refinement of manioc, maize, and other managed plants.
  4. Describe the technological process of processing bitter manioc and explain why Amazonian food systems often blurred the boundary between cultivation and forest management.
  5. Analyze the formation, distribution, and significance of Amazonian Dark Earths, or terra preta, as evidence of long-term human transformation of tropical soils.
  6. Compare earthworks, mounds, causeways, and raised-field systems across at least three distinct Amazonian regions, including the Llanos de Mojos, the Lower Amazon, and the Upper Xingu.
  7. Evaluate the significance and limitations of Gaspar de Carvajal's 1542 chronicle as evidence for the scale of riverine settlement encountered by the Orellana expedition.
  8. Assess the competing population estimates offered for pre-Columbian Amazonia and explain why historians and archaeologists continue to disagree about settlement density and total population.
  9. Explain how rivers functioned as highways of exchange, migration, and linguistic dispersal across the Amazonian lowlands rather than as barriers to interaction.
  10. Distinguish archaeological culture, language family, and political community as separate analytical categories that should not automatically be treated as identical.
  11. Connect pre-Columbian Amazonian land use to contemporary questions of Indigenous knowledge, forest management, and environmental history without projecting modern Indigenous practices unchanged onto the deep past.
  12. Contextualize Amazonia's Indigenous landscapes within the broader Part V argument that Indigenous Latin America extended, in varied and regionally specific forms, well beyond the Mexica and Inca imperial cores examined earlier in this volume.

Historical Context

The preceding chapters of Part V have examined Indigenous societies that earlier chapters of this textbook, focused on the Mexica and Inca imperial cores, risked leaving invisible: the island societies of the Caribbean in Chapter 21 and the metallurgical, agricultural, and politically decentralized world of the Isthmo-Colombian region in Chapter 22. This chapter turns to a region even more persistently misunderstood in popular and, for much of the twentieth century, scholarly imagination: the vast river basin and tropical forest system generally known as Amazonia, encompassing the Amazon River and its enormous network of tributaries, along with related lowland systems such as the Orinoco basin to the north, which shared ecological features and, at points, historical connections with the Amazon proper.

Historians and archaeologists studying Amazonia confront a distinctive evidentiary problem. The region's warm, humid climate accelerates the decomposition of wood, textiles, basketry, and other organic materials that might, in a drier or colder environment, have survived for archaeologists to recover directly. Dense forest cover has historically limited the number of sites identified through surface survey, and the sheer size of the basin — spanning parts of eight sovereign countries (Bolivia, Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Peru, Suriname, and Venezuela, with broader definitions of Amazonia sometimes also including French Guiana) and covering an area larger than the contiguous United States — has meant that archaeological investigation has proceeded unevenly, concentrated in a relatively small number of well-studied regions rather than distributed evenly across the whole basin. As a result, conclusions about Amazonian history must be drawn cautiously, and gaps in the archaeological record should not automatically be read as gaps in the historical record: an absence of documented earthworks in a given area may reflect an absence of archaeological survey rather than an absence of ancient settlement.

A second and closely related problem concerns the intellectual assumptions that long shaped how scholars approached the region. Much twentieth-century anthropological and archaeological thinking about tropical lowland societies rested on a model, developed particularly in the mid-twentieth century, that treated Amazonian soils as inherently poor in nutrients once forest cover was cleared, and treated tropical forest ecosystems generally as offering insufficient concentrated food resources to support large, sedentary, socially stratified populations of the kind found in Mesoamerica and the Andes. This model, sometimes associated with the idea of "environmental limitation" or "environmental determinism," led many researchers to interpret small, mobile, egalitarian Indigenous societies observed in parts of Amazonia during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as representative of Amazonian societies across the entire pre-Columbian past, rather than as one outcome among several — an outcome that, as later sections of this chapter explain, may in some cases have resulted partly from population collapse and social disruption following European contact rather than reflecting a fixed, unchanging ecological ceiling.

Since the 1980s and 1990s, and with accelerating momentum in the twenty-first century, a substantial body of new evidence has challenged this environmental-limitation model without simply reversing it into an equally simple claim that the entire Amazon basin was densely urbanized. Soil scientists identified extensive deposits of unusually fertile, dark-colored soil embedded with pottery fragments, charcoal, and other traces of long-term human habitation. Archaeologists working in Acre, in the Bolivian lowlands, along the lower Amazon, and in the Upper Xingu documented earthworks, raised fields, causeways, and planned settlement networks on a scale that would have been difficult to imagine from earlier, more limited surveys. Historical ecologists studying the distribution of economically useful tree species across supposedly "natural" forest found patterns consistent with centuries of deliberate human management rather than purely wild distribution. Taken together, this evidence has produced a considerably more complex — and, in important respects, still unsettled — picture of Amazonian history, one this chapter presents with appropriate care for both what the evidence supports and what it does not yet establish.

Section 23.1

Rethinking the "Pristine Amazon"

The phrase "pristine Amazon" captures an assumption that dominated both popular and scholarly understanding of the region for most of the twentieth century: that the rainforest, as observed by nineteenth- and twentieth-century travelers, missionaries, rubber tappers, and anthropologists, represented a more or less natural, largely unmodified state, inhabited only by small, scattered, highly mobile Indigenous groups whose impact on their environment was minimal. On this view, the forest itself was the region's dominant historical actor, and human beings had merely lived within it rather than shaping it.

This assumption rested on several intertwined beliefs, each of which subsequent research has substantially qualified. The first was ecological: many researchers believed that tropical rainforest soils, once cleared of vegetation, quickly lost their fertility, making it difficult to sustain the kind of intensive, surplus-producing agriculture that had supported dense populations, cities, and complex political institutions in Mesoamerica and the Andes. The second was demographic: without intensive agriculture, researchers reasoned, Amazonian populations must have remained small and dispersed, since forest hunting, fishing, and shifting cultivation could support only a limited number of people per unit of land. The third was historiographical: early modern European accounts describing large riverside populations, most famously Gaspar de Carvajal's 1542 chronicle, were widely dismissed as exaggeration, since they seemed inconsistent with both the ecological model and with the smaller, more dispersed populations that later European and North American observers actually encountered.

Beginning particularly in the 1980s and 1990s, and continuing with increasing intensity into the twenty-first century, this composite model came under sustained challenge from multiple directions at once. Soil scientists identified extensive deposits of anthropogenic dark earth, discussed fully in Section 23.5, whose fertility directly contradicted assumptions about universally poor tropical soils. Archaeologists mapped raised fields, causeways, mounds, and geometric earthworks across widely separated regions of the basin, discussed in Sections 23.6 through 23.9, demonstrating a capacity for large-scale landscape engineering that the earlier model had not anticipated. Historical ecologists documented patterns in forest composition — unusually dense concentrations of economically useful tree species in areas with evidence of past human occupation — consistent with centuries of deliberate management rather than purely natural distribution. And historians re-examined colonial-era testimony, including Carvajal's chronicle, with greater openness to the possibility that at least some early European observers had, however imperfectly and self-interestedly, described real large-scale settlement that later demographic collapse had erased from view.

This chapter adopts the interpretive position that has emerged from this body of research, while insisting on two important qualifications that recur throughout the sections that follow. First, revising the "pristine Amazon" model does not mean asserting that the entire basin was densely settled or uniformly transformed; the evidence instead supports a mosaic pattern, in which some regions — particularly along major rivers, in seasonally flooded savannas, and around especially productive aquatic environments — supported substantial, long-term, socially complex populations, while other regions, particularly deep interior terra firme forest away from major waterways, likely supported smaller and more mobile populations whose material traces are, for now, harder to detect. Second, revising the model does not mean treating every earthwork, dark-earth deposit, or large settlement as evidence of a single unified "lost civilization"; the societies responsible for these landscapes were regionally diverse, linguistically varied, and politically distinct from one another, a point developed at length in the sections that follow.

Section 23.2

Rivers, Floodplains, Forests, and Ecological Diversity

Before examining specific Amazonian societies, students must understand that "the rainforest" is far too simple a category to describe the region's actual ecological diversity. Amazonia encompasses several distinct environments, each of which shaped human settlement, subsistence, and social organization in different ways.

Várzea refers to the seasonally flooded floodplain bordering the Amazon River and many of its larger tributaries. Each year, snowmelt and rainfall from the Andes and the broader drainage basin cause river levels to rise and fall dramatically, sometimes by ten meters or more, flooding vast stretches of floodplain forest and grassland for months at a time before the waters recede. This annual flood cycle deposits nutrient-rich sediment across the floodplain, replenishing soil fertility in a manner broadly comparable, though not identical, to the annual flooding of the Nile in ancient Egypt. Várzea environments, as a result, generally offer some of the most agriculturally productive and fish-rich conditions available anywhere in the basin, and, as later sections of this chapter explain, they were often associated with the largest, densest, and most socially complex pre-Columbian settlements.

Terra firme, by contrast, refers to the higher-elevation forest that lies above the seasonal floodplain and does not experience regular inundation. Terra firme soils, lacking the annual sediment renewal that enriches várzea land, are frequently more acidic and less naturally fertile, particularly once forest cover is removed, and this distinction was central to the older environmental-limitation model discussed in Section 23.1. Terra firme environments, however, are not uniform either; some terra firme regions, as Section 23.5 explains, were substantially enriched through long-term human soil management, producing fertility levels that departed sharply from the surrounding "natural" baseline.

Beyond this basic várzea–terra firme distinction, Amazonia includes seasonally flooded savannas, such as the Llanos de Mojos examined in Section 23.7; extensive wetlands and swamp forests; upland plateau regions; and the transitional zones where Amazonian lowland forest meets Andean foothills, Caribbean coastal lowlands, or the drier savannas and scrublands to the south and east. The Amazon River itself, along with major tributaries including the Madeira, Negro, Xingu, Tapajós, and Tocantins, and the related but ecologically and historically distinct Orinoco system to the north, created a network of waterways whose combined length and navigability made water transport, rather than overland travel through dense forest, the primary means of movement, communication, and exchange across most of the region — a theme developed fully in Section 23.10.

Understanding this ecological diversity matters historically because it explains why Amazonian societies did not develop uniformly. Communities occupying rich várzea floodplains, seasonally flooded savannas, or the resource-dense waters near the Amazon's mouth generally had access to more concentrated, reliable food resources than communities living in more remote terra firme interior forest, and this variation in resource availability correlates, as the sections that follow demonstrate, with substantial variation in settlement size, social complexity, and the scale of landscape modification across the basin.

Section 23.3

The Earliest Lowland Communities and the Development of Agriculture

Human populations descended from the broader migratory processes examined in Chapter 2 had reached and begun adapting to Amazonian environments by at least eleven thousand years ago, and quite possibly earlier, although the same evidentiary limitations discussed in Chapter 2 — rapid organic decay, uneven survey coverage, and a still-developing radiocarbon and stratigraphic record for the region — mean that the earliest Amazonian chronology remains less securely established than for some other regions of the Americas. These early populations lived as foragers, exploiting an environment that, while ecologically rich in overall biodiversity, distributed its most calorie-dense and reliably harvestable resources unevenly: fish and aquatic animals concentrated along rivers, lakes, and floodplains; certain palm species and other fruit- and nut-bearing trees clustered in particular soil and light conditions; and land animals dispersed more thinly through the forest interior than the popular image of a game-rich jungle might suggest.

Over subsequent millennia, Amazonian populations developed increasingly sophisticated and increasingly managed relationships with the plants and animals around them. Rather than a single moment of "discovery," this process, as in the agricultural histories of Mesoamerica and the Andes examined in Chapters 5 and Part IV of this volume, unfolded gradually and unevenly across many generations, through repeated small-scale interventions — protecting favored plants from competitors, transplanting useful species closer to settlements, selectively harvesting in ways that favored certain genetic traits, and gradually intensifying cultivation of the most productive and reliable species. By roughly eight thousand to four thousand years ago, this process had produced, in various Amazonian sub-regions, increasingly deliberate cultivation of manioc, several palm species valued for fruit, oil, and fiber, and a range of other lowland plants, alongside continued and often still-dominant reliance on fishing, hunting, and the gathering of wild forest resources.

It is essential that students avoid two common errors when considering this process. The first error is assuming that Amazonian agriculture must have followed the same trajectory as Mesoamerican maize agriculture, moving in a simple, linear progression from foraging to full sedentary farming villages. Amazonian subsistence systems, as Section 23.4 explains in detail, generally combined cultivated plants, managed forest resources, and continued fishing and hunting in flexible combinations that varied by region, season, and social group, rather than replacing foraging with farming in a single decisive transition. The second error is assuming that because Amazonian agriculture did not center on a single dominant staple grain in the way Mesoamerican civilization centered on maize, it was therefore less sophisticated or less capable of supporting large populations. As later sections demonstrate, root-crop- and tree-crop-centered food systems, combined with exceptionally productive aquatic resources in várzea and lake environments, supported some of the largest pre-Columbian settlements documented anywhere in the lowland Americas.

Section 23.4

Manioc, Maize, and Indigenous Food Systems

Manioc, also known as cassava or, in many Spanish-speaking contexts, yuca, was one of the most important cultivated plants across Amazonia, and understanding its cultivation and processing is essential to understanding Amazonian food systems more broadly, even though, as Section 23.7 explains, some Amazonian societies relied instead on intensive maize cultivation as their agricultural foundation. Manioc is a starchy root crop that grows well in a wide range of tropical soils, including many terra firme soils too poor to sustain other crops for long, and it can be left in the ground for extended periods without spoiling, functioning as a kind of standing food reserve rather than requiring immediate harvest and storage.

Amazonian communities cultivated both "sweet" manioc varieties, which contain relatively low levels of naturally occurring cyanogenic compounds and can be prepared with comparatively simple cooking, and "bitter" manioc varieties, which contain much higher levels of these toxic compounds and require a specialized, multi-step processing sequence to render safe for consumption. This processing typically involved peeling and grating the root, pressing the grated pulp to extract toxic juices — often using a long woven tube device that could be twisted to squeeze liquid from the pulp inside it — and then further treating, drying, or cooking the remaining material, frequently into flour or flatbread, to eliminate remaining toxins. This sequence represents a genuinely sophisticated food-processing technology, developed and refined over many generations, and it should be understood as comparable in its technical demands to other major food-processing traditions studied elsewhere in this textbook, including the nixtamalization of maize examined in the context of Mesoamerican civilization.

Bitter manioc's toxicity, counterintuitively, offered certain advantages: because the unprocessed root was unpalatable and mildly poisonous, it was less vulnerable to pest damage and could be left growing in the ground as a reserve against periods of food scarcity, reducing the risks associated with concentrated, vulnerable grain storage of the kind more characteristic of highland agricultural systems. Manioc cultivation, however, was rarely the whole of an Amazonian community's food system. Households and communities also cultivated maize in many regions, particularly in drier savanna transitions and certain floodplain contexts, alongside sweet potatoes, various squashes, peppers, and numerous fruit trees, including several palm species whose fruit, oil, and fiber served multiple purposes. Communities near rivers, lakes, and floodplains relied heavily on fishing, using weirs, traps, nets, bows, and, in some regions, fish poisons derived from forest plants, and this aquatic protein source was frequently as important to daily subsistence as any cultivated crop.

This combination of root- and tree-crop cultivation, managed forest resources, and intensive fishing meant that Amazonian food systems often blurred the modern conceptual line between "agriculture," understood narrowly as field cultivation of annual crops, and "forest use," understood as passive exploitation of wild resources. Amazonian communities frequently maintained what researchers now describe as managed forest gardens or agroforestry systems, in which useful tree and plant species were deliberately favored, protected, and propagated within landscapes that, to an untrained outside observer, might appear to be simply "natural" forest. This practice, examined further in Section 23.12, is one of the central pieces of evidence historians now cite for describing much of Amazonia as an anthropogenic, rather than purely wild, environment.

Section 23.5

Terra Preta and the Creation of Fertile Soils

Among the most significant discoveries reshaping historical understanding of Amazonia is the identification and study of Amazonian Dark Earths, widely known by the Portuguese term terra preta, meaning "black earth." Terra preta deposits are patches of unusually dark, carbon-rich, and highly fertile soil found at hundreds of documented locations across the Amazon basin, ranging in size from small patches of a few hectares to areas covering many square kilometers. Unlike the naturally acidic, nutrient-poor soils typical of much surrounding terra firme forest, terra preta soils retain nutrients effectively, support sustained cultivation without the rapid fertility decline typical of cleared tropical forest soil, and often remain identifiable and agriculturally valuable to farming communities today, centuries or in some cases millennia after their formation.

Soil scientists and archaeologists have established that terra preta forms through long-term human habitation and land use rather than through any purely natural geological or ecological process. Characteristic components of terra preta deposits include charcoal — produced through low-temperature, incomplete burning of organic material rather than the complete combustion typical of wildfire — along with fragments of broken pottery, bone, ash, and other organic refuse accumulated through sustained human occupation. Charcoal in particular appears to play a central role in terra preta's distinctive properties: charcoal particles possess a porous structure that helps retain moisture and nutrients in the soil and provides habitat for beneficial soil microorganisms, and researchers studying terra preta formation have drawn on this insight to explore modern agricultural applications of charcoal-based soil amendment, sometimes referred to as biochar.

It is important that students avoid overstating what terra preta evidence establishes in either direction. Not every terra preta deposit reflects a single, uniform process of formation. Long-term settlement, refuse accumulation, burning, food processing, and other domestic activities clearly contributed to Amazonian Dark Earth formation, and the gradual, generations-long deposit of hearth ash, food waste, broken pottery, and other refuse in and around areas of sustained habitation was almost certainly part of the story at many sites. At the same time, research published in the 2020s, drawing in part on comparisons with the deliberate soil-enrichment practices of contemporary Kuikuro communities in the Upper Xingu, has provided evidence that at least some Indigenous communities intentionally created or enriched dark earth to improve soil fertility, rather than producing it only as an incidental byproduct of ordinary settlement. The relative importance of intentional management versus cumulative, unplanned settlement processes probably varied by place and period, and this remains an active area of research that this chapter does not resolve in either direction. What the evidence does establish clearly, whichever balance of processes proves correct for a given site, is that Amazonian communities transformed some of the region's least fertile natural soils into some of its most productive, a finding that directly contradicts the older assumption that tropical lowland environments imposed a fixed and unchangeable ceiling on agricultural productivity.

The geographic distribution of terra preta deposits also offers indirect evidence relevant to the population debate examined in Section 23.11. Because terra preta formation required sustained, multi-generational habitation at a single location, its presence at a given site indicates long-term settlement there, even where surface archaeological remains are otherwise scarce. The identification of hundreds of terra preta sites across the basin, including in areas with little other archaeological documentation, has therefore substantially expanded the geographic range of confirmed long-term pre-Columbian settlement beyond what earlier, more limited surveys had established.

Historical Vocabulary
Terra Preta

Terra preta, or Amazonian Dark Earth, refers to patches of unusually fertile, dark, carbon-rich soil found across hundreds of sites in the Amazon basin, formed through long-term human habitation rather than natural geological processes. Characteristic components include charcoal, pottery fragments, bone, ash, and other organic refuse accumulated over generations of settlement.

Terra preta matters historically because it directly challenges the assumption, once widespread among archaeologists and anthropologists, that Amazonian soils were universally too poor to support intensive, long-term agriculture. Its existence demonstrates that at least some Amazonian communities transformed their local environment in ways that left a durable, centuries-long agricultural legacy, one that some contemporary farming communities in the region continue to rely upon.

Students should be careful not to treat every dark-earth deposit as evidence of a single, uniform process. Some terra preta likely accumulated gradually through ordinary long-term settlement and refuse deposition, while research comparing ancient deposits with the deliberate soil-enrichment practices of contemporary Indigenous communities such as the Kuikuro has provided evidence that at least some terra preta was intentionally created or enriched. The balance between intentional management and incidental accumulation probably varied by site and is not resolved by this textbook.

Historical Vocabulary
Várzea and Terra Firme

Várzea refers to the seasonally flooded floodplain bordering the Amazon River and its larger tributaries, enriched annually by nutrient-rich sediment deposited during seasonal flooding. Terra firme refers to higher-elevation forest above the flood line, generally possessing less naturally fertile soil.

This distinction is central to understanding why Amazonian settlement was not evenly distributed across the basin. Várzea environments, along with terra preta-enriched terra firme sites discussed above, tended to support larger, denser, and more socially complex populations than unmodified terra firme interior forest, a pattern explored throughout the remainder of this chapter.

Section 23.6

Earthworks, Causeways, Mounds, and Managed Landscapes

Alongside the soil evidence discussed in Section 23.5, archaeologists have documented an extensive array of constructed earthworks across widely separated regions of Amazonia, providing some of the most visually striking evidence for large-scale Indigenous landscape engineering anywhere in the Americas. These earthworks take several distinct forms, and students should resist treating them as expressions of a single unified "Amazonian civilization"; they instead represent the achievements of numerous distinct societies, developed independently or through regional interaction across different parts of the basin and at different periods.

Geoglyphs are large, geometric earthen enclosures — typically ditches and low embankments forming squares, circles, and more complex geometric patterns — identified in substantial numbers in the western Amazonian state of Acre, in Brazil, and adjacent areas. Many of these geoglyphs remained unknown to archaeologists until relatively recently, when modern deforestation for cattle ranching exposed their outlines to aerial observation, and subsequent research using satellite imagery and ground survey has continued to identify additional sites. The precise function of Acre's geoglyphs remains debated: some may have served ceremonial or ritual purposes, others may have functioned as defensive enclosures, gathering places, or markers of territorial and social boundaries, and different sites may have served different combinations of these purposes. Their sheer number and scale, however, indicate a substantial and organized labor force capable of large-scale earth-moving construction, undermining assumptions that Acre's terra firme forest environment could support only small, minimally organized populations.

Raised fields are elevated planting platforms, typically constructed by digging surrounding canals and using the excavated soil to build up adjacent planting surfaces, a technique that improves drainage in seasonally flooded environments, concentrates fertile soil, and can also create fish- and water-management opportunities within the surrounding canal network. Raised-field systems appear prominently in the seasonally flooded savannas of the Llanos de Mojos, examined as a dedicated case study in Section 23.7, and in scattered locations elsewhere in the basin.

Causeways are raised, typically linear earthen roadways constructed across seasonally flooded terrain, allowing travel between settlements, fields, and resource areas during periods of high water when the surrounding land would otherwise be impassable on foot. Causeway networks, documented extensively in the Llanos de Mojos and, in a related though geographically distinct tradition, examined already in Chapter 22 in connection with Zenú hydraulic systems in the Isthmo-Colombian region, demonstrate sophisticated engineering responses to the challenges of seasonal flooding, extending the useful range and reliability of otherwise difficult terrain.

Mounds, constructed from accumulated earth, shell, or a combination of materials, served multiple purposes across different Amazonian regions, including elevating settlements and structures above seasonal flood levels, providing ceremonial or funerary platforms, and, in some cases, marking social or political status through their scale and construction effort. Mound-building traditions are especially well documented at Marajó Island and in the broader Lower Amazon region, examined in Section 23.8.

Taken together, this array of earthworks demonstrates that at least some Amazonian societies possessed the organizational capacity to mobilize substantial labor for coordinated, large-scale construction projects extending over years or generations — a capacity generally associated, in other regions studied in this textbook, with settled agricultural populations, some degree of social and political organization above the level of a single small village, and sustained investment in a particular landscape over multiple generations.

A large canopy-penetrating lidar survey published in 2026, covering more than 4,400 kilometers of flight lines across southwestern Amazonia, substantially expanded the documented scale of earthwork construction associated with the geoglyph-building societies of this region, sometimes referred to in the resulting scholarship as the Aquiry culture. Combining new lidar data with previously known sites, the study's authors estimated that this cultural area alone contains more than twenty thousand earthworks — a figure roughly comparable to earlier estimates for the entire Amazon basin — and proposed, on the basis of this settlement density, a population of approximately 1.25 to 3 million people within the studied region around AD 100 to 300. Because the surveyed area represents less than three percent of Greater Amazonia, this finding substantially strengthens the case for dense, organized occupation in at least this part of southwestern Amazonia. The study's authors were explicit, however, that extrapolating this result to the Amazon basin as a whole requires substantial further research, since comparably intensive lidar survey has not yet been conducted across most of the basin, and this chapter follows their caution rather than treating the finding as evidence for a specific basin-wide population figure, a point developed further in Section 23.11.

Section 23.7

The Llanos de Mojos and Southwestern Amazonia

The Llanos de Mojos, a vast seasonally flooded savanna region in the lowlands of present-day Bolivia, provides one of the clearest and most thoroughly documented case studies of large-scale Indigenous landscape engineering in Amazonia. Unlike the dense terra firme forest more commonly associated with popular images of "the Amazon," the Llanos de Mojos consists primarily of open grassland that floods extensively during the wet season and dries substantially during the dry season, creating a challenging but, as archaeological evidence demonstrates, highly manageable environment for agriculture and settlement.

Archaeological survey of the region has documented an extensive network of raised agricultural fields, constructed by digging parallel or grid-patterned canals and using the excavated soil to build elevated planting platforms above the seasonal flood line. This technique improved drainage during the wet season, concentrated fertile soil for cultivation, and, through the associated canal network, likely supported fish management and possibly aquaculture, extending food production beyond field crops alone. Alongside these raised fields, researchers have identified extensive causeway networks connecting settlements, fields, and resource areas, permitting travel across the savanna during flood periods that would otherwise isolate communities from one another and from their fields.

A particularly distinctive feature of the Llanos de Mojos landscape is the presence of numerous forest islands — patches of forest vegetation rising above the surrounding savanna, many of which show clear evidence of long-term human habitation, including terra preta soil deposits, pottery, and other cultural material. Researchers studying these forest islands have proposed that many were themselves at least partly anthropogenic, formed or substantially modified through centuries of human habitation, refuse accumulation, and deliberate planting of useful tree and shrub species atop naturally occurring elevated ground, blurring, much as the managed forest gardens discussed in Section 23.4 do, the line between "natural" and "constructed" landscape features.

The scale and interconnection of raised fields, causeways, canals, and forest-island settlements across the Llanos de Mojos indicate a substantial regional population engaged in sustained, coordinated landscape modification over an extended period, likely encompassing multiple distinct communities linked through shared engineering traditions, exchange, and, quite possibly, political or ceremonial relationships that archaeological evidence alone cannot fully reconstruct. This case study also creates an important point of comparison with the Zenú hydraulic systems examined in Chapter 22: both regions demonstrate that lowland tropical societies, working in seasonally flooded environments hundreds or even thousands of kilometers apart, independently developed broadly similar engineering solutions — raised fields and canal networks — to comparable environmental challenges, suggesting that such solutions represented effective, replicable responses to widely shared ecological conditions across the northern and western reaches of South America's tropical lowlands rather than the product of a single shared cultural tradition.

Recent lidar and archaeobotanical research has made the Casarabe culture of the southeastern Llanos de Mojos one of the clearest known examples of pre-Columbian Amazonian low-density urbanism. Between about AD 500 and 1400, Casarabe communities built a settlement system with at least four tiers of site size and importance, including two unusually large primary centers, generally identified by archaeologists as Cotoca and Landívar, surrounded by smaller secondary and tertiary settlements linked together through long, straight raised causeways and canals extending for several kilometers across the savanna. Airborne lidar survey has revealed monumental earthen platform architecture at these primary centers, including stepped pyramidal mounds reaching heights of roughly twenty-two meters, along with surrounding concentric earthworks, water-management features, and reservoirs integrated into the same engineered landscape already described earlier in this section. Archaeologists have described this settlement pattern as a form of "low-density urbanism": a dispersed, garden-city-like arrangement of monumental centers, satellite communities, agricultural land, and infrastructure spread across a wide area, rather than a single densely built city of the kind found in Mesoamerica or the Andes, but nonetheless functioning as an integrated urban-scale system.

Subsequent archaeobotanical research on the Casarabe culture has also usefully complicated this chapter's broader emphasis on manioc and mixed agroforestry as the typical foundation of Amazonian subsistence. Microbotanical evidence, particularly phytolith analyses from drained fields, farm ponds, and nearby forest settings, indicates that Casarabe communities relied heavily on intensive, year-round maize cultivation, supported by the same drainage canals and by constructed farm ponds integrated into the settlement landscape, suggesting a more specialized, grain-centered agricultural system than the diversified root-crop and tree-crop economies described in Section 23.4. This finding is a useful reminder that, just as this chapter has emphasized throughout, Amazonian societies did not follow a single uniform economic or agricultural model; the Casarabe case demonstrates that intensive, maize-centered farming could support substantial, engineered urban-scale settlement in the tropical lowlands, alongside the very different root-crop- and fishing-centered economies documented at Marajó, Santarém, and elsewhere in the basin.

Section 23.8

Marajó, Santarém, and the Lower Amazon

A second major regional case study concerns the societies that developed near the mouth of the Amazon River, where the river's enormous discharge, extensive floodplain, and rich aquatic resources supported some of the largest and most archaeologically visible populations documented anywhere in the pre-Columbian lowlands.

Marajó Island, a vast island roughly the size of Switzerland formed at the point where the Amazon meets the Atlantic, is associated with a mound-building tradition, generally referred to by archaeologists as the Marajoara tradition, that flourished for centuries before sustained European contact. Marajoara communities constructed substantial earthen mounds, some reaching considerable height and covering large areas, which served as elevated platforms for settlement, ceremonial activity, and burial, protecting inhabitants and structures from the island's extensive seasonal flooding. Marajoara ceramics, recovered in large quantities from mound sites, display elaborate painted and incised decoration and have been studied extensively for evidence of social status, ritual practice, and regional exchange relationships. The scale of mound construction, the elaboration of associated material culture, and the population density implied by settlement size have led archaeologists to describe Marajoara society as one of the more socially complex and politically organized societies documented in the Amazonian archaeological record, though the precise nature of Marajoara political organization — whether centralized chiefdoms, a looser confederation of related communities, or some other arrangement — remains a subject of ongoing scholarly interpretation rather than settled fact.

Further upriver, the Santarém region, situated at the confluence of the Tapajós and Amazon rivers, is associated with another substantial and archaeologically distinctive society, generally identified with the historical and archaeological label of the Tapajós people. Santarém-area sites have produced dense concentrations of pottery, extensive terra preta deposits indicating long-term settlement, and evidence of a well-organized regional exchange network extending along the Amazon and its tributaries. Early European accounts of the region, including testimony gathered during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, described a substantial and, in European estimation, formidable Indigenous population in the vicinity, an assessment broadly consistent with the archaeological evidence of dense, long-term settlement, even though such colonial-era accounts must, as this chapter emphasizes throughout, be read critically rather than accepted uncritically as precise demographic records.

The Lower Amazon case studies connect directly to the earliest and most extensively debated written European testimony regarding Amazonian settlement: the 1542 chronicle of Gaspar de Carvajal, examined in the Primary Source Window below. Carvajal's account of extensive, closely spaced riverside settlements along significant stretches of the Amazon, once widely dismissed as exaggeration, aligns in important respects with the archaeological evidence for dense várzea and confluence-zone settlement that has since been documented at Marajó, Santarém, and other Lower Amazon sites, even though the chronicle's precise claims about settlement scale and population size cannot be verified in detail and should not be treated as a reliable census.

Section 23.9

The Upper Xingu and Regional Settlement Networks

The Upper Xingu region of southeastern Amazonia offers a valuable and instructive contrast to the mound-building societies of the Lower Amazon and the raised-field societies of the Llanos de Mojos, because it demonstrates a different form of pre-Columbian social and spatial complexity: not a single large, centralized settlement or state, but a network of politically distinct, similarly sized communities connected through shared infrastructure, exchange, and ceremonial life.

Archaeological survey of the Upper Xingu has documented a series of substantial, carefully planned villages, many organized around large central plazas, with residential structures arranged in a ring or cluster pattern around the plaza's perimeter. These villages were connected to one another, and to surrounding agricultural land and resource areas, by an extensive network of wide, straight roads, some extending for several kilometers between settlements, along with smaller paths connecting villages to fields, water sources, and other resource areas. Some villages show evidence of surrounding ditches or embankments, which researchers have interpreted variously as boundary markers, defensive features, or components of a broader system for managing water, waste, or movement around the settlement, and it is likely that different features served different combinations of these functions at different sites.

The pattern of roughly similar-sized, evenly spaced villages connected by a shared road network, rather than a single dominant center surrounded by dependent satellite communities, has led archaeologists to describe the pre-Columbian Upper Xingu as organized into a regional settlement network characterized by a relatively decentralized, multi-community political structure rather than a single centralized polity comparable to the Marajoara mound centers or, at a much larger scale, the Mexica or Inca imperial states examined earlier in this volume. This does not mean Upper Xingu communities lacked social complexity or organized political life; rather, it demonstrates that social complexity in Amazonia, as elsewhere in the Indigenous Americas, could take multiple institutional forms, and that political centralization around a single dominant capital was neither the only, nor necessarily the most successful, pathway to organized, populous, infrastructure-building society in the region.

This regional network model also reinforces the broader argument, developed throughout Part V of this volume, that "complexity" and "empire" are not synonyms. The Upper Xingu communities built roads, plazas, and managed forest resources on a considerable scale without producing anything resembling the centralized tribute system, standing military apparatus, or single dominant ruling lineage associated with the Mexica Triple Alliance examined in Part III or Tawantinsuyu examined in Part IV. Students should carry this distinction forward into later chapters of this textbook, where similar questions about the relationship between social complexity and political centralization recur in other regional contexts.

Section 23.10

Rivers as Highways: Exchange, Language, and Regional Networks

A recurring theme throughout the preceding sections deserves direct and sustained attention: rivers functioned across Amazonia not as barriers separating isolated communities, but as highways connecting distant peoples, resources, and ideas. The Amazon River and its major tributaries — including the Madeira, Negro, Xingu, Tapajós, and Tocantins — together with the broader, ecologically related Orinoco system to the north, formed one of the most extensive navigable waterway networks on Earth, and Amazonian communities made extensive use of canoes, rafts, and other watercraft to move people, goods, and information across enormous distances far more efficiently than overland travel through dense forest would have permitted.

Archaeological evidence for this riverine exchange includes the widespread distribution of particular ceramic styles, decorative motifs, and manufacturing techniques across sites separated by hundreds of kilometers of river distance, the presence of stone tools and ornamental materials at sites far from their geological source, and the movement of foodstuffs, particularly dried fish and manioc products, along trade networks connecting resource-rich and resource-poor zones. Ritual objects, including specially crafted ceramics, feather ornaments, and other items associated with ceremonial or status display, likewise appear to have circulated across considerable distances through exchange networks connecting distinct communities and, in some cases, distinct linguistic and archaeological traditions.

Linguistic evidence offers a complementary, though methodologically distinct, line of evidence for long-distance interaction and population movement across Amazonia. Several major Indigenous language families — including Arawakan, Tupian, and Cariban languages, among others — are distributed across enormous, often discontinuous geographic ranges spanning thousands of kilometers, a pattern that linguists generally interpret as evidence of substantial historical population movement, migration, and, in some cases, the spread of particular linguistic communities along river corridors over many centuries. Arawakan languages, for example, are documented from the Caribbean, discussed in Chapter 21, through the Amazon basin and into parts of the Andean foothills, while Tupian languages are documented across much of the Lower and Central Amazon and extend into regions well beyond the basin itself.

Students must exercise particular caution when interpreting this linguistic evidence, however. Language family, archaeological culture, and political community are three distinct analytical categories, and historians and archaeologists should not automatically assume that all speakers of a given language family shared a single political identity, a single archaeological material culture, or a single historical experience. A shared language family indicates common linguistic ancestry and, generally, some degree of historical population movement or contact, but it does not by itself establish shared political organization, shared material culture, or a unified ethnic identity at any given moment in the past. The Arawakan-speaking peoples of the Caribbean and the Arawakan-speaking peoples of the Upper Amazon, for instance, developed along substantially different historical trajectories despite their shared linguistic ancestry, and treating linguistic evidence as though it mapped neatly onto political or cultural boundaries would seriously distort the historical record.

With this caution in mind, the overall picture that riverine exchange and linguistic evidence together support is one of extensive, long-term interconnection across Amazonia: distant communities exchanged goods, technologies, and, quite possibly, people through marriage and migration, and linguistic dispersal along major waterways indicates that population movement across enormous distances was a recurring feature of Amazonian history rather than an unusual exception. This interconnection helps explain both the wide distribution of shared technologies, such as raised-field agriculture and bitter manioc processing, and the significant regional variation in political and social organization documented across the case studies examined in Sections 23.7 through 23.9.

Section 23.11

How Many People Lived in Amazonia?

No question in the study of pre-Columbian Amazonia has generated more sustained scholarly disagreement than the question of total population at the time of sustained European contact in the sixteenth century. Published estimates for the Amazon basin as a whole have varied enormously, ranging from figures in the low hundreds of thousands to figures in the several millions, and this chapter does not attempt to resolve that range into a single settled number. Instead, students should understand why the range is so wide and what kinds of evidence and assumptions produce different estimates.

Several factors contribute to this persistent uncertainty. First, early colonial-era written testimony, including Gaspar de Carvajal's 1542 account discussed in the Primary Source Window below, offers impressionistic descriptions of settlement density rather than systematic censuses, and such accounts are difficult to translate into precise population figures even where historians judge them broadly credible. Second, the demographic catastrophe produced by introduced Eurasian and African diseases, examined in full in Chapter 29, likely struck many Amazonian populations before later European or North American observers had the opportunity to describe them directly, meaning that the comparatively sparse, dispersed populations documented by many nineteenth- and twentieth-century observers may reflect a post-epidemic demographic collapse rather than a stable, unchanging pre-Columbian baseline. Third, archaeological survey coverage remains uneven across the basin's enormous area, and newly identified sites, including additional terra preta deposits and earthwork complexes discovered through satellite imagery and other remote-sensing techniques, continue to expand the documented geographic extent of pre-Columbian settlement, suggesting that current archaeological knowledge likely undercounts the true extent of past habitation rather than overcounting it. A 2026 canopy-penetrating lidar survey of southwestern Amazonia, discussed in Section 23.6, illustrates this point clearly: by documenting more than twenty thousand earthworks and proposing a regional population of roughly 1.25 to 3 million people around AD 100 to 300 within an area covering less than three percent of Greater Amazonia, the study substantially raised confidence in dense occupation for that specific region, while its own authors cautioned against extrapolating the result to the basin as a whole without comparable survey coverage elsewhere. Fourth, scholars disagree about the theoretical question of carrying capacity — how many people a given Amazonian environment, whether unmodified terra firme forest, terra preta-enriched land, or engineered floodplain and savanna landscapes, could realistically sustain using pre-Columbian technology — and different assumptions about carrying capacity, applied to the same underlying archaeological evidence, can produce substantially different population estimates.

Given these uncertainties, the most defensible position available to historians at present is not a single population figure but a set of qualitative conclusions supported with reasonable confidence by the combined archaeological, soil-scientific, and documentary evidence examined throughout this chapter. Population density was almost certainly far higher, in absolute terms, along productive várzea floodplains, at river confluences such as Santarém, on resource-rich locations such as Marajó, and in engineered landscapes such as the Llanos de Mojos, than in more remote terra firme interior forest away from major waterways. Total basin-wide population was very likely substantially higher than the low estimates common in mid-twentieth-century scholarship, given the extent of documented terra preta deposits, earthworks, and settlement networks now identified across regions once assumed to have been sparsely inhabited. At the same time, students should resist the temptation to adopt the highest published estimates as though they were established fact, since these figures typically depend on extrapolations from limited survey data to the entire basin, extrapolations that remain contested among specialists and that future research may substantially revise in either direction.

This chapter's treatment of the population question therefore models, once again, the kind of careful historical reasoning introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of the peopling of the Americas: distinguishing well-supported qualitative conclusions from more speculative quantitative estimates, remaining alert to how a scholarly consensus can shift substantially as new evidence accumulates, and resisting the temptation to replace one oversimplified extreme — a nearly empty pre-Columbian Amazon — with an equally oversimplified opposite extreme, a uniformly and densely urbanized one.

Section 23.12

An Anthropogenic Forest before European Conquest

This chapter's evidence, considered together, supports a central and historically significant conclusion: substantial portions of the Amazonian landscape encountered by European observers beginning in the sixteenth century, and often assumed by later observers to be a purely natural, untouched environment, were in fact substantially shaped by thousands of years of Indigenous habitation, cultivation, soil modification, and forest management. Historical ecologists studying the distribution of economically useful tree species — including various fruit- and nut-bearing palms, cacao relatives, and other plants valuable for food, medicine, construction material, or fiber — have documented statistically significant concentrations of these species in areas with independent archaeological or soil evidence of past human occupation, a pattern difficult to explain except through centuries of deliberate human favoring, protection, and propagation of useful species within otherwise diverse forest ecosystems.

This body of evidence supports describing much of Amazonia's pre-Columbian forest as, in an important sense, a managed or anthropogenic landscape rather than untouched wilderness — a forest that had been shaped, over many centuries and by many distinct communities, through selective cultivation, soil enrichment, controlled use of fire in some regions and contexts, construction of earthworks and raised fields, and the deliberate concentration of useful plant and animal resources near settlements and travel routes. This does not mean that every corner of the Amazon basin was equally transformed; as this chapter has emphasized throughout, the intensity and visibility of human landscape modification varied enormously by region, correlating closely with the ecological factors — floodplain fertility, aquatic resource abundance, and proximity to major waterways — examined in Section 23.2.

Recognizing Amazonia as, in significant part, an anthropogenic landscape carries important historical consequences for how later chapters of this textbook should be read. When Chapter 29 examines the demographic catastrophe produced by epidemic disease following sustained European contact, and when Chapter 39 examines Portuguese colonization, missions, and bandeirante expansion into the Brazilian interior, students should understand that these later processes did not simply encounter and gradually alter a static, natural forest. They disrupted, and in many places effectively erased, a set of long-established, actively maintained Indigenous landscapes — abandoned fields, collapsing earthworks, and unmanaged forest gardens gradually reverting toward a wilder state that later European, and eventually scientific, observers then mistook for the region's original, permanent condition.

This chapter's conclusion should not be mistaken for a claim that Amazonian Indigenous history ended with European contact. Living Indigenous communities across the Amazon basin today continue to maintain agricultural, forest-management, and ecological knowledge systems that draw on, and in some cases directly continue, long historical traditions of the kind examined throughout this chapter, even though, as this chapter has cautioned at several points, modern Indigenous practices cannot simply be projected backward, unchanged, onto ancient societies without careful, evidence-based historical argument. By 1492, Amazonia was neither an untouched wilderness nor a single densely settled civilization. It was a vast mosaic of Indigenous landscapes in which rivers, forests, soils, fields, settlements, and human communities had shaped one another over thousands of years — a mosaic whose full extent archaeologists, historical ecologists, and historians continue, in the present day, to rediscover and reinterpret.

Primary Source Window

Gaspar de Carvajal on the Amazon, 1542

Creator: Gaspar de Carvajal, a Dominican friar accompanying the Spanish expedition of Francisco de Orellana · Date: Chronicle composed during and shortly after the expedition's 1541–1542 descent of the Amazon River · Type: Eyewitness expedition chronicle

Context: Orellana's expedition, separated from a larger Spanish force searching for cinnamon east of Quito, found itself compelled to follow a great river system to the Atlantic rather than returning upstream. Along the way, Carvajal recorded observations of the settlements, peoples, and landscapes the starving and often desperate expedition encountered from their boats.

Rather than reproducing extended passages verbatim, students should understand Carvajal's central claims in summary form: he described long stretches of riverbank lined with villages positioned close together, broad roads leading away from the water toward the interior, and, at certain points along the journey, what he characterized as unusually large and well-organized settlements whose inhabitants in some cases resisted the passing Spaniards with force.

Analysis: For centuries, most historians treated Carvajal's account as unreliable exaggeration, produced by desperate men eager to justify an unauthorized expedition or to magnify the dangers and wonders of an unfamiliar land. The archaeological evidence for dense várzea and confluence-zone settlement documented at Marajó, Santarém, and elsewhere along the Lower and Central Amazon, examined in Section 23.8, has led some historians to treat Carvajal's testimony with greater credibility regarding the general scale of riverside settlement, even though the chronicle's specific numbers and descriptions cannot be independently verified and should not be read as a precise census. Carvajal's account illustrates a broader historical-methods lesson: a source once dismissed because it conflicted with a later, more sparsely populated reality may, on reconsideration, provide genuine evidence of a demographic and settlement pattern that intervening centuries of epidemic disease and colonial disruption had already erased by the time most other European observers arrived.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a desperate, starving expedition have had reasons to exaggerate the dangers or wonders of the lands it passed through?
  2. Why might a desperate, hungry expedition also have had reasons to notice and accurately record large food-producing settlements along the riverbank?
  3. What kind of independent evidence would help historians evaluate whether Carvajal's descriptions were broadly accurate, exaggerated, or a mixture of both?
  4. Why does this chapter caution against treating Carvajal's account as a reliable population count, even where it accepts the account as broadly consistent with archaeological evidence?

Historical Debate

How Populated Was Pre-Columbian Amazonia?

The question: Given the archaeological evidence for terra preta, earthworks, and substantial riverine settlements examined throughout this chapter, how large was the total pre-Columbian population of the Amazon basin, and how confidently can historians and archaeologists answer that question?

Interpretation A — Amazonia supported a large, in places densely settled population, likely numbering in the several millions across the basin: Proponents of this view point to the wide geographic distribution of terra preta deposits, the scale of earthworks documented in Acre, the Llanos de Mojos, and the Upper Xingu, the substantial mound-building traditions at Marajó, and colonial-era testimony such as Carvajal's chronicle, arguing that these lines of evidence, taken together, indicate a basin-wide population considerably larger than mid-twentieth-century estimates allowed, concentrated especially along productive floodplains, confluences, and engineered landscapes.
Interpretation B — Amazonian populations, while locally substantial in favorable environments, remained considerably smaller in total and less densely settled across most of the basin's interior than the highest published estimates suggest: Proponents of this more cautious view argue that dramatic evidence from intensively surveyed regions such as the Llanos de Mojos or the Lower Amazon should not be extrapolated uncritically to the entire basin, much of which remains archaeologically unsurveyed or consists of terra firme interior forest with comparatively limited evidence of intensive habitation, and that basin-wide population extrapolations built on a small number of well-documented case studies risk substantially overstating the total figure.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of archaeological, soil-scientific, and documentary evidence discussed throughout this chapter, but differ in how far they are willing to extrapolate from intensively studied regions to the basin as a whole, and in how much weight they assign to colonial-era testimony such as Carvajal's chronicle.

Why it matters: The resolution of this debate affects how historians characterize the demographic scale of the Columbian Exchange's impact on Amazonia, examined in Chapter 29, and how researchers interpret the relationship between pre-Columbian land management and the forest composition observed by later scientists. This textbook does not adopt either interpretation as settled and instead presents both as reflecting genuine, ongoing disagreement among specialists working from an incomplete evidentiary record.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 23.5 through 23.11, identify what kind of additional archaeological or documentary evidence would most effectively narrow the range between these two interpretations, and explain why basin-wide extrapolation from regional case studies is inherently more uncertain than population estimates for a single, well-surveyed sub-region.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Same Landscape: Perspectives on Amazonia

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns found across scholarship and public discussion. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of a mid-twentieth-century environmental-determinist archaeologist: Tropical rainforest soils, once cleared, lose fertility quickly, and the small, mobile, low-density Indigenous societies documented by nineteenth- and twentieth-century observers reflect a fixed ecological ceiling on Amazonian social complexity rather than the aftermath of catastrophic demographic decline; claims of large pre-Columbian populations or complex earthworks should therefore be treated with considerable skepticism absent direct, extensive excavation.
From the perspective of a contemporary historical ecologist: Terra preta soils, anomalous concentrations of useful tree species, and extensive earthworks across widely separated regions of the basin together indicate that substantial portions of what earlier researchers described as "natural" Amazonian forest were, in fact, actively managed and transformed landscapes, and that the sparse populations documented by later observers likely reflect demographic collapse following European contact rather than a stable, unchanging pre-Columbian baseline.
From the perspective of a contemporary Indigenous community member whose territory includes terra preta soils and forest gardens: Knowledge of which plants to favor, which soils to cultivate, and how to manage the surrounding forest has been passed down through generations, and archaeological findings confirming the antiquity and scale of such practices affirm what many communities have long understood about their own relationship to the land, even as outside researchers continue to debate the precise scale and organization of ancient Amazonian societies.
From the perspective of a sixteenth-century Spanish chronicler descending the Amazon: The riverbanks appeared, at certain points along the journey, to be lined with village after village and crossed by broad roads leading inland, sights that seemed to promise wealth and resources but that also, given the expedition's desperate circumstances and limited time on shore, could be described only briefly and from the vantage point of passing boats rather than through sustained investigation.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Forest Management Before and After Conquest

What Changed

  • Epidemic disease and colonial disruption, examined fully in Chapters 29 and 39, produced severe population decline across much of Amazonia beginning in the sixteenth century, reducing the labor and social organization needed to maintain earthworks, raised fields, and managed forest gardens
  • Many actively cultivated and managed landscapes, once abandoned, gradually reverted toward a less intensively managed state, contributing to the later impression of an untouched wilderness
  • Later European, and eventually scientific, observers frequently encountered smaller, more dispersed, and more mobile Indigenous communities than the settlement patterns documented archaeologically for the centuries immediately preceding contact
  • Scholarly understanding of Amazonian history has itself changed dramatically, from environmental-limitation models to the more complex, still-evolving picture presented in this chapter

What Stayed the Same

  • Terra preta soils, once formed, have generally remained fertile and, in many locations, continue to support cultivation by contemporary communities centuries after their original formation
  • Manioc cultivation and processing, along with fishing, have remained central to Amazonian subsistence across many centuries and into the present
  • Rivers have continued to serve as the primary highways of transportation, exchange, and communication across the basin from the pre-Columbian period into the present day
  • Indigenous peoples have remained continuous historical actors across the region, adapting their knowledge and practices to changing demographic, political, and environmental circumstances rather than disappearing after conquest

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the disruption caused by conquest and the underlying continuities in land, knowledge, and practice helps students avoid two errors: assuming that everything visible in the modern Amazon reflects an unbroken, unchanged pre-Columbian past, and assuming that conquest simply erased Indigenous Amazonian history rather than transforming and, in important respects, interrupting it.

Cause and Effect

How Settlement, Charcoal, Waste, and Cultivation Produced Dark Earths

Long-term causes

The naturally low fertility of many terra firme soils once forest cover is cleared, combined with sustained, multi-generational human settlement at particular favorable locations near rivers, lakes, and travel routes.

Short-term causes

Repeated, cumulative deposition of hearth ash, incompletely burned charcoal, broken pottery, food remains, and other household waste at and around settlement sites over years and decades, combined, at least at some sites, with deliberate incorporation of charcoal and organic matter into garden soil to improve its fertility.

Effect

The transformation of naturally poor soil into terra preta: dark, carbon-enriched, highly fertile earth capable of sustaining cultivation far more effectively than surrounding unmodified soil.

Historical significance

Terra preta demonstrates that Amazonian societies transformed their environment through long-term settlement and, in at least some cases, deliberate soil management, directly challenging assumptions that tropical lowland environments imposed a fixed ecological ceiling on population and social complexity.

Historical complexity: Scholars continue to debate how much of terra preta's formation resulted from deliberate soil engineering versus the incidental byproduct of ordinary settlement and waste accumulation; research comparing ancient deposits with present-day Kuikuro soil-enrichment practices has strengthened the case for intentional management at some sites, but this chapter presents both intentional and incidental processes as contributing causes rather than resolving their relative importance.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in Amazonian Riverine and Forest Communities

Behind the large-scale earthworks, soil transformations, and settlement networks examined throughout this chapter lay the ordinary daily rhythms of Amazonian households and communities. Daily and seasonal life for many riverine communities centered on the rise and fall of the annual flood cycle: planting, fishing, and travel patterns shifted considerably between the high-water and low-water seasons, and households likely maintained multiple, complementary subsistence strategies — manioc gardens, fruit and palm groves, fishing, and hunting — that could be adjusted according to seasonal availability and household need rather than relying on a single dominant food source.

Processing bitter manioc, described in Section 23.4, was almost certainly a significant and often collective household or community task, requiring coordinated labor for grating, pressing, and cooking, and archaeological and ethnographic evidence from related contemporary practices suggests that such food-processing work, along with pottery-making, fishing, and craft production, likely involved distinct, though not necessarily rigid, divisions of labor within Amazonian households and communities. Village life in planned settlements such as those of the Upper Xingu, organized around central plazas, likely combined household-level subsistence activity with communal ceremonial, social, and political gatherings held in or near the plaza space, though the precise details of gender roles, kinship organization, and social hierarchy within specific pre-Columbian Amazonian communities remain difficult to reconstruct with confidence from archaeological evidence alone and are better documented, with appropriate caution about projecting backward in time, through ethnographic study of related contemporary Indigenous communities.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined one of the most significantly revised areas of Indigenous American history: the settlement, agriculture, and environmental transformation of Amazonia and the wider tropical lowlands before sustained European conquest. It began by tracing the decline of the older "pristine Amazon" model, which held that poor tropical soils and a resource-poor forest environment placed a fixed ceiling on Amazonian population and social complexity, and explained how soil science, archaeology, and historical ecology have together produced a considerably more complex picture over the past several decades.

Students have learned to distinguish the seasonally flooded, nutrient-rich várzea floodplain from the higher, less naturally fertile terra firme upland, and to understand why this ecological distinction shaped patterns of settlement density across the basin. The chapter traced the long-term development of Amazonian food systems, from early foraging through the gradual domestication and cultivation of manioc, maize, and other plants, emphasizing the technological sophistication of bitter manioc processing and the way Amazonian subsistence strategies often blurred the boundary between cultivated fields and managed forest resources.

The chapter examined Amazonian Dark Earths, or terra preta, as some of the most significant evidence available for long-term Indigenous transformation of tropical soils, while cautioning that the balance between deliberate engineering and incidental accumulation in terra preta's formation remains an active scholarly question. It then surveyed the diverse array of earthworks documented across the basin — geoglyphs, raised fields, causeways, and mounds — through three major regional case studies: the raised-field and causeway landscapes of the Llanos de Mojos in southwestern Amazonia; the mound-building traditions of Marajó and the dense riverine settlements of Santarém in the Lower Amazon; and the planned village networks and road systems of the Upper Xingu, which demonstrated that social complexity in Amazonia could take decentralized, multi-community forms rather than requiring a single centralized political capital.

Students examined the role of rivers as highways of exchange, migration, and linguistic dispersal, tracing the wide distribution of major language families such as Arawakan, Tupian, and Cariban across the basin, while learning to distinguish language family, archaeological culture, and political community as separate analytical categories that should not be automatically conflated. The chapter then addressed directly the contested question of Amazonian population size, explaining why estimates vary so widely and presenting the range of scholarly opinion without adopting a single figure as settled fact, while introducing Gaspar de Carvajal's 1542 chronicle of the Orellana expedition as an important, though carefully qualified, piece of documentary evidence bearing on that debate.

Finally, the chapter synthesized its evidence around the concept of Amazonia as, in significant part, an anthropogenic landscape — a forest and river system whose plant distributions, soils, and settlement patterns bore the mark of thousands of years of Indigenous habitation and management, disrupted but not entirely erased by the demographic catastrophe and colonial disruption examined in later chapters. Throughout, the chapter has emphasized regional diversity over uniformity, cautioned against both the "untouched wilderness" and "giant lost city" extremes, and modeled the kind of careful, evidence-based historical reasoning about incomplete and evolving evidence introduced earlier in this volume.

Chapter 23 has asked how Amazonian peoples shaped, and were shaped by, one of the world's most ecologically distinctive regions. Chapter 24 turns southward to the Mapuche-speaking societies of south-central Chile, the Guaraní world, the Río de la Plata and pampas, Patagonia, and other Indigenous societies of southern South America, before stepping back to examine the broader Indigenous American world around 1492, completing this volume's survey of Indigenous Latin America before the narrative turns, in Volume II, to the Atlantic encounter and the beginning of conquest and colonization.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Amazonia
The vast river basin and tropical forest region drained principally by the Amazon River and its tributaries, spanning parts of eight sovereign countries, with broader definitions of Amazonia sometimes also including French Guiana.
Várzea
Seasonally flooded floodplain bordering the Amazon River and larger tributaries, enriched annually by nutrient-rich sediment.
Terra firme
Higher-elevation forest above the seasonal flood line, generally possessing less naturally fertile soil than várzea.
Terra preta
Amazonian Dark Earth; unusually fertile, carbon-rich anthropogenic soil formed through long-term human habitation.
Anthropogenic landscape
An environment substantially shaped by human activity rather than representing an untouched natural state.
Historical ecology
The scholarly field examining how past human societies shaped and were shaped by their environments, central to this chapter's reinterpretation of Amazonia.
Manioc (cassava, yuca)
A starchy root crop central to Amazonian food systems, cultivated in both sweet and toxic "bitter" varieties requiring specialized processing.
Bitter manioc processing
The multi-step technique of grating, pressing, and cooking toxic manioc varieties to remove cyanogenic compounds before consumption.
Raised field
An elevated planting platform built using soil excavated from surrounding canals, improving drainage and concentrating fertility in seasonally flooded environments.
Geoglyph
A large geometric earthen enclosure, especially documented in Acre, formed by ditches and embankments and revealed in many cases by modern deforestation.
Causeway
A raised earthen roadway allowing travel across seasonally flooded terrain, documented extensively in the Llanos de Mojos.
Forest island
A patch of forest vegetation rising above surrounding savanna, in some cases substantially shaped by long-term human habitation.
Mound-building society
A society, such as the Marajoara tradition of Marajó Island, that constructed substantial earthen mounds for settlement, ceremony, and burial.
Riverine network
The interconnected system of rivers functioning as the primary transportation and exchange corridors of Amazonia.
Language family
A group of languages descended from a common ancestral language, such as Arawakan, Tupian, or Cariban, distinct from archaeological culture or political community.
Linguistic dispersal
The historical spread of a language family across a wide geographic area, generally interpreted as evidence of past population movement.
Settlement mosaic
A pattern of regionally varied settlement density and organization, avoiding the extremes of uniform emptiness or uniform dense urbanization.
Carrying capacity
The theoretical maximum population an environment can sustain using a given technology, a contested variable in Amazonian population estimates.
Environmental determinism
The now largely revised view that environmental conditions alone fixed the upper limit of Amazonian population and social complexity.
Ethnohistory
The historical study of Indigenous peoples using a combination of archaeological, documentary, linguistic, and oral evidence.
Chronicle
A narrative eyewitness or near-contemporary written account, such as Gaspar de Carvajal's record of the Orellana expedition.
LIDAR survey
A remote-sensing technique using laser measurement to map ground surface features, including earthworks, beneath forest canopy.
Population estimate
A scholarly calculation of past population size, subject to significant uncertainty and revision as new evidence emerges.
Forest management
Deliberate human favoring, protection, or propagation of useful plant species within forest ecosystems.
Confluence
The point where two rivers meet, often associated with especially productive and densely settled locations such as Santarém.
Biochar
Charcoal-based soil amendment inspired partly by scientific study of terra preta's fertility-enhancing properties.
Marajoara tradition
The archaeological mound-building and ceramic tradition associated with Marajó Island at the mouth of the Amazon.
Tapajós people
The historical and archaeological label associated with the substantial society documented in the Santarém region of the Lower Amazon.
Regional settlement network
A pattern of similarly sized, politically distinct communities connected through shared infrastructure and exchange, exemplified by the Upper Xingu.
Agroforestry
A land-use system combining cultivated crops with managed tree and forest resources, characteristic of much Amazonian subsistence.
Demographic collapse
The severe population decline produced by epidemic disease and colonial disruption following sustained European contact, examined further in Chapters 29 and 39.
Low-density urbanism
A dispersed, garden-city-like settlement pattern combining monumental centers, satellite communities, and integrated infrastructure spread across a wide area, documented for the Casarabe culture of the Llanos de Mojos.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to reconsider one of the most persistent misconceptions in the study of the Indigenous Americas: the image of Amazonia as an untouched, timeless wilderness populated only by small, scattered bands with minimal impact on their surroundings. The evidence examined throughout this chapter — terra preta soils, raised fields and causeways, geoglyphs, mound-building traditions, planned village networks, and the wide-ranging exchange systems carried along the basin's rivers — demonstrates instead that substantial portions of Amazonia were actively shaped, over thousands of years, by Indigenous societies whose achievements had gone largely unrecognized by earlier generations of scholars working from a narrower, more environmentally deterministic model of what tropical lowland societies could accomplish.

This chapter has also modeled, once again, a form of historical reasoning introduced earlier in this volume: the willingness to revise a long-accepted scholarly consensus in light of new evidence, while avoiding the temptation to replace one oversimplified extreme with another. Amazonia was neither an empty wilderness nor a single unified civilization, and the honest, evidence-based picture — a mosaic of regionally diverse societies, unevenly documented by archaeology, whose true scale and population remain the subject of active scholarly debate — is more historically valuable, if less immediately dramatic, than either alternative.

The chapter has also reinforced this volume's broader argument that Indigenous Latin America extended, in varied and regionally specific forms, well beyond the great imperial cores of the Mexica and the Inca examined in earlier Parts. The Llanos de Mojos, Marajó, Santarém, and the Upper Xingu each developed distinctive institutional and engineering solutions to the challenges and opportunities of their particular environments, without producing anything resembling a centralized territorial empire, and their achievements deserve to be understood on their own historical terms rather than measured solely against a Mesoamerican or Andean template of what "civilization" should look like.

As this volume moves next to Chapter 24 and the Indigenous societies of the southern Americas on the eve of conquest, students should carry forward the central lesson of this chapter: that the absence of monumental stone architecture, written script, or a single dominant imperial capital does not indicate an absence of historical complexity, environmental sophistication, or social organization. Amazonia's rivers, soils, and forests bear the mark of that complexity to this day, awaiting continued rediscovery by archaeologists, historical ecologists, and the Indigenous communities whose own knowledge of the land remains an essential part of understanding it.
