Paracas, Nazca, and Moche

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Distinct Worlds on a Desert Coast

Along the narrow strip of desert that separates the Pacific Ocean from the western slopes of the Andes, a handful of rivers descending from the highlands cut through some of the driest land on Earth, creating isolated green corridors of settled life surrounded on every side by sand. In these river valleys, over many centuries, coastal Andean peoples wove some of the most technically accomplished textiles in world history, created immense designs and pathways across the desert pavement, some of whose overall forms are most easily appreciated from elevated viewpoints, and built platform temples whose painted friezes still depict processions, combat, and ritual sacrifice.

Chapter 16 traced the deep foundations of Andean civilization, from the monumental centers of Norte Chico and Caral through the widely shared religious imagery associated with Chavín de Huántar. That chapter described an interaction sphere — a set of shared ideas, images, and ritual practices that moved across long distances during the first millennium BCE, connecting distant communities without producing a single unified state. This chapter turns to what happened after that shared Chavín-era horizon began to fragment: coastal societies on the south and north coasts of what is today Peru developed increasingly distinct political, religious, artistic, and economic traditions of their own.

This chapter examines three archaeologically defined traditions — Paracas, Nazca, and Moche — that flourished, in overlapping and sometimes only partially sequential periods, along the desert littoral of Peru during the second half of the first millennium BCE and the first millennium CE. Paracas communities, associated above all with the Ica and Pisco valleys of the south coast, are known chiefly through spectacular funerary textiles preserved by the extreme dryness of the coastal desert. Nazca society, which developed in the Río Grande de Nazca drainage somewhat later than Paracas but drew on shared regional traditions, is famous for its polychrome pottery and for the enormous geoglyphs etched into the desert pavement — the so-called Nazca Lines. Moche society, flourishing in river valleys of the north coast at broadly the same time as Nazca society flourished in the south, built some of the largest adobe platform mounds in the ancient Americas and produced ceramic vessels whose painted and modeled imagery depicts warfare, ritual combat, captivity, and sacrifice in extraordinary narrative detail.

It is essential that students not read Paracas, Nazca, and Moche as three sequential chapters in a single continuous story, as though one society simply gave way to the next like pages turning in a book. Paracas and early Nazca material substantially overlap in time and, in some tombs, even occur together. Nazca society on the south coast and Moche society on the north coast developed at broadly the same time, hundreds of kilometers apart, shaped by different rivers, different neighbors, and different historical circumstances. Treating them as a tidy chronological sequence — Paracas, then Nazca, then Moche — would misrepresent the actual archaeological record and would obscure one of this chapter's central lessons: that the coastal Andes was not a single evolving civilization but a mosaic of related, regionally distinct societies responding in different ways to a shared, demanding desert environment.

This chapter also asks students to handle several categories of evidence with particular care. Much of what survives from Paracas comes from spectacular but incompletely documented early twentieth-century excavations of cemeteries, meaning that modern archaeologists must reconstruct social and political life largely from textiles, ceramics, and human remains removed from their full excavation context. Much of what is known about Nazca ritual life comes from a single very large ceremonial center, Cahuachi, whose exact political role remains debated. And much of what is popularly known about Nazca comes from the geoglyphs themselves, images so large that they are difficult to interpret with confidence and have attracted more speculative, non-scholarly explanation than perhaps any other ancient American archaeological feature. Moche art presents its own interpretive challenge: a rich narrative visual tradition depicting warriors, captives, and ritual specialists that historians must learn to read as evidence of ideology and ritual practice, not as a literal documentary record of specific historical events.

Finally, this chapter insists that the political organization of Paracas, Nazca, and especially Moche society should not be assumed in advance. Older popular accounts sometimes describe "the Moche Empire" as though it were a centralized state comparable to the later Inca empire examined in Chapter 20. Current archaeological scholarship is considerably more cautious, emphasizing regional Moche centers whose political relationships to one another remain a subject of active investigation rather than settled fact. Learning to hold open, evidence-based judgment about political organization — rather than assuming that impressive monumental architecture and elaborate art automatically imply centralized empire — is one of the central historical reasoning skills this chapter develops.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Paracas, Nazca, and Moche not as three stages of one coastal civilization advancing toward later Andean empires, but as three distinct, regionally rooted societies whose textiles, geoglyphs, irrigation systems, temples, ritual practices, and art reveal different solutions to the challenges and opportunities of life on one of the driest inhabited coastlines on Earth — a regional diversification that sets the stage for the very different highland systems of interregional integration examined in Chapter 18, Wari and Tiwanaku.

  3. Analyze Paracas textile production, iconography, and funerary bundles as evidence for social differentiation, ritual belief, and labor organization.

Historical Context

Chapter 16 established that Andean civilization developed through a long sequence of monumental construction, agricultural intensification, and religious interaction beginning with Norte Chico and Caral in the third millennium BCE and continuing through the widely shared Chavín-era religious imagery of the first millennium BCE. That chapter emphasized that Chavín de Huántar functioned less as the center of a unified state than as the hub of an interaction sphere: a network along which particular religious images, ritual practices, and prestige goods traveled across long distances, linking otherwise distinct highland and coastal communities without erasing their local political independence.

This chapter picks up the story as that shared Chavín-era horizon weakened during the last centuries BCE and gave way to a period of much greater regional differentiation. On the south coast of Peru, in the valleys around modern Ica, Pisco, and Paracas, communities that archaeologists group under the label "Paracas" developed increasingly distinctive textile traditions, funerary practices, and settlement patterns. Farther south, in the Río Grande de Nazca drainage, related communities developed the ceramic and ceremonial traditions archaeologists call "Nazca." On the north coast, in the valleys of the Moche, Chicama, and Lambayeque rivers, an entirely separate set of communities developed the monumental adobe architecture and narrative art associated with "Moche" society. These north-coast and south-coast worlds developed largely independently of one another, connected mainly by more general, long-distance Andean networks of exchange and shared religious symbolism rather than by direct, sustained political contact.

Students should understand that "Paracas," "Nazca," and "Moche" are, in each case, archaeological labels — names assigned by twentieth-century researchers to bodies of material culture identified at particular sites, not names that these ancient communities used for themselves or evidence of a single self-conscious ethnic or political identity. The label "Paracas" derives from the Paracas Peninsula, where the pioneering Peruvian archaeologist Julio C. Tello excavated large cemeteries in the 1920s. The label "Nazca" derives from the Nazca River valley. The label "Moche" derives from the Moche River valley near the northern coastal city of Trujillo, where the massive platform mounds called Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna are located. None of these labels should be read as implying a single unified kingdom, a single language community, or a single centralized government; each covers what was very likely a shifting mosaic of related communities, elites, and local polities sharing broadly similar material culture and, in some cases, ritual belief.

The environmental setting is essential to understanding all three traditions. The Peruvian coastal desert is among the driest inhabited regions on Earth, receiving negligible rainfall in most years. Dense agricultural settlement concentrated especially along rivers descending from Andean glaciers and highland rainfall, which cut through the desert and created narrow bands of irrigable land along their courses. Coastal peoples also exploited marine resources, groundwater, and fog-oasis, or lomas, environments beyond the main river valleys, meaning that river irrigation, while central to this chapter's story of dense agricultural settlement, was not the only basis for human occupation of the coastal desert. Coastal communities depended on careful management of this scarce water through canals, and, in some regions examined later in this chapter, more elaborate subterranean water-collection systems. This environmental dependence on irrigation shaped labor organization, settlement patterns, and, very plausibly, the development of political and religious authority capable of coordinating water management, though historians must be cautious about assuming a single, simple causal relationship between irrigation and the rise of centralized power.

Section 17.1

Coastal Andes after Chavín: Regional Worlds, Not One Successor Civilization

By the last centuries BCE, the shared religious imagery and long-distance exchange networks associated with the Chavín interaction sphere, examined in Chapter 16, had begun to weaken. In their place emerged a period of pronounced regional differentiation along the Peruvian coast, during which communities in different river valleys developed increasingly distinctive textile styles, ceramic traditions, funerary customs, and monumental architecture. This chapter examines three of the best documented of these regional traditions: Paracas and Nazca, both associated with the south coast, and Moche, associated with the north coast.

It is essential to establish the basic chronological and geographic relationships among these three traditions before examining any of them in detail. Paracas material is generally dated from roughly 700 BCE to 100 CE, making it the earliest of the three and placing much of its development within, rather than simply after, the broader Chavín-era horizon. Nazca material generally dates from roughly 100 to 700 CE, developing on the same south coast as Paracas but chronologically later, in the same general valleys and drawing on shared regional traditions. Moche material generally dates from roughly 200 to 850 CE — meaning that Moche society on the north coast was substantially contemporaneous with Nazca society on the south coast, not its successor. These three traditions therefore should not be pictured as beads on a single string, one following the next; Paracas and Nazca overlap at their boundary, and Nazca and Moche overlap almost entirely, separated not by time so much as by hundreds of kilometers of desert coastline and by entirely different river systems.

The geographic separation matters as much as the chronological overlap. The south coast, drained by the Ica, Pisco, and Nazca river systems, is separated from the north coast, drained by the Moche, Chicama, Lambayeque, and other rivers, by a very long stretch of coastline with few major rivers of its own. Communities in these two regions maintained some long-distance contact — through highland routes, maritime exchange, and the broader circulation of Andean religious ideas — but they developed largely independent political histories, artistic conventions, and burial customs. A student who assumes that "coastal Peru" functioned as a single, unified cultural zone during this period will misunderstand both the south-coast and north-coast material examined in this chapter.

Section 17.2

Paracas: Life on the South Coast

The label "Paracas" was assigned by archaeologists to the material culture that Julio C. Tello and his collaborators uncovered on the Paracas Peninsula and in the adjoining Ica and Pisco valleys beginning in the 1920s. It is important for students to understand that "Paracas" names a body of material culture and a set of related communities identified archaeologically, not a known unified kingdom with a single ruler or capital. The peninsula itself, though it lends its name to the tradition, was likely used primarily for burial and ceremonial purposes rather than as the center of everyday settlement; most Paracas communities lived and farmed in the irrigated valleys nearby.

Paracas communities practiced irrigated agriculture along the narrow, river-fed valleys of the south coast, cultivating crops such as maize, beans, squash, cotton, and other plants suited to the region's arid conditions, supplemented by marine resources drawn from the rich cold-water fisheries of the adjacent Pacific coast. Settlement was organized around valley-bottom communities linked by exchange networks that moved agricultural produce, marine products, textiles, and other goods between coast and highland. Craft production, and especially textile production examined in the next section, appears to have been an important and highly developed specialization within Paracas society, requiring the coordination of skilled labor over long periods of time.

Evidence for Paracas political organization is more limited than evidence for its material and ritual culture, in part because much of what survives comes from cemetery excavations rather than from extensively documented residential or administrative sites. Archaeologists generally describe Paracas society as organized into ranked local and regional communities of varying size and wealth, integrated by shared ritual practices, exchange, and probably kinship, rather than as a single centralized state; the precise degree of political integration among these communities remains uncertain given the limits of the surviving evidence. Differences in the wealth, size, and elaboration of burials — examined in the following section — provide the strongest evidence for social ranking within Paracas communities, suggesting a society with meaningful status distinctions but not necessarily a single overarching political authority controlling the entire south coast.

Section 17.3

Paracas Textiles, Mortuary Bundles, and Social Difference

Paracas is known above all for extraordinary textiles, preserved in unusually complete condition by the extreme aridity of the coastal desert, which prevents the decay that destroys organic materials in most other archaeological environments. This preservation gives historians a body of evidence — cloth, thread, dye, and needlework — that is exceptionally rare for any ancient society anywhere in the world, and it allows Paracas textile production to serve as this chapter's primary case study in using material culture, rather than written or oral testimony, to reconstruct labor, status, and belief.

Paracas weavers worked primarily with cotton, grown in the irrigated valleys, and with camelid fiber obtained from llamas, alpacas, or related animals through highland exchange, since camelids were herded chiefly in higher-altitude zones rather than on the coast itself. Textile production involved spinning, weaving, and, distinctively, extensive embroidery — the addition of elaborate figural and geometric designs stitched onto a woven base cloth using colored threads. Achieving the vivid, durable colors of Paracas textiles required sophisticated knowledge of organic dyestuffs and mordants, including plant-derived red and blue colorants and, in some examples, cochineal, a technical tradition distinct from the mineral-based pigments more closely associated with Paracas ceramic painting. The iconography embroidered onto Paracas textiles frequently depicts supernatural or composite beings — figures combining human, feline, avian, and serpentine features — along with plants, marine creatures, and ritual specialists, imagery that most scholars connect to religious belief and cosmology rather than to purely decorative design.

The most famous body of Paracas textile evidence comes from elaborate mortuary bundles: the deceased, wrapped in layer upon layer of finely woven and embroidered cloth, sometimes accompanied by ceramics, feathered ornaments, and other grave goods, and interred in dry underground chambers whose conditions allowed remarkable preservation. The scale of some of these bundles — requiring enormous quantities of finished cloth, itself the product of very substantial hours of skilled labor — provides strong evidence for meaningful social differentiation within Paracas communities. Individuals interred with unusually large numbers of elaborately decorated textiles almost certainly held higher social status, greater wealth, or more significant ritual roles than those buried more simply, though historians must be cautious about inferring the precise nature of Paracas political office, since no accompanying written or oral record explains exactly what social positions these individuals held.

Students should also resist treating these textiles merely as museum objects to be admired for their beauty. Each mantle, tunic, or wrapping represents an enormous investment of human time and specialized skill — by some scholarly estimates, thousands of hours of labor for the most elaborate examples — carried out within households and communities whose social organization we can only partially reconstruct. The people who spun the thread, prepared the dyes, wove the base cloth, and embroidered the final designs remain almost entirely unnamed and undocumented as individuals, even as their collective skill produced some of the most technically accomplished textile art in world history.

Historical Vocabulary
Huaca

In Andean usage, a huaca (sometimes spelled waka) refers to a sacred place, object, ancestor, or being imbued with supernatural power or significance. Archaeologists have adopted the term as a conventional label for many monumental adobe or stone platform structures along the Peruvian coast — most famously Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna near the Moche River, examined in Section 17.10 — even though the term's original Andean meaning is considerably broader than "monumental building." Students should understand that a huaca could be a mountain, a spring, an unusual rock formation, a mummified ancestor, or a constructed temple; the term reflects an Andean understanding of sacred power embedded in the landscape and in particular objects and places, a concept that long predates and long outlasted any single archaeological tradition examined in this chapter.

Section 17.4

Paracas, Topará, and the Emergence of Nazca Traditions

Popular accounts sometimes describe Paracas as simply "becoming" Nazca, as though one population were smoothly and completely replaced by its cultural descendants at a clean chronological boundary. The archaeological record does not support so tidy a narrative. During the last centuries BCE and the first century CE, south-coast tombs and settlements show material associated with several overlapping traditions — continuing Paracas-style textiles and ceramics, a distinct tradition archaeologists label Topará, associated particularly with the Chincha and Pisco valleys, and emerging early Nazca ceramic styles — sometimes appearing together within the same burial context rather than in a clean, single-tradition sequence.

Topará is important precisely because it complicates older, simpler narratives sometimes summarized under the label "Paracas Necropolis," an early twentieth-century term for a particular cemetery and associated burial style on the Paracas Peninsula. More recent archaeological analysis has shown that Topará represents a materially and geographically distinct tradition rather than simply a late phase of Paracas itself, and that Topará-related ceramics and other material appear alongside both continuing Paracas-style textiles and emerging early Nazca ceramic forms in the archaeological record of this transitional period. This means that the "boundary" between Paracas and Nazca is better understood as an extended zone of overlap, regional variation, and coexistence than as a single sharp break.

This case offers an important lesson in archaeological method. Ceramic styles, textile traditions, and burial customs are material patterns that archaeologists use to define cultural "phases" or "traditions" for analytical convenience; they do not necessarily correspond to clean, self-conscious ethnic boundaries recognized by the people who produced them. A community on the south coast during this transitional period likely did not think of itself as "becoming Nazca" or "ceasing to be Paracas." Students should therefore treat "Paracas," "Topará," and "early Nazca" as overlapping analytical categories describing a continuous, regionally varied process of cultural change, rather than as three distinct peoples who succeeded one another in strict sequence.

Section 17.5

Nazca Society and Cahuachi

Nazca society developed in the Río Grande de Nazca drainage, a system of several rivers descending from the Andes into the south-coast desert south of the Paracas-associated valleys. Nazca communities, like their Paracas predecessors and contemporaries, depended on irrigated agriculture along narrow river-fed valleys, supplemented by highland exchange and, to a lesser degree than on the immediate coast, marine resources. Settlement in the Nazca drainage was generally dispersed among numerous small to medium-sized communities distributed along the various tributary valleys rather than concentrated in one dominant city.

Within this dispersed settlement pattern, one site stood out dramatically in scale and apparent significance: Cahuachi, a large complex of earthen mounds, plazas, and associated structures located along the Nazca River. Cahuachi has produced some of the richest evidence for Nazca ceremonial life, including large quantities of fine polychrome pottery, textiles, and evidence of feasting activity, and it is generally regarded by archaeologists as the most important ceremonial center of the Nazca world during the early centuries of the first millennium CE.

Students should resist the common temptation to describe Cahuachi simply as "the capital of the Nazca Empire." The evidence for permanent, large-scale residential occupation at Cahuachi is considerably weaker than the evidence for periodic ceremonial use, leading many archaeologists to interpret the site principally as a pilgrimage and gathering center — a place where dispersed Nazca communities converged periodically for religious ceremony, feasting, and the exchange of goods and information, rather than as the administrative seat of a centralized state controlling the entire drainage. This interpretation does not deny that Cahuachi held real religious and social importance, nor that its ceremonial activity required considerable organization and resources; it simply cautions against assuming that ceremonial centrality is the same thing as political centralization. Whether any individual or institution at Cahuachi held political authority over other Nazca communities, and if so how extensive that authority was, remains an open question that current evidence does not resolve with confidence.

Section 17.6

The Nazca Lines and the Ritual Landscape

Among the most famous archaeological features anywhere in the Americas are the enormous geoglyphs etched into the desert pavement of the Nazca and neighboring Palpa valleys — figures and lines popularly known as the Nazca Lines. These images range from simple straight lines running for kilometers across the desert floor to large geometric trapezoids and spirals to detailed figurative representations of animals, plants, and other forms, some measuring hundreds of meters across. They were created by removing the darker, oxidized surface stones of the desert pavement to expose the lighter subsoil beneath, a technique requiring careful planning but relatively simple tools, and one whose effects, in the extremely dry and stable desert environment, have persisted for many centuries.

It is important for students to understand that the geoglyphs were not created in a single episode by a single generation. Current scholarship, reflected in their designation as a UNESCO World Heritage site spanning both Paracas and Nazca periods, dates geoglyph construction across several centuries, from roughly 500 BCE into the first millennium CE — meaning that both Paracas-period and Nazca-period communities contributed to this ritual landscape over a very long span of time, adding new images and lines to a desert surface already marked by earlier generations' work.

This chapter treats the Nazca Lines as a major historical problem rather than as a spectacle to be marveled at uncritically or explained through unsupported speculation. Because the overall forms of some large figurative geoglyphs are most easily appreciated from elevated or aerial vantage points, they have attracted a long history of sensational, non-scholarly explanation, including unsupported claims involving extraterrestrial visitors. Such claims have no credible archaeological basis and should be firmly set aside; the geoglyphs are the demonstrable product of ordinary human labor using simple methods, created by the ancestors of communities whose broader material culture — irrigation systems, ceramics, textiles, and ceremonial centers such as Cahuachi — is well documented throughout this chapter.

Serious scholarly interpretation of the geoglyphs' purpose remains genuinely open rather than settled. Some researchers have proposed that particular lines and figures relate to astronomical observation, marking solstices or other significant celestial alignments, though systematic testing has found this explanation to hold for only a limited number of features rather than for the geoglyphs as a whole. Other researchers emphasize relationships between particular geoglyphs, underground water sources, drainage patterns, and the broader ritual importance of water in this desert environment, suggesting that the geoglyphs may have functioned partly as markers within a ritual landscape connected to securing agricultural water. Proposed associations between specific geoglyphs and the later hydraulic features called puquios, discussed in the next section, require additional chronological caution, since the two kinds of features may not have been contemporary with one another. Still other interpretations emphasize the geoglyphs' apparent function as processional pathways — walked rather than simply viewed — connecting communities, shrines, and Cahuachi itself within a landscape of pilgrimage and ritual movement. This chapter presents these interpretations as complementary possibilities supported by different kinds of evidence rather than as competing claims requiring students to select a single correct answer; the honest scholarly position is that the geoglyphs likely served multiple, possibly overlapping ritual purposes that no single explanation fully captures.

Historical Vocabulary
Geoglyph

A geoglyph is a large design or motif produced on the ground, typically by removing, arranging, or otherwise altering surface material to create a durable image or geometric feature. Some large figurative Nazca geoglyphs are easiest to comprehend from elevated viewpoints, while many lines, tracks, and geometric features were accessible and experienced at ground level. The Nazca geoglyphs, created by clearing darker desert-pavement stones to expose lighter subsoil, are among the most extensive and best preserved geoglyphs anywhere in the world, a result of the extraordinarily dry, wind-sheltered, and geologically stable conditions of the south-coast Peruvian desert. Students should understand "geoglyph" as a descriptive archaeological term referring to how an image was made and where it is located, not as a term that by itself explains why any particular geoglyph was created.

Section 17.7

Water, Irrigation, and Farming in the Desert

Large-scale agriculture in the south-coast river valleys depended on the careful capture and distribution of scarce river water through networks of canals that diverted flow from the rivers into fields planted with maize, cotton, beans, squash, and other crops, along with orchards of fruit trees in some areas. Because river flow in this region varies substantially by season and by year, with some years bringing severe drought, coastal communities faced a persistent challenge in managing water reliably enough to sustain settled agricultural populations, a challenge that plausibly encouraged cooperation, communal labor organization, and forms of authority capable of coordinating water use across multiple communities sharing a single valley.

The Nazca drainage presents a particularly striking engineering response to this challenge: a system of subterranean water-collection galleries known as puquios, which tap groundwater below the desert surface and channel it, often via spiral-shaped access points allowing periodic cleaning and maintenance, into distribution canals feeding agricultural fields. The puquios represent a substantial feat of hydraulic engineering, requiring detailed knowledge of local groundwater conditions and considerable coordinated labor to construct and maintain.

Students should treat the puquios' precise date of construction as an open scholarly question rather than a settled fact. Some archaeological settlement evidence suggests that puquios were not yet present during the earliest, or "Early," phase of the Nazca sequence, implying that they were introduced at some later point in Nazca history, possibly in response to increasing population, agricultural intensification, or changing water conditions. Proposed direct dating of puquio construction, including estimates placing significant construction activity around the seventh century CE, remains methodologically debated among specialists, in part because dating a constructed water channel presents different challenges than dating an artifact or organic material found in a sealed archaeological context. This chapter therefore does not present a single confident date for puquio construction, and it cautions against textbook language — sometimes found in older or more popular accounts — that credits "the Nazca" as a whole with inventing the puquios at one clearly dated moment.

Whatever their precise chronology, the puquios illustrate a broader theme running throughout this chapter: coastal Andean societies achieved genuinely impressive engineering solutions to the challenge of farming in an extremely arid environment, and these solutions required forms of social cooperation, technical knowledge, and labor organization that developed gradually over generations rather than through any single inventive breakthrough.

Section 17.8

Nazca Ritual, Art, and Social Authority

Nazca society is known archaeologically not only through Cahuachi and the geoglyphs but through an extensive and technically accomplished tradition of polychrome ceramics, painted before firing with a wide range of mineral pigments producing vivid, durable colors. Nazca pottery frequently depicts supernatural beings combining human, animal, and plant features, agricultural produce, marine creatures, and ritual specialists, imagery that most scholars interpret as evidence of a rich cosmological and religious system centered on fertility, water, and the relationship between human communities and powerful natural and supernatural forces.

Nazca ceramics and other archaeological evidence also include depictions, and physical examples, of severed or "trophy" heads — human heads that were specially prepared, sometimes with holes for suspension, and apparently kept, displayed, or buried as part of ritual practice. This evidence has sometimes been popularly interpreted as straightforward proof of endemic, large-scale warfare among Nazca communities. This chapter cautions against that simple inference. Trophy-head practices in the Andes and elsewhere in the ancient world have been associated with a range of possible meanings, including warfare and the taking of captives, ancestor veneration, fertility ritual, and the ritualized treatment of already-deceased individuals rather than battlefield killing; the specific mixture of these meanings in Nazca society remains a subject of ongoing archaeological and bioarchaeological research rather than settled fact. Students should therefore treat trophy-head evidence as proof of a significant ritual practice involving human heads, without assuming that it demonstrates a particular scale or character of warfare beyond what the evidence directly supports.

Women's participation in Nazca ritual and craft production deserves explicit attention rather than assumption of an exclusively male-dominated society. Textile production, an activity strongly associated with female labor in many Andean societies both ancient and more recent, remained central to Nazca material and ritual culture, much as it had been for Paracas communities. A notable cache of elaborately feathered garments recovered from Cahuachi — assembled from tropical bird feathers obtained through long-distance exchange with lowland Amazonian regions — provides evidence for specialized textile and featherwork production connected to high-status ritual display, and museum analysis of this material has highlighted its likely connection to women's ceremonial dress and ritual roles at the site. While the full political significance of women's roles at Cahuachi and in broader Nazca society cannot be reconstructed with certainty from currently available evidence, historians should avoid the unsupported assumption that ritual and political authority in Nazca society was held exclusively by men.

Section 17.9

The Moche World of the North Coast

Several hundred kilometers north of the Nazca drainage, and during broadly the same centuries, an entirely separate set of communities developed the archaeological tradition known as Moche, named for the Moche River near the modern city of Trujillo. Moche society flourished across multiple north-coast river valleys — including the Moche, Chicama, Jequetepeque, and Lambayeque valleys, among others — from roughly 200 to 850 CE, making it substantially contemporaneous with Nazca society on the south coast rather than its successor.

The north-coast environment resembled the south coast in its fundamental dependence on river-fed irrigation to sustain agriculture in an otherwise extremely arid landscape, but the north-coast valleys were, in several cases, considerably larger and more agriculturally productive than their south-coast counterparts, supporting larger populations and more extensive irrigation networks. Moche communities cultivated maize, beans, squash, and other crops, supplemented by rich marine resources drawn from the productive waters of the adjacent Pacific, including fish and shellfish harvested using specialized reed watercraft.

Perhaps the single most important methodological caution this chapter offers concerns political terminology. Older popular and even some earlier scholarly accounts have referred to "the Moche Empire," implying a single centralized state controlling all of the valleys where Moche material culture appears. Current archaeological scholarship treats this characterization with considerable skepticism. Moche material culture — shared ceramic styles, shared religious iconography, shared monumental building traditions — spread across a wide geographic area, but the political relationships among the communities and elites who produced and used this material culture remain a matter of active investigation and debate rather than settled fact. This chapter, and the Historical Debate feature later in this section, treats "was there a single Moche state?" as a genuine open scholarly question rather than assuming a confident answer in either direction.

Section 17.10

Moche Political Authority, Cities, Temples, and Elites

The most visible evidence of Moche political and religious authority comes from massive monumental construction, above all the platform mounds known as Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna, built of millions of sun-dried adobe bricks near the Moche River. Huaca del Sol, though heavily damaged by colonial-era looting and erosion, was among the largest structures built anywhere in the pre-Columbian Americas, while Huaca de la Luna preserves extensive painted friezes depicting supernatural beings, ritual processions, and combat scenes that provide crucial evidence for Moche religious belief and ideology. These platform mounds functioned as ceremonial and administrative centers, associated with elite residence, ritual performance, and, in some cases, elite burial, though the precise relationship between religious ceremony and day-to-day political administration at any given center remains an area of ongoing research.

Beyond the major centers near the Moche River itself, other valleys developed their own significant Moche centers, elite residences, and monumental architecture, a pattern that supports the interpretation of Moche society as a network of related but locally rooted regional polities rather than a single centralized state governed from one capital. Archaeologists increasingly describe Moche political organization as varying across space and over time, with some periods and regions showing evidence of greater centralization and others showing more clearly independent local elites.

The 1987 excavation of an undisturbed elite tomb at the site of Sipán, in the Lambayeque Valley, transformed scholarly understanding of Moche political and religious authority. The Sipán tombs contained individuals buried with extraordinarily rich gold, silver, and copper ornaments, elaborate headdresses, and other regalia that corresponded closely to the ornaments worn by important ritual figures depicted in Moche fineline ceramic art — most notably a figure sometimes referred to by researchers as the "Warrior Priest" or "Lord of Sipán." This correspondence between artistic depiction and actual excavated regalia gave historians unusually direct evidence that specific ceremonial roles depicted in Moche art corresponded to real social and religious institutions, rather than existing only as artistic convention or mythological imagery.

Evidence for elite women's authority in Moche society deserves equal attention, though it comes from different periods of the Moche sequence and should not be flattened into a single moment. Excavations at El Brujo, in the Chicama Valley, uncovered the elaborately tattooed and richly adorned remains of a high-status woman, popularly known as the Lady of Cao and dated broadly within the earlier centuries of the Moche sequence, buried with weapons, ceremonial objects, and other regalia associated in Moche art with ritual and political authority, indicating that at least some women held significant ceremonial or political status within Moche society. Excavations at San José de Moro, in the Jequetepeque Valley, have identified a sequence of elaborate female burials, dating substantially to the Late Moche period of roughly 600 to 850 CE, associated with priestly regalia connected to a specific ritual figure depicted in Moche iconography, sometimes called the "Priestess," suggesting that a particular high-status ritual office at this site was held by women across multiple generations, later in the Moche sequence than the Sipán and El Brujo burials discussed above. Historians should incorporate this evidence directly into any account of Moche political and religious authority rather than presenting Moche society as an exclusively male political world.

Section 17.11

Warfare, Captives, Sacrifice, and Moche Art

Moche visual culture — painted and modeled ceramic vessels, wall murals, and metalwork — offers an unusually rich and detailed narrative tradition depicting armed combatants, the capture and humiliation of defeated opponents, ritual processions, and scenes generally interpreted as involving the sacrifice of captives and the ceremonial consumption of blood. Vessels frequently modeled in the shape of individual warriors, and painted "fineline" ceramics depicting elaborate combat and presentation scenes, together constitute one of the most detailed pictorial records surviving from any ancient American society for this general period.

Students must learn to treat this material as evidence of ideology and ritual practice rather than as a literal documentary record of specific historical battles. Moche combat imagery follows recurring conventions — particular weapons, particular clothing, particular gestures of capture and presentation — that suggest a structured ritual or ceremonial system rather than a simple illustrated chronicle of real military campaigns. At the same time, archaeological evidence, including excavated sacrificial contexts containing the remains of young adult males showing evidence of ritualized killing, confirms that at least some Moche combat-and-capture imagery corresponded to real ritual practices involving genuine violence, not merely symbolic or purely mythological representation.

The precise character and purpose of Moche warfare remains genuinely debated among specialists, and this chapter presents that debate honestly rather than resolving it artificially. Some scholars emphasize interpretations centered on competition for irrigable land, water rights, or other valley resources among rival Moche polities, treating captive-taking as one outcome of broader territorial or economic conflict. Other scholars emphasize a more thoroughly ritualized model of highly structured, ceremonial combat, in which fighting served primarily to produce captives for sacrifice within a religious system centered on fertility, ancestor veneration, and the maintenance of cosmic order, rather than to seize territory or resources in a conventional military sense. Current evidence does not allow historians to select confidently between these interpretations as mutually exclusive; both territorial competition and highly ritualized combat may have coexisted, in different times, places, and social contexts, across the broad geographic and chronological span covered by the label "Moche."

Whatever the precise mixture of motives involved, Moche combat and sacrifice clearly carried major ritual and ideological significance rather than representing undifferentiated or purposeless violence. Some archaeological contexts, including sacrificial deposits associated with periods of severe flooding, suggest connections between particular acts of sacrifice and climatic events, water, or agricultural fertility. The meanings and circumstances of Moche sacrifice nonetheless varied across time, place, and context, and should not be reduced to a single universal explanation.

Section 17.12

Regional Coastal Societies and the End of the Moche Era

By the time this chapter's narrative closes, in the centuries around 700 to 850 CE, both the Nazca tradition of the south coast and the Moche tradition of the north coast had undergone significant transformation. On the south coast, ceremonial activity at Cahuachi appears to have diminished over time, and settlement patterns in the Nazca drainage shifted in ways that current scholarship connects to some combination of environmental stress, including possible episodes of severe drought or flooding associated with El Niño climate events, and changing regional political and religious dynamics, rather than to any single sudden catastrophe. On the north coast, Moche settlement patterns and centers of authority likewise shifted considerably over these centuries, with some long-established centers declining in importance while new regional configurations began to emerge in some of the same valleys, including the beginnings of the Sicán, or Lambayeque, tradition; the later Chimú state developed subsequently, centered farther south at Chan Chan in the Moche Valley, and belongs to a later chapter of this volume.

This chapter deliberately avoids describing either process as a simple, sudden "collapse" caused by a single environmental catastrophe. While episodes of drought, flooding, and other environmental stress very likely placed real pressure on irrigation-dependent agricultural systems in both regions, historians increasingly emphasize that political, social, and religious factors interacted with environmental change in complex ways that current evidence does not allow us to reduce to one dominant cause. Societies as sophisticated in water management as Paracas, Nazca, and Moche communities had already demonstrated considerable capacity to adapt to a challenging and variable desert environment over many centuries; their eventual transformation should be understood as a gradual and regionally uneven reorganization rather than as evidence of failure or inevitable decline.

Comparing the three traditions examined in this chapter reveals both shared patterns and significant differences. All three relied heavily on river-fed irrigation to sustain agriculture in an extremely arid coastal environment, alongside marine and other non-riverine resources, and all three developed rich textile and ceramic traditions embedded in complex religious belief connecting human communities to supernatural forces governing water, fertility, and cosmic order. Yet Paracas and Nazca society on the south coast appear to have remained more dispersed and less monumentally centralized than Moche society on the north coast, whose massive platform mounds and increasingly well-documented elite burials suggest more concentrated, though still regionally fragmented, political authority. None of the three, on current evidence, developed the scale of interregional colonization, road-building, and administrative integration that characterizes the highland systems examined in Chapter 18. That chapter turns to Wari and Tiwanaku, larger-scale highland state systems that developed new forms of colonization, administration, religious influence, and interregional integration, although the character and extent of political control differed between them and remain subjects of ongoing archaeological debate — a transformation in the scale of Andean political organization that stands in instructive contrast to the more regionally rooted coastal societies examined in this chapter.

A Paracas Embroidered Mantle

Object type: Embroidered textile mantle (funerary garment) · Origin: South coast of Peru, Paracas Peninsula/Ica Valley region · Approximate date: Within the broad Paracas period, c. 700 BCE–100 CE · Materials: Cotton base cloth with camelid-fiber embroidered decoration

Context: Surviving Paracas mantles of this kind were recovered chiefly from cemetery excavations, most famously those led by Julio C. Tello, in which the deceased were wrapped in numerous layers of finely made textiles before burial in dry underground chambers. A single elaborate mantle could require an enormous number of hours of spinning, weaving, dyeing, and embroidery, and the largest mortuary bundles contained many such garments.

Rather than reproducing a description of one specific museum-catalogued object, students should understand the general evidentiary pattern: such mantles are typically decorated with repeating embroidered figures — often composite beings combining human, feline, avian, or serpentine features, sometimes carrying trophy heads or ritual staffs — arranged in rows across the cloth's surface, worked in multiple vivid dyed colors on a plain woven ground.

Analysis: A textile like this can tell historians a great deal about technical skill, available materials, trade in dyes and camelid fiber, and probably religious belief, since the repeated supernatural imagery suggests shared cosmological ideas rather than purely personal or family symbolism. It can also, through comparison with other burials, provide evidence about relative wealth and status, since the sheer quantity of labor embodied in such a garment was not equally available to every member of Paracas society. What such a textile cannot tell historians, on its own, is the specific name, political title, or exact life history of the person buried with it, nor can it, by itself, establish the precise structure of Paracas political authority.

A Nazca Geoglyph as Landscape-Scale Evidence

Object type: Landscape-scale geoglyph (figurative or geometric) · Origin: Desert pavement of the Nazca and Palpa valleys, south coast of Peru · Approximate date range: Constructed across both Paracas and Nazca periods, roughly 500 BCE–500 CE, according to current UNESCO World Heritage documentation

Context: A geoglyph of this kind was created by clearing a path or shape through the darker, oxidized surface stones of the desert pavement, exposing the lighter subsoil beneath. Although the overall forms of some large figurative designs are easier to comprehend from nearby elevations or from above, many lines and geometric features were physically accessible and traversable at ground level, and current interpretation emphasizes that they were walked as pathways during ritual activity rather than designed solely for distant viewing.

Students examining a geoglyph as evidence should consider its location relative to water sources, settlements, and other geoglyphs; whether it takes a figurative form (an animal, plant, or human figure) or a geometric form (a line, trapezoid, or spiral); its scale and the labor required to construct it; and its relationship, if any, to nearby archaeological remains such as ceramics or offerings left along its course.

Analysis: A geoglyph can provide strong evidence of organized labor, sustained ritual use of a specific landscape over time, and a community's engagement with a landscape marked for movement and viewing, whether from the ground or from nearby elevations. It cannot, on its own, establish a single confirmed purpose. Proposed explanations — astronomical alignment, connection to water sources, and use as ceremonial processional pathways — should be treated as complementary hypotheses supported by different forms of evidence rather than as a single settled answer.

Historical Debate

Did the Moche Form a Single State?

The question: Did Moche material culture — shared ceramic styles, shared monumental architecture, shared religious iconography spanning multiple north-coast valleys — reflect a single centralized political state, or a network of related but politically independent regional polities?

Interpretation A — The Moche formed, at least at certain periods, a substantially unified political system: Proponents of this view point to the striking consistency of religious iconography, architectural conventions, and elite regalia across widely separated valleys, arguing that such consistency is difficult to explain without some meaningful degree of shared political or religious authority extending across the north coast, at least during particular periods of Moche history.
Interpretation B — The Moche were a network of independent regional polities sharing a common cultural and religious tradition: Proponents of this view emphasize that each major valley developed its own significant centers, elite burials, and monumental construction, with no single site clearly dominating all others across the entire chronological span of Moche material culture, and argue that shared art styles and religious ideas can spread through long-distance exchange and religious pilgrimage without requiring a single centralized government, much as the earlier Chavín interaction sphere spread shared imagery without producing one unified state.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — settlement patterns, monumental architecture, elite burials, and iconographic consistency — but differ in how much political unity that evidence is sufficient to demonstrate, and both acknowledge that Moche political organization may have varied meaningfully across different regions and periods rather than following one single pattern throughout.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how the word "Moche" itself should be used — as the name of a single ancient state, or as the name of a shared cultural and religious tradition uniting multiple independent north-coast polities.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 17.9 and 17.10, construct an argument for each interpretation, and identify what kind of additional archaeological evidence would most help resolve the debate.

Historical Debate

What Were the Nazca Lines For?

The question: What purpose or purposes did the geoglyphs of the Nazca and Palpa valleys serve for the communities that created and maintained them across many generations?

Interpretation A — An astronomical and calendrical function: Some researchers have proposed that particular lines align with solstice points or other significant celestial events, potentially serving agricultural or ritual timekeeping purposes. Systematic testing of large samples of lines, however, has found consistent astronomical alignment for only a limited subset of features, suggesting this explanation, while plausible for some lines, cannot account for the geoglyphs as a whole.
Interpretation B — A ritual landscape connected to water and pilgrimage: Other researchers emphasize the geoglyphs' relationship to underground water sources, drainage patterns, and processional pathways connecting communities to Cahuachi and other ceremonial locations, proposing that walking the lines formed part of ritual practice aimed at securing agricultural water in an unpredictable desert environment.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on careful mapping of geoglyph location, orientation, and association with settlements, water sources, and other archaeological remains, but researchers disagree about how much weight to give astronomical alignment versus water-related and processional explanations.

Why it matters: Because no contemporary written explanation survives, historians must reconstruct meaning entirely from physical and spatial evidence, making this an instructive case study in the limits of purely archaeological interpretation.

Student task: Explain why multiple, overlapping purposes are more consistent with the current evidence than a single definitive explanation, and identify what non-scholarly explanations of the Nazca Lines should be rejected and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Living on the Desert Coast: Perspectives on Water, Ritual, and Authority

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified across archaeological scholarship. They are not quotations from any named historical individual.

From the perspective of a Paracas textile worker: Producing an elaborate embroidered mantle required months or years of skilled labor, knowledge of dye preparation passed through generations, and access to camelid fiber obtained through exchange with highland communities — work whose value was measured not only in its beauty but in the social and ritual meaning it carried for the person it would eventually accompany in burial.
From the perspective of a Nazca farmer dependent on river irrigation: Survival depended on the reliable, cooperative management of scarce water shared with neighboring communities, making droughts, floods, and disputes over canal access matters of urgent daily concern, and giving ritual specialists who claimed influence over water and fertility genuine social importance.
From the perspective of a Moche elite associated with a valley center such as Sipán or San José de Moro: Political and religious authority was expressed through elaborate regalia, participation in ceremonial combat and presentation rituals, and association with powerful supernatural beings depicted in art — a form of authority that combined religious legitimacy with control over labor, monumental construction, and valley resources.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist studying trophy heads or combat imagery: Physical evidence must be interpreted cautiously and in combination with multiple lines of analysis — bioarchaeological examination of human remains, careful reading of iconographic convention, and comparison across sites — because striking or violent imagery can too easily be sensationalized or misread as a literal record of specific historical events rather than as evidence of structured ritual practice.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Chavín to Regional Coastal Worlds

What Changed

  • The broadly shared Chavín-era religious imagery of Chapter 16 gave way to more regionally distinctive artistic, textile, ceramic, and architectural traditions on the south and north coasts
  • Political organization diversified regionally, with south-coast Paracas and Nazca communities remaining relatively dispersed while north-coast Moche society developed more concentrated monumental centers
  • Water-management technology developed and intensified, from simple canal irrigation toward more elaborate systems such as the Nazca puquios
  • Artistic media diversified by region, with the south coast emphasizing textiles and painted pottery and the north coast emphasizing monumental adobe architecture and narrative fineline ceramics

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on river-fed irrigation in an extremely arid environment remained the fundamental economic constraint across all periods and regions examined in this chapter
  • Religious belief connecting human communities to powerful supernatural forces governing water, fertility, and cosmic order remained central to ritual life throughout
  • Long-distance exchange with highland communities for camelid fiber, and with more distant regions for materials such as tropical feathers, remained important throughout
  • The absence of a single unified imperial state remained a defining feature of the coastal Andes throughout the period examined in this chapter, in contrast to the highland integration examined in Chapter 18

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the diversification and the underlying continuities helps students understand the coastal Andes as neither a single unchanging civilization nor a series of unrelated, disconnected societies, but as a set of related regional traditions responding differently to a shared set of environmental and religious concerns.

Cause and Effect

How Did Extreme Coastal Aridity Shape Political and Social Development?

Long-term causes

The extreme aridity of the Peruvian coastal desert, combined with the presence of glacier- and rainfall-fed rivers descending from the Andes, created narrow, highly productive but water-limited agricultural zones separated by uninhabitable desert.

Short-term causes

Seasonal and year-to-year variability in river flow, including periodic drought and flooding associated with climate events such as El Niño, created recurring pressure for reliable water management and cooperative labor organization.

Effect

Coastal communities developed increasingly sophisticated irrigation engineering, including canal systems and, in the Nazca drainage, subterranean puquios, alongside religious systems and ritual specialists closely associated with water, fertility, and cosmic order.

Historical significance

This combination of environmental constraint and cooperative water management likely contributed to social differentiation and the development of ritual and political authority in Paracas, Nazca, and Moche society, though it did not, on current evidence, produce a single centralized state comparable to later highland empires.

Historical complexity: Environmental constraint alone does not explain political development; the specific forms of ritual belief, labor organization, and regional interaction found in each society also shaped how communities responded to the shared challenge of desert agriculture.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in Paracas, Nazca, and Moche Communities

Beyond monumental temples, elaborate burials, and famous geoglyphs, most people in Paracas, Nazca, and Moche society spent their lives engaged in agricultural labor, fishing, craft production, and household management within small farming communities along the river valleys. Households cultivated maize, beans, squash, and cotton in irrigated fields, supplemented their diet with fish and shellfish gathered from the adjacent Pacific, and maintained exchange relationships with highland communities for camelid fiber, obsidian, and other materials not locally available.

Textile and ceramic production, examined throughout this chapter as evidence for elite ritual life, also formed part of ordinary household economies, with less elaborate garments and everyday vessels produced alongside the finest ceremonial pieces that survive in the archaeological record. Archaeological evidence from settlement sites, though less abundant than the evidence from elite burials and ceremonial centers, suggests that most households lived in relatively modest structures built of adobe, cane, and other locally available materials, organized into communities whose internal social distinctions, while real, were considerably less dramatic than the differences visible between an ordinary household burial and an elite tomb such as those at Sipán. This unevenness in the surviving evidence — richer for elites than for ordinary farmers and fisherfolk — is itself an important lesson about how archaeological preservation and excavation priorities can shape what historians are able to say with confidence about a given society.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined three archaeologically defined traditions of the ancient Peruvian coast — Paracas, Nazca, and Moche — that developed after the weakening of the shared Chavín-era interaction sphere examined in Chapter 16. Rather than presenting these traditions as sequential stages of a single coastal civilization, the chapter has emphasized their overlapping chronologies and distinct regional settings: Paracas flourishing on the south coast from roughly 700 BCE to 100 CE, Nazca developing in the same general region from roughly 100 to 700 CE, and Moche flourishing on the far distant north coast from roughly 200 to 850 CE, substantially contemporaneous with Nazca rather than following it.

Students have examined Paracas society through its extraordinary embroidered textiles and elaborate funerary bundles, preserved by the extreme aridity of the coastal desert, and have learned to read these textiles as evidence of skilled labor, social differentiation, and religious belief. The chapter has cautioned against a simple narrative in which Paracas "became" Nazca, introducing the Topará tradition as evidence of a more complicated, overlapping transition involving multiple coexisting material traditions rather than a single clean succession.

The chapter has examined Nazca society through the lens of Cahuachi, interpreted cautiously as a major ceremonial and pilgrimage center rather than confidently as an imperial capital, and through the famous geoglyphs of the Nazca and Palpa valleys, whose purpose remains genuinely debated among specialists and should not be reduced to any single confident explanation, still less to unsupported non-scholarly speculation. It has examined Nazca irrigation, including the impressive but chronologically uncertain puquio water-collection systems, and Nazca ceramic art and trophy-head evidence, cautioning against treating such evidence as automatic proof of large-scale endemic warfare, while highlighting evidence for women's participation in ritual textile and featherwork production at Cahuachi.

The chapter has then shifted to the north coast to examine Moche society, emphasizing that the precise political relationship among Moche regional centers remains genuinely debated among specialists, with interpretations ranging from substantial periods of political unity to a network of related regional polities sharing artistic and religious traditions without unified centralized government. It has examined Moche monumental architecture, above all Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna, and the transformative 1987 excavation at Sipán, which connected artistic depictions of ritual authority to real elite regalia. It has also introduced evidence for elite women's ritual and political authority from the Lady of Cao and San José de Moro, insisting that Moche political history cannot be told as an exclusively male story.

Students have examined Moche combat, captivity, and sacrifice imagery as a rich but ideologically shaped artistic tradition rather than a literal historical record, and have encountered the genuine scholarly debate over whether Moche warfare should be understood primarily as territorial conflict, as highly ritualized ceremonial combat producing captives for sacrifice, or as some combination of both varying by time and place. The chapter has closed by comparing the three traditions and by describing their eventual transformation during the seventh and eighth centuries CE as a gradual, regionally uneven reorganization rather than a sudden or uniform collapse, while explicitly declining to reduce that transformation to a single environmental cause.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized that none of the three traditions examined here, on current evidence, achieved the scale of interregional colonization and administrative integration examined in the next chapter. This observation sets up the central transition to Chapter 18, which examines Wari and Tiwanaku — larger-scale highland state systems that developed new forms of colonization, administration, and religious influence, though the character and extent of political control differed between them and remain subjects of ongoing archaeological debate, representing a significant transformation in the scale of Andean political organization compared to the more regionally rooted coastal societies examined in this chapter.

Chapter 17 has asked how distinct coastal societies organized power, ritual, and meaning within the extraordinary constraints and opportunities of the Andean desert coast. Chapter 18 turns to the highlands, where Wari and Tiwanaku developed new systems of roads, colonization, administration, and religious influence that integrated much larger and more diverse territories than any of the societies examined in this chapter achieved.

Key Terms

Paracas
An archaeological tradition of the south coast of Peru, c. 700 BCE–100 CE, known especially for elaborate embroidered textiles and mortuary bundles.
Topará
A distinct material tradition associated with the Chincha and Pisco valleys that overlaps chronologically with later Paracas and early Nazca material, complicating a simple succession narrative.
Nazca
An archaeological tradition of the Río Grande de Nazca drainage, c. 100–700 CE, known for polychrome ceramics and the Nazca Lines geoglyphs.
Cahuachi
A major Nazca ceremonial and pilgrimage center; its political role as an imperial capital remains unproven and debated.
Geoglyph
A large image or design created on the ground, best known from the Nazca and Palpa valleys, constructed across both Paracas and Nazca periods.
Puquio
A subterranean water-collection gallery used in the Nazca drainage to tap groundwater for irrigation; its construction date remains debated.
Moche
An archaeological tradition of multiple north-coast river valleys, c. 200–850 CE, known for monumental adobe architecture and narrative ceramic art.
Huaca
An Andean term for a sacred place, object, or being, also used by archaeologists as a label for monumental platform structures such as Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna.
Sipán
A north-coast Moche site whose 1987 excavation of undisturbed elite tombs transformed scholarly understanding of Moche political and religious authority.
Fineline ceramics
A Moche painted-pottery tradition depicting detailed narrative scenes of combat, capture, presentation, and ritual.
Trophy head
A specially prepared human head, depicted in and recovered from Nazca contexts, associated with ritual practice whose precise meaning remains debated.
Mummy bundle
An elaborately textile-wrapped burial, especially associated with Paracas mortuary practice, providing key evidence for social status and craft production.
Interaction sphere
A network along which shared images, objects, and ritual practices circulate across long distances without requiring political unification, as with the earlier Chavín horizon.
Polychrome
Pottery or other art decorated using multiple colors, especially associated with Nazca ceramic tradition.
Camelid fiber
Wool obtained from llamas, alpacas, or related animals, obtained by coastal communities through highland exchange and used extensively in textile production.
Embroidery
The technique of stitching decorative designs onto a woven base cloth, central to Paracas textile art.
Adobe
Sun-dried mud brick, the principal building material of Moche monumental platform mounds.
Platform mound
A raised, constructed earthen or adobe structure used for ceremonial, elite residential, or administrative purposes, as at Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna.
Lady of Cao
A high-status Moche woman excavated at El Brujo, buried with regalia associated with ritual and political authority.
San José de Moro
A Moche site with a sequence of elaborate female burials associated with priestly regalia, providing evidence for women's ritual authority across generations.
Ritual combat
A proposed interpretation of some Moche warfare imagery as highly structured, ceremonial confrontation producing captives for sacrifice rather than territorial conquest.
Regional polity
A politically organized community controlling a limited territory, used to describe the likely political units of Paracas, Nazca, and Moche society rather than a single unified empire.
Seriation
An archaeological method for establishing relative chronology by analyzing changes in the style of artifacts such as ceramics over time.
Iconography
The study of visual imagery and symbols in art, central to interpreting Paracas, Nazca, and Moche religious and political meaning.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look closely at a set of societies whose achievements are often admired in isolation — spectacular textiles, mysterious desert lines, vivid narrative pottery — without always being placed carefully into their proper historical and regional context. By insisting that Paracas, Nazca, and Moche be understood as distinct, overlapping regional traditions rather than as sequential stages of a single coastal civilization, this chapter has modeled a form of historical reasoning that resists tidy, linear narratives in favor of a more accurate, if more complicated, picture of the past.

The chapter has also modeled appropriate historical caution in the face of incomplete or ambiguous evidence: declining to call Cahuachi an imperial capital, declining to settle definitively on one purpose for the Nazca Lines, declining to date the puquios with false precision, and declining to describe "the Moche" as a single centralized empire when the archaeological evidence supports a more regionally varied picture. This caution is not indecisiveness; it is the appropriate response of careful historical scholarship to evidence that does not yet, and may never, permit a single confident answer.

At the same time, the chapter has shown that uncertainty about political structure does not mean uncertainty about historical significance. Paracas textile workers, Nazca ritual specialists, and Moche elites and artisans built genuinely sophisticated societies, adapted to one of the most challenging environments inhabited by any people in world history, and left behind material evidence — cloth, ceramics, monumental architecture, and an enormous ritual landscape etched into the desert itself — that continues to reward careful historical and archaeological study.

The regional diversity examined in this chapter — dispersed south-coast communities organized around shared ceremonial centers, and more monumentally concentrated north-coast valleys with increasingly documented elite institutions — sets up an important contrast with the subject of the next chapter. Wari and Tiwanaku, examined in Chapter 18, developed new, larger-scale forms of political and religious integration across highland and interregional distances, although the precise nature and extent of political control in each case remain actively debated among archaeologists — a transformation in the scale of Andean political organization that becomes fully intelligible only against the regionally rooted coastal world this chapter has described.
