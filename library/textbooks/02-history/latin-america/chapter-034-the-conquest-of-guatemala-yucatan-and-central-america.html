The Conquest of Guatemala, Yucatán, and Central America

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: If Spain Held Mexico City, Why Did Conquest Continue?

By 1521 Spaniards and their Indigenous allies held the ruins of Tenochtitlan. Yet across the rest of Mesoamerica and Central America, conquest continued for decades, and in some regions remained incomplete for generations.

When Tenochtitlan fell in August 1521, many Spaniards must have thought the hardest part of the enterprise was over. The great imperial capital was in ruins, its current ruler Cuauhtémoc was captive, and the tribute system that had drawn wealth toward the Basin of Mexico lay open to new claimants. Yet the conquest of Mesoamerica and Central America had barely begun. The K'iche' capital Q'umarkaj was burned in 1524; the Kaqchikel alliance that helped to bring that about collapsed within weeks of the founding of Santiago de Guatemala; Cortés's march to Honduras took place in 1524–1526; and the main campaigns in Yucatán ran from 1527 to the 1540s, ending in a rising in 1546–1547 that threatened everything the Spaniards had built. The Itza of Petén remained politically independent until 1697. This chapter asks why conquest took so long, and why in some places it never fully ended.

The question matters because the fall of Tenochtitlan has often served as a template for the whole conquest, with a bold European captain, an imperial capital, a decisive victory, and a rapid collapse. The chapters before this one complicated that template even for central Mexico, showing that Spaniards depended on Indigenous allies and that Tlaxcalan, Totonac, and other leaders made political choices of their own. The regions examined here make the template harder to sustain. Highland Guatemala, Cuscatlán, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Yucatán lacked a single imperial center whose capture could end resistance. They had their own rulers, languages, rivalries, and landscapes, and each had to be entered, fought over, negotiated with, and governed, if it was governed at all, on its own terms.

The armies that moved south were not simply Spanish. Alvarado's expedition to Guatemala included a few hundred Spaniards and a much larger body of Nahua, Mixtec, Zapotec, and other Indigenous warriors and porters from central Mexico and Oaxaca, and later expeditions followed the same pattern. Local communities supplied further allies, guides, food, and labor, sometimes willingly and sometimes under compulsion. These participants were not anonymous: they served under their own leaders, pursued their own goals, and in some cases settled permanently in the lands they helped to invade. Understanding the conquest requires attention to them, even though Spanish sources usually mention them only in passing.

Alliances were conditional and they changed. The Kaqchikel joined Alvarado against their old rivals the K'iche', and then abandoned their capital and fought him when his demands for tribute, labor, and gold became intolerable. Maya provinces in Yucatán allied with the Spaniards at one moment and rose against them at another, according to local interests. Spanish commanders were also divided. Olid defied Cortés in Honduras, Pedrarias had Hernández de Córdoba executed in Nicaragua, and rival expeditions from Mexico, Panama, and the Caribbean competed for the same territory. Conquest was therefore a tangle of Indigenous politics and Spanish factionalism as much as a contest between two sides.

This chapter follows these campaigns in roughly chronological order and by region: the Guatemalan highlands, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and finally Yucatán, whose decentralized political geography made it the most prolonged case. Chiapas and the Soconusco appear as corridors, and Panama appears as a Spanish base rather than a separate subject. It distinguishes military victory from the founding of towns, conversion, legal incorporation, and effective control, since these processes followed different timelines.

The evidence is uneven and must be read with its purposes in mind. Alvarado's letters and Cortés's report on the Honduras expedition were written to justify their authors to superiors and the Crown. Indigenous voices survive mainly in colonial-era writings, such as the Annals of the Kaqchikels, which were composed under Spanish rule, often to defend the standing of particular lineages. Maya-language books and administrative records add perspective, and archaeology at sites such as Q'umarkaj and Iximché supplements the written record. None of these sources offers a transparent view of what happened, and the chapter models how historians weigh them against one another.

This is the final chapter of Part VII. It builds on Chapter 33's account of New Spain's colonial government and takes up the material that chapter deliberately reserved, including Cortés's Honduras expedition. It closes with the observation that a pattern seen here, in which invaders entered divided Indigenous worlds, won spectacular victories, and still failed to create secure rule, would reappear in the Andes, within the very different setting of the Inca civil war that Chapter 35 explores.

Historical Context

The regions studied in this chapter were not blank spaces on the edge of the Mexica world. Chapter 12 described the Postclassic Maya as politically fragmented, with the K'iche', Kaqchikel, and other highland states competing in Guatemala and a patchwork of jurisdictions in Yucatán after the decline of Mayapán. Chapter 22 introduced the Isthmo-Colombian world that included lower Central America, and the Pacific coast of present-day Guatemala, El Salvador, and Nicaragua participated in long-distance exchange networks linking cacao-producing regions and other goods to central Mexico and neighboring parts of Mesoamerica. Mexica merchants and tribute collectors had long been active in the Soconusco, and Nahua-speaking communities such as the Pipil and the Nicarao lived far from the Basin of Mexico.

Chapters 30 through 33 supplied the immediate background. They showed how Spaniards in Mexico relied on interpreters, Indigenous diplomacy, and coalitions to defeat the Triple Alliance, and how the colonial regime that followed rested on encomienda, municipal councils, and the cooperation of Indigenous elites. They also described epidemic disease, which spread from the Caribbean and Mexico and reshaped Indigenous societies, though its timing and effects varied from region to region and cannot always be reconstructed securely. Chapter 33 reserved Cortés's Honduras expedition and the wider campaigns involving Nahua auxiliaries for this chapter.

The invaders carried habits formed in the Caribbean and central Mexico. An entrada was an armed expedition authorized by the Crown, a governor, or in some cases only by the commander himself, and participants expected rewards of land, tribute, labor, and captives. Leaders often held royal contracts called capitulaciones and sometimes the title of adelantado. The encomienda granted a Spaniard rights to receive Indigenous tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services, while formally imposing obligations of protection and Christian instruction; it was not itself a grant of land or ownership of Indigenous people. Enslavement of captives was separately and widely practiced. These institutions created strong incentives for violence and for rapid exploitation, and they contributed to the breakdown of alliances.

The physical setting also shaped events. The volcanic highlands of Guatemala offered fertile valleys and defensible ridges, the Pacific coast provided a corridor for movement and trade, and the forests and swamps of the Gulf of Honduras tested every column that crossed them. The northern Yucatán plain, a limestone landscape with few surface rivers, imposed limits on water, food, and movement. Spanish towns founded in one environment did not automatically control the next, and expeditions repeatedly discovered that terrain mattered as much as numbers.

Finally, political fragmentation framed everything. Where the Mexica empire had been a tribute system centered on a single capital, the societies of the south were organized in dozens of rival polities, from dynastic highland states to Yucatec provinces ruled by lineage heads and local lords called batabs. Such a world offered Spaniards many potential allies and many potential enemies, and it meant that victory over one ruler seldom bound his neighbors. This is the background against which the campaigns that follow must be read.

Place names require care. “Guatemala,” “Honduras,” “Nicaragua,” and “El Salvador” refer here to modern regions, not to sixteenth-century jurisdictions, and Spanish administrative boundaries shifted repeatedly as rival commanders claimed overlapping territories. “The Maya” were never one political bloc, and the Spanish term “pacification” is used in quotation marks as colonial vocabulary. Dates in this chapter come largely from Spanish narratives and sometimes differ by months or years, so approximate dates are marked.

Section 34.1

Beyond Central Mexico: Conquest Moves South

The fall of Tenochtitlan in August 1521 ended the Triple Alliance as a political system, but it did not give Spaniards a map of the lands beyond the Basin of Mexico. What they possessed was a capital, a large Indigenous population paying tribute under new masters, and access to the information that Mexica tribute officials, merchants, and rival communities could supply. Spanish commanders learned quickly that the old imperial system had left behind roads, markets, tribute lists, and specialists in long-distance travel, and that these could be turned to new purposes. Chapter 33 followed the construction of colonial government in the center; this section follows the expeditions that left it.

Southern movement began almost at once. Oaxaca, home to Zapotec, Mixtec, and other communities that had been subject to, allied with, or hostile toward the Mexica in different ways, was entered by Spanish captains and Indigenous allies in the early 1520s, and the Soconusco, a Pacific-coast cacao district that had paid tribute to the Triple Alliance, became a doorway to the south. Chiapas, a mountainous region of Maya-speaking peoples and others, was entered in 1524 by an expedition under Luis Marín, which met strong resistance and did not secure the region; further expeditions and rival Spanish factions followed, including settlement foundations in 1528. These regions were corridors between central Mexico and Guatemala, but they were also places with their own political histories, and the Spanish presence there remained contested by local resistance and by competing Spanish claimants well after the first entradas.

The armies that moved along these corridors were not simply Spanish. Spanish accounts, written to emphasize the achievements of individual captains, usually count Spaniards first and mention allies loosely. Yet scholarship on the invasion of Central America has stressed that thousands of Indigenous warriors and porters from central Mexico and Oaxaca accompanied the expeditions: Tlaxcalans, Cholulans, Mexica of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco, Texcocans, Xochimilca, Huexotzinca, Mixtec, Zapotec, and others. They were not a faceless mass. They served under their own leaders, sought tribute exemptions, plunder, and standing in the new order, and carried with them a long experience of imperial warfare in Mesoamerica. Their numbers are uncertain, because the figures come from participants with reasons to inflate or ignore them.

Why did these people march? Motives differed. Some communities were obliged to supply men under the demands of the new colonial government. Some leaders sought to prove loyalty and secure privileges, as Tlaxcala had after 1521. Some individuals joined for plunder or captives. Whatever the motive, their participation shaped how conquest worked: Spanish commanders relied on Indigenous knowledge of routes, languages, and political relationships, and the invasion of the south reproduced the coalition pattern of Chapters 30 through 32 rather than replacing it with a purely European enterprise.

This chapter therefore asks a question that its opening raised: if Spain held Mexico City by 1521, why did conquest across the rest of Mesoamerica and Central America continue for decades? The answer begins with the terrain through which the armies moved, with the divided political worlds they entered, and with the fact that each victory had to be negotiated again with new allies in new places.

Historical Vocabulary
Entrada

The Spanish word entrada (“entry”) referred to an armed expedition into territory not yet under Spanish control. An entrada might be authorized by the Crown through a contract called a capitulación, by a governor, or, as in several cases in this chapter, by a commander acting on his own initiative and defending his legality afterward. Leaders with royal contracts to govern frontier regions were often called adelantados.

The term matters because it describes what these campaigns actually were: combinations of warfare, exploration, diplomacy, enslavement, tribute collection, and town-founding, financed largely by participants who expected rewards. Entradas were repeated, and a region could suffer several before any town survived.

Modern readers should not equate an entrada with a “conquest” of a place. A successful entrada might defeat a ruler, found a town, and still leave the surrounding territory unsubdued, which is precisely the pattern this chapter traces.

Section 34.2

The Maya Highlands before Alvarado

When Spaniards reached the Guatemalan highlands in 1524, they entered a landscape of competing Maya states, not a single “Maya empire.” The best known were the K'iche', the Kaqchikel, and the Tz'utujil, each with its own capital, ruling lineages, and history. Maya-language writings composed after the conquest, including the Popol Vuh and the Annals of the Kaqchikels, preserve highland traditions about this political world, alongside archaeology at sites such as Q'umarkaj and Iximché. They were written under colonial conditions and from particular lineage perspectives, and they must be read with that in mind.

The K'iche' state, centered at Q'umarkaj (often called Utatlán in Spanish sources), had expanded in the fifteenth century and dominated much of the western highlands, drawing tribute from neighboring peoples. K'iche' tradition celebrated rulers who extended its reach, and the state included allied and subordinate lineages whose loyalty could not be taken for granted. The Kaqchikel, centered at Iximché, had once been associated with the K'iche' but, according to their own annals, broke away and fought them, building a separate polity that expanded in turn. The Tz'utujil, around Lake Atitlán, formed a third power. Other peoples, among them Mam, Poqomam, and Ixil speakers, lived in their own jurisdictions, some under pressure from the larger states.

The relationship between K'iche' and Kaqchikel was therefore one of long rivalry, marked by warfare, shifting alliances, and competing claims to tribute. Neither side was a passive victim of the other. This rivalry matters because it created incentives: a polity threatened by a neighbor could see advantage in alliance with strangers, and strangers who arrived with unfamiliar weapons and a large Indigenous force offered a chance to tip the balance. Spaniards, who had learned in central Mexico how to exploit such rivalries, did so here as well.

The highlands were also in contact with the Mexica world. Long-distance exchange linked the region to the Soconusco and to central Mexico, and merchants and envoys had moved along these routes before 1519. Kaqchikel annals record a severe epidemic in 1520, several years before Alvarado's arrival; its precise pathogen and mortality cannot be reconstructed securely. Highland societies thus faced the invaders already shaken by outside pressures and with their own political divisions unresolved.

Section 34.3

Pedro de Alvarado and the War against the K'iche', 1524

Pedro de Alvarado had been one of Cortés's principal captains during the war for Tenochtitlan, and in late 1523 he departed central Mexico to explore and subdue lands to the south under Cortés's authority. His force combined a few hundred Spaniards, including cavalry, with a much larger body of Indigenous auxiliaries and porters from central Mexico and Oaxaca; Spanish sources give inconsistent numbers, and the Indigenous figures in particular should be treated as estimates. The column moved through Oaxaca and the Soconusco, crossed into the western highlands in early 1524, and met K'iche' resistance.

The K'iche' did not wait passively. According to Alvarado's own letter to Cortés, his army fought several engagements as it advanced, including a major battle near Xelajú, in the area of modern Quetzaltenango, where K'iche' forces were defeated. The fighting involved large Indigenous armies on both sides, and Alvarado emphasized hardship, wounds, and the cavalry's role. These accounts were written to impress a superior and to justify a campaign, so claims about enemy numbers and about Spanish achievements need caution. Highland sources, written later, recall defeat and suffering and attribute the invaders' success partly to alliance with the Kaqchikel.

Indigenous auxiliaries were central to these battles, though the sources say little about them. Alvarado's force relied on warriors from central Mexico and Oaxaca who fought in their own units, scouted, carried supplies, and cleared the road, and who bore a large share of casualties that Spanish writers rarely counted. Their presence meant that K'iche' defenders faced an army that included experienced Mesoamerican fighters as well as horsemen, and it helps explain why a few hundred Spaniards could operate deep in hostile country. It also meant that the burdens of the campaign, including feeding thousands of people, fell on the highland communities through which the army passed.

After the defeat, K'iche' leaders brought Alvarado into Q'umarkaj. In the Spanish account, he suspected a plot to trap his army in the town and seized the rulers, and they were executed and the town burned. Whether such a plot existed cannot be established from the source, which is the report of the man who carried out the killing. What can be said is that the capital of the leading highland state was destroyed and its rulers killed, and that this broke K'iche' political power in the region without ending K'iche' communities, which persisted and later produced important colonial-era writings.

The aftermath for K'iche' communities was severe but uneven. Q'umarkaj's destruction removed the political center that had coordinated tribute and military leadership, and K'iche' towns were soon subject to Spanish demands for food, labor, and tribute, as well as to the slaving and violence that accompanied the expedition. Kaqchikel leaders, who had their own reasons to oppose the K'iche', opened relations with Alvarado, a development examined in Section 34.4. Highland sources written decades later, including K'iche' texts, preserve memories of this period filtered through colonial purposes: they often defend the standing of particular lineages before Spanish authorities, so they are evidence of how K'iche' elites remembered and used the past as much as of what happened in 1524.

The best-known figure of this war in Guatemalan memory is Tecún Umán, honored as a national hero who died fighting Alvarado. That tradition belongs largely to later narratives. Contemporary Spanish accounts, including Alvarado's letter, do not provide the elaborated story of a duel, and later Indigenous and colonial texts differ in how they present K'iche' commanders. A responsible account therefore distinguishes what contemporary evidence supports, the battles and the destruction of Q'umarkaj, from the heroic memory that later generations built around the war.

The larger point is that this was an Indigenous interstate war transformed by the arrival of a new coalition, not the conquest of Guatemala by one Spaniard. The victory over the K'iche' was real, but it opened a period of unstable alliances rather than a settled colony. The Kaqchikel, who had joined against their old rival, are the subject of the next two sections, and their experience shows how quickly such alliances could change.

Geography Study

Central Mexico to Nicaragua, and the Yucatán Peninsula

Using a physical map of Mesoamerica and Central America, students should locate and trace the following, remembering that none of the modern states existed as units in the sixteenth century:

  • The Basin of Mexico, Oaxaca, and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec as the starting corridor southward
  • The Soconusco Pacific coastal plain, a cacao-producing district that had paid tribute to the Triple Alliance
  • The Chiapas highlands and the Grijalva River basin
  • The Guatemalan highlands: Xelajú (Quetzaltenango), Q'umarkaj, Iximché, and Lake Atitlán
  • The Pacific coastal corridor into Cuscatlán, in present-day El Salvador
  • The Gulf of Honduras and the overland route through Tabasco and Acalan
  • The Nicoya Peninsula in present-day Costa Rica
  • The Nicaraguan lakes and the isthmus toward Panama
  • The Yucatán Peninsula: the northern plain, the Gulf and Caribbean coasts, T'Hó (Mérida), and the forests to the south toward Petén

Notice how the terrain varies. The volcanic highlands offered fertile valleys and defensible ridges; the Pacific coast offered a long, warm corridor for trade and movement; the lowland forests and the Yucatán plain had few permanent rivers, so water, food, and porters constrained any army. Spanish towns founded in one environment did not automatically control the next.

Section 34.4

The Kaqchikel Alliance and the Founding at Iximché

After the destruction of Q'umarkaj, the Kaqchikel emerged as the principal Indigenous partner of the invaders in the highlands. Their annals, written in Kaqchikel in the colonial period, describe the arrival of the Spaniards and the decision of the ruling lords to receive them, and they identify the senior rulers as Cahi Imox and Belehe Qat (modern spellings vary). Spanish colonial sources rendered Indigenous royal names inconsistently, so this chapter uses the names preserved in the Kaqchikel tradition. The annals are the main Indigenous account of this alliance, and like all colonial-era writing they were composed with later purposes in mind, including the defense of Kaqchikel lineages before Spanish authorities.

The reasons for alliance were political. The Kaqchikel had been rivals of the K'iche' for generations, and the K'iche' defeat removed a threat while offering the Kaqchikel a chance to strengthen their own position. Alliance also offered protection against a force that had already shown it could destroy a capital. Kaqchikel leaders probably judged, as Tlaxcalan leaders had in 1519, that cooperation with the strangers might serve Kaqchikel interests better than resistance. The sources do not record their deliberations in their own words, so this reasoning is inferred.

Kaqchikel support took practical forms. The alliance gave Alvarado guides, food, warriors, and a secure base in Iximché, from which the combined force campaigned against other highland communities, including the Tz'utujil around Lake Atitlán. For the Spaniards, who were few and far from reinforcement, a friendly capital with a large population was an invaluable asset. For the Kaqchikel, Spanish military strength and the auxiliaries from central Mexico changed the regional balance in their favor, at least for the moment.

On 25 July 1524 Alvarado founded the first Spanish town in Guatemala, Santiago de Guatemala, at Iximché, in Kaqchikel territory. The act established a municipal council and a legal framework for distributing land and Indigenous labor among Spaniards, which is the sense in which it was a founding. But it also placed a Spanish settlement inside an Indigenous capital whose rulers had not agreed to become subjects. The foundation therefore carried the seeds of conflict: the Spaniards expected tribute and labor, and the Kaqchikel expected an alliance among partners.

Both sides, in short, believed cooperation could serve their interests, and both were right for a few weeks. Alliance was not permanent submission, however, and the speed with which it unraveled shows how conditional Indigenous cooperation could be. The next section follows that unraveling.

Section 34.5

From Allies to Rebels: The Kaqchikel War

Within weeks of Santiago's founding the alliance began to fail. The cause was the weight of Spanish demands. Alvarado and his men expected provisions, labor, women, and, above all, gold, and the Kaqchikel annals recall demands that the rulers could not or would not meet. Spanish soldiers took what they wanted and enslaved captives, and the communities that had hosted the Spaniards found themselves supporting a growing demand for tribute from a town that depended on them. According to the annals, the Kaqchikel lords decided that they would no longer comply.

In late summer 1524 the Kaqchikel abandoned Iximché and withdrew into the surrounding country, turning from allies into enemies. The Spaniards were left in a town that had lost its population and its food supply, and they were soon compelled to campaign against the people who had been their closest partners. Kaqchikel resistance did not take the form of a single pitched battle. Kaqchikel fighters used the ravines and forests of the highlands, attacked supply lines, and avoided the cavalry charges that had been effective against the K'iche'. The terrain that had favored Alvarado in open country now favored his opponents.

The Spanish response combined force and diplomacy. Alvarado campaigned against Kaqchikel communities with his Indigenous auxiliaries, burned towns, and took captives, but he could not produce submission quickly. The war lasted for years, and during that time the Spaniards' own position was unstable. In 1527 the Spanish settlement moved from Iximché to a new site at Almolonga, leaving the old Kaqchikel capital behind. Alvarado himself was absent in Spain for part of this period, and the government of the colony was left to his brother and others, which made policy less consistent.

A major phase of the war ended not with a decisive victory but with gradual exhaustion and negotiated subordination. Major Kaqchikel rulers submitted around 1530 after years of war, but submission did not settle the region: Cahí Imox led another Kaqchikel uprising in 1533–1535, and Spanish consolidation remained incomplete through the mid-1530s. Thereafter the Kaqchikel population was drawn more firmly into the Spanish tribute and labor system. Submission did not mean disappearance: Kaqchikel communities and their elites survived and adapted, and the annals themselves are evidence of that survival. The precise sequence and dating of the later war vary among sources, so students should treat the dates here as approximate.

The Kaqchikel experience offers a revealing comparison with Tlaxcala. Both were Indigenous polities that allied with Spaniards against regional enemies, but the results differed. Tlaxcala was a politically complex confederation of four principal altepetl with substantial military capacity and bargaining leverage, and it was able to convert its long military partnership with the Spaniards into enduring privileges. The Kaqchikel faced a Spanish settlement planted in their own capital, demands that began almost immediately, and no comparable bargaining position. The lesson is that Indigenous alliance with Spaniards could produce very different outcomes depending on political circumstances, and that alliance was never the same as submission.

What the sequence shows: The dates do not describe a single advance. Several campaigns overlap, and the pace differs by region. In the highlands, the K'iche' defeat came quickly in 1524, but the Kaqchikel war that followed lasted years and ended in negotiation, not in a battle. In Yucatán, the interval between the first campaign in 1527 and the founding of Mérida in 1542 is longer than the entire war in the highlands, and even that foundation was followed by a major uprising.

Three patterns stand out. First, acceleration: Spanish forces won rapid victories over particular rulers. Second, delay: turning victory into rule took far longer, and sometimes never happened. Third, fragmentation: Spanish commanders pursued rival enterprises in Honduras and Nicaragua while the highland war continued. The sequence therefore supports the chapter's argument that conquest, settlement, and control followed different timelines.

Section 34.6

Cuscatlán and the Pacific Corridor

From Guatemala, Alvarado moved southeast along the Pacific corridor into the territory of present-day El Salvador. This region was home to Pipil communities, Nahua-speaking peoples whose language was related to the Nahuatl of central Mexico, and to others, including Lenca, Pokomam, and Ch'orti' speakers. It was an area of agricultural wealth and active trade: the Pacific coast produced cacao and the balsam for which the Salvadoran coast later became known, and its communities were tied into the long-distance networks that ran from the Soconusco toward Nicaragua. At its center lay Cuscatlán, a Pipil polity whose name came to be applied to the wider region.

The expedition of 1524 met serious resistance. Alvarado's own letter describes fighting in the Cuscatlán region, and Spanish sources place a hard battle near the coast at Acajutla, where he was badly wounded in the leg, an injury that, according to later accounts, left him permanently affected. These are Spanish reports of Spanish suffering, and Pipil accounts of the same events have not survived in comparable form, so the Indigenous side of the story must be inferred from the archaeology and from later documents. What is clear is that Alvarado's force, even with its Indigenous auxiliaries, could not impose lasting control and withdrew without establishing a secure colony.

The wound and the withdrawal matter for what they show about the limits of Spanish power. A seemingly irresistible coalition had defeated the K'iche', but the same army, operating in a different political and physical environment, was checked. Cuscatlán itself was a substantial Nahua/Pipil tributary state, but defeating or occupying its center did not secure the wider region, which contained other Nahua and non-Nahua communities and required repeated Spanish campaigns.

Spanish control came only through repeated campaigns and foundations. Relatives and subordinates of Alvarado returned in the mid-1520s and founded a settlement that became San Salvador, but early towns were unstable, were moved, and depended on Indigenous labor that was resisted and also devastated by enslavement and disease. The conquest of El Salvador therefore cannot be told as one march by Alvarado. Including this region matters for another reason: without it, “Central America” becomes synonymous with Guatemala, and the experience of Pipil and neighboring peoples disappears.

Section 34.7

Honduras: Rival Conquistadors and Cortés's Overland Expedition

Spanish conquest was not only a war against Indigenous peoples; it was also a competition among Spaniards. This became plain in Honduras. In 1524 Cortés sent Cristóbal de Olid by sea with ships and men to found a settlement on the coast of Honduras. Olid reached the area, but instead of remaining loyal he broke with Cortés, in part because of ties to Cortés's rival Diego Velázquez, the governor of Cuba, and in part because the opportunity to carve out an independent domain was attractive. The area was already contested by other Spanish claimants, including Gil González Dávila, who had approached from the Caribbean and Panama side.

Cortés learned of Olid's defection and responded with a force sent by sea to arrest him. Olid was captured and killed in the conflict that followed, but Cortés, still uncertain of what had happened, decided to go to Honduras himself. In October 1524 he left Tenochtitlan on an extraordinary overland march with a large party that included Spaniards, Indigenous lords from central Mexico, porters, and Cuauhtémoc, whom Cortés took along as a precaution. Cuauhtémoc's execution during the expedition, in the Acalan region, was discussed in Chapter 33 and is mentioned here only as a cross-reference.

The route took the expedition through Tabasco, the Acalan region, and the Maya lowlands of the interior, without a road for most of the distance. The party crossed rivers and swamps, built bridges, ran short of food, and depended on local guides and on Indigenous communities for supplies. Many people died. Cortés's own account, the Fifth Letter, is a justification written to the Crown, and it emphasizes hardship and leadership. It also shows that Indigenous rulers along the way, including the lord of Acalan, were sources of food, information, and diplomacy, and that the expedition passed through the Itza region of Petén, whose rulers remained independent for another century and a half.

When Cortés reached the Honduran coast, he found that Olid's conflict had already been resolved, and the area was in disorder. He spent time establishing Spanish settlements and managing rival claimants, then returned by sea to Mexico in 1526, where he found that his authority had suffered during his absence. The episode showed how fragile Spanish rule was in the new colony and how a commander's long absence could unsettle it.

Honduras itself remained contested for years. Spanish towns were founded and moved, jurisdiction was disputed among Spanish factions from Mexico, Guatemala, Panama, and the Caribbean, and Indigenous peoples resisted. In the 1530s, Lenca communities in the highlands mounted prolonged resistance, and a Lenca leader called Lempira or Elempira, attested in Rodrigo Ruiz's 1558 probanza de méritos, is associated with that resistance. Accounts of his death conflict: the later Herrera tradition says he was shot during a parley, while Ruiz claimed to have killed him in combat. Honduran national memory has elaborated his story heroically, so documented resistance should be distinguished from later heroic elaboration. The analytical point is that conquest involved Spanish civil conflict as well as Spanish–Indigenous warfare, and that each weakened the other's ability to establish control.

Primary Source Window

Alvarado's Letters to Cortés and the Annals of the Kaqchikels

Creators: Pedro de Alvarado; Kaqchikel authors of the Annals · Dates: 1524 (letters); later sixteenth century (Annals) · Type: A conquest report to a superior and a Maya-language Indigenous annals composed under colonial rule

Context: In 1524 Alvarado wrote to Cortés, who had authorized his expedition, to report on the war in Guatemala. He was reporting to the man who commanded him and needed his campaigns to appear lawful, successful, and necessary. The Annals of the Kaqchikels (also called the Memorial de Sololá) were composed in Kaqchikel by members of the Xajil lineage in the decades after the conquest. They preserve lineage history and an account of the Spanish arrival, written when the authors lived under Spanish rule and needed to defend their families' standing.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: Alvarado describes a series of hard-fought battles against large K'iche' forces, presents the burning of Q'umarkaj's rulers as a response to a plot against his army, and portrays his campaigns as bringing the Crown new subjects. The Kaqchikel annals describe the arrival of the Spaniards, refer to Pedro de Alvarado as Tonatiuh, and recount the reception of the newcomers by Kaqchikel lords. They then recall the demands made on the Kaqchikel and the decision to abandon the capital and resist.

Analysis: The two sources illuminate the same events from opposite positions. Alvarado's letter supplies dates, places, and tactical detail, and reveals what a conquistador wished his commander and the Crown to believe. It says little about Indigenous motives and almost nothing about the thousands of Indigenous auxiliaries. The Annals preserve a Kaqchikel point of view on alliance and disillusion, but they were written years later, after defeat and under colonial scrutiny, and they serve lineage interests. Their account of the early alliance may be shaped by what later generations wanted Spanish authorities to read.

Neither source gives voice to K'iche' survivors, to porters and auxiliaries from central Mexico, or to ordinary Kaqchikel households. Read together, they show that the same conquest could be narrated as heroic expansion and as a story of betrayal and suffering, and that both narratives were written for audiences with power.

Questions for students:

  1. What does each source claim about the Spanish arrival and the war?
  2. Who created each source, when, and for what likely purposes?
  3. What can historians learn about alliance and its breakdown from reading them together?
  4. What can historians not establish from these sources alone?
  5. Whose voices are absent from both, and how might that absence affect the conclusions historians draw?
Section 34.8

Nicaragua and the Southern Central American Contest

The southern end of the Central American isthmus was approached from a different direction. Panama, where Spaniards had settled since the 1510s, became a base from which expeditions moved northwest along the Pacific coast, while conquerors associated with Mexico pressed southeast. Nicaragua lay where these movements met, and it became a meeting zone of Caribbean, Panamanian, and Mexican enterprises. Panama itself was part of the Isthmo-Colombian world described in Chapter 22 and does not receive a separate conquest narrative here; it appears as a Spanish colonial base shaping events farther north.

Nicaragua was densely populated and politically varied. Chorotega-Mangue communities lived in much of the Pacific plain; the Nicarao, Nahua-speaking people with linguistic and cultural ties to central Mexico, lived near the great lakes; Matagalpa and other peoples occupied highland and eastern areas. These groups had their own rulers, markets, agriculture, and rivalries, and they were not a single people. Spanish accounts often reduce them to a generic native population, so reconstructing individual communities requires care.

The first major Spanish expedition was led by Gil González Dávila, who traveled north from Panama in 1522 and 1523. Spanish accounts say he met Nicarao and other leaders in negotiations in which gifts were exchanged and many people were baptized, and that he collected gold. These reports of mass conversion come from the expedition itself and reflect Spanish expectations as much as Indigenous belief. The Spaniards were also attacked by Indigenous forces, including those associated with a leader remembered as Diriangén, and González withdrew with his gold. The pattern of diplomacy alongside coercion, seizure of food and gold, and violent response recurred in later expeditions.

In 1524 Francisco Hernández de Córdoba, sent by the governor of Panama, Pedrarias Dávila, returned with a larger force and founded Spanish towns, among them León and Granada, which later became centers of colonial Nicaragua. Quickly founded towns did not equal secure control. Spaniards still relied on Indigenous food and labor, and surrounding communities resisted or abandoned their settlements. Hernández de Córdoba soon came into conflict with Pedrarias, who suspected him of seeking independence, possibly in coordination with commanders from Mexico, and Pedrarias had him executed in 1526. Spanish commanders thus fought one another as readily as they fought Indigenous peoples.

Costa Rica shows the same unevenness. González's northward expedition passed through Nicoya, in present-day Costa Rica, before entering Nicaragua, but Spanish control farther into Costa Rica remained shallow and intermittent for decades. Early conquest efforts failed, and more durable expansion did not begin until the 1560s. Costa Rica thus illustrates the chapter's central distinction between an entrada, a founded settlement, and effective territorial control.

The human costs were severe. Spaniards enslaved large numbers of Indigenous people in Nicaragua and shipped many to Panama and beyond, where labor was in demand, and this traffic devastated communities. Disease and displacement compounded the effects of violence. Indigenous peoples resisted by fleeing, fighting, and negotiating, and the conquest of Nicaragua, like those in Honduras and Guatemala, remained incomplete in surrounding regions for years. The significance of Nicaragua for the larger story is that it shows Central America as a contested zone shaped by rival Spanish enterprises from several directions, not merely as an extension of Mexico.

Section 34.9

Yucatán Was Not an Empire Waiting to Fall

Before turning to the campaigns of the Montejo family, students need to understand what the Spaniards faced in northern Yucatán. The peninsula was not an empire waiting to fall. After the decline of Mayapán in the fifteenth century, northern Yucatán contained a patchwork of Maya jurisdictions, often called provinces or kuchkabalo'ob in Maya sources, each governed by a ruler and councils and containing towns led by local lords called batabs. Among the best known were the Xiu, centered at Maní, and the Cocom, centered at Sotuta, rival lineages with a long history of conflict, and other provinces such as the Cupul, Cheles, Canul, and Ceh Pech had their own leaders and interests.

This decentralization had two consequences. First, Spaniards could exploit local rivalries and make alliances, as they had in central Mexico and Guatemala. Xiu and Cocom hostility offered obvious openings, and later events show that some Maya leaders sought alliances with the invaders against old enemies. Second, and more importantly, there was no capital whose capture would produce submission across the peninsula. Seizing one ruler or town gave a Spanish force at best a single province. Conquest therefore had to proceed province by province, with each requiring its own war or negotiation, which is why it took so long.

The environment compounded the political fragmentation. The northern plain is a limestone landscape with few surface rivers, so water came from cenotes and wells, and food depended on maize agriculture and on communities that Spanish forces had to supply or seize. The south and east were forested and thinly settled by Spanish standards, offering refuge to those who withdrew. The peninsula was also not untouched by outside forces. Spaniards had visited the coast since 1517, and Maya sources such as the Books of Chilam Balam, along with later Spanish writers such as Bishop Diego de Landa, record disease, hurricane, and famine in the decades before Montejo's arrival. These sources were written or compiled under colonial conditions and must be used with caution.

Chapter 30 described the earlier encounters on the Maya coast, including the voyages of Córdoba and Grijalva and the shipwreck survivors Aguilar and Guerrero, and these need not be retold. What matters here is that Yucatán in the 1520s was a divided and resilient world that Spaniards understood poorly. The conquest narrative of Tenochtitlan, with its central capital and imperial structure, does not fit, and Yucatán's history shows why that model cannot be applied everywhere.

Historical Vocabulary
Pacificación

The Spanish term pacificación (“pacification”) was administrative vocabulary used by the Crown and by officials for the subjugation of a region and its incorporation into the empire. In official documents it often appeared alongside promises to convert Indigenous people and bring them to “peace and obedience.”

The word presents Spanish rule as the restoration of order. In practice, a campaign of pacification might involve warfare, burning of towns, enslavement of captives, forced resettlement, and the imposition of tribute and labor. It therefore describes what Spaniards intended and the language they used to justify it, not the experience of the people on whom it was imposed, and it conceals negotiation, alliance, and resistance.

Modern readers should be cautious of the equivalent English “pacification,” which can sound neutral or even benevolent. Historians generally treat it as a colonial term to be analyzed, not adopted. In this chapter it is used in quotation marks, and when Spaniards declared a region pacified, students should ask by whom, by what means, and how long the declaration held.

Section 34.10

The Montejo Campaigns, 1527–1540

The Spanish conquest of Yucatán is conventionally divided into three stages, a scheme that makes the long process easier to follow but should not hide its contingency. It was led by Francisco de Montejo, called the Elder, a veteran of the Mexican campaigns who obtained a royal capitulation in the mid-1520s giving him the title of adelantado and the right to conquer and govern Yucatán. Like most such contracts, it promised rewards to the leader and required him to raise and finance the expedition largely at his own risk. The enterprise was thus a private venture under royal license, and its backers expected a quick return.

The first campaign, from 1527 to about 1529, began with landings on the east coast of the peninsula and on the island of Cozumel. Montejo's force, which included several hundred Spaniards, moved along the coast and into the interior, meeting resistance from Maya communities and suffering from heat, thirst, hunger, and disease. Spanish accounts record serious fighting in the east, and the region around Chetumal is associated with Gonzalo Guerrero, a Spaniard who had lived among the Maya since a shipwreck and who, according to tradition, aided Maya resistance. After repeated reverses, the expedition withdrew from the peninsula without establishing a lasting colony.

Montejo did not give up. In the early 1530s he returned, this time approaching from the west after securing Tabasco, and he and his lieutenants founded settlements on the west and north, including a short-lived Spanish town at the ancient site of Chichén Itzá in the early 1530s, though sources vary on the exact dates. Maya communities responded with sustained resistance, including a large uprising by the Cupul and other provinces that left the Spaniards isolated and forced another withdrawal by the mid-1530s. Again, there was no capital to seize. Each province had to be fought for, and Spanish gains in one place did not carry over to another.

Why did the Spaniards fail? Several factors reinforced one another. Spanish manpower was small, and the supply of food and water on the limestone plain was limited, so a force operating inland could not easily sustain itself. Disease struck Spaniards as well as Maya communities, and Indigenous resistance was determined and adaptive, with fighters attacking columns, withholding provisions, and retreating. The shifting politics of Maya provinces also mattered: some leaders allied with Spaniards at one moment and resisted at another, depending on local interests, so no alliance could be relied upon. Finally, the decentralization described in the previous section meant that victories were local.

Two further features of these campaigns deserve emphasis. The first is the dependence of every Spanish column on Maya communities for survival. Soldiers who could not find water or maize had to take it, and requisitions turned neutral or friendly towns into enemies. The second is that Maya resistance was not a single movement. Provinces such as the Cupul fought fiercely, others negotiated, and still others waited to see which side would prevail. Spanish accounts, written by participants who wished to present their hardships as heroic, often reduce this variety to a story of hostile “natives,” and Maya perspectives on these campaigns survive mostly in later colonial-era writings that must be read with care. The repeated withdrawals were therefore not simply military defeats: they reflected the failure of the invaders to secure the food, allies, and political leverage on which control depended.

The third and decisive phase began around 1540 under Francisco de Montejo the Younger, assisted by relatives and by Indigenous auxiliaries from central Mexico. The younger Montejo advanced from Campeche, and the campaign culminated in the founding of Mérida in 1542, the subject of the next section. Even this final phase depended on a combination of military force and alliances with Maya lords, and its success was not complete. Scholarship describes the conquest of Yucatán as a process of roughly two decades beginning in 1527, with repeated Maya resistance, and the repeated failures are the most important point: Spanish conquest here was not inevitable but depended on contingent political developments that earlier campaigns lacked.

Section 34.11

Mérida, Maya Alliances, and the Limits of “Pacification”

The founding of Mérida in 1542, at the Maya site of T'Hó, marked a turning point. Francisco de Montejo the Younger established the Spanish city on the ruins of an important Maya settlement, using its stone buildings in construction, after fighting that included a significant engagement near T'Hó. Spanish victory there was possible in part because of Indigenous allies. Auxiliaries from central Mexico fought alongside the Spaniards, as in earlier campaigns, and Maya leaders also allied with them. The Xiu of Maní, longtime rivals of the Cocom, are the best-known example: their ruler accepted Spanish rule and baptism, and the alliance gave the Spaniards crucial support and political legitimacy in the interior.

Other Spanish towns followed. Campeche, on the coast, was established earlier and consolidated, and Valladolid was founded in the east in 1543 at Chauac-Há and transferred to Sací in 1544, in a region of strong resistance. These towns were centers for Spaniards, for the allocation of encomiendas, and for administration, and they allowed the Crown's officials to claim that Yucatán was subdued. Encomienda districts gave individual Spaniards rights to receive Indigenous tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services, while formally carrying duties of protection and Christian instruction; it was not itself ownership of land or of Indigenous people. In practice these demands were burdensome, though Maya town councils and lords continued to manage local affairs.

Resistance did not end with Mérida's founding. In 1546 and 1547, a major uprising erupted among Maya communities in the east and elsewhere, aimed at expelling Spaniards and ending tribute and labor demands. Spanish accounts describe attacks on encomenderos and Maya communities, and the rising was suppressed with the help of Maya allies and auxiliaries, after severe violence. It showed that the Spanish hold on the peninsula remained fragile after more than a decade of campaigning and that many Maya communities had not accepted the new order.

Daily life under the new order mixed accommodation and pressure. Maya town councils, batabs, and local lords continued to manage village affairs, assign tribute shares, and mediate with Spanish officials, and Spaniards depended on them to deliver cotton cloth, maize, and labor. The Spanish towns themselves were small, and their residents relied on Indigenous markets and servants. Many Maya families moved, hid, or withdrew to the forests to avoid tribute, and the Spanish presence outside the towns was often thin. Evidence for these experiences comes from Spanish administrative records, Maya-language texts, and later church sources, all of which reflect the interests of their writers.

The word pacification must be treated carefully here. Spanish officials used it to describe the imposition of rule, but it concealed the continuing violence, negotiation, and resistance. Spanish towns and encomienda districts did not equal complete control of the Maya world. Large parts of the south, in the forests and lowlands, remained beyond Spanish power, and communities fled to them to escape tribute and labor. Far from the Spanish towns, Maya polities maintained their own governance.

The most striking example is the Itza polity centered at Nojpetén on an island in Lake Petén Itzá, which Cortés had passed in 1525 and which remained politically independent until 1697, when Spanish forces took it. A fuller account belongs to a later period, and it appears here as a closing example of the limits of conquest. A chapter that ended with the founding of Mérida would present the peninsula as conquered; the survival of independent Maya polities for another century and a half shows that conquest, settlement, and control proceeded on different timelines.

Historical Debate

What Does “Conquest” Mean When Regions Remained Autonomous?

The question: Should historians date and define conquest by military victories and the founding of Spanish towns, or by the establishment of effective Spanish rule over a region and its people? The answer shapes how scholars narrate Central America and Yucatán, and whether Indigenous resistance appears as exceptional or typical.

Interpretation A — Conquest as defeat and foundation: On this view, conquest occurred when Spaniards defeated the principal Indigenous powers and founded towns that became colonial centers, for example 1524 for the K'iche' or 1542 for Yucatán. This approach follows how Spaniards themselves described events and how the Crown assigned jurisdictions, and it recognizes that the founding of cities created institutions, such as municipal councils and encomienda grants, that organized colonial life.
Interpretation B — Conquest as a prolonged, incomplete process: On this view, conquest was a long series of wars, alliances, rebellions, and negotiations in which large regions remained autonomous for decades or centuries. Dating it by foundations overstates Spanish power and downplays Maya, Kaqchikel, Lenca, and Itza resistance and political choices. Scholars of Chiapas, for example, note that there is no final date for its conquest.

The two views are not mutually exclusive. Many historians combine them, treating foundations and defeats as real turning points while insisting that they marked the start of long processes of consolidation and negotiation, not their end. The disagreement is therefore often one of emphasis and periodization rather than of facts.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same sources, but with different emphases. Foundation records, encomienda grants, and royal reports support A, while later rebellions, the Kaqchikel war, the 1546–1547 uprising, and the independence of the Itza until 1697 support B. Spanish documents are shaped by officials' interest in claiming success, and Indigenous sources were written under colonial conditions.

Why it matters: If conquest is dated by foundations, Indigenous peoples seem to disappear after a decisive defeat. If it is understood as prolonged, Indigenous agency stays visible and colonial rule appears uneven. The distinction also matters for later chapters, since frontier regions shaped colonial economies, missions, and politics.

Student task: Write a short argument supporting each interpretation, then decide which better explains events in Yucatán, and identify what additional evidence could help.

Section 34.12

Conquest without Complete Control

The preceding sections traced separate campaigns across a wide geography, and they share patterns that explain why conquest continued for decades after Tenochtitlan. First, conquest depended on Indigenous alliances. Alvarado's victory over the K'iche' relied on Kaqchikel support and on auxiliaries from central Mexico, and Montejo's final success depended on Maya allies. Second, alliances frequently changed. The Kaqchikel moved from partnership to rebellion within weeks, and Maya provinces in Yucatán allied and resisted according to local interests. Third, Indigenous auxiliaries often outnumbered Spaniards, though Spanish sources rarely say so clearly.

Spanish commanders competed with one another, as Olid, Cortés, Hernández de Córdoba, and Pedrarias show, and this rivalry weakened every enterprise. Settlements did not automatically control their hinterlands: Santiago, León, Granada, and Mérida were bases, not proof of control over surrounding territory. Tribute and labor demands could turn allies into rebels, and geography mattered enormously, from the volcanic highlands to the waterless limestone plain. Disease devastated Indigenous societies but did not eliminate resistance, since communities that survived epidemics continued to organize and to fight.

Most importantly, military victory, Christian conversion, settlement, legal incorporation, and effective governance occurred on different timelines. A ruler could be defeated in a season, a town founded in a month, and a province brought into the legal framework of encomienda in a year, while real control, if it came at all, took decades. Chiapas illustrates the point especially well: scholars note that there is no final date for its conquest, because resistance and competing Spanish claims continued while frontier regions remained unresolved. The Itza of Petén, independent until 1697, make the same point for the Maya lowlands.

Some Indigenous auxiliaries did not return home. Nahua and other central Mexican participants settled in Guatemala and elsewhere, forming communities that kept distinct identities for generations. They were conquerors in Spanish eyes and subjects in their own, and their history shows that the invasion created new Indigenous as well as new colonial communities.

The same pattern, in which invaders entered divided Indigenous political worlds, gained local allies, and won spectacular victories without immediately creating secure colonial rule, would appear again in the Andes, but within the very different political landscape of the Inca civil war. Chapter 35 turns to that story.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Autonomous Polities to a Patchwork of Rule

What Changed

  • Independent states such as the K'iche' lost their political independence: capitals were destroyed, senior rulers were killed, and dynastic authority was disrupted and reorganized, although noble lineages survived under colonial rule
  • Spanish towns, cabildos, and encomienda jurisdictions were imposed alongside and over existing Indigenous political and territorial institutions
  • Tribute, labor, and enslavement were reorganized around Spanish demands
  • Epidemic disease, violence, and flight reduced and displaced populations
  • Many former allies were incorporated into colonial tribute and labor systems, while some allied and auxiliary groups secured privileges or maintained distinct corporate identities

What Stayed the Same

  • Maya languages, community structures, and local lords persisted in many places, and surviving noble lineages continued to claim authority and to write histories defending it
  • Indigenous political rivalries, such as those between Xiu and Cocom or K'iche' and Kaqchikel, continued to shape alliances and grievances
  • Large frontier regions, including the Petén forests and parts of Chiapas, stayed outside effective Spanish control
  • Spaniards remained dependent on Indigenous food, labor, and knowledge, and ritual and agricultural practices continued under colonial rule

Who experienced change most directly: ruling lineages in defeated states, enslaved captives, and communities bearing tribute and labor demands. Greater continuity: remote lowland and forest communities, and many village-level institutions and practices. Why the pattern matters: it shows colonial rule as a patchwork of towns, allied communities, subject districts, and unconquered zones, with Indigenous authority persisting in altered forms, not as a uniform replacement of one order by another.

Cause and Effect

From Indigenous Rivalry to Renewed War

Long-term causes

Long rivalries among highland and Yucatec polities, regional political fragmentation and multiple competing polities, and tribute relationships that left communities seeking allies against neighbors. Established Mesoamerican roads and exchange networks also made rapid movement possible.

Short-term causes

The arrival of Spanish and central Mexican armies after 1521, epidemic disruption in the highlands, and Spanish need for food, labor, and gold to reward followers and repay investors.

Event or transformation

Indigenous leaders allied with the newcomers against local rivals, as the Kaqchikel did against the K'iche' in 1524.

Immediate effects

Defeat of a leading power, the founding of Santiago at Iximché, and rapid growth of Spanish demands on allied communities for provisions, tribute, and labor.

Long-term consequences

Breakdown of the alliance, years of warfare, negotiated subordination of major rulers, and a patchwork of Spanish towns, subject districts, and unconquered zones.

Historical complexity

Alliance was calculated, not naive, and its failure came from unequal power and escalating demands. Geography, disease, Spanish rivalries, and Indigenous politics all contributed, so no single cause explains the outcome.
Everyday Life and Social History

Porters, Provisions, and Settlers

Campaigns rested on ordinary labor. Porters carried food, weapons, and baggage over mountains and through forests, and many died of hunger, exhaustion, and disease. Households along the routes had to feed passing armies, and women ground maize and prepared food for thousands of people. Farmers lost harvests to requisition, and communities that hosted columns for days could be left without seed or stores. Spanish sources mention these burdens briefly, because their authors cared about strategy and rewards.

Warfare also meant enslavement, displacement, and flight. Captives were branded and sold or put to work, and families fled to forests and ravines, as the Kaqchikel did when they left Iximché. Communities that returned had to rebuild houses, fields, and local government, often under new tribute demands. In Nicaragua, enslaved Indigenous people were shipped to Panama, Peru, and other Spanish settlements, uprooting people from their communities and often separating families.

Not all Indigenous participants experienced the conquest in the same way. Auxiliaries from central Mexico received plunder, privileges, and standing, and some settled in Guatemala, founding communities that kept distinct identities for generations. Their experience differed from that of the people whose land they entered. The silences in the sources about women, children, porters, and commoners are evidence of whose lives the surviving texts recorded and whose they did not.

Chapter Summary

After Tenochtitlan fell in 1521, conquest of the rest of Mesoamerica and Central America took decades. This chapter has argued that the cause lay in the political worlds Spaniards entered and in the conditional nature of the alliances they made.

Expeditions moved south along existing roads and trade networks through Oaxaca, Chiapas, and the Soconusco, using interpreters and local knowledge. The forces were multinational: Nahua, Mixtec, Zapotec, and other Indigenous auxiliaries from central Mexico and Oaxaca greatly outnumbered the Spaniards in the Guatemala invasion and in other major campaigns, though Spanish sources count them poorly and the proportions varied by expedition.

In the Guatemalan highlands, Alvarado's coalition entered a landscape of rival Maya states. The long K'iche'–Kaqchikel rivalry gave the Kaqchikel reason to ally with the newcomers. In 1524 the coalition defeated the K'iche', and Q'umarkaj was burned. The later tradition of Tecún Umán is distinct from what contemporary sources establish.

The Kaqchikel alliance and the founding of Santiago at Iximché in July 1524 seemed to serve both sides. But Spanish demands for tribute, labor, and gold broke the alliance within weeks. The Kaqchikel left Iximché and resisted for years, and major rulers submitted around 1530 after prolonged war, with consolidation continuing afterward.

In Cuscatlán, Pipil resistance and Alvarado's wounding showed that the same coalition could be checked in a different setting, and control required repeated campaigns. In Honduras and Nicaragua, Spanish conquest was itself fragmented: Olid defied Cortés, Cortés crossed the Maya lowlands in 1524–1526, and expeditions from Panama, the Caribbean, and Mexico competed, with Pedrarias executing Hernández de Córdoba.

Indigenous peoples in these regions negotiated, resisted, and suffered. Nicaragua's Indigenous slave trade, the Lenca resistance in Honduras, and the forced labor of porters show the human cost, while the survival of local communities and their writings shows that Indigenous history continued.

Yucatán differed because it had no single center. Its many Maya provinces allowed alliances but prevented quick victory, and the Montejo campaigns of 1527–1540 failed repeatedly before Mérida was founded in 1542 with Maya and central Mexican allies. The 1546–1547 uprising showed that control remained contested.

Across these regions, Spanish towns did not equal control of their hinterlands. Chiapas has no final date of conquest, and the Itza of Petén remained independent until 1697. Military victory, conversion, settlement, legal incorporation, and governance followed different timelines.

The chapter's evidence is partial. Spanish letters justified their authors, and Indigenous annals were written under colonial rule for later purposes. Reading them together shows both the course of events and the limits of what can be known.

The pattern, in which invaders entered divided Indigenous worlds, gained allies, and won victories without secure rule, leads to the Andes in Chapter 35.

Key Terms

Entrada
An armed expedition into territory not yet under Spanish control, authorized by the Crown, a governor, or sometimes only the commander.
Adelantado
A leader holding a royal contract to conquer and govern a frontier region.
Capitulación
A royal contract granting rights and obligations to a conquest leader.
Pacificación
Spanish administrative term for subjugating a region; it concealed violence, negotiation, and resistance.
Encomienda
A grant giving a Spaniard rights to Indigenous tribute and, early on, labor or services, with formal duties of protection and instruction; not a grant of land or ownership of people.
Cabildo
A Spanish municipal council, created when a town was founded.
Auxiliary
An Indigenous ally or participant in Spanish campaigns, with goals and status distinct from Spanish soldiers.
Tamemes
Indigenous porters who carried loads in the absence of draft animals.
K'iche'
A highland Maya polity centered at Q'umarkaj, defeated by Alvarado's coalition in 1524.
Kaqchikel
A highland Maya polity centered at Iximché that allied with Alvarado and later rebelled.
Tz'utujil
A Maya polity around Lake Atitlán, also campaigned against by Alvarado.
Pipil
Nahua-speaking peoples of Cuscatlán and neighboring regions of present-day El Salvador.
Chorotega, Nicarao, Matagalpa
Indigenous peoples of Nicaragua with distinct languages and histories.
Lenca
Indigenous people of Honduras whose resistance is associated with the tradition of Lempira.
Batab
A local Maya lord who governed a town within a Yucatec province.

A Yucatec Maya territorial jurisdiction, often translated as province.

A Kaqchikel-language colonial text preserving lineage history and an account of the Spanish arrival.

The K'iche' capital, often called Utatlán, burned in 1524.
Iximché
The Kaqchikel capital where Santiago de Guatemala was founded in 1524.
Xiu and Cocom
Rival Yucatec lineages centered at Maní and Sotuta, whose rivalry shaped alliances.
Hinterland
The territory around a town, which a Spanish foundation did not automatically control.
Frontier
A zone where state authority was contested or incomplete.

The distinction between military victory and effective political, legal, and religious rule.

A Maya polity at Nojpetén in Petén, independent until 1697.
Tecún Umán
A K'iche' figure of Guatemalan national memory; the heroic duel with Alvarado is not securely contemporary.
Lempira
Lenca leader attested in a 1558 probanza and associated with resistance in the 1530s; Spanish accounts disagree about the circumstances of his death.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter closes Part VII by dismantling a familiar story: that a few Spaniards conquered millions of people through superior weapons and daring. Across Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Yucatán, small Spanish forces operated alongside much larger Indigenous armies, entered political worlds they understood poorly, and relied on food, labor, guides, and allies they did not control. Spanish letters, written to honor their authors and justify their conduct, magnified individual heroism and obscured these dependencies. Reading against them, as this chapter has tried to do, reveals a conquest made by coalitions.

Indigenous alliance must not be confused with passive submission. The Kaqchikel allied with Alvarado to serve their own interests against the K'iche', and when Spanish demands outweighed the benefits, they abandoned their capital and fought. Maya provinces in Yucatán made and unmade alliances according to local calculations. Central Mexican auxiliaries occupied an especially complicated position: they were conquerors in Spanish eyes, sought privileges and plunder, and in many cases settled in Guatemala and elsewhere, only to become subjects in the colonial order they had helped create. Alliance opened possibilities and also created new vulnerabilities.

The chapter also shows that victory, settlement, conversion, legal incorporation, and effective control occurred on different timelines. A capital could be burned in a season, a town founded in a month, and a province assigned in encomienda within a year, while real governance took decades or never fully arrived. Frontier autonomy therefore matters for the colonial chapters ahead. Chiapas, Petén, and other zones of contested authority shaped labor, tribute, missions, and violence, and they remind us that colonial Latin America was a patchwork, not a uniform territory ruled from a few cities.

Chapter 35 turns to the Andes, where the conquest again began with a few hundred Spaniards entering a divided political world, this time shaped by the civil war between Huáscar and Atahualpa. Students should carry these questions forward: Who were the local actors, what did they hope to gain, and what did Spaniards need from them? What did victory in a single battle or capture of a single ruler actually secure? And whose voices remain in the evidence, and whose have been lost?
