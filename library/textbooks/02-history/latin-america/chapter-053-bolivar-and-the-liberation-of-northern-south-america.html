Bolívar and the Liberation of Northern South America

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Republics That Fell, and the Republic That Had to Be Built

Between 1810 and 1823, northern South America produced at least four republics, saw three of them destroyed, and finally founded a fourth that stretched from the Orinoco to the equator. The decisive difficulty was never only defeating Spanish armies. It was assembling a coalition of landowners, soldiers, free people of color, enslaved people, and Indigenous communities that could win a war and then agree on how to govern.

When the Junta of Caracas deposed Spain's Captain General on 19 April 1810, nobody could have predicted that the movement would last thirteen years. The junta claimed to act in the name of the imprisoned Ferdinand VII, and many of its supporters hoped for autonomy within a reformed monarchy. Within fifteen months Venezuela had declared independence and adopted a federal constitution. Within twenty-seven months the republic had collapsed in the face of an earthquake, a royalist counteroffensive, and its own divisions. That sequence, a rapid rise and an equally rapid fall, would be repeated in 1813–1814 and again, in different form, in 1815–1816.

The wars of northern South America were fought across very different landscapes: the Caribbean coast and its cacao valleys, the vast cattle plains (llanos) of the Orinoco basin, the Andean cordilleras of New Granada and Quito, the river ports of the Magdalena, and the Pacific lowlands. Each region had its own elites, labor systems, and loyalties. Royalism was not an alien imposition. It drew on real regional interests, on the loyalties of many pardos and enslaved people who distrusted creole planters, on Indigenous communities protecting their lands from patriot taxation, and on the military power of the Spanish state. Patriots fought royalists, but they also fought one another.

Simón Bolívar was born into one of Caracas's wealthiest slaveholding families, and this chapter follows him through defeat, exile, and reinvention. But it does not treat him as the only author of liberation. Francisco de Miranda, Antonio Nariño, Francisco de Paula Santander, José Antonio Páez, Manuel Piar, Antonio José de Sucre, José Prudencio Padilla, and royalist commanders such as Domingo de Monteverde, José Tomás Boves, and Pablo Morillo all shaped events. So did women, such as Policarpa Salavarrieta and Manuela Sáenz, who acted as couriers, informants, financiers, and political participants, and so did the llaneros, whose shifting allegiances changed the war.

Slavery runs through the story. Enslaved people fled, enlisted, rebelled, and bargained with both sides. Bolívar's 1816 pledge to Haiti's president Alexandre Pétion, his decrees of emancipation, and the 1821 law of gradual emancipation passed at Cúcuta show how a leader and a congress balanced principle, military need, and slaveholder interests. They did not produce universal freedom. The Haitian Revolution, treated in Chapter 50, is therefore part of this chapter's argument, not merely its background.

Evidence deserves attention from the outset. Bolívar was a prolific writer, and his letters, proclamations, and speeches survive in abundance, but they were composed to persuade, to justify, and to mobilize, and they were later collected and curated within a nationalist cult of the Liberator. Royalist officers' reports, ecclesiastical records, congressional proceedings, British diplomats' dispatches, and the memoirs of foreign volunteers such as Daniel O'Leary supply other angles. Plebeian participants left comparatively few written records, so historians infer their motives from military, legal, and fiscal documents, and the silences are themselves a historical problem.

This chapter belongs to Part XII, which treats independence as several interconnected wars. It begins where Chapter 51 left the Iberian crisis, with the legitimacy of Spanish rule broken, and it follows northern South America to the end of the 1823 campaigns. Chapter 52 treated Mexico; Chapter 54 turns to San Martín in the south, and Chapter 55 to the last wars in the Andes, including Junín and Ayacucho. The institutional crisis and dissolution of Gran Colombia belongs to Chapter 60. The task here is to explain how independence was won in the north and what kind of state it created.

Historical Context

Chapter 48 described how Bourbon reform increased taxation and centralization after 1760, and Chapter 49 treated the colonial uprisings that preceded independence, including the Comunero revolt in New Granada (1781). Chapter 51 explained the collapse of imperial legitimacy after Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain: when the Crown was captive, many Americans asked who held sovereignty, and the Cádiz Cortes and the Spanish Regency offered competing answers. This chapter begins where that argument turned into government, with American juntas claiming to hold authority in Ferdinand VII's absence.

In 1810 the region was administratively unstable. The Viceroyalty of New Granada, created in 1739 with its capital at Santafé de Bogotá, included the territory of present-day Colombia, Panama, and, in many respects, the Audiencia of Quito (present-day Ecuador). Venezuela formed a separate Captaincy General, created in 1777, with its capital at Caracas and its own intendancy. These jurisdictions had long histories of mutual rivalry. Caracas looked to the Atlantic cacao and coffee trade; Bogotá to the highlands; Quito to its textile workshops and the Andean sierra; Cartagena and Santa Marta to the Caribbean and to royal garrisons.

Atlantic slavery shaped all of these societies. The Venezuelan coast and Caribbean New Granada had substantial enslaved and free African-descended populations, who worked cacao, sugar, and cattle estates and the gold mines of the Pacific coast, while the Andean highlands relied on Indigenous tribute and labor. The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) had shown both the possibility of emancipation and the danger that planters feared, and creole elites in Caracas remembered Chirino's 1795 uprising in Coro. The influence of the United States, Britain, and France shaped political ideas, and Britain's naval power and commercial interests shaped what was possible.

The chapter also treats earlier scholarship cautiously. Nineteenth-century national histories made Bolívar the founding hero of Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Bolivia, and Peru, and that nationalist inheritance shaped what sources were collected and how they were read. Recent research has restored attention to royalism, to race and plebeian politics, to local studies of cities and rural districts, and to the transnational context. This chapter follows that more complex picture.

Division I — The First Republics and Their Collapse · Section 53.1

Northern South America on the Eve of Independence

Venezuela's population in 1810 was roughly 800,000, with estimates varying, and its social structure was marked by a large free population of mixed ancestry and a large enslaved population concentrated in the coastal valleys. A creole elite of cacao planters, merchants, and officials, often called mantuanos in Caracas, held landed wealth and municipal office and cherished social privileges that pardos resented. The elite worried less about Spain than about maintaining its authority over a majority that was neither white nor wealthy. Beyond the coast lay the llanos, a vast savanna region where cattle ranches (hatos) employed free and enslaved horsemen, and where the state's presence was thin.

New Granada's population was larger, perhaps one to two million, and far more diverse by region. Highlands around Bogotá and Tunja held large Indigenous and mestizo populations, many living in Indigenous communal landholdings called resguardos and paying tribute. The Caribbean coast, including Cartagena, Santa Marta, and Mompox, had substantial African-descended populations and a mercantile elite. The Cauca valley and Pacific lowlands depended on enslaved labor in gold mining and plantations. Quito's economy rested on highland textile production and Indigenous labor under concertaje (debt-based labor obligations), and its elite had long resented Bogotá and Lima. Regional loyalties were deep, and cities often saw themselves as sovereign communities, a legacy that mattered once central authority disappeared.

Historical Vocabulary
Pardo

In Venezuela and New Granada, pardo referred to free people of mixed African, European, and sometimes Indigenous ancestry. It was a legal and social category, not a biological classification, and its meaning shifted with place, wealth, occupation, and local reputation.

Pardos formed a large share of artisans, militia soldiers, small farmers, and ranch hands. Many held militia commissions yet were barred from elite privileges, and the elite's fear of a racial uprising, sometimes called "pardocracia," shaped republican politics. Students should avoid imagining pardos as a single bloc: they fought on both sides, and their motives varied.

Royal authority had been uneven. Spanish regiments were few, and colonial militias, often composed of pardos officered by creoles, carried much of the burden of defense. Colonial taxation, monopolies on tobacco and aguardiente, and the alcabala sales tax generated grievances. Yet before 1808 few Americans had called for independence. The crisis arrived from Europe, as Chapter 51 explained, and its first effects were to stir up the question of who would govern in the king's absence.

Section 53.2

Caracas, the Junta of 1810, and Venezuelan Independence

On Holy Thursday, 19 April 1810, the Caracas cabildo met in an expanded session, rejected the authority of Captain General Vicente Emparan and the Spanish Regency, and formed the Junta Suprema in the name of Ferdinand VII. Creole elites led the move, but they also needed popular acceptance and worried about royal reprisal. The junta sent diplomats to Britain, including Simón Bolívar, Luis López Méndez, and Andrés Bello, seeking recognition and support, and it called a congress that met in March 1811. Bolívar, still young and without military rank, took part in the Sociedad Patriótica, a club that pressed for independence.

Francisco de Miranda, back in Venezuela after decades of European and Atlantic revolutionary experience, joined the radicals. After debate, Congress declared independence on 5 July 1811, and in December adopted a federal constitution modeled partly on the United States. The text abolished the slave trade and extended formal equality before the law to free pardos, but it kept slavery and property qualifications for the vote. These compromises reflected elite priorities: independence from Spain without social upheaval.

Not all of Venezuela followed Caracas. Coro, Maracaibo, and Guayana declined to join, and the Spanish Regency declared a blockade. A revolt in Valencia in mid-1811 was crushed by Miranda and Bolívar's first major experience in the field. Meanwhile the new republic's finances were weak, its army inexperienced, and its federal structure left provinces with substantial independence. The republic had formal institutions but little capacity to enforce them, and royalist enclaves remained within striking distance of the capital.

Section 53.3

New Granada, Quito, and Competing Revolutionary Governments

In New Granada the crisis unfolded differently. A confrontation in Bogotá on 20 July 1810, sparked by a dispute over a loaned flower vase and exploited by creole leaders, led to a junta that deposed the viceroy's authority, a date later celebrated as Colombia's independence day. Cartagena, the strongest Caribbean city, formed its own junta in 1810 and declared absolute independence in November 1811, while other cities such as Socorro, Tunja, and Mompox did likewise. New Granada therefore fragmented into city-states and provinces, each asserting sovereignty in the king's absence and each suspicious of Bogotá.

Quito had already experienced a junta in August 1809, which royal troops suppressed, and on 2 August 1810 soldiers and crowds killed imprisoned patriots and many civilians, an event that radicalized the region. A new Junta Suprema in 1811 produced a constitution in February 1812 for a "State of Quito," but royalist forces from Lima and Popayán reconquered the city by November 1812. Pasto, in the southern highlands, became the strongest royalist base in the north, supported by local elites, Indigenous communities, and the Church.

Taken together, these movements show why northern independence was not a single revolution. Some governments aimed at autonomy under the king, others at full independence, and still others at preserving the privileges of a single city. Slavery itself survived under every early patriot government. The First Venezuelan Republic restricted the slave trade in its 1811 constitution, and Antioquia enacted a free-birth law in 1814, but restricting a trade or freeing future children was not abolishing slavery, and no early government ended it. Indigenous communities were courted when they were needed for tribute or troops and neglected otherwise. The political vocabulary of popular sovereignty, representation, and rights was new, but it was applied by narrow elites to a society organized by hierarchy.

Section 53.4

Federalism, Centralism, and the Divisions of New Granada

Within New Granada, the debate over how to organize a free state quickly became a civil war. Antonio Nariño, a Bogotá intellectual who had earlier been imprisoned for printing a Spanish translation of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man, led the State of Cundinamarca, which favored a strong central government around Bogotá. Camilo Torres and other leaders in Tunja and elsewhere championed a federation, and in 1811 the provinces formed the United Provinces of New Granada. Each side feared domination by the other, and neither could assume the loyalty of the cities it claimed to lead.

The conflict produced skirmishes in 1812 and 1813 and left the patriots divided at the moment royalist threats were growing in Pasto, Popayán, and Santa Marta. Nariño's expedition south in 1813–1814 won initial successes but ended in defeat near Pasto and his capture in May 1814. In December 1814, Bolívar, then commanding forces of the United Provinces, entered Bogotá and compelled Cundinamarca to join the federation, but unity was provisional. The lesson that Bolívar drew, that federal weakness had cost the patriots a reconquest, shaped his later preference for stronger central authority.

Students should be careful not to read this conflict as a simple contest of good and bad constitutional models. Both federalism and centralism served the interests of particular regions and social groups, and both were defended with arguments drawn from Enlightenment political writing. The weaknesses revealed were practical: shortages of money, discipline, and trained officers; competing local militias; and the reluctance of elites to share power or revenue.

Section 53.5

Miranda, Monteverde, and the Fall of the First Venezuelan Republic

In early 1812 a Spanish naval captain, Domingo de Monteverde, advanced from Coro with a small force and gathered local royalist support. On 26 March 1812, Holy Thursday, a powerful earthquake struck Caracas, La Guaira, Mérida, and other towns, and damaging patriot-held areas far more than royalist ones. Mortality estimates must be handled with care: older accounts gave very high figures for the whole affected region, while research in burial registers and archaeology suggests on the order of 2,000 deaths in Caracas itself, and city and regional figures should not be combined. Priests blamed divine anger at the rebellion, and royalists used the disaster. Miranda was appointed to supreme military command with dictatorial powers, but he lacked reliable troops and money.

A slave uprising in the Barlovento region, along with unrest among free people of color, added to the crisis in the east. These movements are often interpreted as loyalist because their participants claimed to act for the king, but historians also see in them an opportunity seized to challenge planters who were also patriot leaders. In early July, royalist forces took Puerto Cabello, where Bolívar commanded the fortress and the garrison was guarding royalist prisoners; he lost the fortress after an internal betrayal, an episode that deeply marked him.

Miranda signed a capitulation with Monteverde at San Mateo on 25 July 1812. Monteverde did not honor its guarantees, and Miranda was arrested at La Guaira on 31 July, partly through the action of a group of patriot officers including Bolívar, who feared Miranda's decision to surrender. Miranda was shipped to Spain and died in a Cádiz prison in 1816. The first republic's fall highlights the limits of an elite-led revolution with weak institutions, divided loyalties, a hostile Church, and an enslaved and mixed-race majority that had no clear stake in its survival.

Division II — Bolívar, Civil War, and Royalist Reconquest · Section 53.6

The Cartagena Manifesto and the Admirable Campaign

Bolívar escaped to Curaçao and then to Cartagena, where on 15 December 1812 he published a memorial addressed to the citizens of New Granada. The text offered an explanation for the fall of Venezuela's republic and an argument for an immediate campaign. He then received a small command from the authorities of Cartagena and the United Provinces, and in early 1813 he cleared the Magdalena River of royalists and occupied Cúcuta. From there he decided, without clear authorization, to invade Venezuela.

Primary Source Window

The Cartagena Manifesto (1812)

Creator: Simón Bolívar · Date and place: 15 December 1812, Cartagena de Indias · Type: Political memorial and public appeal

Context: Bolívar wrote weeks after fleeing Venezuela, as a refugee seeking the support of New Granada's governments. He had no official rank among them and needed to persuade them to fight.

Paraphrase: Bolívar argues that Venezuela's first republic fell because its leaders chose an idealistic federal system unsuited to wartime, relied on untrained militia, were too lenient with internal enemies, allowed the clergy to side with royalism, and wasted resources. He urges New Granada to learn from this experience, to unify, and to help liberate Venezuela, because Caracas's survival is bound up with its own security.

Analysis: The document is both diagnosis and advocacy. It reveals Bolívar's early preference for strong executive power and for treating the war as an emergency that justified exceptional measures. It also minimizes his own role at Puerto Cabello and the roles of social and racial conflict. As a text addressed to a specific audience, it is better evidence of what Bolívar wanted to persuade people to believe than of an objective account of the republic's fall.

Questions for students:

  1. What explanations does Bolívar give for the republic's collapse?
  2. Who was his audience and what did he need from them?
  3. What can historians learn about early republican political thought?
  4. What social or racial factors does the document leave largely unexamined?

The ensuing "Admirable Campaign" of 1813 moved from Cúcuta through Mérida and Trujillo to Caracas. In Mérida in May the local cabildo hailed Bolívar as Libertador, a title that would later be formalized in Caracas. At the same time, Santiago Mariño and other eastern leaders had launched a separate campaign from Trinidad into the east, so the republic's revival was shared among regional commanders whose cooperation was uneasy. Bolívar entered Caracas on 6 August 1813, after Monteverde's forces retreated to the coast.

Section 53.7

The Second Venezuelan Republic and the War to the Death

In June 1813, from Trujillo, Bolívar had proclaimed the "War to the Death" (guerra a muerte). The decree declared that Spaniards and Canary Islanders who did not actively support independence would be killed, while Americans would be spared even if they had served the royalist side. Its authors intended to separate American-born royalists from Peninsulars and to frame the war as a conflict between Americans and Europeans. In practice, it licensed brutality and drew a reciprocal response from royalist commanders.

The second republic that Bolívar led from Caracas was very different from the first. He ruled as a dictator with the backing of the cabildo, which in October 1813 conferred on him the title of Libertador, and he concentrated power in a military-run state. It never controlled more than the central region and parts of the east. Its leaders also competed. Mariño commanded in the east, Bolívar in the center, and Piar and others held independent commands. Arrests, summary executions, and in February 1814 the killing of several hundred Spanish prisoners at La Guaira and Caracas deepened royalist resolve.

Women participated actively on both sides and suffered consequences. Juana Ramírez, remembered as "La Avanzadora," is associated with an artillery action at Maturín in 1813, though documentation is thin and her story was embellished by later memory. The wives and families of patriots endured confiscation, imprisonment, and exile, and the republic's fiscal demands fell heavily on households. Meanwhile, the royalist response drew in a new force from the plains.

Section 53.8

Boves, the Llaneros, and the Social Dimensions of Civil War

José Tomás Boves, an Asturian-born merchant and ranch hand who had been imprisoned by the patriots, raised an army in the llanos in 1813–1814. His following, composed largely of llaneros, free and enslaved men, and pardos, fought in the name of the king but pursued their own goals: plunder, social advancement, an end to patriot privilege, and in some cases freedom or land. Boves's army defeated Bolívar and Mariño at La Puerta in February and again in June 1814, and in July took Valencia and entered Caracas.

Historical Vocabulary
Llanero

A llanero was an inhabitant of the llanos, the savanna plains of the Orinoco basin, especially the cattle herders and horsemen who worked the region's ranches. They included free people of mixed ancestry, enslaved ranch workers, and some landowners.

Llaneros were not a military caste by origin. They were rural workers whose skills as riders and lancers, and whose independence from coastal elites, made them formidable in war. Their allegiance was negotiated, and they supported whichever side offered rewards, protection, or status. Treating them merely as "royalists who switched sides" misses the dynamics of leadership, local rivalry, and plebeian ambition.

The patriot population of Caracas and the central valleys fled eastward in what became known as the Emigración a Oriente in July 1814, thousands of civilians marching toward Barcelona and Cumaná amid hunger and disease. Bolívar's Second Republic collapsed and he left for New Granada. Boves was killed at the battle of Urica in December 1814, after which his officers, especially Francisco Tomás Morales, continued the campaign. Boves himself was feared for his brutality and revered by his followers, a contradiction that reveals the social complexity of royalism.

The Boves episode shows why historians describe the wars as civil. Many of the soldiers who defeated the patriots were Venezuelans, and their grievances were aimed at creole landowners as much as at Bolívar. The revolutionary elite had neither offered the plebeian majority a stake in independence nor shown any inclination to end slavery, and this oversight shaped the strategies that Bolívar would later adopt.

Section 53.9

Bolívar in New Granada and the Fragmentation of the Revolution

The year 1814 left New Granada with several rival patriot governments and no single authority able to command them. The United Provinces, governed by a Congress presided over by Camilo Torres, sat first in Tunja and then in Bogotá; Cundinamarca had been Antonio Nariño's state until his defeat and capture at Pasto in May 1814; and Cartagena de Indias, the wealthiest and best-fortified port, treated its own junta as the sovereign of a separate state. Cartagena's independence in November 1811 had been shaped by the popular quarter of Getsemaní, whose artisans and free people of color, led by figures such as Pedro Romero, pressed the elite toward a break with Spain. That popular base made Cartagena's republicanism distinctive, and it also made its leaders reluctant to subordinate themselves to Bogotá.

When Bolívar entered Bogotá in December 1814 at the head of troops sent by the United Provinces, he compelled Cundinamarca to join the federation and received a commission to subdue Santa Marta, which remained in royalist hands and threatened Cartagena's rear. The campaign required supplies, boats, and cooperation that Cartagena's authorities were unwilling to give. Manuel del Castillo y Rada, the senior military figure in Cartagena, distrusted Bolívar, and personal rivalry combined with a fear that an outsider would dominate the port. Bolívar's demands in early 1815 produced a standoff in which patriot forces faced one another while a royalist fleet was already sailing from Spain.

The dispute illustrates a recurring pattern. Each patriot government rested on a city or province whose elites defended local sovereignty and local revenue, and the Congress of the United Provinces had neither an army of its own nor a tax system that could pay one. Commanders such as Bolívar often saw such resistance as sabotage, while local leaders saw outsiders' requisitions as the first step toward domination. Neither view captures the whole situation, because both sides were improvising institutions in the middle of a civil war. In May 1815, Bolívar resigned his command and left for Jamaica, partly in the hope of obtaining foreign help and partly to avoid becoming the focus of a patriot civil war.

Section 53.10

Morillo's Expedition and the Spanish Reconquest, 1815–1816

The context of Morillo's expedition needs careful statement. After Napoleon's defeat, Ferdinand VII returned to Spain in 1814, abolished the liberal constitution of 1812, and prepared a large expeditionary army, approximately ten thousand soldiers on a fleet of more than fifty vessels (estimates vary), under General Pablo Morillo. By the time it reached the coast of Venezuela in April 1815, royalists already controlled much of the country: Boves's llanero campaign of 1814 had destroyed the Second Republic, and his successors, notably Francisco Tomás Morales, held the center. The expedition therefore did not so much conquer Venezuela as consolidate and regularize royalist authority there, bringing regular troops, a Captain General's administration, and a program of pacification, and then it turned to the real target, New Granada.

Morillo's soldiers and ships reached Cartagena in August 1815, and the city endured a siege of roughly four months. Its defenders, supplied by sea only intermittently, ran short of food, and hunger and disease killed several thousand people before the city surrendered on 6 December 1815; many survivors fled by sea in small vessels, some under the protection of Luis Brión's privateers. The reconquest of the interior followed, and Morillo's columns entered Bogotá in May 1816. The viceregal administration was restored, with Juan de Sámano arriving as viceroy in 1817.

Spanish rule after the reconquest relied on special tribunals and confiscation. Courts-martial and juntas of purification tried officials, lawyers, and officers who had served the republics, and executed many, including Camilo Torres, Francisco José de Caldas, Jorge Tadeo Lozano, and other figures of the intellectual and political elite. The patriots later called this period a reign of terror, and the memory of the executions strengthened separatist feeling among creoles who had once hoped for autonomy within the monarchy. Policarpa Salavarrieta, a Bogotá seamstress who passed information and supplies to guerrilla groups, was executed on 14 November 1817 and became a symbol of resistance. Her case also illustrates how women's work in intelligence and logistics was vital and dangerous.

Spanish restoration did not produce stability. Royalist armies were expensive, relied heavily on locally recruited soldiers, and increasingly antagonized creoles through taxation, requisitions, and executions. Yet by the end of 1816 the Crown nominally governed the entire north, and the patriots survived only as dispersed guerrilla bands in the llanos and Casanare, in eastern Venezuela, and on the island of Margarita, and as exiles in Haiti and the Caribbean. Their return depended on rethinking the war.

Division III — Exile, Emancipation, and Revolutionary Reconstruction · Section 53.11

Bolívar in Exile and the Jamaica Letter of 1815

Bolívar arrived in Kingston in May 1815 as a defeated commander without office. Jamaica was a British slave colony, and its planters and officials were wary of revolutionary politics, which they associated with Haiti. The British government remained officially neutral in Spain's colonial conflicts, since it was Spain's ally against France and valued the Spanish connection, even as British merchants traded with patriot ports and some officers and investors showed sympathy. Bolívar lived on loans and the help of merchants, including the house of Maxwell Hyslop, and tried to interest British opinion and officials in recognition, arms, and credit. These efforts produced little official result in 1815.

His circumstances shaped his political writing. As an exile addressing foreign readers, he had to present the American cause as both just and viable, and he needed to explain why the earlier republics had failed without implying that the project itself was hopeless. He had seen federal experiments fail in Venezuela and New Granada, the dispersal of patriot forces, and the arrival of a large Spanish army, and he concluded that Spanish America lacked the habits of self-government and the institutions for a loose confederation. His analysis also reflected his hope that Britain might support a stable, orderly American government rather than one that resembled the radical republics of the French and Haitian revolutions.

In December 1815 an assassin, apparently paid by Spanish agents, killed Bolívar's servant in his place, and soon afterward Bolívar left for Haiti. The Jamaican months were therefore a period of intellectual redirection rather than of military action. They closed with the recognition that British help would not come soon, that the war would depend on resources the patriots could assemble themselves, and that Haiti, the only independent American state governed by people who had abolished slavery, might offer both refuge and aid.

Section 53.12

Haiti, Alexandre Pétion, and the Promise of Emancipation

Haiti in 1815 was divided between a southern republic under Alexandre Pétion, based at Port-au-Prince, and a northern state under Henri Christophe. Pétion, a free man of color who had fought in the revolution, governed a state that no major power recognized and that France still threatened with reconquest or demands for compensation. Aid to a Spanish American rebellion carried risk, but it also served Haitian interests by weakening a hostile colonial power and by creating potential partners in a region dominated by slaveholding states.

Bolívar reached Les Cayes in December 1815, and his first documented meeting with Pétion took place on 2 January 1816. Sources agree that Pétion provided weapons, ammunition, ships, supplies, and a printing press, and that he linked his assistance to a condition that Bolívar accepted: that the patriots proclaim freedom for enslaved people in the territories they liberated. The quantities involved, including the number of muskets and the sums of money, are variously reported in later accounts and should be treated as approximate. Pétion preferred discretion, and the aid was arranged with the help of the merchant-admiral Luis Brión of Curaçao, who commanded the squadron.

The expedition sailed from Les Cayes on 31 March 1816 and reached Margarita Island in early May, where a patriot assembly recognized Bolívar as supreme chief and proclaimed a renewed republic. On 2 June 1816 at Carúpano, he issued a decree freeing enslaved people, and on 6 July, after landing at Ocumare de la Costa, he issued another proclamation tying freedom to military service and calling on all able-bodied men to enlist. The Ocumare operation failed within days, and Bolívar fled again to Haiti, where Pétion nevertheless helped him prepare a second expedition that sailed in December.

Historians debate how much the decrees reflected principle and how much military need. Bolívar came from a slaveholding family, and an enslaved woman in his household, Hipólita, had cared for him as a child, a fact he later acknowledged with affection. He spoke in terms of republican liberty and justice, but he also feared racial war and needed soldiers, and the decrees were not enforced consistently in areas the patriots could not hold. Enslaved people did respond. Many joined patriot forces, others fled estates, and many bargained with owners and officers on both sides. The Haitian connection was thus not only an episode of solidarity but part of an Atlantic political economy in which emancipation, recruitment, diplomacy, and security were intertwined. Haiti itself received no formal diplomatic reward from the new republic, a fact that later historians have noted.

Section 53.13

The Return to Venezuela and the Reorganization of the Insurgency

The expedition that landed on the coast near Barcelona on 31 December 1816 returned to a Venezuela in which royalist and patriot control was sharply regional. Spanish regulars and Venezuelan royalist units held Caracas and the central valleys, but they could not suppress insurgent bands in the east, in the llanos of Apure and Guárico, on Margarita under Juan Bautista Arismendi, and in Guayana. The patriots had capable local commanders, including Santiago Mariño and José Francisco Bermúdez in the east, Manuel Piar in the southeast, José Antonio Páez in Apure, and llanero leaders such as Pedro Zaraza and José Tadeo Monagas. These men had their own followings and their own ideas about who should lead the revolution, and Bolívar's first task was to establish his authority over them.

The year 1817 began unevenly. On 27 September 1816, before Bolívar's second landing, republican forces under Piar and the Scottish adventurer Gregor MacGregor had won a victory at El Juncal, near Barcelona, which helped keep the eastern insurgency alive. But Bolívar's attempt to operate in the center failed, and in April 1817 royalist troops took the Casa Fuerte of Barcelona, killing many of its defenders and civilians in an episode that discouraged republican hopes in the east. A rival assembly led by Mariño at Cariaco in May 1817 created a government that challenged Bolívar's supremacy. Bolívar's response was to turn toward Guayana, where Piar's conquests offered a way to escape the royalist armies and build an independent base.

Bolívar's setbacks continued into 1818. In the early months of that year he joined forces with Páez in the plains, and Morillo, using the central valleys as a base, counterattacked. On 16 March 1818 at Semén, a place also known as the second battle of La Puerta, Bolívar's army was defeated, and he escaped narrowly. Earlier in 1818, at Calabozo, Páez's cavalry had fought a successful engagement against Morillo's army. The military record of these months was mixed, but the pattern was important: the patriots could not defeat Morillo in the mountainous central region, while the llanos and Guayana offered conditions where cavalry, rivers, and distance favored them. The war's center of gravity shifted from the coast to the plains and the Orinoco.

Section 53.14

Guayana, the Orinoco, and the Establishment of Angostura

Guayana, the region south of the lower Orinoco, was thinly populated but strategically valuable. The Orinoco connected the Atlantic to the llanos through the Apure and its tributaries, so control of the river meant access to cattle, to horses, to foreign shipping, and to a route to the Andean foothills of New Granada. The region's Capuchin missions, which had organized Indigenous communities into agricultural and pastoral settlements, held large herds of cattle. Royalist authorities had used them as economic and political bases.

In 1817 Manuel Piar, a pardo officer born in Curaçao who had served in the eastern campaigns, led patriot forces into Guayana. His victory at San Félix on 11 April 1817 destroyed a royalist column and opened the missions and cattle estates, and after a siege supported by Brión's naval squadron, Angostura (modern Ciudad Bolívar) fell in July. The campaign gave the patriots something they had never had: a secure rear area with food, a navigable river, and a port where arms and credit could arrive from the Caribbean. Bolívar moved his headquarters to Angostura and used the base to create institutions.

Angostura became the republic's capital, with a Council of State, a Supreme Court, and in 1818 a newspaper, the Correo del Orinoco, which printed proclamations, news, and arguments for the patriot cause and was read abroad. Revenue came from customs duties, sales of cattle from the mission herds, confiscated royalist property, and sales of goods to foreign merchants. British, Irish, and German volunteers began to arrive in 1817, and by 1819 they formed a substantial portion of the army's infantry, although many died of disease and others deserted when pay failed. Luis López Méndez and other agents recruited volunteers and procured arms in Britain, where the end of the Napoleonic wars had created a supply of veteran officers and surplus weapons.

The Indigenous communities of the missions experienced the conquest of Guayana as an upheaval in an already fragile order. Some mission residents fled, others were conscripted or put to work supplying the army, and the missions' economic system, based on collective labor and communal herds, was dismantled under patriot administration. Because mission communities left few written records of their own, historians reconstruct their experience from missionary reports, military records, and later petitions, and much remains unclear.

Section 53.15

Páez, Piar, the Llaneros, and the Making of a New Army

The army that the patriots assembled between 1817 and 1819 was composed of several elements: commanders with regional followings, foreign volunteers, and plebeian soldiers drawn from the llanos and from the free and enslaved populations of the coasts and rivers. José Antonio Páez, who had risen among the llaneros of Apure through cattle work, banditry, and war, accepted Bolívar as supreme chief at a meeting in January 1818, though Páez remained effectively in command of his own forces. The pact brought into the patriot ranks a cavalry unmatched for mobility, familiarity with the terrain, and endurance, and it showed that Bolívar's authority had to be negotiated with armed regional leaders.

The execution of Manuel Piar in October 1817 was the most dramatic episode in this process of consolidation. Piar, the conqueror of Guayana, resisted Bolívar's supreme command, and in 1817 he sought to leave the service and was accused of desertion, insubordination, and conspiracy. He was arrested at the end of September, tried by a court-martial under the presidency of Admiral Brión, and shot in Angostura on 16 October 1817. The charges that mattered politically were that he had conspired against the government and had stirred up racial hostility against white leaders, a charge with particular force in a society haunted by fear of Haiti.

Historians must distinguish between the allegations and the evidence. Piar's resistance to Bolívar's authority and his unwillingness to submit to command are established. The claim that he was organizing a racial conspiracy, a "war of colors" against whites, rests on accusations by his adversaries and on thin documentary evidence, and scholars such as Sibylle Fischer and others have emphasized that the evidence is weak and that the charge reflected the anxieties of white and creole leaders as well as Bolívar's need to eliminate a rival with a large pardo following. Piar was himself of mixed ancestry, and his career shows that men of color could rise in patriot ranks, but that their ambition could also be portrayed as dangerous. The execution disciplined other commanders and strengthened Bolívar's position, and it also left a legacy of suspicion among the pardo soldiers who formed a large part of the army.

To pay and retain soldiers, patriot authorities in 1817 and after announced that confiscated royalist property would be distributed to soldiers as rewards. The promises were only partly honored and later produced claims and grievances. Foreign volunteers, including British and Irish veterans under officers such as James Rooke and Thomas Ilderton Ferriar, supplied disciplined infantry, but many died in the tropics, and some of the foreign legions mutinied or disbanded when pay failed. The army that emerged was a composite of llanero lancers, free-colored infantry, freed and fugitive enslaved men, Indigenous and mestizo recruits, and foreigners, held together by a mixture of loyalty, plunder, discipline, and leadership.

Section 53.16

The Congress of Angostura and Bolívar's Republican Vision

By 1818 Bolívar recognized that military success required political legitimacy. His authority had been challenged by Mariño's rival assembly at Cariaco in 1817 and by Piar's defiance, and the patriots needed a government that could pass laws, borrow money, and represent the republic abroad. He called a congress, which met at Angostura in February 1819 with a small number of delegates, drawn largely from the regions under patriot control and from exiles who represented areas still held by the royalists. The Congress was thus a representative body only in a limited sense, and its legitimacy rested on the war as much as on the vote.

The postponement of emancipation shows how deeply slaveholding interests were embedded even in a patriot assembly. Division IV — The Liberation Campaigns and Gran Colombia · Section 53.17

The Crossing of the Andes and the Campaign of 1819

In 1819 Bolívar decided to strike west across the Andes into New Granada. The strategic reasoning was clear in outline. Morillo and his army were concentrated in central Venezuela, while New Granada was held by thinly spread royalist forces under Viceroy Sámano and General José María Barreiro, whose troops had been worn down by guerrilla war and who governed a population exasperated by taxation, requisitions, and executions. A successful invasion would bring the patriots a populous region with revenue and recruits, and it would shift the war away from Morillo's strengths. Páez's cavalry, whose victory at Las Queseras del Medio in April 1819 had shown their skill in open country, stayed in the plains to distract the royalists.

Francisco de Paula Santander had organized a force in Casanare, in the eastern foothills of the Andes, from New Granadan exiles and local recruits, and the union of this force with Bolívar's army was essential. After a council of war at the hamlet of Setenta in Apure in late May, Bolívar led an army of some 2,000 to 2,500 men (estimates vary), including the British Legion, across the flooded plains of Arauca in the rainy season. The plains were so waterlogged that men marched for days through water up to their waists, and the army's cattle, horses, and supplies were lost or consumed. In June they reached Tame and joined Santander's division.

The ascent of the Páramo de Pisba in early July 1819 was the central ordeal. The pass lies at over 3,000 meters, and the army crossed it in cold rain, hail, and sleet, with soldiers poorly clothed and many barefoot, and with few animals. Horses died in large numbers, men suffered from exposure, and the British Legion, unaccustomed to such altitude and cold, lost many men. The crossing succeeded because of surprise and logistics: local guides, the food and shelter provided by communities on the far side, and the royalist assumption that the pass could not be crossed in that season. Chroniclers later turned it into a national epic, but its cost was high and its success depended on Barreiro's failure to guard the passes.

Section 53.18

Boyacá and the Liberation of New Granada

The patriots reached the high valleys of Boyacá in early July and fought a series of engagements with Barreiro's army as it moved to block them. At Gámeza on 11–12 July, the patriots met a royalist vanguard in an inconclusive encounter, and on 25 July at Pantano de Vargas a charge of llanero lancers under Juan José Rondón, a pardo cavalry officer, helped rescue an infantry position that was close to collapse. Both sides were exhausted, and the patriots' strategic position was precarious as long as they could not reach Bogotá.

Bolívar then ordered a march by a different route to threaten Tunja and the road to the capital, and on 7 August 1819, near the bridge over the Teatinos at Boyacá, the patriot vanguard under Santander struck Barreiro's column while the rest of the royalist army was strung out on the road. The fighting lasted about two hours and ended in the surrender of Barreiro and much of his army. Viceroy Sámano fled Bogotá, and Bolívar entered the city on 10 August. The patriots captured the royal treasury, arms, and supplies, which helped finance the next stages of the war.

Barreiro and several other royalist officers were executed in October on Santander's orders, a decision that Bolívar later criticized and that shows that the brutality of the civil war persisted after victory. Boyacá was strategically decisive but not complete. Royalist garrisons held Santa Marta, Cartagena, Popayán, and Pasto, and the Spanish continued to control Quito and most of Venezuela. Bolívar left Bogotá in September, leaving Santander as vice president of Cundinamarca with responsibility for administration and recruitment, and returned to Angostura to secure the political consequences of the victory.

Section 53.19

The Fundamental Law of Colombia and the Creation of Gran Colombia

On 17 December 1819 the Congress of Angostura passed the Fundamental Law uniting Venezuela and New Granada into the Republic of Colombia, with the department of Quito intended to join upon liberation. Bolívar was confirmed as president and Zea named vice president of the republic, while Juan Germán Roscio and Santander served as vice presidents of the departments of Venezuela and Cundinamarca respectively. The law provided for a general congress to meet at Cúcuta to draft a constitution, with Bogotá designated as the capital. The union was an old idea, developed in Bolívar's Jamaica Letter and supported by the strategic logic of joining the resources of the llanos, the Andes, and the coast.

Historical Vocabulary
Gran Colombia

Gran Colombia ("Greater Colombia") is the modern historians' name for the republic that its founders called simply the Republic of Colombia, created in 1819 and 1821 and lasting until the early 1830s. The "Gran" distinguishes it from the later and smaller Republic of Colombia.

Students should avoid confusing the two states. Gran Colombia included territory of present-day Venezuela, Colombia, Panama, and Ecuador, and parts of other neighbors. Its dissolution and the creation of successor states belong to Chapter 60.

The union was declared before it existed in practice. Much of Venezuela remained royalist, and large parts of New Granada were still outside patriot control. The new government depended heavily on Bolívar's personal authority and on the administrative work of Santander, who organized recruitment, taxation, and supply in New Granada. Resources flowed from New Granada's population and mines to the army in Venezuela, a transfer that created resentment in the provinces that bore the cost. Venezuelans and New Granadans also differed in their expectations: Venezuelans, who had suffered most in the war, feared domination by Bogotá, while New Granadans resented the burden of financing a liberation of a country they regarded as richer in cavalry than in revenue.

Territorial consolidation proceeded through 1820 and 1821 in a series of regional campaigns. The royalist garrison of Santa Marta fell in November 1820 and Popayán and the Cauca valley were occupied by Colombian forces, while Maracaibo, which had long been a royalist stronghold, declared for Colombia in January 1821 during the armistice, an act that led to renewed fighting. Cartagena, after a long siege under Mariano Montilla, surrendered in October 1821, and the isthmus of Panama declared its independence from Spain and its adhesion to Colombia in November 1821. The political structure of the republic was therefore built in the midst of continuing campaigns.

Section 53.20

The Campaign for Venezuela and the Battle of Carabobo

In 1820 events in Spain transformed the war. A liberal military revolt led by Rafael del Riego restored the Constitution of 1812 and brought to power a government that ordered Morillo to negotiate with the insurgents. In November 1820, at Trujillo, Bolívar and Morillo agreed to a six-month armistice and a treaty regularizing the conduct of war, ending the practice of killing prisoners, and met in person at Santa Ana on 27 November. Morillo, who was tired of the war and in poor standing with the new government, returned to Spain in December 1820 and left Miguel de la Torre in command. The armistice gave the patriots time to reorganize and obtain reinforcements, and Maracaibo's pronouncement in January 1821 provided a pretext to end it in April.

The campaign for Venezuela converged on the plain of Carabobo, southwest of Valencia. Bolívar's army, including Páez's llaneros, the British Legion, and units from New Granada, numbered several thousand (estimates vary), and La Torre's force, a mix of Spanish regulars and Venezuelan royalists, held a strong defensive position. On 24 June 1821 Páez's units, guided by local informants, circled the royalist flank through a difficult path and attacked, and the royalist line collapsed after a hard fight. Casualties included officers of the British Legion and the llanero Pedro Camejo, called "Negro Primero," a formerly enslaved man who had risen in Páez's ranks. Bolívar entered Caracas on 29 June, and La Torre withdrew to Puerto Cabello.

Carabobo ended Spanish field armies in central Venezuela, but not the war. Royalist garrisons held Puerto Cabello until 1823, and the Spanish navy, led by Admiral Ángel Laborde, still dominated Lake Maracaibo and parts of the coast. The pattern was one of consolidation by regional columns and naval forces, with Bolívar's major battles providing the political momentum for local pronouncements and surrenders.

Section 53.21

The Congress of Cúcuta, the Constitution of 1821, and Republican Citizenship

The Congress that met at Villa del Rosario de Cúcuta from May 1821 was charged with writing a constitution for the union. Its delegates, elected by provinces under limited suffrage, included lawyers, clergy, landowners, and officials from Venezuela and New Granada, many of them veterans of the earlier republics. They debated whether to organize the republic as a federation or a unitary state, and a majority concluded that wartime conditions and the fragility of the union required central authority. The Constitution was adopted on 30 August 1821 and established a unitary republic with a capital at Bogotá, an elected president and vice president, a bicameral legislature, and departments governed by intendants appointed by the president.

The roles of the principal leaders need to be distinguished. The delegates wrote and voted on the constitution. Bolívar, as president under the Fundamental Law of 1819, was in the field in Venezuela during the debates and influenced the Congress through correspondence and through allies, but he was not a delegate. Santander, as vice president of Cundinamarca, held administrative authority in Bogotá and shaped the practical work of governing and supplying the republic. The vice-presidency of the republic passed through several hands: Nariño served temporarily in Bolívar's absence but resigned in the face of attacks from rivals, an interim administration under José María del Castillo y Rada followed, and the Congress elected Santander vice president in 1821.

Primary Source Window

The Constitution of Cúcuta and the Law of Free Birth (1821)

Creator: Congress of Cúcuta · Date and place: 1821, Villa del Rosario de Cúcuta · Type: Constitutional text and legislation

Context: The Congress met in a country still at war. Delegates were elected under limited suffrage and drew on the constitutions of Spain, the United States, France, and Angostura. The delegates were provincial representatives; Bolívar, as president under the Fundamental Law, was campaigning in Venezuela, and Santander held administrative authority in Bogotá.

Paraphrase: The Constitution establishes a centralized republic with a strong executive, defines citizenship through male sex, age, and property or occupation, and grants the president extraordinary powers in regions threatened by war. A separate law declares free the children born to enslaved mothers after its publication, requires them to work for their mother's master until adulthood in return for their upkeep, creates boards to fund the purchase of freedom for others, and prohibits the slave trade. Other measures abolished Indigenous tribute and began dismantling communal landholding.

Analysis: The documents show a compromise between republican principle and elite interest. Gradual emancipation did not free the currently enslaved and left many people in bondage for decades, and slavery was not abolished in Colombia until 1851 or in Venezuela until 1854. The abolition of tribute relieved Indigenous people of a colonial burden but also weakened legal protections for their communal lands. The texts reveal intentions and law, not the unevenness of implementation, which court records and local archives illuminate.

Questions for students:

  1. What kind of state does the Constitution establish, and why?
  2. Who counted as a citizen, and who was excluded?
  3. How does the law of free birth balance freedom and the interests of slaveholders?
  4. What can laws tell historians, and what can they not?

The Constitution's emergency clause allowed the president to exercise extraordinary powers in regions that were theaters of operations, and Bolívar, reelected president, used it to remain with the army while Santander governed in Bogotá. The division of labor kept the army supplied but concentrated authority in the capital and in two men with different priorities. Citizenship was defined for adult men who met requirements of property or occupation, with a literacy requirement postponed to 1840. Women could not vote or hold office, though they continued to play roles in patriot networks, and Indigenous and Afro-Colombian men could be citizens only if they met the property and other qualifications.

Other legislation of 1821 abolished Indigenous tribute, banned the slave trade, and set up the legal framework for dividing communal resguardo lands among individual families, a measure presented as freeing Indigenous people from colonial burdens but one that in practice exposed communal lands to purchase and pressure. The Congress also abolished the Inquisition and passed measures on the Church. The centralist Constitution would be tested in the 1820s, a story reserved for Chapter 60.

Division V — Ecuador, the Completion of Northern Liberation, and Its Contradictions · Section 53.22

Sucre, the Quito Campaign, and the Battle of Pichincha

Guayaquil, the main port of the Quito region, had declared independence on 9 October 1820 through a local junta headed by José Joaquín de Olmedo, a poet and lawyer, and it became the base for the liberation of the highlands. Quito's economy depended on highland textiles and Indigenous labor, while Guayaquil's depended on cacao exports and shipbuilding, and the two regions had long-standing differences. Bolívar sent Antonio José de Sucre, then in his twenties, to Guayaquil in April 1821 with troops and diplomatic authority to coordinate the liberation of the highlands and to negotiate with the Guayaquil junta.

Sucre's first highland campaign ended in defeat at Huachi in September 1821, but he reorganized his forces, obtained reinforcements from Colombia and a division sent by San Martín from Peru under Andrés de Santa Cruz, and advanced through Cuenca and Latacunga in early 1822. The army that fought in Quito's highlands thus included Colombians, Peruvians, veterans from the Río de la Plata, and local recruits, a reminder that northern and southern independence movements overlapped. On 24 May 1822, on the slopes of Pichincha above Quito, Sucre's forces defeated the royalists of Melchor Aymerich, who capitulated the following day. The young officer Abdón Calderón, mortally wounded in the fight, became a national hero.

Bolívar entered Quito on 16 June 1822 to a ceremonial welcome. There he met Manuela Sáenz, a Quito-born woman of a prominent family who had supported the patriot cause and who became his companion and, in later years, a political actor and the keeper of his papers. The capitulation of Quito brought the highlands into the republic, and Sucre was left in command of the southern district. Pichincha was thus a military victory that depended on local juntas, cooperation among several independence movements, and the political arrangements made in the south, a theme taken up further in Chapter 54.

Section 53.23

Guayaquil, Ecuador, and Competing Visions of Political Union

After Pichincha, Quito joined Colombia, but the status of Guayaquil became contested. Its elite was divided among those who favored joining Colombia, those who looked to Peru and San Martín, and those who wanted to remain independent under Olmedo's junta. Guayaquil's commercial interests depended on cacao exports, customs revenue, and its Pacific position, and its leaders worried about domination by the highland administration. The presence of Colombian troops, a request by Bolívar for the incorporation of the province, and the arrival of San Martín's agents created a crisis in the summer of 1822.

Bolívar reached Guayaquil on 11 July 1822 and two days later, on 13 July, proclaimed it incorporated into Colombia, arguing that circumstances left no real alternative and citing the support of part of the elite and the army. Critics, including Olmedo's supporters, saw the act as annexation under military pressure. Shortly afterward, on 26 and 27 July, Bolívar met San Martín at Guayaquil. The content of their conversations, and San Martín's subsequent withdrawal from Peru, are reserved for Chapter 54, and what was said remains uncertain because no verified transcript survives.

The Guayaquil dispute shows that "liberation" could mean different political things. For Bolívar, a large union offered security and international weight, and it would let him concentrate resources on the war against Spain. For Guayaquil's merchants it raised questions of trade, customs revenue, and autonomy. For highland landowners and Indigenous communities in Quito, the republic's promises about tribute, labor, and land meant something different than for the coastal elite. The southern departments would later form the core of Ecuador, but that outcome was neither inevitable nor foreseen in 1822, and the Guayaquil question left a legacy of regional tension.

Section 53.24

The Last Royalist Strongholds and the Human Costs of Liberation

Royalism persisted in the south and along the coast. In April 1822 Bolívar fought a costly battle at Bomboná, near Pasto, and the city's royalist commander, Basilio García, capitulated in June after the fall of Quito. Pasto, however, remained unreconciled. Its economy depended on Church institutions, trade with Quito and Popayán, and a rural society of Indigenous and mestizo communities who had seen patriot armies as sources of requisitions and conscription. A rebellion in late 1822 was suppressed in December by Bolívar's forces, who sacked the city and committed killings and plunder, and Pasto rose again in 1823 under local leaders including Agustín Agualongo, a mestizo officer commanding Indigenous and peasant troops loyal to the king. Agualongo was captured and executed in 1824. Pasto's royalism illustrates that loyalty to the Crown was rooted in local religion, trade, and fears of patriot demands, not only in imperial coercion.

On the coast, a naval force under José Prudencio Padilla, a free man of African descent from Riohacha, defeated the Spanish squadron on Lake Maracaibo on 24 July 1823, completing the liberation of western Venezuela, and on 8 November 1823 Páez accepted the surrender of Puerto Cabello, the last major Spanish stronghold in the north. These victories closed the northern military narrative. Bolívar left for Peru in 1823, where the story continues in Chapters 54 and 55.

The human and economic costs were severe and difficult to quantify. Venezuela lost a substantial share of its population between 1810 and the mid-1820s through combat, executions, famine, disease, and flight. One economic-history estimate puts the decline at about a quarter, while other reconstructions give somewhat different figures depending on the dates used and on how migration and disease are treated. The siege of Cartagena, the exodus of 1814, forced recruitment, the loss of cattle in the llanos to requisition and slaughter, and the destruction of cacao estates impoverished many households and shifted wealth. Venezuela's colonial export economy took years to recover, and landowners' dependence on enslaved and dependent labor continued.

Demobilization and finance posed further problems. Soldiers had been promised pay and land, but the treasury was nearly empty and the army remained large and costly. The republic relied on customs duties, the sale of confiscated property, forced loans, and, from 1822, loans raised in London, which created a foreign debt before the state had a stable tax system. Veterans' claims for payment became a continuing source of political pressure, and the army's officers, accustomed to authority, remained a powerful group in civil politics.

Everyday Life and Social History

Soldiers, Households, and the Cost of Supplying a Revolution

Armies lived off the countryside. Villages were asked to provide cattle, maize, mules, and porters, and when requisitions were not met, soldiers seized them. Cattle ranching in the llanos suffered from the removal of herds to feed troops, and in Casanare and the Andes, Indigenous and peasant communities supplied food and carried loads across passes like Pisba. Women ground grain, nursed the wounded, washed clothes, and followed the armies as camp followers (rabonas in later Andean usage), and some acted as couriers or spies, as Policarpa Salavarrieta did.

Enslaved men who joined the army hoped for freedom, but their families often remained enslaved, and the patriots' policy of conscripting enslaved men from estates generated resistance from owners. Households also suffered the confiscation of property from suspected royalists or patriots, depending on who controlled the area. Disease, including smallpox, typhus, and fevers, killed more soldiers than battle. The surviving records, mostly military and fiscal, capture these burdens unevenly, and most plebeian voices are available only indirectly.

Section 53.25

The Promise and Limits of Gran Colombia

By the end of 1823 the republic proclaimed in 1819 existed in fact over a territory larger than most European states. It had a constitution, a capital, an army, a diplomatic corps, and, from 1822, recognition by the United States, which was among the first governments to acknowledge a Spanish American republic. Its achievements were considerable: it had defeated Spanish field armies, created common institutions across several colonial jurisdictions, begun a legal process of gradual emancipation, and ended Indigenous tribute and the Inquisition.

Its foundations, however, were fragile. The treasury depended on foreign loans and customs duties, the army was large and costly, and civilian institutions were new and thinly staffed. Regional leaders such as Páez in Venezuela, powerful commanders in Quito and Guayaquil, and city councils across the republic retained substantial local power, and the unitary constitution gave them little formal voice. Distances were great, travel and communication slow, and the Andean chain, Caribbean coast, and Orinoco plains looked in different economic directions. Venezuelans in particular felt governed from a distant capital in Bogotá by officials who did not share their experience of the war.

Unresolved problems included slavery, which persisted under the gradual-emancipation law and would not be abolished in Colombia until 1851 and Venezuela until 1854; Indigenous communities, whose legal protection and land security were being reshaped by the abolition of tribute and the division of resguardos; racial inequality, which formal equality among citizens did not remove; and regional resentments over taxes, representation, and appointments. Santander and Bolívar differed over how to govern, and their rivalry would become a central feature of the 1820s, as Chapter 60 will explain. The northern wars had produced a state and also a set of conflicts that its founding constitution could contain only temporarily.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Centralization Necessary for Military Survival?

The question: Did the centralized constitution of 1821 and the concentration of power in the executive reflect a necessary response to the demands of war, or did it deepen regional divisions that later threatened the union?

Interpretation A — Centralization as wartime necessity: Supporters, including Bolívar and Santander in their time and many later historians, argue that the failures of 1812–1814 demonstrated that federal systems could not mobilize resources, command armies, or enforce discipline. A unified command, central finances, and emergency powers allowed the campaigns of 1819–1823. Without centralization, regional rivalries would have delivered the north back to Spain.
Interpretation B — Centralization as a source of later conflict: Critics, including regional leaders at the time and historians emphasizing local politics, argue that strong central authority overrode the legitimate claims of provinces, especially Venezuela, concentrated power in a few individuals, and made the army's cost fall on regions unevenly. On this view, military success depended as much on negotiated regional alliances, such as Bolívar's pact with Páez, as on constitutional centralism, and the centralist state stored up grievances.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same sources: congressional debates, constitutional texts, military correspondence, financial records, and the later political history of the union. Historians cannot run a counterfactual, so the debate turns on causal inference. Recent work also stresses that "centralism" and "federalism" were fluid labels used by actors with differing interests.

Why it matters: The question affects how Gran Colombia is judged, as a visionary project undermined by ambitious rivals or as an overextended state whose design contained its own seeds of dissolution. It also informs broader debates about whether strong executives are necessary for state-building in war.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, and identify what additional evidence, such as local fiscal records, might shift the balance.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Colonial Jurisdictions to a Republic, 1810–1823

What Changed

  • Spanish sovereignty was replaced by a republic claiming popular sovereignty and a written constitution
  • Separate colonial jurisdictions were joined in a large union
  • The slave trade was banned and a path to gradual emancipation created
  • Indigenous tribute was abolished and communal landholding put under pressure
  • Armies, once colonial militias, became national forces with new leaders

What Stayed the Same

  • Slavery persisted for decades, and landowning elites kept social authority
  • Regional identities and urban rivalries remained strong
  • Citizenship stayed limited by sex, property, and often literacy
  • The Catholic Church remained influential, though its institutions were disrupted

Who experienced change most directly: Soldiers, enslaved men who enlisted, and families in war zones. Greater continuity: Rural laborers and women outside the army, whose daily conditions changed less. Why the pattern matters: The wars changed who ruled and under what language of legitimacy more than they changed the structure of inequality, a gap that shaped later politics.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Defeat in 1816 to Victory at Carabobo and Pichincha

Long-term causes

Bourbon reform grievances, creole exclusion, regional rivalries, the crisis of 1808, and social tensions between elites and a mixed-race majority.

Short-term causes

The failure of the first republics, Morillo's harsh reconquest, Haitian assistance, the Orinoco base, and the spread of Spanish liberal revolution in 1820.

Event or transformation

A reorganized insurgency, a composite army, and a political framework at Angostura and Cúcuta defeated royalist forces between 1819 and 1822.

Immediate effects

Control of Bogotá, Caracas, and Quito; creation of a large republic; emancipation measures; foreign recognition.

Long-term consequences

Regional tensions, military influence in politics, slow abolition, and the eventual breakup of Gran Colombia (Chapter 60).

Historical complexity

Victory depended on shifting social alliances, regional commanders, foreign help, and events in Spain, not on Bolívar alone or on military skill alone.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the liberation of northern South America from the juntas of 1810 to the last royalist surrenders of 1823, and has argued that independence required building coalitions and institutions as well as winning battles. The central problem was how a set of divided movements produced a large but fragile republic.

On the eve of independence, Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito were societies marked by regional rivalry, slavery, Indigenous tribute, and a large population of free people of mixed ancestry. The crisis of 1808 prompted juntas that claimed authority in the king's name and, in time, independence. The first republics were elite-led, federal or fragmented, and weakly institutionalized.

The First Venezuelan Republic fell in 1812 under the combined weight of an earthquake, royalist invasion, internal division, and social tension, and Miranda was surrendered to the Spanish. In New Granada, the contest between federalists and centralists consumed patriot energy while Quito's junta was reconquered. Bolívar drew from this experience a preference for unified command and strong government, set out in the Cartagena Manifesto.

The Second Republic of 1813–1814 was launched by the Admirable Campaign and marked by the War to the Death. It was destroyed by Boves's llanero army, which mobilized plebeian resentment against patriot elites. The episode shows that royalism had real social roots and that the wars were civil and social conflicts, not simply contests between Americans and Spaniards.

Morillo's expedition of 1815–1816 consolidated royalist rule in Venezuela, reconquered New Granada, executed patriot leaders, and drove the insurgents into exile and the periphery. In Jamaica and Haiti, Bolívar rethought his strategy. The Jamaica Letter offered an Atlantic analysis, while Pétion's aid and the emancipation decrees of 1816 linked freedom to recruitment and to a new legitimacy, though enforcement was weak and slaveholding remained widespread.

From a base on the Orinoco, the patriots built a composite army out of llaneros, pardos, freed men, and foreign volunteers, and consolidated their leadership by negotiation, as with Páez, and by force, as with Piar. The Congress of Angostura in 1819 provided a political framework and Bolívar's republican vision, including a strong executive and a moral power, while postponing general emancipation.

The crossing of the Andes, Pantano de Vargas, and Boyacá liberated New Granada in 1819, the Fundamental Law created Colombia, and after the 1820 armistice, Carabobo secured Venezuela in 1821. The Congress of Cúcuta adopted a centralist constitution and a law of free birth that began a slow end to slavery, abolished tribute, and defined a restricted citizenship.

Sucre's campaign, ending at Pichincha in 1822, brought Quito into the republic, while disputes over Guayaquil and the persistence of royalism in Pasto showed how contested union remained. The naval victory at Lake Maracaibo and the fall of Puerto Cabello in 1823 closed the northern campaigns, at heavy human cost.

The result was a state of remarkable extent and fragile foundations. Its internal tensions over regional autonomy, slavery, Indigenous rights, military power, and civilian authority are the inheritance that Chapter 60 takes up. Students should also take away a method: sources written by Bolívar and other protagonists are persuasive texts, and the experiences of enslaved people, women, Indigenous communities, and plebeian soldiers must often be reconstructed indirectly.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that independence in northern South America was a long, uncertain process in which republics were created, destroyed, and re-created. The liberators won when they combined military reorganization with political and social change: a secure base, a composite army, Haitian and British assistance, and a promise, however limited, of freedom for those who fought.

The chapter also shows how war shaped what followed. The offices, officers, debts, and regional commanders produced by the campaigns were not easily dissolved, and the republic's constitutional design, centralist and designed for war, would face the test of peace. Slavery, Indigenous land, and racial hierarchy were altered in law but changed slowly in life, a pattern that recurs across Latin America in Part XIII.

Chapter 54 turns south to San Martín's campaigns in the Río de la Plata, Chile, and Peru, where the northern and southern armies would eventually meet, and Chapter 55 follows them to Junín and Ayacucho. Students should carry forward the idea that Bolívar's northern victories were part of a continental process, that many people other than Bolívar made independence, and that winning the war was only the beginning of the problem of building nations.
