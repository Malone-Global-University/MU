Chapter Opening: The Evening of March 18, 1938

On March 18, 1938, President Lázaro Cárdenas announced that the Mexican government would expropriate the assets of foreign companies operating in the country's petroleum industry. The decision followed a labor dispute, legal proceedings, and months of confrontation with powerful corporations. For many Mexicans, it represented a long-awaited assertion of the national sovereignty promised by the Revolution. For foreign governments and oil companies, it raised urgent questions about compensation, commercial relations, and the security of investments. For the Mexican state, however, the announcement created a further challenge: it now had to operate and finance the industry it had taken over.

The announcement was made in a country that had changed considerably in the three years and a few months since Cárdenas took office. Peasant communities held land that had belonged to haciendas. A new national labor confederation, the Confederación de Trabajadores de México (CTM), claimed hundreds of thousands of members. Rural schools had spread under a constitutional mandate for "socialist education." The railway system had passed into state ownership. And the great political fixture of the late 1920s and early 1930s, former president Plutarco Elías Calles, was in exile. The oil expropriation was thus not an isolated act of a single president. It was the most dramatic expression of a broader effort to turn the promises of 1910 to 1917 into land, wages, schools, and public ownership.

This chapter explains how that effort worked, whom it benefited, and what it left behind. It returns first to 1934, when Cárdenas, a general from Michoacán who had been selected by the official party, became president amid expectations that Calles would remain the real source of authority. It shows how Cárdenas built an independent political base by mobilizing peasants, workers, teachers, and some military officers, and how he used that base to end the era known as the Maximato. It then follows the three great programs of his government: agrarian redistribution, the organization and support of labor, and the assertion of national control over strategic industries, culminating in the oil expropriation. Finally, it traces the reorganization of the governing party from the Partido Nacional Revolucionario (PNR) to the Partido de la Revolución Mexicana (PRM) in 1938 and to the Partido Revolucionario Institucional (PRI) in 1946.

The chapter advances a two-part argument. First, the Cárdenas years marked a decisive phase of social and institutional transformation. Millions of hectares changed hands, collective bargaining and strikes became central features of public life, and the state asserted ownership over assets that foreign companies had controlled. Second, the same years strengthened the federal executive and organized popular groups into a party structure directed from the center. The institutions that carried reform to the countryside and the factory also became channels through which later governments distributed benefits, mobilized votes, disciplined organizations, and limited independent competition. Neither half of the argument cancels the other. The Mexican Revolution produced real redistribution and a durable system of centralized control.

Students should not read the later PRI system as the inevitable result of Cardenismo, nor Cardenismo as a fulfillment of every revolutionary demand. Many Cardenista institutions had real constituencies, internal disagreements, and capacity for bargaining. Peasant petitioners, striking workers, rural teachers, and Indigenous communities pressed their own claims, often forcing the government to go further than some officials wished and sometimes resisting its direction. At the same time, the government supplied the legal tools, credit, military backing, and political recognition that made those claims effective, and it expected loyalty in return. The relationship between mobilization from below and incorporation from above is the chapter's central analytical problem.

Dating requires care throughout. Cárdenas took the oath of office in a ceremony on November 30, 1934, while his constitutional term began on December 1; the two dates refer to different things. The petroleum decree, the creation of Pemex, the founding of the PRM, and the formation of the PRI each involved several distinct steps, such as a decision, a decree, a publication, and a later institutional act. Statistics on land distribution depend on the source series and the categories counted. The chapter identifies these distinctions as it proceeds, and it marks places where documentary collections give different dates.

The evidence is rich and uneven. Presidential decrees, the Diario Oficial de la Federación, agrarian resolutions, party pacts, union statutes, censuses, and the records of the Archivo General de la Nación allow historians to follow policy closely. U.S. and British diplomatic correspondence records the reactions of governments and companies. Press coverage, photographs, memoirs, and the published notes of Cárdenas himself add texture. Yet most official documents describe what authorities intended and announced, not what happened in a village or on a plantation. Petitions, local archives, teachers' reports, oral histories, and regional studies provide a view from below, though they survive unevenly and are often filtered by those who recorded them. The chapter's nine Primary Source Windows are designed to show students how to weigh such evidence, and each flags where an edition must be verified before quotation.

The chapter completes Part XVI. Chapter 71 examined the Porfirian regime, Chapter 72 the revolutionary wars, and Chapter 73 the Constitution of 1917 and the reconstruction of Mexico through the end of the Maximato; their detailed material is not repeated here. The chapter also looks ahead to Chapter 75, which places the Mexican case within the wider Latin American shift to import-substitution industrialization. Chapter 74 treats industrial policy only as far as it explains political institutionalization. After 1946 the narrative stops at a bounded synthesis rather than a history of PRI rule.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Lázaro Cárdenas moved from the official party's candidate to a president with an independent political base, distinguishing the Plan Sexenal from the measures actually enacted.
  2. Analyze the Cárdenas–Calles conflict and the end of the Maximato without implying that presidential centralization ended.
  3. Assess socialist education as a specific constitutional and pedagogical project rather than evidence that Mexico became a socialist state.
  4. Describe the legal framework of Article 27 and the 1934 Agrarian Code, and distinguish ejidal, communal, and private property.
  5. Evaluate the achievements and limits of agrarian reform, using regional cases (La Laguna, Yucatán, Sonora, Mexicali, Michoacán, Morelos) and attending to land quality, credit, gender, and counting methods.
  6. Trace the formation of the CTM and the changing relationship among unions, the Communist Party, employers, and the state.
  7. Distinguish the 1917 constitutional claim to subsoil resources, the 1937 railway expropriation and 1938 worker administration, the March 18, 1938 oil expropriation, and the June 1938 creation of Pemex.
  8. Interpret competing explanations of the oil expropriation as a labor settlement, an assertion of sovereignty, and an instrument of state-building.
  9. Connect Mexican diplomacy under Cárdenas to the Good Neighbor Policy, the Spanish Civil War, asylum policy, and the reception of Leon Trotsky.
  10. Compare the PNR (1929), the PRM (1938), and the PRI (1946) as party structures, and explain the role of the CNC and CNOP.
  11. Analyze the contested election of 1940, wartime national unity, and the 1946 transition to a civilian president.
  12. Evaluate the PRI system as a combination of reform, representation, patronage, electoral management, and political exclusion, while separating its 1946 foundations from its later evolution.

Historical Context

The Constitution of 1917, discussed in Chapter 73, gave postrevolutionary Mexico a legal vocabulary of reform. Article 27 vested original ownership of land and water in the nation, authorized the redistribution of land through restitution and grants, and declared direct national domain over subsoil resources. Article 123 recognized labor rights, including the right to organize and strike, and Article 3 set out the principles of public education. Between 1917 and 1934, governments from Carranza to Calles and the interim presidents of the Maximato implemented these provisions unevenly. Land was distributed, but mostly in modest amounts and often as a tool for managing rural unrest. Labor law was codified in the Federal Labor Law of 1931, but union life depended heavily on negotiated relations with officials.

The political order was held together by military men and their networks. After the assassination of president-elect Álvaro Obregón in 1928, Calles supervised the creation of the PNR in 1929, which brought regional caudillos, local parties, and officials into a national structure, and presidents Emilio Portes Gil, Pascual Ortiz Rubio, and Abelardo Rodríguez governed under Calles's influence. Observers called him the Jefe Máximo, and the period the Maximato. The Cristero conflict had hardened church–state relations. The Great Depression, which struck Mexico after 1929 through collapsing mineral and agricultural exports, repatriation of Mexican workers from the United States, and unemployment, weakened the credibility of cautious governance and strengthened demands for state action.

By 1934 the agrarian question remained unresolved. Large estates still dominated much of the best irrigated land in northern commercial regions and the plantation zones of the south, while resident workers on haciendas, the peones acasillados, had been legally excluded from petitioning for land. Many villages had received ejidos, but the ejido covered only a modest share of cultivated land, and it often lacked water, credit, and technical support. The labor movement was fragmented: the once powerful Confederación Regional Obrera Mexicana (CROM) had declined with the fortunes of its leader, Luis N. Morones, while communists, independent unions, and the Confederación General de Obreros y Campesinos de México (CGOCM), led by Vicente Lombardo Toledano, competed for influence. Strikes were common, but few national unions could negotiate across an industry.

Foreign capital also shaped the setting. British, American, and Dutch companies controlled petroleum, and foreign interests owned important mines, railways, utilities, and agricultural lands. After the peak of oil production around 1921, Mexican output declined as Venezuela and the United States expanded, yet the Mexican fields remained a strategic and symbolic subject. Internationally, the election of Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1932 and the announced Good Neighbor Policy reduced the likelihood of armed U.S. intervention, and Mexico's long-standing commitment to nonintervention, associated with the Estrada Doctrine of 1930, shaped its diplomacy. Rising fascism and civil war in Spain soon added new international pressures.

Into this setting came a politician whose career had been military and regional. The chapter therefore begins with who Cárdenas was, what the Plan Sexenal promised, and what kinds of political power were available to a president who was widely expected to remain subordinate to Calles.

Section 74.1

The Presidential Election of 1934, the Plan Sexenal, and Cárdenas's Reform Program

Lázaro Cárdenas was born in 1895 in Jiquilpan, in western Michoacán, and joined the revolutionary forces as a young man. He rose through the army during the civil wars, served under Calles in northern campaigns, and became a trusted officer of the Sonoran leadership. His political career combined military rank with regional administration. As governor of Michoacán from 1928 to 1932 he encouraged the organization of peasant leagues, supported schools and agrarian petitions, and cultivated relations with peasant leaders and teachers. He later served as president of the PNR, as secretary of the interior, and as secretary of war. Historians such as Marjorie Becker and Christopher Boyer have shown that his Michoacán years were shaped by negotiation with local agrarian movements, not simply by orders from above.

In 1933 the official party faced the question of succession. The Maximato's leaders wanted a candidate who would preserve party unity, and Calles accepted Cárdenas, whose loyalty seemed assured and whose appeal among agrarian and military constituencies was real. The PNR convention at Querétaro in December 1933 nominated him and adopted the Plan Sexenal (Six-Year Plan), a program of economic planning and social reform for 1934–1940. The Plan called for state direction of economic development, expanded land distribution through the ejido, labor protection, public education, rural health, roads, irrigation, and greater national control of natural resources. Written by party committees and shaped by competing currents within the PNR, it was a statement of intention rather than a binding legal code. Its significance lies in giving Cárdenas a programmatic framework that he could later invoke against opponents within the party.

The campaign itself was part of the political education of the country. Cárdenas traveled tens of thousands of kilometers, often by car, train, horse, or on foot, visiting communities that had rarely seen a presidential candidate. He listened to petitions from villagers, union delegations, and teachers, and made commitments on land, schools, water, credit, and public health. His style differed from that of the Sonoran generals: he cultivated an image of austerity and direct contact, and he would continue the practice as president. The practice mattered politically, because it informed him about local conflicts and created expectations among petitioners that the central government now owned.

Opponents competed in the July 1, 1934 election, including candidates associated with the left and the Communist Party, but the electoral machinery of the PNR and the weakness of the opposition gave Cárdenas an overwhelming official victory. The election therefore reflected the official party's dominance more than an open contest. Cárdenas took the oath in a ceremony on November 30, 1934, and his constitutional term, the first full six-year presidential period after the reform extending the term, began on December 1. Students should not treat the two dates as interchangeable: November 30 denotes the public oath-taking, while December 1 is the date from which his term is counted.

The inaugural period showed both continuity and change. Cárdenas appointed many callista officials to his first cabinet, and the Senate and several governors remained loyal to Calles. At the same time, he emphasized the Plan Sexenal, declined to live in the traditional presidential residence in Chapultepec Castle, and began restructuring relations with peasants and workers. The distinction between program and practice is essential: the Plan Sexenal promised much, but the measures actually implemented after 1934 depended on the political struggle for control of the presidency, the budget, and the party. The following section examines that struggle.

Primary Source Window

The Plan Sexenal and Cárdenas's Program

Creator or issuing authority: Partido Nacional Revolucionario, Plan Sexenal 1934–1940; Cárdenas's inaugural address · Date: December 1933 (Plan); late 1934 (inaugural address) · Source type: Party program and presidential speech · Edition to consult: Memoria Política de México (digital documents on the Revolution and the PNR) and INEHRM collections; the Plan Sexenal text should be verified against a published edition before quotation

Context: The Plan was approved by a party convention in Querétaro after the PNR's leadership had agreed on Cárdenas as candidate. The inaugural speech was delivered as the new president took office with a cabinet and Congress still shaped by callista influence.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): The Plan Sexenal sets out goals for agrarian reform through ejidos, protection of labor, public education, health, communications, irrigation, and the state's growing role in economic development. Cárdenas's inaugural address, as summarized in standard biographies, committed his government to carrying out the Plan and to supporting organized workers and peasants. Verification requirement: the exact wording of both documents must be checked in a verified edition; no quotation is supplied here.

Analysis: The Plan shows what the governing party promised in the depths of the Depression: a state with a planning role, an expanded ejido, labor rights, and national control of resources. The speech shows how the new president framed his own mandate. Together they reveal a program broad enough to unite party factions, which also meant that its implementation could be steered in different directions.

Limitations: Both are statements of intention written by political actors seeking support. Neither records what was implemented, who benefited, or how local conflicts resolved. The Plan was also a product of committees with competing views and cannot be read as Cárdenas's personal program.

  3. What can historians learn about the politics of 1933–1934 from the breadth of the Plan's promises?

Historical Vocabulary
Plan Sexenal

The Plan Sexenal was the Six-Year Plan adopted by the PNR convention of December 1933 as the program for the 1934–1940 presidential term. It was a party document, not a statute, and it set priorities in agrarian reform, labor, education, health, and state-guided development.

Students should distinguish the Plan as a political promise from the measures actually adopted, and should avoid treating it as the personal blueprint of Cárdenas.

Cardenismo

Cardenismo refers to the policies, coalition, and political style associated with Cárdenas's presidency. Historians disagree about how coherent or radical it was: some emphasize genuine social reform, others the strengthening of the state. The term should not be used as though it were a single ideology with fixed boundaries.

Jefe Máximo

Jefe Máximo ("Supreme Chief") was the title applied to Calles during the Maximato, when he exerted decisive influence over presidents without holding the office. It describes informal power resting on party, military, and patronage networks rather than on any constitutional position.

Socialist education

Socialist education was the constitutional label for the educational orientation adopted in the Article 3 amendment of December 1934. It combined secular instruction, hostility to religious doctrine in schools, and an emphasis on collective social goals. It did not mean that Mexico became a socialist state, and its definition was disputed among reformers.

Section 74.2

The Break with Plutarco Elías Calles and the Destruction of the Maximato, 1935–1936

The conflict between Cárdenas and Calles developed gradually. In 1934 and early 1935 a wave of strikes spread across the country, as workers took advantage of Cárdenas's statements that the government would not use force against legal labor action. Calles and his allies viewed the strikes as destabilizing and as evidence that the new president could not control the labor movement. Tensions also arose over cabinet appointments, over the role of Tomás Garrido Canabal and his paramilitary Red Shirts in Tabasco, whose violent anticlericalism had embarrassed the government, and over who would control the party and the Senate. These disputes were not only personal. They concerned who could make decisions in the postrevolutionary state.

In June 1935 the conflict became public. Calles gave statements to the press criticizing labor agitation and warning that the country's economic progress was endangered; the statements, which appeared around June 12, were read as an attack on the president. Cárdenas replied within days, defending the right of workers to organize and strike and stating that the government would not be intimidated. The labor movement responded by forming the Comité Nacional de Defensa Proletaria, uniting unions that had previously competed, including the CGOCM and railway and electrical workers, in support of the president. The cabinet resigned, and Cárdenas reconstituted it, removing figures associated with Calles and bringing in allies. The episode showed that organized labor was a political resource that Calles lacked.

Over the following months Cárdenas replaced Callista governors and legislators, and the Senate used its power to declare the "disappearance of powers" in several states, removing governors who sided with Calles. He reassigned military commanders, strengthened ties with peasant organizations, and undermined the Red Shirts in Tabasco. Calles, who had at first tried to minimize the dispute, found his support eroding. In April 1936, after reports of conspiratorial activity, the government arrested Calles and a few associates, including Luis N. Morones, and put them on a plane to the United States. The date usually given for the departure is April 10, 1936. The Maximato was over.

The destruction of the Maximato did not mean the end of presidential centralization. Cárdenas replaced the informal supremacy of the Jefe Máximo with the formal and informal powers of the presidency itself, backed by the party, the army, and mass organizations. The practice of a president dominating the party, appointing governors, and arbitrating among organized interests was reinforced rather than abandoned. This is a pivotal point for understanding the later political system: the removal of a personal supreme chief increased, not decreased, the authority of the office of the president. Cárdenas also did not, after 1936, make a habit of unilateral removal of political rivals; he proved restrained with Calles himself, who returned to Mexico in 1941 and died in 1945.

Section 74.3

Presidential Authority, State Governors, the Military, and Mass Mobilization

With Calles removed, Cárdenas rebuilt relations with the groups on which political authority depended. Governors, who commanded patronage and local security, were the first. He replaced several with allies and, through the Senate and the party, reasserted the principle that governors owed their position to national politics rather than to regional bosses. Military commanders, many of whom had acquired regional power and business interests, were rotated among zones, and officers were encouraged to see themselves as defenders of the Revolution's social program. The government also armed peasant communities as agrarian reserves in some regions, providing a counterweight to landlord guards and, in some cases, to unreliable military units.

The most distinctive feature of his method was direct contact. Cárdenas continued the tours begun in the campaign, traveling to villages, receiving petitions, hearing disputes between communities and officials, and ordering investigations. These visits did not abolish bureaucracy. They signaled that the president would listen, and petitions to the Department of Agrarian Affairs, the governors, and the president's own office multiplied. Federal appointments in education, agrarian administration, and rural credit offered the center a way to bring local leaders into relationships with the state. The practice of intervening personally strengthened the president's popularity while also making local actors dependent on presidential favor.

Historians therefore distinguish between popular participation and political incorporation. Peasant leagues, teachers' groups, and unions acted voluntarily in many cases: petitioners had grievances, organizers pursued agendas, and villagers used the government's language of reform to press local claims. Yet the state also created, funded, recognized, and sometimes dissolved organizations, and officials directed some mobilization for political purposes. Both processes occurred at once. A peasant league might owe its legal recognition to the government while also winning land the landlords had fiercely resisted. Scholars such as Arnaldo Córdova have emphasized the state's organizing role, while Alan Knight and others have emphasized the autonomy and contingency of local movements.

Resistance was real. Landowners hired armed guards, used the courts, and in some regions attacked agrarian leaders. The Catholic Church and lay organizations opposed socialist education, and the Unión Nacional Sinarquista, founded in 1937, drew support in parts of the Bajío. Business groups, especially in Monterrey, resisted union organization, and Cárdenas's speech in the city in February 1936 warned employers that the government would not tolerate lockouts. Among military men, discontent produced the most serious armed challenge of the period: General Saturnino Cedillo, a regional cacique of San Luis Potosí, rose against the government in May 1938 and was defeated, and he died in early 1939. The government's ability to prevail reflected both military loyalty and the political weight of mobilized peasants and workers. Cárdenas also lowered tensions with the Church by easing the enforcement of anticlerical laws after 1936, a quiet reversal that anticipated later accommodation.

The relationship between the president and the army deserves separate emphasis. Cárdenas had been a general, and he understood that the officer corps held the capacity to veto any government. His response combined patronage, professionalization, and political education: he raised pay and benefits for some units, promoted loyal officers, shifted commanders away from regional strongholds, and encouraged the idea that the army's duty was to protect the Revolution's social reforms. The decision to give the military its own sector in the party in 1938 grew out of the same logic, which was to bring the army inside a civilian-led political framework rather than leave it outside as a competing institution.

Section 74.4

Socialist Education, Rural Teachers, and the Cultural Politics of Reform

The constitutional reform of Article 3, promulgated in December 1934, declared that public education would be socialist. It excluded religious doctrine from schools, combated fanaticism and prejudice, and sought to give students a rational view of the universe and of social life. The amendment had been prepared in the closing months of the Rodríguez government and the PNR convention, and it reflected earlier controversies, including the 1933–1934 debate over sex education championed by Narciso Bassols. It preceded the consolidation of Cárdenas's independent authority, so its adoption cannot be read as an expression of his mature program. Under his secretary of public education, Gonzalo Vázquez Vela, the amendment became the basis of a nationwide policy.

Socialist education was a pedagogical and political project, not a transformation of the economy. Its advocates differed on what "socialism" meant. For some it meant collective cooperation and class consciousness, for others a rationalist, secular school focused on work, health, and community improvement. The state created rural schools, regional peasant schools, technical programs, and literacy efforts; teachers organized in unions and acted as agrarian advisers, helping villagers prepare petitions, form ejido committees, and understand the law. Mary Kay Vaughan's study of schools, teachers, and peasants in the 1930s showed that teachers often mediated between villagers and the state, and that communities adapted the school to their own purposes.

Opposition was intense. Catholic groups, parents, and local priests objected to the exclusion of religious teaching and to some curricular content, and in some regions they boycotted schools. Violence against rural teachers occurred, particularly in parts of central and western Mexico where religious conflict remained strong; estimates of the number of teachers killed range into the low hundreds depending on the years counted, and the figures should be treated as approximate. Teachers were targeted as agents of agrarian reform and anticlericalism as well as of schooling. The Church condemned the program, and the government partly softened its application after 1936, as the pressure of violence and the need for national unity grew.

Education also addressed inequality, though not without cost. Rural schools extended literacy and technical skills to communities long excluded from instruction, and teachers' organizations gave rural educators a voice in policy. But the school also carried national cultural models. In Indigenous regions, instruction was often in Spanish and aimed at integrating communities into a national culture, an assimilationist logic that some Indigenous families resisted and others turned to their advantage. Reformers also disagreed about the balance between community autonomy and national uniformity. The educational project therefore reveals the same duality as other Cardenista programs: it enlarged opportunity and participation while placing communities within an increasingly centralized national framework.

Section 74.5

Article 27, the Agrarian Code, and the Expansion of Ejido Land Distribution

Article 27 of the Constitution of 1917 established that original ownership of land and water belonged to the nation, which could transfer it to private persons and could impose limits on private property in the public interest. It authorized two principal routes to land for rural communities: restitution, the return of lands taken from villages, notably after the liberal land laws of the nineteenth century, and dotación, the grant of land to communities that lacked enough. Land granted to a community became an ejido, a form of landholding in which the community, not the individual, held legal title, and individual members had rights to use parcels but not to sell them. Separate from the ejido were bienes comunales, communal lands held by Indigenous or peasant communities under older titles, and private property of various sizes.

Between 1917 and 1934 land distribution proceeded cautiously. Governors and presidents used grants selectively, procedures were slow, and landowners used legal challenges to delay reform. The Agrarian Code issued on March 22, 1934, under President Abelardo L. Rodríguez and published in April 1934 reorganized agrarian authorities and procedures before Cárdenas assumed office. It did not itself establish the later explicit right of resident hacienda workers (peones acasillados) to obtain ejidos. In La Laguna, Cárdenas's government made substantial distributions to estate workers in 1936 amid labor mobilization and administrative improvisation. A subsequent presidential decree dated August 9, 1937 amended Article 45 of the Agrarian Code to recognize the right of hacienda laborers to obtain ejidos and to form new agricultural population centers, including their consideration in agrarian censuses. That legal sequence distinguishes the pre-Cárdenas framework, the 1936 regional intervention, and the 1937 statutory extension. Cárdenas's administration also depended on political will, surveying capacity, rural organization, and credit to turn legal claims into effective possession.

Land distribution under Cárdenas proceeded through a cycle of petition, survey, provisional possession, and presidential resolution. Villages or groups of workers petitioned; engineers surveyed estates and measured affectable land; state commissions made recommendations; and the president signed the final resolution. Landowners could keep a core of land protected as small property, with limits varying by crop and quality, and the government issued certificates of nonaffectability to some owners, notably cattle ranchers, as a guarantee against expropriation. Landowners sometimes divided estates among relatives to avoid claims, used armed guards, and sought judicial protection through the writ of amparo, though Cárdenas limited some of these legal tools. The process combined legal procedure with political mobilization, since a petition without organized backing might languish for years.

Possessing land and being able to farm it productively were different things. New ejidatarios often received plots without irrigation, tools, seed, or working capital. The government responded by creating the Banco Nacional de Crédito Ejidal in December 1935 to provide credit, by expanding irrigation through the National Irrigation Commission, and by sending agronomists and engineers. Peasant leagues and agrarian committees helped organize petitions and defend newly granted land. Yet credit reached some regions more fully than others, technical help was thin, and the banks' administrative control over ejido finances raised complaints. The expansion of distribution was therefore only the first step in a longer struggle over production, marketing, and autonomy.

Administration was as important as law. The Department of Agrarian Affairs had to process thousands of petitions with a small staff of engineers and lawyers, and delays could extend for years even when a presidential resolution was signed. Provisional possession, physical delivery of boundaries, and the issuing of titles were separate steps, and communities sometimes waited long after the resolution before they could farm the land. This is one reason historians distinguish between the date of a resolution and the date on which a community actually controlled its land, a distinction that also complicates the statistics discussed in Section 74.8.

Historical Vocabulary
Ejido

The ejido was a form of landholding in which a rural community received land from the state and held it under legal rules that restricted sale. Members held rights to use individual parcels or to work land collectively. The ejido should not be confused with traditional Indigenous communal lands, which rested on older titles.

Collective cultivation

Collective cultivation refers to arrangements in which ejidatarios farmed land together and shared proceeds, often with a credit society. It was important in some regions, notably La Laguna and Yucatán, but it was not the norm: many ejidos were divided into individual parcels. Students should not treat all ejidos as collective farms.

Agrarian restitution

Restitution was the return of land to communities that could prove it had been taken illegally, usually on the basis of colonial or older titles. It required documentation that many communities could not produce, which is why most land was distributed through grants (dotación) rather than restitution.

Rural credit

Rural credit refers to loans for seed, equipment, and working capital. The Banco Nacional de Crédito Ejidal, founded in 1935, made credit available to ejidos, but its control over loans also gave officials influence over ejido decisions and sometimes produced debt and dependency.

Section 74.6

Collective Ejidos, Cotton Agriculture, and the Land Reform of La Laguna, 1936

La Laguna, a lake-basin region straddling the states of Coahuila and Durango around the cities of Torreón, Gómez Palacio, and Lerdo, was one of Mexico's most commercially developed agricultural districts. Irrigation from the Nazas River supported large-scale cotton production for textile mills in Mexico and for export. Landownership was concentrated in a small number of large and modern estates, some owned by Mexican families and some by foreign investors, and the labor force included resident workers, seasonal pickers, and sharecroppers. The region also had an unusually well-organized rural labor movement, shaped by earlier strike activity and by the presence of unions and political organizers.

In 1936 agricultural workers in La Laguna struck for a collective labor contract and improved conditions. The dispute escalated as landowners resisted, and organized workers pressed agrarian claims. In October Cárdenas directed the redistribution of major estates; the decisive agreement was dated October 6, 1936, and the reform was implemented rapidly thereafter. In Lourdes Romero Navarrete's scholarly reconstruction, approximately 447,515 hectares in total were assigned, of which approximately 146,277 hectares were irrigated; the remainder included dry and other lands. Her account reports 296 ejidos and approximately 34,816 campesino beneficiaries. A separate contemporary-derived statistical series gives approximately 447,516 hectares and 34,734–34,743 beneficiaries. These figures differ modestly by source and counting rules, but they must not be collapsed into a claim that only 150,000 hectares were redistributed. The distinction matters because cotton production depended disproportionately on the irrigated portion. Former owners could retain protected holdings under the applicable small-property rules, while ejidatarios faced the difficult task of reorganizing land, water, and credit.

The reform created a collective system. Rather than divide the land into small family plots, which would have fragmented the irrigation works and the cotton economy, the government organized collective ejidos managed through credit societies financed by the Banco Ejidal. The societies bought machinery, hired technicians, marketed the crop, and distributed income to members, and ejidatarios worked under a division of tasks. The arrangement aimed to preserve scale economies while changing ownership. It was an unusual experiment in applying agrarian reform to a highly commercial economy, since much land reform in Mexico addressed subsistence farming.

The results were mixed. The reform ended the dominance of the large estates and gave thousands of families land and a stake in cotton revenue. But the credit societies depended heavily on bank officials, whose control of finance, technical decisions, and marketing generated complaints about bureaucracy and favoritism. Weather, water allocation, and cotton prices affected income, and the withdrawal of private capital and the hostility of former owners complicated operations. Over time, internal divisions and financial stress led some ejidatarios to press for greater autonomy or individual parcels. La Laguna therefore became a laboratory in which collective farming and state credit were tested, with consequences both for the ejidatarios and for the national debate over what agrarian reform should be.

Section 74.7

Yucatán, Sonora, Mexicali, and Regional Experiences of Agrarian Transformation

Cardenista agrarian reform varied by region because Mexico's rural economies varied. In Yucatán the henequen (sisal) economy had produced fortunes for planters and misery for workers, many of them Maya, during the Porfiriato, and the revolutionary period had brought fitful reform. In 1937 Cárdenas issued resolutions that redistributed henequen lands to ejidatarios, numbering in the tens of thousands, while the large processing facilities remained in different hands and were later brought under an organization created to coordinate the industry. The ejidos faced falling prices, mismanagement, and dependence on a single export crop. Because henequen cultivation required organized processing and marketing, the reform produced a mixture of collective and individual arrangements rather than a uniform model.

In the Yaqui River valley of Sonora, the agrarian question was bound to the history of the Yaqui people, who had endured nineteenth-century and Porfirian military campaigns, deportation, and land dispossession and who continued to organize for territory and control of water. Cárdenas met with Yaqui representatives, and an agrarian agreement published in the Diario Oficial on October 30, 1937 addressed claims in the Yaqui region. A further resolution, published on October 22, 1940, defined and titled the lands to be restituted to Yaqui communities. These acts were significant acknowledgments of territorial rights, but documentary recognition and effective demarcation, water access, and autonomous control were different matters. Expanding commercial agriculture and claims by non-Indigenous farmers in the irrigated valley continued to generate disputes. The two dates show a process of negotiation and incomplete enforcement, not a single, final settlement in 1937.

The Mexicali valley, in Baja California, had been developed with Colorado River irrigation and was dominated by large holdings, notably those of the American-owned Colorado River Land Company, with a labor force that included Mexican and Chinese workers. During the Cárdenas years, land was distributed to ejidatarios, partly through pressure from organized agricultural workers and the government's aim to settle the frontier with Mexican farmers and secure national control of border lands. In Morelos, the heartland of Zapatismo, ejidos had been established earlier, and the Cárdenas period brought further support, including the cooperative sugar mill at Zacatepec founded in 1938. In Michoacán, the large Italian-owned estates of Lombardía and Nueva Italia were broken up in 1938 and distributed to ejidatarios, a change that drew on the president's long familiarity with the region.

Across these regions, Indigenous claims and national policy interacted in complex ways. Many Indigenous communities pressed claims through ejido and communal land procedures, using the government's language of reform to recover or defend territory. Others resisted programs that imposed collective organization or national administrative structures, or that required them to define their land in terms of ejido categories that did not match local practice. The government created a Department of Indigenous Affairs in 1936, and policies oscillated between assimilation and respect for community organization. Regional variation therefore shaped not only who received land but also the terms on which communities encountered the state, and national statistics conceal differences in ownership, irrigation, labor, and autonomy.

Section 74.8

Achievements and Limits of Cardenista Land Reform: Production, Gender, Credit, and Rural Inequality

Quantitative estimates of the Cárdenas land reform vary by source and method. Scholarly work commonly places the total distributed in his term above 18 million hectares, while some official summaries give more than 20 million. The difference depends on the years covered, on whether the totals include restitutions, grants, expansions, and communal confirmations, and on whether they count land at the time of presidential resolution or at the time of physical possession. Beneficiaries are commonly estimated in the high hundreds of thousands. For context, the total distributed in the preceding two decades was far smaller, and by 1940 the ejido sector accounted for roughly half of cultivated land by commonly cited census-based estimates, up from a much smaller share in 1930. Students should treat these as approximate and should identify the source series in any citation.

Land quality and access to resources shaped outcomes as much as area. Large portions of the land distributed were marginal, rain-fed, or poorly located, and the irrigated land that went to ejidatarios was a minority of the total. Credit was crucial but unevenly distributed: the Banco Ejidal reached important regions, yet many ejidatarios relied on local moneylenders or received little at all. Technical assistance and market access were limited, and ejido plots were often too small to sustain families. Average productivity in many ejidos lagged behind private commercial farms, although studies differ on how much of the gap reflected structural disadvantages rather than the ejido form itself.

Gender profoundly structured access. The agrarian code treated the male household head as the normal beneficiary, and women could be recognized as ejidatarias only under restricted conditions, typically as single women or widows with dependents. In practice, very few held titles. Women nonetheless participated in agrarian movements, strikes, petitions, and household and field labor, and they ran households and small enterprises while men migrated or organized. Their exclusion from formal rights meant that benefits of reform flowed through male household heads, and equal agrarian rights for women came only decades later, with legal reforms in the 1970s. Studies of women in rural Mexico, such as those collected by Stephanie Mitchell and Patience Schell, show how formal law and local practice diverged.

Cardenista land reform was historically transformative without solving the agrarian problem. It broke the political and economic power of many hacendados, it gave hundreds of thousands of families land and legal recognition, and it integrated peasants into national politics as organized actors. It also left millions of rural people landless or on inadequate plots, preserved large private holdings protected by nonaffectability certificates, and created new dependencies on state credit and officials. Under later governments, support shifted toward commercial private agriculture and irrigation, and the ejido's fortunes declined. The reform thus appears as both a real redistribution and the foundation of a rural political order in which land access, credit, and political loyalty were closely intertwined.

The politics of memory also shaped assessments. Supporters of Cárdenas remembered the reform as the moment the Revolution reached the villages, and many ejidatarios and their descendants held that view for generations. Critics, including some agronomists and later neoliberal reformers, argued that the ejido tied peasants to small plots and state credit, discouraging investment and mobility. Both judgments reflect later debates as much as 1930s conditions. A careful historian asks what alternatives were realistically available to the government in 1936, which political forces demanded redistribution, and what peasants themselves said they wanted, as recorded in petitions and testimony.

Section 74.9

The CTM, Vicente Lombardo Toledano, and the Reorganization of Mexican Labor

The Confederación de Trabajadores de México was founded at a congress in Mexico City in February 1936. It grew out of the Comité Nacional de Defensa Proletaria, which had united unions behind Cárdenas in June 1935, and it brought together industrial unions of railroad workers, miners, electricians, oil workers, and others with the CGOCM and many regional organizations. Its first secretary general, Vicente Lombardo Toledano, was a Marxist intellectual and former CROM figure who was not a member of the Communist Party but cooperated with it. The CTM adopted the goal of a classless society and aimed to unify a labor movement that had been divided among competing federations. It claimed a membership in the hundreds of thousands, though such figures are organizational claims and should be treated cautiously.

The relationship among the CTM, the Communist Party, employers, and the government was complicated. The Communist Party, following the international shift toward popular fronts, supported unity behind the government and played an influential role within the CTM. Employers, organized in groups such as the Confederación Patronal de la República Mexicana, resisted collective bargaining, and in Monterrey, where Cárdenas visited in February 1936 during a labor conflict, the president warned that employers could not use lockouts as a weapon against workers. Union leaders disagreed about how closely to tie the CTM to the government. Some, like Lombardo, argued that the labor movement should support the Cárdenas program while preserving its own organization; others, like Fidel Velázquez and his associates, favored closer cooperation with officials.

Under Cárdenas, strikes and collective bargaining became central features of public life. The Federal Labor Law of 1931 gave the Federal Conciliation and Arbitration Board a major role, and the government used it to support wage increases, collective contracts, and the extension of unionization. Industrial unions, built around entire industries rather than single workplaces, negotiated nationally in sectors such as railways, mining, and petroleum, and unions gained influence over hiring, promotion, and workplace rules. The right to strike was widely used; the number of strikes rose in the mid-1930s. Yet legal recognition and arbitration also placed unions under government supervision: strikes could be declared legal or illegal, and union leaders dependent on official favor were vulnerable to pressure.

Organized industrial workers were a small part of the Mexican workforce. Most Mexicans in the 1930s still lived in rural areas, and the great majority of rural laborers, domestic workers, artisans, street vendors, and informal workers were outside national unions. The benefits of collective bargaining flowed principally to relatively privileged groups in strategic industries, and the CTM's political weight exceeded its share of workers. Its relationship with peasants, who were organized separately and at times in competition, also shaped the party reorganization examined later. The CTM thus combined genuine representation and bargaining power with dependence on the state, a combination that would harden after Lombardo left the leadership in 1941.

Section 74.10

State Economic Policy, the Expropriation Law, and the Nationalization of Railroads

The Cárdenas government combined private-sector development with expanding state roles. It promoted public works, irrigation, roads, and electrification, created institutions such as the Comisión Federal de Electricidad in 1937, and expanded state credit. It also regulated foreign-owned public utilities and, as labor disputes grew, increasingly mediated between employers and workers. Economic nationalism in this period meant the assertion of national control over strategic resources and services, not a rejection of private investment as such; the government continued to seek domestic and foreign capital for many activities, and business groups with Mexican ownership often prospered.

A central legal instrument was the Expropriation Law of November 1936, which set out the grounds on which the government could take private property for reasons of public utility, with compensation. It gave the executive broad authority and specified that indemnity would be calculated on the basis of declared fiscal value and could be paid over time. The law drew on Article 27 and became the basis of major expropriations. Critics argued that it granted excessive discretion and threatened investment, while supporters regarded it as a lawful means of implementing the constitutional principle that property serves a social function.

The first major expropriation of an industry under the law was the national railway. Ferrocarriles Nacionales de México, formed in the early twentieth century through the consolidation of lines under government control, was a mixed enterprise in which private shareholders retained interests; it suffered from debts, aging equipment, and labor conflict. By a decree of June 23, 1937, the government expropriated the remaining private holdings and brought the system under public administration. The decree distinguished the expropriation from the experiment that followed: beginning in May 1938, the railroad workers' union took over management of the system under a regime of worker administration, a form of workplace participation unusual in the Americas.

Operating a national railway proved difficult. The system required heavy investment in track, rolling stock, and maintenance, and it carried a fiscal burden. Worker administration faced technical, financial, and organizational problems, including disputes between union leaders and professional managers, deficits, and the pressures of political expectations; the experiment was curtailed after 1940. The episode shows that nationalization did not by itself resolve questions of management and finance, and that the state became responsible for enterprises whose efficiency affected the whole economy. The same challenge would soon face the government in petroleum.

Section 74.11

Petroleum Companies, Oil Workers, the Labor Dispute, and the Road to Expropriation

Petroleum had been a foreign-dominated industry since the early twentieth century. British, American, and Dutch companies developed fields in the Huasteca and around Tampico and Tuxpan, and Mexico became one of the world's major producers by around 1921. The principal firms included the Anglo-Dutch Compañía Mexicana de Petróleo El Águila, the Huasteca Petroleum Company, a subsidiary of Standard Oil of New Jersey, and Sinclair and other American firms. Article 27 of the Constitution of 1917 declared direct national domain over subsoil resources, and the companies resisted its application, leading to years of diplomatic and legal conflict resolved in the 1920s through compromises that protected many existing claims. By the 1930s production had declined from its peak, but the industry remained central to export earnings, fuel supply, and national symbolism.

The legal distinction between ownership of underground resources and ownership of operating assets was essential. The constitutional claim to national domain over subsoil petroleum did not itself transfer wells, pipelines, refineries, tanks, and ships to the state. Those were the property of the companies, which held rights under concessions and earlier titles. The expropriation of 1938 would therefore concern the companies' installations and assets, while the principle of national subsoil ownership had been declared in 1917. Students should not confuse the two.

The immediate origin of the confrontation was labor. Oil workers had organized in unions that merged in December 1935 into the Sindicato de Trabajadores Petroleros de la República Mexicana (STPRM). The union sought a single collective contract with the companies, and its demands included higher wages, improved benefits and housing, a shorter work week, and a greater role for Mexican workers and union influence over personnel. Negotiations failed, and the union struck on May 28, 1937. The strike disrupted fuel supply and prompted government intervention; after about two weeks the workers returned to work at the president's urging while the Federal Conciliation and Arbitration Board examined the economic claims.

A panel of experts, appointed under the Board's procedures, reported in 1937 on the companies' financial capacity, concluding that they could afford a significant increase in labor costs. The companies disputed the findings and argued that the calculations misrepresented their profits and obligations. In December 1937 the Board issued an award requiring the companies to meet a portion of the union's demands, in an amount substantially below what the union had asked. The companies sought protection from the Supreme Court through amparo, objecting to the award and in particular to clauses affecting management control. The Court rejected the appeal in early March 1938, confirming the award.

A dispute that began as a conflict over employment conditions thus became a confrontation over state authority. The companies refused to comply fully with an award upheld by the highest court, and they were widely perceived in Mexico as placing their interests above national law. The government, for its part, had backed the workers and the arbitration process and could not accept open defiance of the Court and the Board. The Board declared the companies in noncompliance in mid-March, and negotiations between Cárdenas and company representatives failed to produce agreement. The question was no longer wages but whether foreign firms could refuse to obey a decision of Mexican authorities. The government responded on March 18.

The companies' position was not simply obstruction. Their managers argued that the union's demands, together with taxes and falling production, threatened the viability of operations, and they pointed to the competition of Venezuelan and American producers. Some of their objections, especially to the clauses that would have given the union influence over management decisions, raised genuine issues about control of the enterprise. Yet the companies also underestimated the political climate in Mexico and relied on the expectation that their governments would shield them from consequences. The dispute shows two sets of actors reading the same legal and economic situation in incompatible ways.

Historical Vocabulary
CTM

The Confederación de Trabajadores de México was founded in February 1936 as a national labor confederation. It combined genuine collective bargaining power with political alignment to the government, a combination that shaped labor politics for decades.

Expropriation

Expropriation is the taking of private property by the state for public purposes, usually with compensation. In 1938 Mexico expropriated the assets of foreign petroleum companies under the Expropriation Law of 1936 and Article 27. It differs from nationalization of subsoil resources, which had a constitutional basis in 1917.

National domain

National domain (dominio directo) is the constitutional principle that subsoil resources belong to the nation. Article 27 declared it in 1917. The declaration did not transfer company-owned equipment and installations to the state; that required the separate act of expropriation in 1938.

Pemex

Petróleos Mexicanos (Pemex) was created by presidential decree on June 7, 1938, as the public enterprise to administer the expropriated industry. Its creation followed, and was distinct from, the March 18 expropriation.

Section 74.12

March 18, 1938: The Petroleum Expropriation and the Assertion of National Economic Sovereignty

The decision to expropriate was made after consultation with advisers, including Francisco Múgica and others in the cabinet, and after a final effort to secure compliance. On the evening of March 18, 1938, Cárdenas broadcast a message to the nation announcing the decree. The decree, issued under the Expropriation Law and Article 27, declared the machinery, installations, pipelines, refineries, storage tanks, and other assets of the named companies to be expropriated in the public interest. Roughly seventeen companies were affected, including El Águila and the Huasteca Petroleum Company. The decree provided for compensation, specifying that indemnity would be paid by the government over a period of years.

The legal logic of the decree was precise. The subsoil already belonged to the nation under Article 27, and the government did not claim to be establishing national domain in 1938. It expropriated the companies' operating assets, citing their defiance of the Court-sustained award and the public interest in uninterrupted fuel supply. The decree also distinguished between expropriation and confiscation: it recognized the obligation to compensate, though the amount, form, and timing became disputed. The creation of Pemex in June was a separate institutional step to manage the assets the state now held.

Public response in Mexico was strong. Organized labor supported the decision, the CTM and oil workers mobilized, and in the days after the announcement large demonstrations filled the central plaza of Mexico City. Across the country, individuals and groups, including many women, donated money, jewelry, livestock, and household goods to a national fund to help pay compensation. Such contributions were symbolically important though small relative to the amounts at stake. The enthusiasm was not unanimous: some Mexican business interests worried about investment and relations with the United States, and some opponents of Cárdenas saw the act as a move toward statism. But expropriation proved one of the most widely supported actions of his government, and it became a durable symbol of national sovereignty.

Foreign reactions were hostile. The oil companies denounced the decree, organized boycotts of Mexican oil and refused to sell equipment and spare parts, and lobbied their governments. Britain, whose interests were involved through El Águila, reacted sharply, and relations were broken in May 1938. The United States, under the Good Neighbor Policy and with an ambassador, Josephus Daniels, sympathetic to Mexico, did not retaliate militarily, but Secretary of State Cordell Hull insisted in diplomatic correspondence that compensation must be prompt, adequate, and effective. The U.S. government pressed for settlement and for compensation of other claims, including agrarian ones, while weighing its strategic interest in good relations as war loomed in Europe and Asia. Mexico's position was that the nation had the sovereign right to expropriate and to determine compensation under its own law.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Petroleum Expropriation Primarily a Labor Settlement, an Assertion of National Sovereignty, or an Instrument of State-Building?

The question: Historians agree on the sequence of events but differ on how to weigh the motives and meanings of the expropriation.

Interpretation A — A labor dispute that escalated: Emphasizing the origins of the conflict, some scholars argue that expropriation resulted from the companies' refusal to comply with a legal award and from the government's commitment to the arbitration system and to the workers it had mobilized. On this view, the decision was a response to a specific crisis rather than the execution of a long-planned nationalization.
Interpretation B — An assertion of national sovereignty: Other historians, including Mexican scholars writing in the tradition of Jesús Silva Herzog and diplomatic historians such as Lorenzo Meyer, emphasize the long conflict over Article 27, foreign control of resources, and the symbolism of defying powerful companies. On this view, the labor dispute was the occasion and sovereignty the underlying cause.
Interpretation C — An instrument of state-building: A third line of interpretation, associated with studies of the Mexican state, stresses that expropriation expanded the state's economic role, generated revenue and patronage, and strengthened the executive and the party's base among workers and nationalists.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same documents: the Board's award, the Court's ruling, company and union records, presidential statements, diplomatic correspondence, and later accounts by participants. They differ in emphasis on short-term triggers versus long-term causes.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand Cardenismo: as a reactive reformism, a nationalist project, or a state-expanding strategy. The interpretations overlap more than they conflict, and many historians combine them.

Section 74.13

The Creation of Pemex, Petroleum Administration, and the International Consequences of Expropriation

After March 18, 1938, the government had to run an industry it had taken over. In the first weeks an administrative body managed the installations, and on June 7, 1938, a presidential decree created Petróleos Mexicanos as the public enterprise responsible for exploration, production, refining, and marketing. The sequence matters: the 1917 Constitution had declared national ownership of subsoil resources, the March decree expropriated the companies' operating assets, and the June decree established the institution to manage them. A separate distribution company was also created to handle domestic and export sales.

The practical challenges were severe. Foreign firms withdrew many managers and technical personnel, denied spare parts and chemical supplies, and tried to obstruct markets and transport; Mexico consequently faced shortages of specialists in geology, refining, and logistics. Experienced Mexican petroleum workers, engineers trained before 1938 at the national university and other institutions, and a new generation of technical students helped sustain operations. The Instituto Politécnico Nacional, founded in 1936, represented an important longer-term training initiative, not an already established reservoir of numerous graduates in March 1938. Production and maintenance faced financial pressure, and the government had to manage large payrolls while constructing a functioning public enterprise. Labor representatives participated in administration, but disagreements over hiring, technical management, and union influence led to subsequent curbs on their powers. National ownership transferred responsibility for financial and operational decisions to the state; it did not immediately create the necessary expertise or capital.

Markets and diplomacy shaped the early years. Shut out of important traditional markets, Mexico sold some petroleum to buyers in Germany, Italy, and Japan, including through barter arrangements before the widening world war changed those relationships. Mexico also supplied domestic consumers and sought other foreign purchasers. U.S. and British pressure had different trajectories. Britain severed diplomatic ties in May 1938; a resumption was announced in 1941 and fuller diplomatic exchange followed thereafter. Washington, seeking hemispheric cooperation as the international crisis deepened, increasingly favored an agreed compensation mechanism. Neither accommodation by the United States nor renewed contact with Britain meant an immediate resolution of every commercial or petroleum claim.

Compensation was negotiated in stages rather than settled in a single 1942 action. A November 19, 1941 U.S.–Mexico agreement established a framework for appraisal and payment of American petroleum claims; a joint assessment by Morris L. Cooke and Manuel J. Zevada followed in April 1942, and implementing arrangements and installments continued in subsequent years. U.S. diplomatic records still anticipated a final installment in 1947. British-connected interests, including those associated with El Águila, reached a later compensation settlement in 1947. The different dates refer to framework agreements, valuations, implementing payments, and separate national claimants. Mexico retained public control of the industry, but running and expanding it demanded continuing technical expertise, financing, and negotiations with labor. Pemex became both an emblem of economic sovereignty and a complex enterprise whose finances shaped subsequent policy.

The labor dimension of the new enterprise was especially difficult. The oil workers' union had won the dispute and expected a share in the benefits of national ownership, while the government needed discipline, productivity, and financial restraint. Payroll grew, and the union secured positions in administration and substantial benefits. Disagreements over wages, appointments, and the use of revenue recurred, and Cárdenas himself warned that the nation's sacrifice should not be wasted. The tension between workers as beneficiaries of nationalization and the state as its manager anticipated the long and conflicted relationship between Pemex and its union.

Section 74.14

Education, Women, Indigenous Communities, Public Health, and the Social Dimensions of Cardenismo

The social programs of the Cárdenas years extended beyond land and labor. Rural education grew through federal schools, regional peasant schools, and cultural missions, and technical education received new emphasis. In 1936 the government founded the Instituto Politécnico Nacional in Mexico City to train engineers and technicians for national development, and a workers' university and other institutions pursued related goals. Public health efforts included sanitary campaigns, rural clinics, and the creation of a Secretariat of Public Assistance in 1937. Their reach was limited by budgets and personnel, but they reflected a view of the state as responsible for welfare, and they gave teachers, doctors, and agronomists new roles in rural life.

Women pressed their own demands. Organizations such as the Frente Único Pro Derechos de la Mujer, founded in 1935 and associated with activists like Refugio García, campaigned for suffrage, labor protections, and legal equality. In 1937 Cárdenas sent Congress a proposal to amend the Constitution to recognize women's right to vote. Congress and state legislatures approved the amendment, but the formal declaration that would have made it effective was never issued. Historians cite several reasons, including fears among some politicians that women would vote for conservative or Catholic candidates and the political cost of the 1940 succession. Nationwide women's suffrage came only in 1953, and the 1937 initiative therefore did not become effective law. Women nevertheless participated in unions, peasant movements, teachers' organizations, and the party's popular sector.

Indigenous communities encountered the state in several ways. The Department of Indigenous Affairs, created in 1936, sought to coordinate policy; experimental programs such as the Tarascan Project in Michoacán, begun in 1939, used Indigenous-language literacy as a bridge to Spanish; and the 1940 Inter-American Indigenist Congress at Pátzcuaro reflected the growing international discussion of indigenismo. Policy oscillated between assimilation and respect for community organization. Communities also pressed their own demands for land, schools, and recognition, sometimes using the language of reform to defend local autonomy and sometimes resisting programs that weakened it. National cultural policy did not speak with one voice, and Indigenous responses varied by region and community.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Composite Profiles of Cardenista Mexico

The following are composite analytical profiles built from patterns described in the scholarship. They are not testimony attributed to any historical individual.

An ejidatario household. A family receiving an ejido parcel in the 1930s typically combined maize and bean cultivation with seasonal wage labor. The household head held the legal rights, while wives and children contributed field work, food preparation, and small-scale production. Success depended on water, credit, and access to markets, and household income often remained precarious.

A rural teacher. A federal teacher might serve a village with little prior schooling, teaching literacy and hygiene by day and helping petitioners prepare agrarian paperwork at night. Teachers often lived with hostility from landowners or religious opponents, earned low and irregular pay, and risked violence.

An industrial worker. A union member in the railroad or oil industries gained wage and benefit protections through collective contracts, participated in union assemblies and strikes, and depended on union leaders who also served as political intermediaries. Such workers formed a privileged minority compared with rural and informal laborers.

A woman political organizer. Women activists in suffrage, peasant, and teacher organizations built networks and lobbied officials without formal voting rights. They faced opposition from conservative politicians and often from within reform movements, yet they shaped public debate about citizenship.

Section 74.15

Mexico, Roosevelt's Good Neighbor Policy, the Spanish Civil War, and International Antifascism

Mexican foreign policy under Cárdenas combined principle and pragmatism. The Good Neighbor Policy of Franklin D. Roosevelt had reduced the likelihood of U.S. military intervention, and Ambassador Josephus Daniels favored negotiation. The oil dispute, the agrarian expropriations affecting American owners, and the question of compensation tested that policy. Washington pressed Mexico to compensate promptly but avoided coercive measures beyond diplomatic pressure and some economic leverage, and it sought stable relations as war approached. Mexican leaders defended their sovereign right to expropriate and negotiated payments over time while seeking markets and credit.

The Spanish Civil War, which began in July 1936, became a central issue. Mexico supported the Spanish Republic diplomatically, sent some arms and supplies, and refused to recognize the Franco regime, maintaining that position for decades. Cárdenas's government also offered asylum. In June 1937 the first group of Spanish children, known as the Niños de Morelia, arrived and were housed in a boarding school in Morelia. In 1938 the government sponsored La Casa de España to host exiled Spanish intellectuals, which became El Colegio de México in 1940, and in 1939 the ship Sinaia brought a large contingent of refugees to Veracruz. Estimates of the number of Spanish exiles accepted in Mexico over the following years range around twenty thousand or more, and they made major contributions to Mexican science, publishing, and universities.

Mexico's asylum policy had limits. The government accepted political exiles selectively, and its immigration policy remained restrictive toward other groups, including many Jewish refugees. One of the most visible decisions was the admission of Leon Trotsky, who arrived in January 1937 after the intercession of artists and political figures, and who lived in Coyoacán until he was assassinated in August 1940. The reception of Trotsky displeased communists and the Soviet government, and also illustrated a policy of asylum for persecuted political figures, though its motives and consequences were debated within the Cardenista coalition.

These policies combined nonintervention, anti-fascist commitments, sovereignty, and commercial pragmatism. Mexico protested aggression at the League of Nations, including the German annexation of Austria, supported collective security in principle, and rejected involvement in foreign wars while selling oil where it could. Its stance won it prestige among progressive governments, and its willingness to settle with the United States once war began reflected shared strategic interests. Mexican diplomacy thus exercised agency within constraints: the country asserted principle while recognizing the economic and strategic power of its northern neighbor.

Section 74.16

From the PNR to the PRM: The Workers', Peasants', Popular, and Military Sectors

The PNR, founded in 1929, had been a federation of regional caudillos, local parties, and officeholders, held together by Calles's authority. Its weaknesses were evident by the mid-1930s: it had no mass membership of its own, its nominations were controlled by leaders, and its regional components pursued independent interests. Cárdenas proposed to reorganize it around organized social groups, bringing workers and peasants into the party as constituencies and reducing the independence of regional bosses. His proposal reflected a political logic: the groups that had supported him against Calles would become the party's base.

On March 30, 1938, twelve days after the oil expropriation, the Partido de la Revolución Mexicana was constituted through a founding pact signed by representatives of the participating organizations. The PRM was organized in four sectors: a workers' sector, including the CTM, the CROM, the CGT, and other unions; a peasant sector, centered on peasant leagues; a popular sector, which included cooperatives, public employees, artisans, small merchants, youth, and women; and a military sector, representing the armed forces. The peasant sector took more definite form with the founding of the Confederación Nacional Campesina on August 28, 1938, which united peasant organizations under a single body. Membership was largely collective, through affiliation of organizations, rather than individual.

The PRM expanded political participation by giving organized workers, peasants, and other groups formal representation in nominations and party structures, and it provided channels for bargaining over policies and candidacies. The declaration of principles used the language of class struggle and of a long-term transformation of society. At the same time, it subordinated electoral activity to a centralized structure in which the president and party leadership exercised decisive influence, and it linked organizational recognition to loyalty. The inclusion of the military sector, defended as a way to channel the army's political role into civilian structures, also underscored the continuing weight of the armed forces.

The question of independence versus incorporation does not have a single answer. The CTM, CNC, and other groups had real constituencies, distinct leaderships, and internal disagreements, and they won concrete gains for members. Membership in the governing coalition brought access to officials, credit, labor rights, and recognition. It also created mechanisms of discipline: leaders who displeased the president or party could lose resources and recognition, and rival organizations had little room. Scholars such as Arnaldo Córdova have stressed the state's construction of mass politics, while Knight and others stress the continuing autonomy and tensions of the constituent groups. The PRM therefore stands as an institution that represented organized groups and also placed them under centralized direction.

Party Organization: Essential Terminology

PRM

The Partido de la Revolución Mexicana, constituted on March 30, 1938, replaced the PNR with a party organized by social sectors. It is the intermediate stage between the PNR of 1929 and the PRI of 1946.

CNC and CNOP

The Confederación Nacional Campesina (CNC) was founded on August 28, 1938 to unify peasant organizations. The Confederación Nacional de Organizaciones Populares (CNOP), founded in 1943, brought together the heterogeneous popular sector.

Corporatism

Corporatism here means a system in which organized interest groups are incorporated into the state or governing party, which recognizes and sometimes regulates them in exchange for loyalty. Scholars debate how fully it describes Mexico, but it captures the sector-based structure of the PRM.

PRI and party-state

The Partido Revolucionario Institucional succeeded the PRM in 1946. Party-state is an analytical term for a system in which one party and the government are closely fused. Its application to Mexico is debated, and it should be used with attention to how elections, patronage, and institutions actually worked.

Section 74.17

The Presidential Election of 1940, Manuel Ávila Camacho, and the Conservative Turn

The succession of 1940 tested the system Cárdenas had built. The no-reelection rule barred him from continuing, and the PRM's sectors had to choose a candidate. Francisco Múgica, a veteran of the 1917 Constitutional Convention and a leading radical, had supporters among reformers. Cárdenas instead favored Manuel Ávila Camacho, his secretary of war, a moderate who seemed able to unite the army, business, and the sectors in a period of economic strain, foreign pressure after the oil expropriation, the Cedillo revolt, and approaching war. Labor and peasant organizations endorsed Ávila Camacho, and he became the party's candidate.

The opposition gathered around General Juan Andreu Almazán, a regional military leader and businessman who ran under a separate organization. His campaign drew support from business groups, middle-class voters, conservatives, Catholics, some disaffected military men, and others who feared radicalism or resented the party machine. The campaign was marked by rallies, polemics, and violence. On July 7, 1940, election day, clashes at polling places in Mexico City and elsewhere left many dead, and accusations of ballot-box seizures and fraud were widespread. Historians document substantial electoral violence and serious allegations of manipulation, and estimates of casualties vary by source.

Official results gave Ávila Camacho an overwhelming majority, which Almazán rejected, claiming victory and threatening resistance. He did not mount a successful uprising, and the United States, which had ties with Ávila Camacho and wished stability, signaled support for the official outcome; Vice President-elect Henry Wallace attended the inauguration on December 1, 1940. The 1940 election therefore cannot be described as an uncontested or fully competitive transfer. It showed both the real discontent of segments of society and the capacity of the governing coalition to prevail through organization, control of the electoral machinery, and force.

Ávila Camacho's presidency emphasized national unity, moderation, and conciliation. He told an interviewer that he was a believer, a statement understood as a signal of religious accommodation, and his government eased tensions with the Church and softened socialist education, which was formally removed from Article 3 in 1946. He promoted private enterprise alongside state action, and agrarian distribution slowed. Yet Cardenista institutions were not dismantled at once. The CTM, CNC, ejidos, Pemex, and the party sectors persisted, and the shift was gradual: a change of tone and priority within a framework that the new government inherited and used.

The election also revealed the limits of Cardenista radicalism. Cárdenas's decision to support a moderate candidate has been explained in several ways: as a recognition that radical measures had reached political limits, as a response to the threat of a military or conservative challenge, and as an effort to protect the reforms already achieved by choosing a successor acceptable to many sectors. Historians such as Luis González emphasize his belief in institutional continuity, while others stress the pressures of business, the Church, the United States, and the army. The decision illustrates that Cárdenas, however committed to reform, governed within constraints that he judged it prudent to respect.

Section 74.18

World War II, National Unity, Organized Labor, and Industrial Development, 1940–1945

The Second World War reshaped Mexico's economy and diplomacy. After German submarines sank the Mexican oil tankers Potrero del Llano and Faja de Oro in May 1942, the Ávila Camacho government declared a state of war against the Axis powers. Mexico supplied raw materials, strategic minerals, and labor to the Allied effort and deepened bilateral trade arrangements with the United States. The governments also advanced the settlement of longstanding claims through steps originating in their November 1941 agreement and the 1942 Cooke–Zevada appraisal; all payments and petroleum disputes were not completed in 1942. Cárdenas served as secretary of national defense from 1942 to 1945, helping coordinate mobilization; legislation for obligatory military service had already been adopted. Mexican Squadron 201, trained in the United States, served in the Philippines in 1945. Mexico's military contribution should be understood alongside the larger economic, labor, and diplomatic dimensions of its Allied involvement.

The Bracero Program, established by an agreement in August 1942, recruited Mexican agricultural workers for U.S. farms and railroads, and over its lifetime until 1964 involved millions of contracts. Wartime migration eased labor demand in some regions, produced remittances, and generated abuses and disputes over wages, housing, and treatment. Within Mexico, wartime demand and import shortages stimulated manufacturing, including textiles, steel, chemicals, and food processing, supported by state institutions such as the national development bank, Nacional Financiera. Industrial growth, urbanization, and inflation accompanied the war years, and the economy's balance shifted from agriculture toward industry. These developments are examined comparatively in Chapter 75; here the interest lies in their political effects.

National unity was the governing slogan. Organized labor, now led by Fidel Velázquez after Lombardo Toledano stepped down in 1941, accepted a no-strike pledge and cooperation agreements with employers and the government, including the Workers' Unity Pact of 1942 and an industrial labor pact in 1945. Real wages for many workers fell as prices rose, and strikes were discouraged, which strained union members while strengthening the position of leaders who bargained with officials. The government also issued a measure creating the crime of "social dissolution" in 1941, later used against dissidents. Unity was therefore real in official discourse and in the coalition's institutions, but it was not universal agreement: workers, peasants, and political opponents experienced wartime policy unequally.

Social experiences varied. Industrial workers in Mexico City and other centers saw employment gains alongside price pressures. Braceros faced separation from their families and uncertain conditions abroad. Women entered factories, offices, and commerce in growing numbers while remaining excluded from nationwide suffrage. Rural communities experienced a shift in government attention from land redistribution toward irrigation, commercial agriculture, and industry, with uneven continued support for ejidatarios. The Social Insurance Law was published in the Diario Oficial de la Federación on January 19, 1943, creating the legal framework for the Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social (IMSS); the institution began providing services in 1944. Initial coverage chiefly benefited particular groups of formal urban workers, not the entire population. Industrialization was part of a wider hemispheric phenomenon, not solely a Mexican one, and Mexico's wartime growth reflected both domestic policy and Allied demand.

Mexican workers also experienced the war through the Mexican labor movement's growing dependence on the government. Union leaders who supported national unity secured recognition, benefits, and political positions, while dissident currents, including communists and independent unions, found less room to operate. The pact structure reinforced the notion that economic growth and labor peace were national priorities, and it helped lay the groundwork for the stable relationship among government, employers, and official unions that would characterize the following decades.

Section 74.19

The Creation of the PRI, Civilian Presidential Leadership, and the Reorganization of the Governing Party, 1943–1946

The reorganization of the party continued under Ávila Camacho. In February 1943 the popular sector was consolidated as the Confederación Nacional de Organizaciones Populares (CNOP), bringing public employees, professionals, small merchants, cooperatives, and other urban and middle-class groups into an organization that diluted the earlier weight of workers and peasants. The military sector was eliminated from the party structure in December 1940 at the outset of Ávila Camacho's government; this exclusion, subsequently confirmed by the PRI's organization, signaled the army's changing relationship to civilian political institutions. Officers remained influential in the state and participated politically as individuals, but the armed forces no longer constituted a formally represented party sector. The 1940 and 1943 steps should therefore not be treated as one undated 'early 1940s' reform.

In January 1946 the PRM was transformed into the Partido Revolucionario Institucional (PRI). The reorganization convention at Mexico City's Teatro Metropólitan is documented for January 18–19; a documentary chronology in Memoria Política de México identifies January 18 as the party's founding decision, while another institutional chronology gives January 26. The specific act underlying that latter date has not been independently established here and should not be invented. The national electoral institute separately records March 30, 1946 as the party's registration date. Founding convention, reported reorganization date, and registration are distinct categories of evidence. The PRI retained workers', peasants', and popular sectors, accepted individual members alongside collective affiliations, and recast party language toward democracy, social justice, and national unity. Its founding documents still acknowledged class conflict, however, so the shift was a change of political emphasis rather than the complete disappearance of that vocabulary.

The PRI's formation coincided with a new federal electoral law, promulgated in January 1946, which centralized oversight of elections in federal bodies under the Secretariat of the Interior and set national requirements for the registration of parties. The law restricted regional and small parties and favored large national organizations, strengthening the governing party's advantage. Opposition parties existed, including the National Action Party, founded in 1939, and conservative movements such as sinarquismo, but their access to office was limited. The party's relationship with government institutions deepened as it became the vehicle for nominating candidates for most offices.

Miguel Alemán Valdés, formerly secretary of the interior, secured important support from organized labor and other PRM constituencies during 1945. He was acclaimed as the workers' sector candidate in June 1945 and was selected as the PRM's presidential candidate later that year; the new PRI inherited and adopted his candidacy in January 1946 rather than initiating it from nothing. In the July 1946 election Alemán defeated Ezequiel Padilla, the former foreign minister who stood in opposition. Alemán took office on December 1, 1946. As a civilian lawyer from a postrevolutionary political generation, he embodied a marked break with the succession of revolutionary military leaders, although earlier civilian presidents and interim officeholders complicate any literal 'first civilian ever' formula. His administration promoted industrialization, infrastructure, cooperation with business, and increased protection for certain small private landholders; those longer-term policies belong primarily to subsequent chapters.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: The PNR (1929), the PRM (1938), and the PRI (1946)

What Changed

  • The PNR (1929) was a federation of regional bosses and local parties under Calles; the PRM (1938) organized workers, peasants, popular groups, and the military in sectors; the PRI (1946) kept three civilian sectors and restored individual affiliation
  • Ideological language moved from revolutionary nationalism to class-oriented reform and then to democracy, social justice, and national unity
  • The army moved from party sector to individual participation, and civilians replaced generals in the presidency
  • Electoral oversight became more centralized in 1946

What Stayed the Same

  • A single dominant party closely linked to the presidency
  • Nomination and patronage controlled from the center
  • Appeal to the Revolution as a source of legitimacy

Who experienced change most directly: Organized workers, peasants, and officers, whose roles shifted with each reorganization. Greater continuity: Regional politicians and local officials who continued to depend on party nominations. Why the pattern matters: Each stage built on the preceding one, so the PRI cannot be understood as a sudden creation or as the inevitable product of Cardenismo.

Section 74.20

The PRI System: Revolutionary Legitimacy, Social Reform, Political Control, and Historical Legacy

Return to the evening of March 18, 1938. The institutions that mobilized support for the expropriation, notably the CTM, the oil workers' union, the peasant leagues that became the CNC, and the party that incorporated them, did not disappear with the oil dispute. By 1946 they were components of a centralized governing-party system. The mobilization of 1938 was real, voluntary in much of its enthusiasm, and linked to concrete gains. It was also organized through channels that governments could later direct. The question is not whether the PRI system grew from the Revolution's popular energies, but how those energies were institutionalized.

By the late 1940s the system rested on several linked elements. The presidency held extraordinary power, including informal authority over party nominations, the choice of governors and legislators, and the designation of a successor, powers that the legal scholar Jorge Carpizo later called metaconstitutional. The party, through its sectors, distributed patronage: jobs, credit, land, union posts, and public works. Unions and peasant confederations delivered votes and discipline in return for benefits and recognition. Governors and municipal officials depended on the center for resources and careers. Electoral authorities were controlled by the government, and the army, withdrawn from the party, was kept loyal through professional and material inducements.

The system offered representation and also restricted competition. Organized groups obtained real material benefits, including labor rights, social insurance, land, and public services, and the Revolution's language of nationalism and social justice conferred legitimacy. Opposition parties such as the PAN could exist but faced electoral rules, resource disadvantages, and manipulation that made victory difficult; independent unions and peasant groups were pressured, co-opted, or repressed; and the 1941 crime of social dissolution gave authorities a legal tool against dissent. Historians therefore stress the combination of reform and control: the system delivered enough to sustain loyalty while limiting the means by which loyalty could be withdrawn.

The achievements and contradictions were intertwined. Revolutionary nationalism proved durable, and the symbols of land reform and oil nationalization remained powerful in Mexican politics for generations. Yet agrarian and labor promises were incompletely realized: millions remained landless or poor, and union leadership increasingly served as an instrument of control, a pattern known as charrismo that became explicit after the government's intervention in the railway workers' union in 1948. The institutions created by 1946 also evolved. Later decades brought economic crises, challenges from independent movements, electoral reforms, and eventually the loss of the presidency by the PRI in 2000. Students should therefore distinguish what existed by 1946 from what developed after, and should resist reading either the 1938 mobilization or the later decline back into the founding moment.

A final point concerns historical method. The PRI system was long described in Mexico and abroad either as the heir of the Revolution or as an authoritarian machine, and both descriptions contain evidence. Scholars have emphasized electoral manipulation, corporatist control, and clientelism, while others have stressed responsiveness to organized demands, social spending, and regional variation in how the system worked. The reasonable historical position is that the same institutions performed both functions, in proportions that changed over time and place, and that careful local study is required to see how.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the revolutionary promises of land, labor rights, national economic sovereignty, and political institutionalization were implemented, transformed, and incorporated into a durable governing system between 1934 and 1946. Its opening scene, the announcement of the petroleum expropriation on March 18, 1938, framed three themes: economic nationalism, mass mobilization, and the growing authority of the revolutionary state.

Lázaro Cárdenas entered office as the official party's candidate, selected under the Maximato and committed to the Plan Sexenal. The Plan was a statement of intention, and what happened after 1934 depended on political struggle. Between 1935 and 1936 Cárdenas mobilized labor, peasants, and officials to defeat Calles, who left Mexico on April 10, 1936. The break ended the Jefe Máximo's personal authority but strengthened the presidency itself, so presidential centralization was reinforced rather than abolished.

Cárdenas also reorganized relations with governors, military commanders, and mass organizations through tours, petitions, appointments, and arming of peasant reserves. Resistance from landowners, employers, the Church, sinarquistas, and some officers, including the Cedillo revolt of 1938, was real. Distinguishing popular participation from political incorporation proved essential, because both operated simultaneously. Socialist education, enacted in December 1934, was a specific constitutional and pedagogical project that expanded rural schooling and generated violent opposition, while also carrying national cultural models into Indigenous communities.

Agrarian reform was the largest social program. Using Article 27 and the 1934 Agrarian Code, which opened hacienda resident workers to petitioning, the government distributed an estimated 18 million hectares or more to several hundred thousand beneficiaries, with differences among sources arising from counting methods. La Laguna in 1936 applied collective ejidos to a commercial cotton economy, while Yucatán, Sonora, Mexicali, Morelos, and Michoacán showed regional variation. The reform broke the power of many hacendados but left inequalities of land quality, credit, gender, and technical support. Not all ejidos were collective, and women had little access to titles.

Labor policy supported collective bargaining and union consolidation in the CTM, founded in February 1936. Unions gained rights and bargaining power, and government supervision and political alignment grew. The Expropriation Law of November 1936 provided the legal tool for the railroad expropriation of June 23, 1937, followed by worker administration from 1938, and for the oil expropriation. The oil dispute began with a union strike in May 1937, passed through arbitration and the Supreme Court, and became a confrontation over sovereignty when the companies refused to comply. The March 18 decree expropriated company assets; national ownership of the subsoil had been established in 1917, and Pemex was created separately on June 7, 1938.

The expropriation drew broad domestic support and provoked boycotts, diplomatic conflict, and a prolonged settlement with the United States and Britain. Historians interpret it as a labor settlement, an assertion of sovereignty, and an instrument of state-building, and these explanations overlap. Operating the industry challenged a government short of skilled personnel, markets, and capital.

Cardenista social and foreign policy included education, public health, the Instituto Politécnico Nacional, Indigenous programs, and an unsuccessful women's suffrage initiative in 1937, with national suffrage coming only in 1953. Mexico supported the Spanish Republic, received exiles, and admitted Trotsky, combining nonintervention, antifascism, and pragmatic relations with Washington.

The creation of the PRM on March 30, 1938, and the CNC in August organized workers, peasants, popular groups, and the military into sectors. The PRM expanded participation and gave organized groups bargaining power, while subordinating electoral activity to central direction. Whether Cardenismo created independent organizations or incorporated them has no single answer; both occurred.

After 1940 the political system became more moderate. The disputed election of 1940, marked by violence and fraud allegations, brought Manuel Ávila Camacho to power, and wartime national unity, the Bracero Program, labor no-strike pledges, and early industrial growth shifted priorities. CNOP was founded in 1943, the military sector left the formal party structure, and in January 1946 the PRM became the PRI, with documentary collections giving different dates for the founding acts. A new electoral law centralized oversight, and Miguel Alemán took office as a civilian president on December 1, 1946.

The resulting PRI system combined reform, representation, patronage, electoral management, and exclusion. It was neither the inevitable result of Cardenismo nor a simple fulfillment of revolutionary promises. Land reform, labor incorporation, public education, national ownership of strategic industries, and mass mobilization gave the revolutionary state legitimacy, and the same institutions gave governments means to manage organized interests and constrain independent competition. Later developments, including charrismo, economic crises, and electoral reform, belong to subsequent chapters and decades.

MGU Library Research Project

A Regional Study of Cardenista Reform: Comparing Documents with Outcomes

This project asks you to study one region and test official statements of reform against evidence of outcomes.

  1. Select a region. Choose La Laguna, Yucatán, the Yaqui valley, Mexicali, Morelos, or Michoacán.
  2. Collect documents. Identify the relevant agrarian resolutions, decrees, and official accounts, noting dates and editions.
  3. Collect outcomes. Using scholarly studies, censuses, and oral histories, summarize production, credit, labor, gender, and Indigenous experiences.
  4. Compare. Identify where decrees and outcomes diverged, and why.
  5. Write. Produce a 4–6 page essay and a text-based map of the region, stating what can be established and what cannot.

Library resources to use: Archivo General de la Nación finding aids, INEHRM digital collections, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on twentieth-century Mexico.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the Mexican Revolution did not end with the Constitution of 1917 or the wars of the 1910s. It continued through policies that moved land, protected labor, built schools, and placed strategic industries under national control. The Cárdenas years gave concrete meaning to promises that had long been rhetoric, and they changed the lives of hundreds of thousands of families, workers, and teachers. Their significance cannot be reduced to the policies of one president, since peasants, workers, women, teachers, and Indigenous communities pressed claims that shaped what government could and would do.

The chapter also shows how reform and centralization grew together. The organizations that carried reform into the countryside and factory became pillars of a governing party that assigned benefits, mobilized voters, and limited competition. This is the paradox the chapter has traced: the same institutions gave legitimacy to the revolutionary state and tools of control to its successors. It would be a mistake to read the PRI system back into 1938 as an inevitable outcome, just as it would be a mistake to read Cardenismo as a complete fulfillment of the Revolution.

The Mexican case also speaks beyond Mexico. Land reform, labor incorporation, resource nationalism, and the party-state appear in other Latin American countries, and Chapter 75 turns to the Great Depression and import-substitution industrialization to place Mexico within the region's broader developmental turn. The oil expropriation became a model and warning in later debates over resource sovereignty, a theme that returns in Bolivia, Venezuela, and elsewhere in Volume V.

Students should carry forward the habit of reading institutions both ways: as achievements won by organized people and as structures that shape what those people can do next.
