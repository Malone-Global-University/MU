Disease, Demographic Catastrophe, and the Columbian Exchange

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: An Ocean That Had Kept Worlds Apart

For millennia the Atlantic had separated the plants, animals, microbes, and human populations of the Americas from those of Europe, Africa, and Asia. After 1492, that separation ended, and within a few generations the biological makeup of the Americas, and to a lesser extent of the rest of the world, had been transformed. The transformation was reciprocal, but it was not equal: its costs fell most heavily on Indigenous peoples.

In the early 1500s, Spanish officials on Hispaniola carried out counts of the Indigenous people assigned to Spanish settlers in grants of labor. A distribution of 1514 is generally cited as having recorded a few tens of thousands of Indigenous people on an island that, two decades earlier, had supported a dense population of Taíno communities. The precise number of people who lived on Hispaniola in 1492 remains disputed, and the dispute is itself part of this chapter's subject. What is not disputed is that the Indigenous population of the island collapsed to a small fraction of its earlier size within a single generation, and that similar catastrophes, differing in timing, scale, and cause, struck many other parts of the Americas in the following century.

A common shorthand holds that Europeans brought diseases and Indigenous peoples died. The shorthand contains a great deal of truth and a serious distortion. Epidemic disease was a major agent of mortality, and its effects were magnified by the long isolation of the American disease environment from that of the Old World. But the evidence shows that disease worked together with warfare, enslavement, forced labor, tribute demands, hunger, displacement, the breakup of families and communities, and declining birth rates. Treating the catastrophe as purely biological obscures human decisions and institutions, and it risks making the conquest look inevitable. This chapter's central argument is that Atlantic contact produced an enormous biological and ecological transformation, but that Indigenous demographic collapse cannot be explained by pathogens alone.

The same contact moved plants, animals, and foods in both directions. Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats, along with wheat, sugarcane, citrus, grapes, and bananas, crossed the Atlantic westward. Maize, potatoes, cassava, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, chili peppers, peanuts, beans, and cacao crossed eastward, and in time spread to Africa and Asia. Scholars call this set of transfers the Columbian Exchange. Its effects reshaped diets, agriculture, and landscapes on four continents, and Latin America was one of the places where they were most dramatic. It also shaped the political and economic conditions of colonial rule, which is why this chapter closes Part VI.

The chapter also takes up a question that every student of this subject confronts: how do historians know how many people died? Pre-contact population totals and regional mortality figures are reconstructed from incomplete and indirect evidence, including tribute records, archaeological surveys, early counts of Indigenous households, and later censuses. Estimates vary enormously, and the methods used to produce them have themselves changed over time. Section 29.6 and the Historical Debate feature treat this as an evidence problem rather than offering a single number.

A final theme concerns survival. Indigenous communities across the Americas endured losses that would have destroyed many societies, and they responded by reorganizing households and villages, migrating, adopting and adapting new animals and crops, preserving knowledge, and negotiating with colonial authorities. Their survival, and the reshaping of their worlds under transformed conditions, is part of the demographic story. Disease altered the balance of power between Indigenous peoples and Europeans, but it did not make conquest automatic, a point taken up in Part VII.

Chapter 29 follows Chapter 28's account of treaties and competing claims and closes Part VI. It introduces epidemic networks and population collapse but does not narrate the siege of Tenochtitlan, where smallpox is examined in Chapter 32, and it does not treat the mature plantation economy, reserved for later chapters. The evidence it draws on is unusually varied: Spanish and Portuguese administrative records, Indigenous-authored and Indigenous-informed texts, archaeology, palaeoecology, and, increasingly, ancient DNA.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Columbian Exchange and explain why the Atlantic had previously separated many biological worlds.
  2. Explain why epidemic disease had unequal effects, using concepts of prior exposure, population-level immunity, and disease environment rather than racial explanations.
  3. Trace the first demographic catastrophe in Hispaniola and the wider Caribbean, distinguishing the causes of mortality.
  4. Analyze why disease alone does not explain Indigenous population collapse, identifying the roles of violence, coerced labor, hunger, displacement, and fertility decline.
  5. Describe the spread of smallpox and other epidemics through colonial and Indigenous networks, emphasizing repeated waves.
  6. Evaluate competing estimates of pre-contact populations and mortality, and explain the evidence and methods behind them.
  7. Assess the effects of introduced livestock on Indigenous and colonial landscapes, economies, and warfare.
  8. Compare the transfer of Old World plants to the Americas with the transfer of American foods to Africa, Europe, and Asia.
  9. Explain how the Columbian Exchange transformed ecosystems, not only diets.
  10. Distinguish well-supported conclusions from contested ones, including the evolving debate over the origins of syphilis and the concept of the "virgin soil" epidemic.
  11. Interpret an Indigenous-informed description of epidemic illness as historical evidence, noting its authorship and limits.
  12. Connect demographic catastrophe and Indigenous survival to the conditions of conquest and colonial rule examined in Part VII and beyond.

  2. Why did epidemics strike Indigenous populations so severely, and why is a racial explanation historically mistaken?
  3. How can historians separate deaths caused by specific diseases from deaths caused by violence, forced labor, and social collapse?
  4. Why does the shorthand "disease killed most Indigenous people" distort the historical record?

  7. Why were the effects of disease so asymmetric while the movement of crops was largely reciprocal?

Historical Context

Volume I showed that the Americas were not an empty or static continent in 1492. They contained dense agricultural populations, cities, and complex polities in Mesoamerica and the Andes, chiefdoms in the Caribbean and northern South America, and many autonomous farming, fishing, and foraging societies elsewhere. Their agriculture rested on maize, beans, squash, potatoes, manioc, and many other domesticated plants. They possessed some domesticated animals, including dogs, turkeys, guinea pigs, llamas, and alpacas, but no large herd animals comparable to the cattle, sheep, horses, and pigs of Eurasia.

This difference mattered for disease. Agriculture, large and interconnected human populations, long-distance exchange, and repeated contact with animal reservoirs helped create conditions in Afro-Eurasia in which many epidemic diseases could emerge, persist, and circulate. Some had zoonotic histories, but the origins of individual pathogens were complex. Eurasia and Africa had been linked by trade and travel for thousands of years, and their populations had repeatedly encountered and adapted to these diseases. The Americas had their own infections, among them forms of tuberculosis and treponemal disease and intestinal and parasitic infections, but evidence suggests that many of the major epidemic diseases of the Old World were absent before 1492.

Chapters 27 and 28 described the first European settlements and the legal framework of Iberian claims. Spanish settlers on Hispaniola and nearby islands established systems of tribute and coerced labor, soon formalized as encomienda and examined in later chapters. They also brought livestock, seeds, and cuttings, since settlers sought familiar foods and materials. Portuguese traders on the Brazilian coast likewise carried European goods and, unintentionally, pathogens. Enslaved Africans, whose forced transport to the Americas began in this same period, brought still other disease exposures, a subject developed in Chapter 44.

The evidence for this chapter requires care. Written sources were produced by Europeans and by Indigenous people educated or interviewed by Europeans. They describe epidemics in vivid but imprecise language, often without distinguishing diseases by modern criteria. A description of a disease with skin eruptions may be smallpox, measles, or another illness. Historians combine texts with archaeology, palaeopathology (the study of disease in skeletal remains), palaeoecology (the study of past environments through pollen and sediment), and, increasingly, genetic analysis of ancient remains. Each approach has limits, and the chapter notes them where they matter.

Section 29.1

Biological Worlds before Sustained Atlantic Contact

When Europeans arrived in the Caribbean, they did not enter a biologically empty land. They entered environments shaped by thousands of years of Indigenous cultivation, burning, hunting, and settlement, and populated by plants and animals that had evolved largely apart from those of the Old World. The Atlantic had been crossed only rarely and in limited ways before 1492, and there was no sustained biological traffic between the hemispheres. One consequence was that each side possessed crops, animals, and pathogens unknown to the other.

Students should avoid two opposite errors. One is to imagine the Americas as biologically static before 1492. Indigenous peoples had domesticated dozens of plants, bred varieties adapted to very different environments, managed forests and grasslands with fire and cultivation, and moved crops across large distances within the hemisphere, as the Volume I chapters on maize, the Andes, and Amazonia showed. The other error is to imagine that the Old World was a single, uniform disease environment. Africa, Europe, and Asia differed widely, and their disease histories and immunities varied accordingly.

The regions that would become Latin America illustrate the variety of starting conditions. Dense populations in the Basin of Mexico, the Andean highlands, and the Caribbean islands were especially exposed to epidemic diseases, which spread most readily where people live in large numbers and travel frequently. More dispersed populations, for instance in parts of Amazonia, the Gran Chaco, and the southern cone, were sometimes affected later or differently, though not spared. Portuguese contact with coastal Brazil, which began in 1500 as Chapter 28 described, brought the same exchange of organisms to a different set of environments.

Historical Vocabulary
Columbian Exchange

The Columbian Exchange is the term, popularized by the historian Alfred Crosby in 1972, for the transfer of plants, animals, microbes, foods, and people between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492. It includes both deliberate transfers, such as planting wheat or bringing cattle, and accidental ones, such as the movement of pathogens, weeds, and rats.

The term is useful but can mislead. "Exchange" suggests balance, yet the consequences were unequal, with the heaviest mortality falling on Indigenous populations. The word "Columbian" focuses attention on Columbus, although the processes continued for centuries and involved many actors, including Portuguese, Africans, and Indigenous peoples who adopted and carried new species. The term also does not specify who controlled or benefited from the transfers, which this chapter examines.

Section 29.2

Why Epidemic Disease Had Such Unequal Effects

Explaining why Indigenous populations suffered so severely requires care with language. The explanation is historical and biological, not racial. No population is inherently "immune" or "susceptible." Immunity to an infectious disease depends on an individual's prior exposure, the circulation of the pathogen within a population, and a range of factors including nutrition, age, stress, and access to care. At the level of a population, long experience with a disease tends to mean that most adults have survived it in childhood and carry some protection, and that the disease persists at low levels (becoming endemic), striking mainly the young.

When a disease is introduced into a population with no previous exposure, all age groups may be vulnerable at once. A large proportion of people can fall ill simultaneously, and social functions collapse: there are too few healthy people to nurse the sick, gather food, tend fields, or carry water. Mortality then rises not only from the disease itself but from neglect, hunger, and dehydration. This interaction between infection and social disruption helps explain why the same pathogen could be far deadlier in a newly exposed population than in one that had known it for generations.

Other conditions affected mortality. Dense settlements, frequent travel and trade, and the movement of people as laborers and soldiers helped pathogens spread. Colonial labor drafts and relocations carried infections across regions. Malnutrition and overwork, common under coerced labor, weakened resistance. Stress, disruption, and the loss of caregivers affected outcomes. European and African populations also suffered enormously from diseases, including new ones they encountered in the Americas, especially tropical infections, and many European settlers died in the early colonies. The asymmetry lay in the combination of newly introduced diseases, colonial coercion, and the political conditions that followed.

Historical Vocabulary
Virgin Soil Epidemic

A virgin soil epidemic is one that strikes a population with no prior exposure to the pathogen involved. The concept, prominent in the work of Crosby and others in the 1970s, helped explain why Old World diseases were so destructive in the Americas.

Historians have since criticized the term when it is used as an all-purpose explanation. Critics point out that it can imply that biology alone determined outcomes, understating the role of warfare, enslavement, famine, and colonial policy. Some also note that the language of "virgin soil" can suggest passive victims. This textbook uses the concept to describe lack of prior exposure but treats it as one factor among several, not as a sufficient explanation for demographic collapse.

Section 29.3

Hispaniola and the First Demographic Catastrophe

Chapter 27 described Columbus's voyages, the first settlements, and the beginnings of coerced labor on Hispaniola. This section follows what happened to the island's Indigenous population. The Taíno communities of Hispaniola, organized in chiefdoms (cacicazgos) and sustained by cultivation of manioc and other crops supplemented by fishing and hunting, faced from the 1490s the demands of a colonial regime: tribute, labor, and the seizure of land and food.

The population of the island in 1492 is unknown, and contemporary estimates range very widely, from well under a million to several million. Spanish writers of the time gave figures, some of them large, but these were impressionistic and often made for argument, including those of Bartolomé de las Casas, who wished to condemn Spanish treatment of Indigenous people. Modern estimates, based on archaeology, agricultural capacity, and the early counts, differ by an order of magnitude. A distribution of Indigenous people among Spanish holders in 1514, however, recorded only a few tens of thousands of people in the grants, and subsequent records show further drastic decline. By the middle of the sixteenth century, few Indigenous people remained on the island as recognized communities, though historians continue to study descendants and survivors and to debate how completely Taíno identity and lineage disappeared.

What caused this collapse? Historians emphasize several factors operating together. Violence accompanied conquest and the suppression of resistance. Coerced labor in mining and farming, and tribute demands, disrupted subsistence. Relocation and the breakup of communities undermined food production. Hunger and malnutrition increased vulnerability. Enslavement and the movement of people between islands spread illness. Epidemic disease also played a role: sources record illness on Hispaniola from the 1490s, although whether a specific epidemic, for example of influenza carried by pigs as some have proposed, struck the island in 1493 is debated. The first epidemic that historians can identify as smallpox with reasonable confidence began in 1518.

The Caribbean pattern extended beyond Hispaniola. Slave raids, most intensive between about 1509 and 1515 and continuing afterward, removed most of the Lucayan population of the Bahamas, carrying captives to Hispaniola and elsewhere; the archipelago was effectively depopulated by the 1520s. Spanish expansion into Puerto Rico and Cuba involved similar combinations of violence, coerced labor, and disease. The demand for labor to replace the declining Indigenous workforce was one reason enslaved Africans began to be brought to the Caribbean in increasing numbers, a development treated in Chapter 44. The Caribbean thus became a laboratory, in a grim sense, for patterns that later recurred on the mainland.

Section 29.4

Disease Was Not the Only Cause

The shorthand that Europeans arrived and disease killed almost everyone flattens a complex process. Disease was a major cause of death in many regions, but the evidence for the Caribbean and for later mainland regions shows that mortality resulted from several interacting forces. Distinguishing them is difficult, because they reinforced one another, but historians have identified the main mechanisms.

Violence and warfare. Conquest involved direct killing, the destruction of settlements, and the disruption of political systems. Wars of conquest and resistance caused deaths among combatants and civilians, and punitive expeditions and massacres occurred in many regions. Enslavement. Indigenous people were enslaved and transported, sometimes over long distances, and the conditions of capture, transport, and labor were often lethal. Coerced labor and tribute. Systems of forced labor in mines, fields, and construction, together with tribute demands in goods and food, imposed heavy burdens, especially where labor drafts removed men from their communities for long periods.

Hunger and disrupted agriculture. When epidemics, labor drafts, or relocation left fields untended, famine followed. Livestock could trample crops. Seizure of land and food for colonial needs reduced supplies. Malnourished populations were more vulnerable to infection. Displacement and social breakdown. Flight, forced resettlement, and the fragmentation of villages scattered families and weakened the networks of reciprocity and care on which survival depended. Declining fertility. Sickness, separation of spouses by labor drafts, stress, and the deaths of women reduced births. A population facing high mortality and low fertility cannot replace itself, and the demographic effect is cumulative across generations.

The relative importance of these factors varied with time and place. In some regions, such as the Caribbean islands, direct coercion and labor extraction were central. In others, epidemic waves struck communities that had not yet experienced sustained conquest. In many cases, all the mechanisms operated together. This matters historically because it shows that demographic collapse was not simply a natural event that happened to Indigenous peoples but was bound up with the institutions, decisions, and violence of colonial rule. It also matters for avoiding a teleological reading of conquest. Disease did not by itself deliver the Americas to Europeans. Political alliances, military decisions, and colonial policies shaped where, when, and how control was established, as Part VII will show.

Section 29.5

Smallpox and the Expansion of Epidemic Networks

Smallpox, a viral disease that causes fever and a characteristic rash and has a high fatality rate, became the best-known epidemic of the early colonial period. The epidemic documented on Hispaniola in 1518–1519 spread to nearby islands and then, in 1520, to the mainland of Mexico. This chapter notes its arrival but leaves its role in the conflict between the Mexica and Spanish forces to Chapter 32. The important general point for this chapter is that Atlantic contact created networks of movement through which pathogens spread, and that those networks expanded as colonies grew.

Epidemic disease in the sixteenth century was not a single event but a series of waves. Smallpox returned repeatedly. Measles, recorded in the 1530s and later, and influenza, typhus, and other infections followed. In central Mexico, severe epidemics known by the Nahuatl-derived term cocoliztli struck in 1545–1548 and 1576–1578, killing very large numbers. The cause of cocoliztli has been debated; ancient DNA from individuals buried at the epidemic cemetery of Teposcolula-Yucundaa identified Salmonella enterica Paratyphi C, making enteric fever a strong candidate there, but this does not establish a single cause for the entire cocoliztli epidemic. Each wave hit populations already reduced by earlier epidemics and stressed by colonial demands, and repeated waves, especially those that killed young people of childbearing age, had long-lasting effects on the capacity of communities to recover.

In the Andes, historians have argued that epidemic disease may have moved along Indigenous roads and trade routes before Spanish forces arrived, but the dating, identity of the disease, and scale are debated and belong to the discussion of the Inca civil war in Chapter 35. Later sixteenth-century epidemics in the Andes are better documented. In Bahia, a devastating smallpox epidemic spread in 1562–1563 through coastal communities and Jesuit settlements. It occurred within a colonial economy already heavily dependent on Indigenous labor and enslavement, and its mortality intensified the region's labor crisis, a context examined in later chapters. Epidemic networks thus reached Mexico, the Andes, Brazil, and the Caribbean by different routes and at different times.

One consequence of this geography was that Indigenous peoples often experienced epidemics before, or independently of, sustained contact with Europeans. A village might be struck by an illness carried by trade or travel from a colonized area, without a Spaniard or Portuguese having visited. This complicates the picture of contact as a single moment and is one reason why historians treat conquest and colonization as prolonged processes, as the textbook's framework stresses.

Section 29.6

How Many People Died? Reconstructing Population Collapse

No census exists for the Americas in 1492. Estimating the pre-contact population and the extent of decline therefore requires indirect methods, each with strengths and limits. Historians use early colonial counts and tribute lists, which record the number of households or tributaries in particular areas but were made for taxation and often undercount or exclude groups. They use archaeological surveys, which document the number and size of settlements but cannot easily translate them into populations. They use estimates of the carrying capacity of agricultural systems, and ecological and palaeoenvironmental evidence, such as pollen and charcoal records indicating changes in land use.

Researchers also use backward projection, taking a later, better-documented population and extrapolating backward using an assumed rate of decline. This method is sensitive to the assumed rate, and critics argue that it can produce inflated figures. The estimates produced by these methods vary enormously. Figures proposed for the whole hemisphere in 1492 have ranged from under ten million in some early twentieth-century studies to more than a hundred million in some later ones, with many scholars placing the total in the tens of millions. Estimates for particular regions, such as central Mexico and the central Andes, have likewise ranged by factors of several. Most scholars agree that declines were extremely large in many regions, often a majority of the population within a century, but they disagree about the scale, the timing, and the regional variation.

Because of these uncertainties, this textbook does not offer a single hemisphere-wide figure as established fact. Students should treat estimates as claims with stated assumptions and ranges. They should also attend to regional variation: declines were steep in the Caribbean and in densely settled Mexico and Andes, varied elsewhere, and were differently timed in Brazil and in more remote regions. Some populations recovered partially by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, sometimes through demographic mixing, as the later chapters on colonial society show.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Large Was the Pre-Contact Population, and How Much of It Was Lost?

The question: How many people lived in the Americas, or in particular regions, in 1492, and what proportion died in the following century?

Interpretation A — High estimates, very large declines: Researchers such as Sherburne Cook and Woodrow Borah, working on central Mexico, and later Henry Dobyns for the hemisphere, argued that pre-contact populations were very large and that declines of ninety percent or more occurred in many regions. They drew on tribute records, projections of decline rates, and the evidence of dense settlement. Some recent work using archaeological and ecological data has also proposed large totals.
Interpretation B — Lower estimates, more cautious reconstruction: Other scholars, such as Angel Rosenblat, argued that the high figures rest on questionable assumptions, including uniform rates of decline and the extrapolation of limited data. They propose lower totals and emphasize that declines, while catastrophic, varied by region and period.
Intermediate syntheses: William Denevan's revised estimate of roughly 54 million for the hemisphere in 1492 is an influential middle-range synthesis, sitting between very low estimates and Dobyns's much higher totals. Many later scholars work within a broad middle range while emphasizing regional variation and uncertainty.

Evidence: Both sides use the same kinds of evidence: tribute and tax records, early Spanish counts, archaeological surveys, agricultural capacity, and demographic modeling. They differ in how they weigh and interpret it. Recent work has added palaeoecological and genetic evidence, which is promising but has not resolved the disagreement.

Why it matters: The numbers shape how historians understand the scale of the catastrophe, the relative weight of epidemic disease and colonial violence, and the political and moral character of conquest. They also bear on debates about the extent to which the pre-contact Americas were densely settled and environmentally transformed. The disagreement is not between those who deny and those who recognize the catastrophe: nearly all scholars agree that it was immense. It concerns magnitude and mechanism.

Student task: Explain why both high and low estimates depend on assumptions, and identify what additional evidence would most help narrow the range for a region of your choice.

Section 29.7

Horses, Cattle, Pigs, Sheep, and New Animal Landscapes

Before 1492, the Americas had no cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, or surviving domesticated horses. Columbus brought the first of these animals to Hispaniola in 1493, and Spanish and Portuguese colonists soon established breeding populations. Livestock provided meat, hides, transport, and traction, and they allowed settlers to reproduce familiar diets and economies. They also transformed the landscapes into which they were introduced.

Horses gave Spanish forces mobility and, in some battles, tactical advantages, though their military importance was often limited by terrain, supply, and the adaptation of Indigenous opponents. Their greater historical effect came later and more widely. In the following centuries, Indigenous peoples of the Plains of North America, the Pampas, and parts of Patagonia adopted horses and transformed their own economies and military capacities, as later chapters on the Mapuche frontier and the Río de la Plata will show. This adoption often occurred a century or more after the first arrival of horses, and it reflected Indigenous choices, not simple diffusion. Cattle multiplied on Hispaniola and then across Mexico, the Andes, the Río de la Plata region, and northeastern Brazil, supplying hides, tallow, and meat, and shaping the ranching economies of later centuries.

Pigs reproduced rapidly and were often allowed to range freely. They became an important source of meat for settlers and, in time, for Indigenous people, but they also invaded and damaged cultivated fields, creating conflict. Sheep and goats spread widely, especially in the highlands. In central Mexico, sheep flocks expanded extremely quickly in regions such as the Mezquital Valley in the sixteenth century, and historians have argued that this expansion transformed land use, contributed to erosion, and altered Indigenous agriculture and livelihoods. Wool also became the basis of textile industries in Mexico and the Andes. In the Andes, sheep coexisted with Indigenous camelids, and Indigenous herders adopted sheep in many places. Chickens, an Old World domestic bird, spread widely after European colonization and were adopted rapidly by Indigenous households, an example of easy and voluntary uptake. (Whether chickens reached South America from Polynesia before 1492 is debated, with published studies reaching opposing conclusions.)

The effects of livestock were neither immediate nor uniform. They depended on local environments, on the ratio of animals to land, on whether animals were fenced or free-ranging, and on the political relations between Indigenous communities and colonial ranchers. Indigenous villages frequently complained in colonial courts about damage to crops from Spanish cattle and pigs, and their litigation is an important source for historians. Livestock also shifted labor: herding and ranching created new occupations, including those filled by Indigenous, African, and mixed-descent workers.

Section 29.8

Wheat, Sugar, and Other Old World Plants in the Americas

Spanish settlers wished to eat familiar foods and to produce goods for trade, and they brought seeds and cuttings from Iberia, the Canary Islands, and Madeira. Wheat, the basis of the European bread diet, was grown in Mexico and the Andes from the early colonial decades, particularly in temperate highland areas, and became a marker of Spanish culture and status. Wheat did not, however, replace maize for most people. Grapes and olives were planted for wine and oil, and their cultivation, especially in Peru, Chile, and later Mexico, produced colonial industries and, at times, royal restrictions intended to protect Iberian exports. Citrus fruits, including oranges and lemons, adapted readily and spread widely, as did bananas and plantains, which were brought to the Caribbean by the early sixteenth century and became staples in many tropical regions. Other introduced plants included onions, garlic, chickpeas, and numerous vegetables and herbs.

Sugarcane deserves particular attention. It had already been established by Portuguese and Spanish planters on Madeira and the Canaries, and Columbus brought it to Hispaniola in 1493. Sugar production on Hispaniola expanded in the early sixteenth century, and the crop was soon introduced to other Caribbean islands, Mexico, and Brazil. This chapter treats sugar as an agricultural and ecological transfer. It required clearing land, often forest, and large quantities of fuelwood, and it was closely tied to labor systems that increasingly relied on enslaved Africans. The mature plantation economy, with its social and racial structures, is the subject of later chapters, especially Chapters 43 and 44.

Not all Old World crops adapted equally. Some failed in tropical lowlands or at high altitude. Indigenous farmers adopted some introduced crops selectively, integrating them into existing systems, for example by planting fruit trees or adding vegetables to gardens. Indigenous women, who did much of the cultivation and food processing, were often central to these decisions. Colonial authorities sometimes required Indigenous communities to grow wheat or other crops for tribute, which could strain local resources. The story of introduced plants is therefore not only one of botanical spread but of negotiation, coercion, and adaptation.

Section 29.9

Maize, Potatoes, Cassava, and American Foods across the Atlantic

The eastward movement of American crops is the clearest reciprocal dimension of the Columbian Exchange. Indigenous farmers had domesticated maize in Mesoamerica, potatoes in the Andes, manioc (cassava) in lowland South America, and many other crops over thousands of years, as Volume I described. After 1492, these crops entered new regions, carried by Iberian and other European ships and traders, and, in time, by Africans and Asians.

Maize reached Spain early in the sixteenth century and spread through Mediterranean Europe, where it was sometimes grown as a garden crop before becoming a field crop in regions such as northern Iberia, Italy, and the Balkans. It also entered West and Central Africa, carried by Portuguese ships and traders, and later became a staple in many African regions. It reached China and other parts of Asia by various routes. Potatoes were brought from the Andes to Spain by the later sixteenth century and spread slowly in Europe, meeting resistance and suspicion before becoming a mainstay in some regions, including Ireland and northern Europe, mostly in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Cassava, a drought-tolerant root crop that Tupi-speaking peoples and others had long cultivated, was taken by the Portuguese to West and Central Africa, where it became a major food source and is still central to diets across the region.

Sweet potatoes reached the Philippines and China in the later sixteenth century, and chili peppers, tomatoes, peanuts, beans, squash, pineapples, vanilla, and cacao also traveled. Chili peppers transformed cuisines from South and East Asia to Africa. Tomatoes were slow to become staples in parts of Europe. Cacao, prepared by Indigenous Mesoamericans as a drink, became fashionable in Spain and later across Europe, and its cultivation, like that of sugar, came to depend on coerced labor, a subject for later chapters. Tobacco, though not a food, spread rapidly and became an important commodity.

Claims about these crops should be measured. Historians caution against asserting that American crops immediately revolutionized world population. The adoption of each crop occurred over decades or centuries, varied by region, and depended on environmental suitability, cultural preference, and economic and political conditions. Some scholars argue that maize, potatoes, and sweet potatoes contributed to population growth in Europe, Africa, and China in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, but the magnitude and mechanisms are debated. What is clear is that Indigenous American agricultural knowledge, built over millennia, became a foundation of food systems across the world, a fact that contrasts sharply with the devastation that contact brought to the people who had developed those crops.

Section 29.10

Ecological Transformation: Fields, Forests, Pastures, and Unintended Species

The Columbian Exchange changed ecosystems, not just diets. Introduced livestock grazed and trampled vegetation, altered the composition of grasslands, and in some places contributed to soil erosion. Feral animals, including cattle, pigs, and horses, established self-sustaining populations in parts of the Caribbean, Mexico, and the southern Americas, changing local fauna and flora. Sheep flocks, as in central Mexico, transformed land use and, in some accounts, aggravated erosion, although the extent of erosion and its causes remain subjects of research.

Many organisms crossed the Atlantic accidentally. Old World weeds arrived in seed stocks, in the dirt of ship ballast, and on the coats of animals, and some spread widely, colonizing disturbed ground. Rats and other pests traveled on ships and settled in ports and towns. Plantains, sugarcane, and other crops escaped cultivation in places. Earthworms and other soil organisms may also have had effects that are only beginning to be understood.

Human depopulation had ecological consequences as well. Where Indigenous populations declined sharply, cultivated fields, terraces, and managed forests were abandoned or neglected. In the Andes, agricultural terraces, which required collective maintenance, fell into disuse in some areas, and historians debate how far this reflected population loss, labor drafts, or changes in land tenure. Some researchers argue that forest regrowth on abandoned farmland in parts of the Americas may have absorbed enough carbon to affect atmospheric carbon dioxide and contribute to cooling in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, an idea that remains contested. Other researchers emphasize how much Indigenous land management had shaped Amazonian and other forests, so that changes after 1492 were also changes in human-managed landscapes.

The mining and sugar economies examined later added their own ecological pressures: deforestation for fuel, mercury contamination, and soil exhaustion. The ecological history of colonial Latin America is therefore inseparable from its demographic, economic, and political history. It also reminds students that environmental change was not external to human events but bound up with decisions about land, labor, and trade.

Section 29.11

An Unequal but Reciprocal Biological Exchange

Why were the consequences of disease so asymmetric, while the movement of crops and foods ran strongly in both directions? Part of the answer lies in the different disease histories described in Section 29.2. Eurasia and Africa had a long history of shared epidemic diseases, and the Americas lacked several of the major ones. Part lies in the political context: the diseases were introduced in circumstances of conquest, enslavement, and labor extraction, which magnified their effects. And part lies in the nature of crops, which can be deliberately adopted by any farming society when they prove useful, whereas pathogens spread regardless of human intention.

Some diseases moved from the Americas or were possibly transmitted in both directions. The most debated case is syphilis. A serious outbreak of a new-seeming venereal disease appeared in Europe in the 1490s, notably in Naples in 1495, and some contemporaries and later scholars linked it to Columbus's voyages. Others argue that treponemal diseases, the family of infections that includes syphilis, existed in the Old World before 1492 and that the 1490s outbreak reflected a new or more virulent form, or a change in diagnosis. Recent ancient-genomic evidence, including pre-contact American Treponema pallidum genomes, has shifted the debate substantially toward an American origin for the T. pallidum complex. The exact emergence of venereal syphilis itself remains less certain, including whether its modern form differentiated in the Americas or shortly after an ancestral strain crossed the Atlantic. Students should not treat syphilis as a simple counterpart to American mortality from Old World diseases: even given American origins for the pathogen lineage, its effects were far less catastrophic for Europeans than smallpox and other epidemics were for Indigenous peoples.

Other diseases moved into the Americas from Africa. Malaria and yellow fever, which are associated with African and Old World origins, became important in the tropical Caribbean and coastal lowlands, most clearly in the seventeenth century and later. They shaped colonization and warfare in those regions, and they affected Europeans as well as Indigenous and African populations, although with differences in prior exposure that later colonial societies sometimes interpreted in racial terms. Students should be alert to the later history of such claims, which were often ideological rather than biological.

The eastward movement of American crops had large effects on African, European, and Asian societies. It also intersected with the Atlantic slave trade: cassava and maize helped provision slave ships and African communities affected by the trade, and American crops and African labor combined in plantation systems. The Columbian Exchange is therefore best understood as a web of interconnected transfers, in which biology, economics, and power were intertwined, and in which the same processes could both enrich some societies and devastate others.

Section 29.12

Demographic Catastrophe, Survival, and a Transformed Atlantic World

The losses described in this chapter were catastrophic. In many regions a majority of the population died within a century, and the social consequences, including the loss of elders and specialists, the breakup of communities, and the disruption of ritual and political life, were profound. Yet Indigenous peoples did not disappear from the history of Latin America. In Mesoamerica and the Andes, substantial Indigenous populations survived and, by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, began to recover. In Brazil, many Indigenous communities persisted in the interior and, in transformed ways, along the coast. In the Caribbean, where the loss was most severe, scholars continue to examine the survival of Indigenous ancestry, knowledge, and cultural practices in later populations.

Survivors reorganized. They consolidated villages, moved to new locations, reconfigured kin groups, and adapted households to new demographic conditions. They adopted chickens, pigs, sheep, and new crops when it suited them, and they used colonial institutions, including courts and municipal councils, to defend land and labor, a pattern examined in Chapters 33 and 40. They preserved languages, religious practices, and historical memory, sometimes in Christian forms. Indigenous women played central roles as cultivators, food processors, healers, midwives, and keepers of household and community knowledge. Leaders negotiated with colonial officials and with one another. Such responses show Indigenous agency under conditions not of their choosing.

The demographic catastrophe also shaped the colonial order. Labor shortages encouraged the expansion of African slavery, the use of draft labor systems, and the incorporation of mixed-descent populations into colonial economies. Land left vacant by depopulation became available to Spanish and Portuguese settlers, contributing to the growth of estates. The reduction and concentration of Indigenous communities facilitated tribute and religious instruction, and they reshaped the economic geography of colonial rule, as Chapters 42 and 43 will show. In these ways, disease and demographic change altered the balance of power, though they did not make colonial domination automatic.

This last point connects to the next part of the volume. Part VII examines the conquest of Mexico and Central America as a multinational conflict shaped by Indigenous alliances, rivalries, diplomacy, disease, and Spanish intervention. Students should carry forward from this chapter an understanding that epidemic disease was one important element in a larger story, and that Indigenous decisions and political relationships, not only biological vulnerability, determined how conquest unfolded.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: The Florentine Codex on the Smallpox Epidemic

Creators: Bernardino de Sahagún, Franciscan friar, with Nahua scholars and informants · Date: Compiled in central Mexico over several decades in the mid-to-late sixteenth century · Type: Bilingual Nahuatl and Spanish illustrated encyclopedic manuscript, here the section concerning the conquest

Context: The Florentine Codex was produced in New Spain through a collaboration between Sahagún and Nahua scholars trained at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco and Nahua elders who served as informants. The section on the conquest, compiled decades after the events, includes a description of the smallpox epidemic that struck Tenochtitlan in 1520. The manuscript is now held in Florence, hence its name.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): The Nahua account describes a sickness that spread across the city, marked by widespread pustules on the body. It reports that many people were unable to move or to care for others, that people lay helpless, that hunger followed because food could not be gathered or prepared, and that many died, including notable leaders. It also describes survivors left scarred.

Analysis: The passage is valuable because it gives an Indigenous-informed description of the social effects of epidemic illness, including the collapse of caregiving and the onset of hunger, which matches the mechanisms described in Section 29.2. It also illustrates how disease and social disruption interacted. However, it was composed decades after the events, in a colonial setting, with a Franciscan friar directing the project and Nahua elders remembering a traumatic period; it may reflect later memory, the interests of those telling it, and the editorial shaping of the compilers. It describes Tenochtitlan in particular and cannot be generalized to all regions. The Spanish text calls the illness smallpox (viruelas), and modern scholarship overwhelmingly agrees; the passage does not address how Indigenous communities outside the capital experienced the epidemic.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the passage say about the immediate effects of the epidemic on daily life?
  2. Who produced the Florentine Codex, and how might its authorship shape its content?
  3. What can historians learn from it about how disease and hunger interacted?
  4. What can historians not establish from this passage alone?
  5. How does the fact that it was composed decades later affect its use as evidence?
Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Living through an Epidemic

These are reconstructed analytical perspectives, not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not hold equal power.

Indigenous villagers: Epidemics struck without precedent, killing caregivers and elders, disrupting planting and harvest, and leaving survivors with grief and with the task of reorganizing households, leadership, and ritual. Explanations often drew on religious and cosmological frameworks.
Spanish colonists and officials: Many saw epidemics through providential or practical lenses: as divine judgment, as a threat to the labor supply and tribute, or as an opportunity to acquire land. Some friars cared for the sick and later founded hospitals, while officials worried about declining revenues.
Enslaved Africans and free people of African descent: Transported in crowded ships and worked in harsh conditions, they faced high mortality from the same and other diseases, and were brought in increasing numbers to replace labor lost as Indigenous populations declined.
Portuguese colonists and Jesuits in Brazil: Epidemics on the coast weakened Indigenous communities, deepened labor shortages, and prompted missionary efforts that concentrated Indigenous people in villages, often increasing the spread of disease.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Catastrophe and Indigenous Survival

What changed?

  • Indigenous populations fell sharply in many regions, and communities were restructured.
  • Animals, crops, and organisms from the Old World altered landscapes and economies.
  • American crops entered the food systems of Europe, Africa, and Asia.

What remained continuous?

  • Indigenous languages, agricultural knowledge, and community institutions persisted in transformed forms.
  • Indigenous farmers continued to grow maize, beans, squash, potatoes, and manioc as staples.
  • Indigenous leaders and communities continued to negotiate with colonial authorities.

Who experienced change most directly? Indigenous communities in the Caribbean, Mexico, and the Andes, and coastal Brazil, where losses were steepest. Who experienced greater continuity? Some interior and peripheral peoples, for whom colonial pressure arrived later. Why the pattern matters: It shows that catastrophe and survival coexisted, and that Indigenous history did not end with demographic collapse.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Indigenous Demographic Collapse

Long-term causes

The long isolation of the American disease environment from Old World epidemic diseases, dense settlement in some regions, and the expansion of Iberian empires across the Atlantic.

Short-term causes

Introduction of smallpox and other diseases, combined with conquest, enslavement, tribute, forced labor, relocation, and hunger.

Event or transformation

Repeated epidemic waves and colonial coercion across the sixteenth century.

Immediate effects

High mortality, loss of caregivers and leaders, disrupted agriculture, declining births, fragmented communities.

Long-term consequences

Labor shortages, expansion of African slavery, transfer of land to colonists, reorganized Indigenous communities, and changed ecological landscapes.

Historical complexity

Disease combined with coercion, hunger, and displacement; it was not a single cause, and it did not make conquest inevitable.
Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Healing, and New Foods

In a village struck by epidemic, daily life could come to a halt. Families cared for the sick until they themselves fell ill, fields went unplanted, and the dead sometimes could not be buried according to custom. Women, who were often the principal caregivers, healers, and midwives, bore much of this burden, and their deaths removed knowledge and labor from communities. Orphaned children were absorbed by relatives, taken into Spanish households, or left without support. Clergy and colonial officials in some regions established hospitals for Indigenous people in the 1520s and 1530s; among the best known were the hospital-villages associated with Vasco de Quiroga, a royal judge and later bishop, beginning with Santa Fe de México in 1532, although care was limited and uneven.

New foods and animals entered daily life unevenly. Indigenous households adopted chickens readily, acquired pigs and sheep where they could, and added fruit trees and vegetables to gardens. Spanish households sought wheat bread, wine, and olive oil, often at great expense or through Indigenous tribute. In the Caribbean, cassava bread, a Taíno staple, became part of colonial diets. Across the Atlantic, peasants in parts of Europe and Africa gradually incorporated maize, potatoes, and cassava into their own routines. Evidence for these ordinary changes is thin and scattered, and historians draw on wills, inventories, court cases, and archaeology, noting that most written sources reflect elite and colonial concerns.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the demographic and ecological consequences of Atlantic contact, which closed Part VI by explaining conditions that shaped the beginning of colonial rule. It began with the observation that the Atlantic had long separated the plants, animals, microbes, and human populations of the Americas from those of the rest of the world, and that after 1492 that separation ended. The resulting Columbian Exchange transformed the Americas and affected Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Epidemic disease had unequal effects because many Old World infections were new to the Americas. The explanation is historical and biological, not racial. Populations with no prior exposure could fall ill simultaneously, leaving too few healthy people to provide care, food, and water, so that mortality rose beyond what the disease itself would cause. The concept of the virgin soil epidemic captures lack of exposure but is misleading if used as a sole explanation.

The Caribbean suffered the first demographic catastrophe. On Hispaniola, the Taíno population collapsed within a generation amid violence, coerced labor, relocation, hunger, enslavement, and disease. The pre-contact population is unknown, but early counts record only a small fraction of it by 1514. Lucayan communities in the Bahamas were largely removed by slave raiding. These cases show that mortality had multiple causes from the beginning.

Disease was not the only cause of Indigenous decline. Violence, enslavement, tribute, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and declining fertility interacted with epidemics, and the balance varied by time and place. This understanding avoids treating the catastrophe as a natural event and avoids making conquest seem inevitable.

Smallpox, measles, influenza, and other diseases spread through expanding networks in repeated waves. Smallpox was documented on Hispaniola in 1518–1519 and reached Mexico in 1520, where its role is examined in Chapter 32. Later epidemics, including the cocoliztli epidemics in Mexico, struck already reduced populations. Epidemics also reached the Andes and coastal Brazil, sometimes ahead of European contact, which complicates the image of contact as a single moment.

Estimates of pre-contact populations and mortality vary enormously, reflecting different evidence and assumptions. Most scholars agree that declines were very large in many regions but disagree about the magnitude and the timing. This textbook treats the numbers as an evidence problem and stresses regional variation.

Introduced animals, including horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, and chickens, reshaped landscapes, economies, and, eventually, Indigenous warfare and herding. Old World plants, including wheat, grapes, olives, citrus, bananas, and sugarcane, were planted in the Americas, with uneven adoption. Sugarcane introduced an agricultural and ecological transfer tied to later plantation systems. American foods, including maize, potatoes, cassava, sweet potatoes, chili peppers, tomatoes, cacao, and peanuts, spread to Europe, Africa, and Asia, contributing over centuries to changes in diets and agriculture.

The Columbian Exchange transformed ecosystems through grazing, deforestation, weeds, feral animals, and the abandonment of Indigenous-managed landscapes. The exchange was reciprocal in the movement of crops but profoundly unequal in its consequences for disease. Recent genomic evidence favors an American origin for the syphilis-causing pathogen complex, though the emergence of venereal syphilis itself remains uncertain, and later tropical diseases such as malaria and yellow fever shaped colonial societies in distinct ways.

Indigenous peoples survived catastrophe, reorganizing communities, adopting new animals and crops, using colonial institutions, and preserving knowledge. Demographic collapse altered the balance of power and shaped colonial labor and land systems, but it did not make conquest automatic. Part VII turns to the conquest of Mexico and Central America as a multinational conflict in which Indigenous diplomacy, alliances, and rivalries played decisive roles alongside disease and Spanish intervention.

  1. Choose a subject. Select a crop (such as maize, potatoes, cassava, wheat, or sugarcane) or an animal (such as horses, cattle, pigs, or sheep).
  2. Trace its movement. Using library sources, document its origin, routes, dates of introduction, and the people who carried or adopted it.
  3. Examine effects. Identify effects on diet, labor, landscape, and politics in at least two regions, including any Indigenous responses.
  4. Assess the evidence. Note what kinds of sources support your account and where the evidence is weak or disputed.
  5. Write. In a 3–5 page essay or map-based presentation, explain the subject's history, noting uncertainty with careful language.

Library resources to use: Environmental and agricultural history reference works, studies of the Columbian Exchange, collections of colonial records, and the MGU History Library's materials on Atlantic history (see also Appendix A).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the conquest and colonization of the Americas were biological and ecological events as well as political and military ones. The Columbian Exchange altered what people ate, how they farmed, where they lived, and whether they lived at all. It is among the processes that most deeply connect Latin America to the histories of Europe, Africa, and Asia.

It also shows the importance of resisting simple explanations. Disease was central, but it acted within a context of coercion, violence, and social disruption produced by human decisions. Keeping both the biological and the political in view allows students to understand the catastrophe without treating it as natural or inevitable, and to appreciate the agency of those who survived it.

The consequences of this demographic and ecological transformation run through the rest of the textbook: in the labor systems and land tenure of colonial society, in the expansion of African slavery, in the reorganization of Indigenous communities, and in the agricultural and commercial economies of later centuries. The next part turns to the conquest of Mexico and Central America, where students will see how Indigenous alliances, rivalries, and diplomacy interacted with epidemic disease and Spanish intervention to produce outcomes that neither biology nor European arms alone could explain.
