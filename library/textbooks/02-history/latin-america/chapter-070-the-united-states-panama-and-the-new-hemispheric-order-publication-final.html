The United States, Panama, and the New Hemispheric Order

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Narrow Strip Between Two Oceans

On 3 November 1903, leading figures in Panama City proclaimed that the isthmus was no longer a department of Colombia. Within days a new government was recognized in Washington, and within weeks it had signed a treaty that allowed the United States to build and govern a canal.

Why did the separation of a narrow strip of land become one of the defining events in the transformation of relations between Latin America and the United States? The answer lies in the convergence of several histories: Panamanian regional ambitions, Colombian civil war and weak finances, a United States newly confident in its industrial and naval power, and an old dream of joining the Atlantic and Pacific. No single one of these explains the event.

This chapter concludes Part XV and Volume III. Chapters 68 and 69 showed how export economies and foreign capital reshaped land, labor, and wealth. Chapter 70 asks how those economic ties intersected with the rise of the United States as the leading external power in the Caribbean and Central America. It distinguishes among different mechanisms of power: diplomatic influence, investment, treaty privileges, financial supervision, military occupation, and formal annexation. They had different legal bases and different consequences.

The chapter's argument is that between 1898 and the early 1920s the United States turned growing economic and naval power into a far more assertive hemispheric position, but that Latin American governments and peoples were not merely acted upon. They negotiated, accommodated, protested, fought, litigated, and formulated principles of sovereignty and nonintervention that shaped later inter-American law.

The evidence is uneven. United States treaties, diplomatic correspondence, and military reports are abundant and are often written to justify policy. Latin American sources include treaty texts, diplomatic notes, congressional debates, newspapers, and the testimony of workers and resisters, but many Haitian, Dominican, and West Indian voices survive only in fragments. Students should read official documents as evidence of intention and justification, not as neutral verdicts.

Chapter 67 covered the wars of Cuban independence and the war of 1898; this chapter examines their strategic consequences. The Mexican Revolution, touched here only as diplomatic background, is the subject of Chapters 71–74. The Good Neighbor Policy and the Organization of American States belong to later decades and must not be projected backward into this period.

  4. Analyze Panama's separation from Colombia as the interaction of Panamanian, Colombian, and U.S. choices.
  5. Interpret the language of the 1903 canal treaty and the Canal Zone's divided sovereignty.

Historical Context

By 1900 most Latin American states had been independent for three generations, and many were integrated into world markets as exporters of coffee, sugar, bananas, minerals, and other commodities (Chapter 68). Foreign loans and investment, chiefly from Britain, France, Germany, and increasingly the United States, financed ports and railroads but also created debts that governments with narrow tax bases struggled to service. Customs duties were often the principal public revenue, which made ports the natural point of leverage.

The United States had meanwhile completed continental expansion, including the war with Mexico (Chapter 63), and by the 1890s was a leading industrial economy with a modernizing navy. Cuba and Puerto Rico remained Spanish until 1898 (Chapter 67). Britain, long the dominant naval power in the Caribbean, was reducing its commitments there as it faced rivals in Europe, a shift that made U.S. predominance in the region more feasible.

Division I — From the Monroe Doctrine to American Hemispheric Ascendancy

Section 70.1

The Monroe Doctrine and the Changing Meaning of Hemispheric Sovereignty

President James Monroe's annual message of 2 December 1823, shaped substantially by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams, responded to fears that the conservative powers of restoration Europe might help Spain recover its American colonies, and to Russian claims on the Pacific coast. The message declared that the American continents were not open to new European colonization and that the United States would regard European efforts to extend their political system to the hemisphere as dangerous, while pledging not to interfere in existing European colonies or in Europe's own affairs.

The doctrine grew out of a particular diplomatic calculation, not an international agreement signed by the American republics. The Spanish American revolutions had weakened European empires, but the newly independent governments feared attempts at restoration by Spain or its continental allies. British merchants and ministers wished to preserve access to the new markets; London therefore had little interest in renewed Spanish monopoly. Washington declined a proposed joint British-American declaration, preferring to speak independently. The result was a unilateral presidential message that asserted a strategic view of the hemisphere while making no promise of automatic military assistance to any republic facing danger.

A second context was the Pacific Northwest. Russian claims and settlements on the northwestern coast informed the noncolonization principle, while Monroe's warning about European political intervention arose primarily from affairs farther south. The message also pledged that the United States would not disturb existing European colonies. These distinctions matter: it was neither a universal guarantee of Latin American independence nor a demand that all European possessions depart. Washington's own expansion across North America and its treatment of Indigenous peoples further demonstrated that American proclamations of hemispheric principle coexisted with policies of territorial acquisition and domination.

In 1823 the United States could not enforce this stance; its practical protection came from British naval power, which also opposed Spanish restoration. Latin American leaders therefore received the message with mixed feelings. Some welcomed it as recognition of independence, while others, including Simón Bolívar's circle, doubted that Washington would act and placed more faith in Britain. Over the century the doctrine was invoked unevenly, and it was not a statute or a treaty but a statement of policy.

The later claim that the doctrine empowered the United States to supervise neighbors was an addition, not part of the text. The distinction between noncolonization and supervision is the hinge of this chapter.

Latin American governments confronted a practical dilemma. Britain could provide commercial credit and naval protection, yet it was itself an imperial power. The United States was a fellow former colony and republican state, but its growing reach could threaten other republics. Bolívar's 1826 Panama Congress sought a form of American cooperation that did not depend solely on Washington, though the meeting produced no binding collective-security regime. During subsequent decades, leaders interpreted the Monroe statement through particular disputes involving Mexico, the Caribbean, Central America, and European creditors. There was never one unchanging Latin American response to the doctrine.

The history of the doctrine is therefore partly a history of reinterpretation. Later presidents invoked it to resist European intervention, as in the French intervention in Mexico and the Venezuelan boundary controversy, while sometimes tolerating the continuation of European economic power. In 1895 Secretary of State Richard Olney advanced an exceptionally broad understanding of American diplomatic supremacy during the Venezuela dispute. By 1904 Theodore Roosevelt would claim a right to act within neighboring states themselves. Students should compare the actual dated statements rather than project the later corollary into Monroe's 1823 language.

Historical Vocabulary

Monroe Doctrine and Roosevelt Corollary

The Monroe Doctrine (1823) opposed new European colonization and political intervention in the independent Americas. The Roosevelt Corollary (1904) claimed that the United States might itself intervene in the affairs of unstable neighbors to forestall European intervention. The second is a distinct policy, and later writers who merge the two obscure how far U.S. claims expanded.

Section 70.2

American Industrial Power, Naval Expansion, and Strategic Interest in the Caribbean

By the 1890s the United States ranked among the world's foremost industrial producers and sought export markets and secure shipping. The navy was transformed from wooden vessels to steel, steam-driven ships that required coaling stations. Alfred Thayer Mahan, in The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890), argued that commerce, a strong fleet, and overseas bases were linked, and that an isthmian canal would be of great strategic importance. His ideas were influential among naval officers and politicians but were arguments, not official policy.

The expansion of U.S. manufacturing changed the scale on which foreign-policy planners imagined national interests. Steel, machinery, petroleum, agricultural products, and manufactured goods required overseas customers and reliable maritime transportation, while banks and business organizations increasingly followed commercial opportunities abroad. Still, there was no single business consensus on empire. Some exporters wanted stable agreements and ports without political annexations; some naval advocates demanded bases and strategic control; and opponents of annexation feared militarism, constitutional conflict, and the government of subject peoples without consent. Foreign policy grew through argument among these groups, not through an automatic consequence of industrial output.

Mahan's writings offered a strategic vocabulary for those arguments. Drawing historical lessons from British maritime ascendancy, he tied powerful battle fleets to an international network of harbors, coal supplies, repair facilities, and commercial routes. A canal across the Central American isthmus would reduce the time needed to shift American warships between the Atlantic and Pacific. Its entrances would consequently increase the strategic value of the Caribbean and adjacent Pacific waters. The U.S. Navy's expansion began before 1898, but the war with Spain made the case for a modern two-ocean fleet more compelling to politicians and newspaper readers.

The 1895 boundary crisis between Britain and Venezuela showed the new assertiveness. Secretary of State Richard Olney asserted that the United States was practically sovereign on the continent in its dealings, and the Cleveland administration insisted on arbitration. Britain conceded the principle and later arbitration largely favored its claim. Venezuela, whose interests were at stake, participated through counsel in the subsequent arbitration, even though U.S.–British diplomacy had framed the negotiation.

The Venezuela–British Guiana boundary dispute illustrates how language could outpace material capacity. In 1895 Washington pressed Britain to accept arbitration and Olney portrayed the United States as the decisive diplomatic authority in the Americas. Britain ultimately agreed to arbitration; Venezuela was represented in the legal process by counsel appointed on its behalf, and the 1899 award largely upheld British claims. It is inaccurate to say that Venezuelans played no part in the arbitration. At the same time, London's willingness to compromise with Washington reflected imperial priorities extending far beyond the Orinoco frontier.

The Caribbean was not an empty strategic chessboard. Cuban revolutionaries fought Spain for objectives of their own; Puerto Ricans disagreed about independence and autonomy; Haitians defended a century-old republic; and Central American governments sought investment without surrendering sovereignty. Britain, France, Germany, Spain, and the Netherlands maintained different economic and colonial positions around the basin. U.S. policymakers were thus responding to an established world of competing political actors. This chapter must evaluate American strategic purposes alongside the actions of the people whose ports, territories, and finances those purposes affected.

Section 70.3

The War of 1898 and the Transformation of the Caribbean Balance of Power

The war with Spain, covered in Chapter 67, ended with the Treaty of Paris of December 1898. Spain gave up sovereignty over Cuba, ceded Puerto Rico and Guam, and transferred the Philippines for a payment of twenty million dollars. Cuba was occupied by U.S. forces rather than annexed, because Congress's Teller Amendment had disclaimed any intention to exercise sovereignty there.

The 1898 war was short, but it had a long prehistory in Cuban anticolonial resistance. Cuban armies had been fighting a renewed independence war since 1895, following the unsuccessful Ten Years' War and later struggles. Spanish counterinsurgency devastated the countryside, while U.S. newspapers and Cuban exile organizations publicized suffering and appealed for intervention. The explosion of the battleship Maine in Havana harbor increased public pressure, although its cause remains contested. The American declaration of war turned a colonial conflict already underway into a war among states, with Cuban insurgents fighting alongside U.S. forces in important operations.

The Treaty of Paris drew sharp legal distinctions. Spain relinquished sovereignty over Cuba rather than ceding it to the United States as a possession; Puerto Rico and Guam were ceded, and the Philippines transferred under a separate article. The Teller Amendment had disclaimed a U.S. intention to establish permanent sovereignty over Cuba, but military occupation followed Spanish evacuation. Cuban revolutionary representatives had not participated as equal treaty negotiators. The settlement therefore ended Spanish rule without immediately giving Cubans control of their own national government, which explains the later constitutional confrontation over the Platt Amendment.

Strategically, the war eliminated Spain as a Caribbean power, left the United States with island bases, and made the Isthmus more urgent: in 1898 the battleship Oregon took roughly two months to sail from the Pacific coast to Florida, a voyage widely cited as proof that a canal was needed.

American victory also expanded the debate over the limits of republican government. Critics of imperialism asked whether Congress could govern distant populations who lacked the political rights enjoyed in the states. Supporters argued that naval security, commerce, and a duty to establish orderly government justified new overseas commitments. These arguments crossed party lines and united people who otherwise disagreed over domestic policy. The conflict's Pacific dimension, including the Philippines, made it clear that the United States was not merely adjusting local Caribbean relations but rethinking its place in world politics.

The voyage of the battleship Oregon from the Pacific to the Caribbean illustrated the logistical problem that a canal could solve, although contemporary accounts vary in the way they calculate the duration and stages of its journey. A warship could not move between oceans rapidly without rounding South America. Once Puerto Rico and the approaches to Cuba acquired greater value as military positions, the idea of an American-controlled interoceanic passage became inseparable from wider naval planning. The strategic afterlife of 1898 therefore belongs in this chapter even though the battles themselves belong to Chapter 67.

Section 70.4

Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the New Architecture of American Influence after 1898

Cuba and Puerto Rico received different statuses. Cuba's constitutional convention was required by the U.S. to accept the Platt Amendment of 1901, which limited Cuba's treaty-making and debt, gave the United States a right to intervene to preserve independence and orderly government, and provided for naval stations. The republic was inaugurated in May 1902, and in 1903 Cuba leased Guantánamo Bay and Bahía Honda sites to the United States. Cubans protested the amendment but accepted it under the threat of continued occupation.

Under occupation Cuba was governed by U.S. military authorities from 1899 until the inauguration of the republic in May 1902. The occupation undertook sanitary reforms, school construction, administrative reorganization, and the demobilization of Cuban insurgents. Such measures could deliver practical improvements while leaving the fundamental question of sovereign decision-making unanswered. The Cuban constitutional convention elected in 1900 drafted institutions for republican government, only to be told that withdrawal depended on accepting conditions drawn up in Washington. Cuban representatives debated whether refusal would preserve principle or prolong foreign military control.

The Platt Amendment restricted the making of treaties that could impair Cuban independence, constrained debt obligations, allowed U.S. intervention under stated circumstances, and required arrangements for naval stations. It was not simply a trade agreement or an advisory declaration. Cuban lawmakers accepted it under pressure, then embodied its provisions in an American-Cuban treaty of 1903. The Guantánamo lease created a naval presence that endured beyond the period discussed here; the originally contemplated Bahía Honda site was later relinquished under a revised arrangement. The distinction between treaty language and implementation should be maintained throughout the chapter.

Puerto Rico became an unincorporated territory under the Foraker Act of 1900, with a U.S.-appointed governor and a limited elected assembly. In the Insular Cases beginning in 1901 the Supreme Court held that the Constitution did not apply fully in newly acquired territories, creating a category of belonging without full incorporation. These contrasting models, a nominal republic under tutelage and a colony without statehood, illustrate the difference between sovereignty, occupation, and constitutional incorporation.

Puerto Rico followed a different constitutional path. The 1900 Foraker Act established a civil government with a presidentially appointed executive and an elected lower legislative chamber whose decisions remained constrained by federal authorities. The Supreme Court's Insular Cases developed the category of unincorporated territories, and the constitutional rights of inhabitants were debated in American courts and Puerto Rican political organizations. Puerto Rican nationality and citizenship were not identical to U.S. citizenship before the Jones–Shafroth Act of 1917. Territorial government did not extinguish Puerto Rican campaigns for independence, greater self-government, or alternative constitutional arrangements.

The contrast mattered to business interests as well as political leaders. Puerto Rican sugar, coffee, and tobacco producers navigated changing currency and customs arrangements; Cuban sugar interests operated under a formally independent government with intense U.S. commercial influence. Neither island was politically homogeneous. Cuban Afro-descendant veterans faced discrimination even as they had helped win independence, and Puerto Rican autonomists, nationalists, and pro-American politicians offered competing proposals. Legal categories such as republic, territory, protectorate, and occupation must therefore be tested against everyday powers over taxation, courts, armies, and foreign relations.

Division II — Panama, Separation from Colombia, and the Interoceanic Canal

Section 70.5

The Isthmus of Panama, Colombian Sovereignty, and the Search for an Interoceanic Canal

Panama, a department of Colombia, had long been valued for transit. The Panama Railroad, built by U.S. investors and completed in 1855, carried gold-rush travelers and then commerce, and it created a transit economy centered on Panama City and Colón. Panamanian elites often resented rule from distant Bogotá and made several separatist attempts in the nineteenth century, though a canal-based economy also bound them to Colombian policy.

Panama's role as a transit corridor predated any proposal for a modern canal. Spanish imperial officials transported goods and communications across the narrowest parts of the isthmus, and the nineteenth-century republics sought to profit from the same geography. After independence from Spain in 1821 the region joined Gran Colombia, and it later remained connected to the state that became the Republic of Colombia. Relations with Bogotá were marked by recurring debates over federalism, regional autonomy, customs revenue, and local appointment of officials. Panamanian political opinion cannot be treated as one continuous separatist program, but demands for more control over the isthmus had substantial historical roots.

The American-built Panama Railroad opened in 1855, and the California gold rush had already made crossing the isthmus a global commercial business. The line employed workers from different countries, fostered settlement and labor conflict, and gave foreign transport companies substantial influence over local trade. Its profits rose and fell with changes in global routes. A ship canal promised a larger transformation: if large ocean-going vessels could cross without unloading cargo, maritime travel between Atlantic and Pacific destinations would become much shorter. That prospect attracted engineers, speculators, diplomatic negotiators, and governments with different expectations about who should administer the passage.

Ferdinand de Lesseps's French canal company, after the Suez success, began excavation in the 1880s, but yellow fever, malaria, engineering errors, and financial scandal ended the venture by 1889. Later efforts continued under a successor company. Nicaragua offered a competing route, favored by many U.S. politicians.

Colombia was meanwhile consumed by the Thousand Days' War (1899–1902), a civil war between Liberals and Conservatives that included fighting on the isthmus and left the state financially exhausted. This weakness shaped its response to U.S. demands.

The French enterprise illustrates why geographical narrowness did not mean engineering simplicity. De Lesseps initially pursued a sea-level design modeled on Suez, underestimating tropical rainfall, the Chagres River, landslides, and the scale of excavation. Mosquito-borne diseases caused enormous losses before their mechanisms were adequately understood. Financial promoters attracted investments from large numbers of French savers, and the company's collapse in 1889 became a national scandal in France. A successor company preserved some rights and equipment, creating assets that later figured in American bargaining and in the political role of Bunau-Varilla.

Colombia's Thousand Days' War deepened the crisis of national authority. Liberal and Conservative forces fought over governmental power and constitutional arrangements, and combat reached Panama. The peace settlements of 1902 ended much of the fighting, but they did not resolve tensions over the isthmus or strengthen Bogotá sufficiently to bargain on equal terms with Washington. A canal treaty required the consent of Colombia's constitutional institutions and affected local Panamanian prospects. The problem facing both governments was not whether trade mattered, but who would receive the revenues, exercise policing power, and retain effective control of territory.

Section 70.6

The Hay–Herrán Treaty and Panama's Separation from Colombia, 1903

The Hay–Pauncefote Treaty of 1901 freed the United States from the earlier arrangement with Britain that restricted either country from obtaining exclusive control over a proposed isthmian canal. In 1902 Congress authorized the Panama route, conditional on acquiring rights from Colombia and the French company. The Hay–Herrán Treaty was signed in January 1903, offering Colombia a payment and annual rent for a canal strip, and the Colombian Senate rejected it in August 1903, objecting to the terms and to the surrender of jurisdiction.

The 1901 Hay–Pauncefote Treaty replaced the Clayton–Bulwer framework of 1850, which had restricted exclusive American or British canal control. This was a major diplomatic change: London increasingly accepted U.S. primacy in the Caribbean while managing other imperial challenges. Congress's 1902 decision to favor Panama was conditional, not a blank instruction to take the isthmus by force. Washington expected to buy the French company's interests and acquire canal rights through an agreement with Colombia, which still possessed internationally recognized sovereignty over the territory.

The Hay–Herrán convention signed January 22, 1903 offered a framework for construction and payment, but Colombia's Senate disapproved it in August. Colombian legislators objected to jurisdictional restrictions, compensation, constitutional authority, and the treatment of the French company's assets. Their rejection was neither evidence of hostility to a canal in principle nor proof of irrational obstruction. Colombian diplomatic notes emphasized that a treaty negotiated by an executive could not override the legislature's constitutional power to reject or seek modifications. U.S. messages, by contrast, warned that delay could jeopardize relations. The surviving correspondence documents an increasingly unequal negotiation rather than a consensual technical agreement.

Panamanian conspirators, including Manuel Amador Guerrero, and Philippe Bunau-Varilla, who held interests in the French company's assets, then organized a rising. Bunau-Varilla sought assurances in Washington. On 3 November a declaration of independence was proclaimed in Panama City, and U.S. warships, notably the USS Nashville at Colón, prevented Colombian troops from moving across the isthmus. The United States recognized Panama on 6 November.

Panamanian separatists acted in this political opening. They organized contacts, considered the support they would need to withstand Colombian forces, and pursued the independence that some of their predecessors had advocated under different circumstances. Bunau-Varilla was important because he connected canal-company interests with American officials and Panamanian conspirators. Nevertheless he did not embody all Panamanian interests, and local leaders did not simply wait for an American command. A responsible account of November 1903 distinguishes the decisions of political elites in Panama City, the actions of Colombian commanders, and the consequences of naval deployments ordered in Washington.

The U.S. warships and orders constraining the movement of Colombian troops made the outcome more likely and helped prevent effective suppression of the rising. American recognition on November 6 followed rapidly; formal diplomatic relations and treaty negotiations then proceeded under exceptional speed. The November declaration was therefore both a local act of separation and an episode of foreign intervention. Historians disagree over how to weight the causes, but a defensible interpretation requires contemporaneous Colombian protests, Panamanian proclamations, U.S. operational records, and attention to ordinary isthmian populations outside the small group of treaty negotiators.

Section 70.7

The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty and the Creation of the Canal Zone

The treaty was signed on 18 November 1903 in Washington by Hay and Bunau-Varilla, who had been appointed Panama's representative though he was not Panamanian and had little contact with the new government. Panamanian delegates arrived after the text was largely settled. Panama received ten million dollars and, after nine years, an annual payment of 250,000 dollars. The United States gained rights over a zone ten miles wide across the isthmus, with the use, occupation, and control needed for a canal.

The hastily negotiated convention gave Panama important benefits, including a formal U.S. guarantee of independence, an immediate payment, and the expectation that canal construction would generate jobs and commercial activity. Those benefits were politically meaningful after decades of unstable relations with Bogotá. Yet the scope of U.S. jurisdiction was unusually broad. Article II granted use, occupation, and control of the zone in perpetuity; Article III allowed the United States to exercise powers as though it were sovereign, excluding Panama from exercising comparable authority there. The treaty also addressed sanitation, auxiliary lands, and rights beyond the most familiar central strip.

The cities of Panama and Colón were formally excluded from the ordinary ten-mile zone, but the treaty and subsequent administration affected their ports, public works, and relations with the Canal Zone. Residents encountered a complex geography of boundaries, police authority, courts, railway lands, water supply, and employment. The phrase 'divided sovereignty' summarizes this practical complexity but does not itself resolve the competing legal positions. Some American officials depicted the arrangement as necessary for an international work; many Panamanians saw a lasting restriction upon the independence they had just achieved.

The treaty did not annex Panama and recognized its independence. But Article III gave the United States rights, power, and authority within the zone as if it were the sovereign, and Article II granted them in perpetuity. Panama thus retained titular sovereignty while the United States exercised governing powers, a divided arrangement that produced later disputes about legitimacy. Panama's convention ratified the treaty in December 1903, and the U.S. Senate in February 1904. Colombia continued to object; the Thomson–Urrutia Treaty (signed 1914, ratifications exchanged 1922) eventually gave Colombia a 25 million dollar payment, paid over several years.

Consent requires more analysis than the fact of ratification. Bunau-Varilla negotiated as an accredited representative of the new republic, but he was a French citizen with strong interests in the canal company and was operating before Panama had organized ordinary constitutional government. The provisional junta approved the treaty promptly, and local municipal bodies also produced statements of support; some Panamanian elites welcomed the protection they expected it to provide. The terms nevertheless became the object of extensive later national criticism. Treating the treaty as either entirely imposed or entirely freely bargained conceals the differences among formal legal approval, unequal bargaining power, and subsequent public legitimacy.

Colombia never accepted the separation as a straightforward transfer of its rights. Its government protested American involvement and sought diplomatic settlement, while relations with Panama and Washington remained unsettled. The Thomson–Urrutia Treaty was signed in 1914 but was not put into effect through exchanged ratifications until 1922, after amendment and prolonged political debate. Its settlement included a payment by the United States to Colombia, not a retroactive declaration that the 1903 process had been uncontroversial. This longer chronology shows how the canal transformed not only transportation but the language and practice of international responsibility.

Historical Vocabulary

Canal Zone and Sovereignty

The Canal Zone was the strip in which the United States exercised governing authority. It was neither annexed territory nor ordinary Panamanian land. It shows that sovereignty can be divided between title and jurisdiction, and it explains why Panamanians later spoke of the treaty as imposed.

Section 70.8

Building the Panama Canal, 1904–1914: Engineering, Workers, Disease, and Global Commerce

Construction began in 1904 under U.S. government control. After early difficulties, John Stevens reorganized the work and George W. Goethals, an army engineer, became chief engineer in 1907. The final design used locks: a great dam at Gatún created an artificial lake, locks raised and lowered ships, and the Culebra Cut was excavated through the continental divide, with frequent landslides. Sanitation was equally critical. William Gorgas, applying the mosquito theory of yellow fever and malaria transmission, led drainage, fumigation, and screening campaigns that sharply reduced yellow fever on the isthmus.

Canal construction required the coordination of engineering systems on a scale unusual for the early twentieth century. The Americans ultimately adopted a lock canal rather than a sea-level cut. Gatun Lake, formed by damming the Chagres, became a navigable reservoir; three-stage locks raised vessels to the lake and lowered them toward the opposite coast. Excavators, steam shovels, railcars, and temporary tracks moved immense quantities of earth from the Culebra Cut. Slides destroyed work already completed and demanded constant redesign. Managers also needed workshops, housing, hospitals, docks, and a reliable system for bringing coal, machinery, and food into the construction zone.

Public health was as consequential as machinery. Carlos Finlay's earlier mosquito theory and later research on yellow fever and malaria helped guide the American sanitation campaign. Under William Gorgas, workers drained standing water, screened buildings, managed water supplies, and attacked mosquito breeding sites. Yellow fever was eliminated as an epidemic threat during the construction period, while malaria remained a continuing challenge. The triumphalist language of some official accounts should not obscure the uneven access to medical care, the difficulty of comparing mortality data, or the suffering during the earlier French enterprise.

The workforce was recruited from Barbados, Jamaica, and other Caribbean islands, along with Europeans and U.S. citizens and Panamanians. Tens of thousands of West Indians did the dangerous digging, blasting, and track work. Accidents and disease killed thousands; counts differ by source and definition. The canal opened on 15 August 1914, with the transit of the SS Ancon, just as war began in Europe, so its early commercial role was shadowed by wartime disruption. It shortened sea routes between the two coasts of the United States and shifted global shipping patterns.

The workforce was international and internally divided. Caribbean migrants, especially from Barbados and Jamaica, formed much of the heavy labor force; Panamanians, Europeans, and Americans also participated. Recruitment letters and contracts conveyed promises of wages, but workers discovered conditions shaped by heat, danger, injury, and racial hierarchy. The gold-roll and silver-roll classification distinguished pay, housing, schooling, transport, leave, and social facilities, with classification increasingly aligned with race rather than simply skill. Black American employees could also encounter discrimination. These divisions survived beyond the excavation itself and shaped the identities of later Afro-Panamanian communities.

Canal towns were also inhabited by cooks, shopkeepers, teachers, religious leaders, laundresses, and families who made construction society possible. Women often supported households through informal labor absent from engineering reports. Disputes over food prices, accommodation, contract terms, and workplace treatment reveal that the canal was a lived social system as much as an engineering project. When the vessel Ancon traversed the canal in August 1914, its opening transformed naval and commercial route calculations. But tolls, wartime shipping disruptions, and the geography of global trade meant the canal's economic significance unfolded over time rather than arriving fully formed on opening day.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life and Work in the Canal Zone

The Zone's employment system was divided into a gold roll, mainly white U.S. employees paid in gold-based currency with housing, schools, and commissaries, and a silver roll for most West Indians and others, paid in lower-value currency with poorer housing and fewer services. Segregation shaped schools, clubs, and towns. Workers formed mutual-aid societies, churches, and benefit groups, and a legacy of West Indian Panamanians grew, whose citizenship and status were disputed in later decades. Women worked as laundresses, cooks, and vendors, and ran boarding houses, labor largely missing from official reports.

Section 70.9

Venezuela's Debt Crisis, European Naval Intervention, and the Drago Doctrine, 1902–1903

Venezuela under President Cipriano Castro was financially disordered after civil wars, and owed money to European creditors. In December 1902 Britain, Germany, and Italy blockaded Venezuelan ports and seized vessels. The Roosevelt administration did not forbid debt collection by force but pressed for arbitration, and the blockade ended in early 1903 with Venezuela pledging a share of customs revenue. The dispute was handled at The Hague over the question of preferential treatment for the blockading powers.

The 1902–1903 confrontation arose from several kinds of claims. Some European demands concerned unpaid sovereign debt and others compensation for injuries to foreigners or damage sustained during civil conflict. Venezuela's government under Cipriano Castro resisted claims it regarded as excessive or politically motivated, while creditors accused the administration of bad faith. Britain and Germany used naval coercion, joined by Italy. The blockade interfered with trade and made the dispute a test of whether large creditor states could force a smaller republic into compliance without violating international norms of independence.

The U.S. position was not simply that European states had no right to act. Roosevelt's administration made distinctions among temporary coercion, debt settlement, and territorial acquisition, while seeking to prevent permanent European strategic footholds. Arbitration appeared preferable because it offered a way of regularizing claims without annexation. The dispute over preferential payment to countries that had used force exposed a paradox: successful coercion might give aggressive creditors an advantage, thereby encouraging similar actions elsewhere. Venezuela was not merely an object of theoretical debate; its revenues, ports, and reputation were at stake.

Argentine foreign minister Luis María Drago responded with a note of late December 1902 arguing that public debts should not justify armed intervention or occupation of an American nation's territory. The Hague Convention of 1907 on contractual debts (the Porter Convention) later limited force to cases where arbitration was refused.

Drago, writing to Argentina's minister in Washington on December 29, 1902, framed the problem as a defense of sovereign equality. A creditor who lent to a sovereign knew that enforcing judgment through international armed force could destroy the debtor state's political independence. His note maintained that obligations should be honored, yet rejected territorial occupation and violent collection as legitimate consequences of default. It did not claim that a state could never be taken before a court; indeed Drago noted the possibility of suits against the Argentine state under domestic law. The crucial distinction was between adjudicating a debt and executing collection through foreign coercion.

The later Hague convention of 1907 introduced limits upon the use of armed force to recover contractual debts, especially when arbitration had been accepted. Its exceptions and ratifications mattered, and it did not end international financial intervention. The Drago episode nevertheless demonstrates that Latin American diplomats proposed principles with international consequences. They used the language of sovereign equality to contest practices that might be presented in European capitals as routine debt enforcement. The Roosevelt Corollary would answer the same instability problem with a very different claim about who had authority to act.

Section 70.10

Theodore Roosevelt, the Big Stick, and the Roosevelt Corollary of 1904

Roosevelt, who championed the canal and a strong navy, favored a policy often summarized as "speak softly and carry a big stick." In his annual message of December 1904 he argued that chronic wrongdoing or impotence in an American country might require intervention by some civilized nation and that, in flagrant cases, the United States might be forced to exercise an "international police power." The Corollary thus shifted the Monroe Doctrine from warning Europe away to claiming a U.S. right to act.

Roosevelt connected regional stability to the protection of American naval and commercial interests. A European seizure of a Caribbean customs house, even if originally intended to enforce a creditor's claim, might create pressure for an enduring military presence near the approaches to Panama. His proposed answer was preventive American action. The December 1904 annual message argued that persistent wrongdoing or governmental incapacity might justify international police power exercised by the United States. That statement assigned Washington a discretionary judgment over the conduct of formally independent neighbors, even while Roosevelt continued to declare that the United States desired their prosperity and independence.

The original Monroe Doctrine had been addressed chiefly to outside powers, warning against colonization or political interference by Europe. The corollary shifted the focus toward the internal conduct of American republics. This difference did not make the older doctrine benign in every application, but it marked a major conceptual expansion. The standard of 'chronic wrongdoing' was not defined by a regional court representing all the American states; it would be interpreted by the executive of the strongest government. Critics could therefore argue that the doctrine violated precisely the equality of sovereign states it purported to protect.

The statement was not a law, and its use varied by administration. Roosevelt said he sought no territory and wanted stable, prosperous neighbors, but the doctrine left to Washington the judgment of what counted as wrongdoing, creating tensions among security, sovereignty, and intervention.

Roosevelt's administration did not implement identical measures everywhere. Financial arrangements in the Dominican Republic offered a method of paying creditors without European military occupation; Cuba's Platt Amendment established a distinct treaty-based justification for intervention; Panama's canal treaty depended on an exceptional territorial concession. The corollary supplied language that could rationalize such measures, but it cannot substitute for examining their actual legal instruments and local political circumstances. Political leaders in some countries requested American help against rivals, while others resisted any external supervision, and positions shifted with changes of government.

The terminology of 'Big Stick' can encourage students to imagine one continuous sequence of gunboat threats. American power also operated through diplomatic recognition, loans, customs agreements, and the expectation of intervention rather than through permanent occupation alone. Its outcomes were mixed: certain debts were reorganized and some public works financed, yet supervision deepened disputes over popular consent and national dignity. By the time Taft advanced dollar diplomacy, the corollary had become a widely recognized part of the regional political environment. Latin American legal arguments against intervention developed in answer to this experience.

Section 70.11

Dominican Debt, Customs Receivership, and American Financial Supervision

The Dominican Republic owed substantial sums to European and U.S. creditors and was beset by factional conflict. In 1905 a provisional arrangement placed customs collection under an American official, and the 1907 convention, approved by the U.S. Senate and Dominican authorities, set up a General Receiver of Customs who collected duties and provided for the allocation of collections to receivership costs, service of the reorganized debt, and remaining Dominican public revenues under its specified financial procedures. The convention also barred increases in the public debt without U.S. consent.

The Dominican case illustrates why control of public revenue could become an instrument of international authority. Repeated changes of government had generated overlapping loan contracts, disputed obligations, and claims held by European and American creditors. Dominican customs duties were among the state's most reliable sources of income because foreign goods entered through identifiable ports. Creditors consequently sought a claim upon receipts at the point of collection rather than relying on promises made by shifting administrations. Dominican leaders also saw advantages in restructuring debt to end recurring demands, yet the proposed solution placed a key function of government under foreign supervision.

Under a provisional arrangement in 1905, American personnel began administering customs revenues, with a prominent formula directing part of collections toward creditors and part to the Dominican government. The convention signed February 8, 1907 created a more formal mechanism. It authorized a General Receiver and assistants, established procedures for paying the expenses of collection and the service of a reorganized bonded debt, and restricted certain debt and tariff changes without American approval. Students should not carry the 1905 percentage split mechanically into a description of every clause of the 1907 convention: the later agreement contained its own priorities and administrative rules.

The arrangement reduced European pressure and restored creditor confidence, and revenues rose in some years. But it limited fiscal autonomy and was open to political use, since control of customs conditioned the capacity of any Dominican government.

The financial arithmetic reflected politics. The 1907 convention described a plan to settle disputed claims and issue new bonds with terms intended to restore confidence. Creditors might accept substantial reductions of nominal claims in exchange for a more secure repayment stream. American officials argued that the arrangement forestalled European coercion and improved government services. Dominican politicians assessed who would control appointments, taxation, and negotiations with foreign firms. Even when revenues increased, the question remained whether elected Dominican officials could determine fiscal priorities without approval from Washington.

Receivership differed from annexation and from a formal military government. Dominican institutions continued to exist; the state did not lose its internationally recognized independence. Yet a government denied full control over its primary tax stream faced limits on military expenditure, public employment, and development policy. The arrangement therefore demonstrates how formal sovereignty could coexist with constrained autonomy. It also provides the necessary background for the 1916 occupation: the latter was not simply a repetition of the 1907 convention but an escalation from financial oversight into direct control of governing institutions.

Section 70.12

Dollar Diplomacy, Nicaragua, and the Expansion of American Financial Influence

President William Howard Taft and Secretary of State Philander Knox sought to link bank loans, stability, and U.S. security. In Nicaragua, President José Santos Zelaya had angered Washington by resisting U.S. and foreign interests and by conflict with neighbors. A rebellion in 1909 was supported by conservative opponents and some foreign firms; the execution of two Americans who had joined the rebels preceded the U.S. break with Zelaya, who resigned in December 1909.

Taft's administration emphasized loans and banking arrangements as instruments of diplomacy, presenting American investment as a means of stabilizing public finances and promoting commerce. In practice, the boundary between a private loan and official pressure could be narrow. Banks depended on expectations of governmental support, while local politicians might see loans as resources for defeating opponents or rebuilding a treasury. Officials in Washington could make recognition, negotiations, or protection of investments conditional on favored political outcomes. The resulting policy was often described as dollars replacing bullets, yet armed intervention remained available when financial arrangements could not withstand local conflict.

Nicaragua's internal divisions were essential to this process. José Santos Zelaya had pursued a strong regional position and resisted restrictions that would subordinate his government to American preferences. The 1909 rebellion involved conservative opponents, disaffected local interests, and diplomatic controversies, including the execution of two U.S. citizens serving with insurgents. Zelaya resigned in December, but the country's competing factions did not thereby agree on the legitimate future of the state. Conservative governments sought outside support while opponents criticized dependence on Washington. The 1912 landing of U.S. marines occurred during renewed civil war and was justified as protection of lives, transportation, and government; it also influenced the outcome of local power struggles.

Loans from U.S. banks and U.S. customs supervision followed, and in 1912 marines landed to protect U.S. interests during a civil conflict; a legation guard remained for years. The Bryan–Chamorro Treaty, signed on 5 August 1914 and in force in 1916, gave the United States exclusive rights to a possible canal route across Nicaragua and lease options on naval bases for three million dollars, thereby also foreclosing a rival canal.

The subsequent financial agreements were shaped by the same constraints that marked Dominican receivership but not by precisely the same institutions. Customs security, railway interests, national-bank proposals, and constitutional control were recurrent issues. American recognition could strengthen an administration lacking extensive domestic support; conversely, the presence of American troops could turn local disputes into controversies about foreign domination. A small guard remained after the major military operations of 1912, but this should not be described as a single unbroken occupation of the same type lasting to 1933. The later Sandino war belongs to a subsequent historical period.

The Bryan–Chamorro Treaty added a more explicit strategic purpose. Nicaragua granted perpetual exclusive rights to construct an interoceanic canal if the United States chose to do so. It also leased Great and Little Corn islands for ninety-nine years and granted a comparable, renewable concession for a naval base on the Gulf of Fonseca. The financial consideration was three million dollars, and U.S. Senate amendments shaped the final agreement brought into force in 1916. Costa Rica and El Salvador argued that aspects of the arrangement infringed their interests and challenged it through the Central American Court of Justice. The canal option was thus an instrument for preventing alternatives to Panama as well as a potential development project.

Section 70.13

Cuba's Second American Occupation and the Limits of Republican Sovereignty, 1906–1909

President Tomás Estrada Palma's reelection in 1905 was contested by Liberals, who rose in revolt in 1906. Estrada Palma asked for U.S. intervention and then resigned. Secretary of War Taft arrived, and Taft and Robert Bacon negotiated; Taft himself became provisional governor on September 29, 1906, then transferred the office to Charles Magoon on October 13; Magoon governed until January 1909. The administration carried out public works, new elections, and military reorganization, but also expanded patronage and spending, which later critics condemned.

The crisis of 1906 arose from Cuba's own partisan conflicts but unfolded under the extraordinary power claimed in the Platt Amendment. Tomás Estrada Palma's supporters sought to secure their position in disputed elections, while Liberal opponents condemned electoral manipulation and armed insurgents contested the government's legitimacy. Estrada Palma preferred U.S. intervention to bargaining with political rivals under unfavorable conditions. Washington sent negotiators and determined that direct provisional administration was necessary after the president and other leading officials resigned. This was not a newly conquered Spanish colony: Cuban sovereignty existed in law, even as the American occupation exposed how circumscribed its exercise had become.

Taft assumed office as provisional governor on September 29, 1906, and transferred authority to Charles E. Magoon on October 13. Those separate appointments are documented in contemporary proclamations and should not be compressed into a single handover. The occupation administration managed elections, public works, policing, fiscal spending, and civil institutions while maintaining the promise that an elected Cuban government would return. Some Cubans considered U.S. administration a temporary means of restoring order; others resented the precedent and the government's inability to resolve domestic disputes without foreign arbitration backed by force.

José Miguel Gómez was elected and took office in 1909. In 1912 a rising linked to the Partido Independiente de Color, a party of Afro-Cuban politicians that had been banned, was suppressed by Cuban forces with heavy loss of life; U.S. marines were landed to protect property, but the repression was above all a Cuban government action. The Platt Amendment remained until 1934.

Political stabilization was complicated by patronage and competing claims of legitimacy. The intervention did not abolish Cuban party competition, but it shaped the conditions under which parties operated. Administrative reforms and road construction could coexist with allegations that occupation spending favored particular networks. Cubans demanded reliable elections, control over their own public offices, and the withdrawal of foreign troops, but they did not agree on how these objectives should be reconciled. When José Miguel Gómez took office in January 1909, the formal occupation ended; the Platt Amendment, however, continued to place a powerful external condition upon Cuban decision-making.

Racial politics showed further limits of the republic. Afro-Cuban veterans had fought for independence yet encountered persistent barriers to office, employment, and equality. The Partido Independiente de Color organized against such exclusion, and the government's prohibition of the party helped create the crisis of 1912. Cuban forces brutally suppressed the subsequent uprising, causing extensive deaths whose numerical totals remain contested; American marines landed in circumstances described as protection of U.S. property. The violence cannot responsibly be narrated as solely an American intervention, nor should the importance of American economic interests be erased. The episode connects national sovereignty to the question of who counted as a full political citizen.

Section 70.14

The American Occupation of Haiti, 1915–1934: Finance, Governance, and Resistance

Haiti, independent since 1804 (Chapter 50), experienced recurrent political upheaval and carried foreign debt; French and German interests held important commercial positions, and the National Bank was controlled largely by French and U.S. interests. After President Vilbrun Guillaume Sam ordered executions of political prisoners and was killed by a Port-au-Prince mob in late July 1915, U.S. marines landed on 28 July. A treaty imposed in September 1915 gave the United States control of customs and finances, and created a gendarmerie under U.S. officers. A new constitution in 1918, which dropped the prohibition on foreign land ownership, was approved in a plebiscite amid U.S. pressure and boycott.

Haiti's political crises were not the result of an absence of a national tradition. The republic, created through revolution against French colonial slavery, had maintained independence for more than a century. Yet recurring struggles among presidential factions, military commanders, rural constituencies, and urban economic elites weakened central administration. Haitian foreign borrowing placed fiscal pressure on customs receipts, while French and German interests competed with growing American involvement in banking and trade. U.S. officials also feared that rival powers might gain a strategic position near the Caribbean approaches to Panama. These concerns formed part of the decision to land marines following the violent upheaval of July 1915.

The immediate sequence matters. President Vilbrun Guillaume Sam ordered the execution of political prisoners, was killed during the ensuing crisis, and American troops entered Port-au-Prince on July 28. They occupied key positions and influenced the choice of Philippe Sudré Dartiguenave as president. The Haitian–American convention of September 16 authorized U.S.-nominated supervision of customs and finance and the establishment of a constabulary organized under American officers. Haitian negotiators were not wholly silent: diplomatic correspondence records objections to humiliating terms and to American seizure of public institutions before ratification. A September 4 Haitian ministerial protest provides direct evidence of those objections.

The occupation brought roads and public health measures, but also racial segregation by U.S. personnel and the use of forced labor on roads, the corvée, which provoked the Second Caco War beginning in 1918. Charlemagne Péralte led armed resistance in the north until he was killed in 1919 by U.S. forces. Haitian writers, lawyers, and activists mounted a nationalist press and legal opposition, and in 1929–1930 strikes and killings led to inquiry commissions and a negotiated withdrawal, formally ending in 1934, though fiscal supervision lingered.

The occupation reshaped constitutional politics. In 1917 Haitian legislators resisted proposals associated with an American-sponsored constitutional settlement, and the legislature was dissolved. The constitution approved through a plebiscite in 1918 changed rules concerning foreign ownership of land and expanded the political framework under which occupation operated. Officials defended these changes as conditions of economic development; critics interpreted them as assaults on the autonomy and legal inheritance of the Haitian Revolution. A historical account must distinguish the text of a treaty or constitution from the actual distribution of power between elected Haitian institutions and foreign officials.

Rural society bore heavy costs. The gendarmerie, road-building campaigns, and forced-labor practices generated resistance, including major Caco mobilization from 1918 onward. Charlemagne Péralte became a prominent leader and was killed in 1919; resistance continued under other leaders. U.S. forces also carried out public-health and infrastructure projects, but those activities cannot cancel the record of coercion, censorship, racial inequality, and civilian suffering. Haitian journalists, teachers, lawyers, and political organizations developed arguments for constitutional government and sovereignty, while people in rural districts defended their communities through forms of resistance rarely fully documented in official reports. The formal military occupation ended in 1934, but foreign financial influence continued afterward.

Section 70.15

The Dominican Republic under American Military Government, 1916–1924

After renewed political turmoil, and when Dominican leaders resisted U.S. demands over finance and a constabulary, U.S. marines landed in May 1916. In November 1916 Captain Harry S. Knapp proclaimed military government. The occupiers disbanded the army, created the Guardia Nacional, built roads and schools, and censored the press. In the eastern provinces, guerrillas known as gavilleros resisted, and U.S. forces responded harshly; abuses were investigated by a U.S. Senate inquiry in 1921–1922.

The Dominican Republic entered occupation with a different institutional background from Haiti. The customs receivership begun in 1905 and formalized in 1907 had already restricted fiscal autonomy, but Dominican presidents, legislators, local military commanders, and political parties continued to contend over government. When the Wilson administration demanded reforms of the armed forces and further concessions, Dominican leaders resisted what they perceived as an attempt to convert financial oversight into political subordination. U.S. forces landed in May 1916 amid these disputes, and the subsequent proclamation of military government in November removed ordinary Dominican control over central administration.

Military rule was justified by American officials as necessary to restore stability and safeguard revenues. It brought investments in roads and public administration and helped create new security institutions. Yet censorship, executive decrees, restrictions on political activity, and prosecution by occupation authorities generated strong criticism. The Guardia Nacional established under American supervision would become an important part of the Dominican military system after withdrawal. The impact was therefore not limited to the period of foreign rule; it altered the distribution of coercive power within the future Dominican state.

Dominican elites organized a nationalist campaign, and Dominican emissaries, including Francisco Henríquez y Carvajal, appealed internationally. Following the war the Harding administration negotiated withdrawal through a provisional Dominican government in 1922 and elections in 1924. The Guardia Nacional it left behind later became the base of Rafael Trujillo's power, a consequence beyond this volume.

Resistance was uneven in geography and form. In the eastern provinces rural fighters known as gavilleros challenged occupation forces and the social and economic changes associated with American-backed authority. Officials depicted them as bandits, while local histories reveal complex relations among peasant communities, regional patrons, and armed men. Elsewhere lawyers, newspaper editors, civic associations, and established politicians argued for an end to military government. U.S. Senate hearings documented allegations of abuses and provided an official forum in which occupation practices were contested. Their existence shows that American policy was debated at home as well as internationally.

Withdrawal came through negotiations rather than an abrupt military defeat. Political pressure within the United States, Dominican public mobilization, and changing diplomatic priorities produced arrangements for provisional government and elections, leading to the restoration of civilian rule in 1924. Not every occupation-era institution disappeared. The security force's later role in Rafael Trujillo's rise demonstrated a consequence that the chapter can identify without retelling subsequent dictatorship. Comparing the Dominican and Haitian occupations reveals a shared capacity for foreign control but differing political histories, institutional structures, local responses, and timetables for ending formal military administration.

Section 70.16

Resistance, Diplomacy, and the Defense of Sovereignty in Central America and the Caribbean

Responses varied. Panama's leaders, bound to the canal economy, negotiated within unequal terms and sought treaty revision. Nicaragua was divided between Liberal and Conservative factions that sought U.S. support or resisted it; Augusto Sandino's later war lies beyond this chapter. Cuban politicians used Platt Amendment crises for partisan advantage while protesting the amendment. Haiti and the Dominican Republic combined armed rebellion with press campaigns and international appeals.

The responses of Caribbean and Central American societies cannot be classified simply as submission or resistance. Some political leaders sought American assistance to defeat rivals, obtain credit, or secure recognition; others tried to negotiate narrower concessions while avoiding armed confrontation. Nationalists objected to the loss of effective sovereignty, while commercial groups might support arrangements that stabilized port revenues or increased trade. Such positions sometimes changed within a single career as governments rose and fell. Historians therefore need locally produced newspapers, legislative debates, party materials, and court records as well as American diplomatic archives.

Panamanian criticism of the canal treaty emerged alongside dependence on the new canal economy. Questions involving police authority, workers, shops, sanitation, housing, and access across the Zone offered practical tests of the relationship. Dominican civic organizations demanded restoration of constitutional government, and Haitian resistance combined rural fighting with legal and editorial argument. In Nicaragua, liberals and conservatives had sharply different attitudes toward American involvement, though neither camp spoke for every Nicaraguan. Cuba's elected politicians balanced the advantages of U.S. recognition and trade with resentment toward continuing intervention powers. What sovereignty meant was constantly negotiated through ordinary administrative decisions.

Costa Rica and El Salvador filed suits before the Central American Court of Justice, established in 1907, arguing that the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty violated their rights; the court ruled in their favor on key points, but Nicaragua and the United States did not accept the judgment, and the court lapsed in 1918. Writers such as José Enrique Rodó, Rubén Darío, and José Vasconcelos developed cultural criticism of U.S. materialism, while later nationalists expanded these themes.

The Central American Court of Justice demonstrated another strategy. Established by the 1907 agreements among Central American republics, the court offered legal proceedings instead of military retaliation. Costa Rica and El Salvador challenged rights granted under the Bryan–Chamorro Treaty, arguing that a foreign naval base and canal option infringed regional interests. The tribunal delivered judgments favorable to their objections, but governments did not fully comply and the institution ceased operation in 1918. Its limited enforcement does not render its history irrelevant: it represented a serious attempt by relatively small states to turn international law into an instrument of collective defense.

Intellectuals also interpreted the new hierarchy in cultural terms. José Enrique Rodó's Ariel, published in 1900, contrasted ideals of education and humanistic culture with a caricature of utilitarian materialism, and it was widely read amid anxiety about U.S. ascendancy. Rubén Darío's poetry and commentary criticized intervention and unequal power while participating in transatlantic literary networks. Their writings should not be treated as the single opinion of Latin America; they were interventions in a much broader conversation among liberals, nationalists, socialists, Catholics, and regional reformers. Political agency included the production of ideas, regional institutions, and legal doctrines as well as military force.

Section 70.17

Pan-Americanism and the Origins of Inter-American Institutions, 1889–1910

The First International Conference of American States met in Washington from October 1889 to April 1890 under Secretary of State James G. Blaine, who sought trade and arbitration. Delegates from most independent states debated a customs union, arbitration, and transport. A customs union and a general arbitration plan were not adopted, but the conference created the International Union of American Republics and a commercial bureau in Washington. Later conferences, including those in Mexico City (1901–02), Rio de Janeiro (1906), and Buenos Aires (1910), enlarged the agenda. In 1910 the commercial bureau became the Pan American Union. The Pan American Sanitary Bureau, founded in 1902, linked public-health work across states. The Organization of American States did not exist until 1948.

Pan-American cooperation developed through successive conferences rather than a single founding act of the later Organization of American States. James G. Blaine and other U.S. officials saw commercial and diplomatic benefits in bringing the republics together. The first conference, held in Washington from October 1889 to April 1890, discussed customs classification, railway and steamship communication, information exchange, arbitration, and the improvement of trade. Governments sent delegates with their own agendas and reservations, and not all proposals became binding agreements. The conference's creation of a bureau for commercial information gave the movement an institutional continuity that earlier gatherings had generally lacked.

The original Commercial Bureau of the American Republics evolved as conferences continued in Mexico City, Rio de Janeiro, and Buenos Aires. By 1910 the institution was known as the Pan American Union, housed in Washington and tasked with distributing information, supporting conferences, and facilitating contact among governments. The institution did not possess authority to overrule the national legislatures or to command military forces. Its importance lay in providing a regular forum for diplomacy and technical work at a time when existing international law offered smaller states few means to constrain stronger neighbors.

Latin American participants valued forums for arbitration and trade, but many, including Argentine delegates, resisted U.S. leadership and a customs union that might favor U.S. exporters.

Latin American reactions were mixed because the same Washington-centered process could promote useful cooperation and raise fears of dominance. Delegates might support more reliable shipping connections or common sanitary standards while rejecting proposals that appeared to threaten tariff autonomy or constitutional authority. The controversy surrounding broader customs arrangements demonstrated that the republics' economic interests were not identical. Some participants wanted neutral arbitration to resolve disputes; others questioned whether cooperation could be effective while the United States reserved a separate right of intervention under the Roosevelt Corollary.

Public health offers a concrete example of cooperation with durable results. The Pan American Sanitary Bureau, established in 1902, organized exchange concerning epidemic disease, maritime quarantine, and public health. Doctors, port officials, and national ministries could pursue practical common objectives despite disagreements over diplomacy. At the same time, unequal financial resources and the strategic priority placed on major ports influenced the direction of the work. The chapter must distinguish the Pan American Union of 1910 from the Organization of American States founded in 1948; the former was a predecessor, not the later organization under an earlier name.

Historical Vocabulary

Pan-Americanism, Arbitration, and Nonintervention

Pan-Americanism was the idea of cooperation among the American republics; in practice it meant conferences and institutions in which U.S. and Latin American aims overlapped and clashed. Arbitration is settlement of disputes by a neutral decision. Nonintervention is the principle that states may not interfere in one another's internal affairs.

Section 70.18

Latin American Diplomatic Responses: Sovereignty, Arbitration, and International Law

The Argentine jurist Carlos Calvo had argued in the nineteenth century that foreigners should seek redress in local courts rather than through their governments' diplomatic or armed protection. The Drago note extended that logic to public debt. At the Hague conferences of 1899 and 1907, Latin American delegations, such as Brazil's Ruy Barbosa, defended the legal equality of states. Mexico, Argentina, Brazil, and Chile followed distinct policies, and Central American states experimented with their own court and treaties.

The claim that states were formally equal under international law acquired particular force when economic or military capacities were unequal. The Calvo Doctrine, associated with the nineteenth-century Argentine jurist Carlos Calvo, argued that foreigners investing or residing abroad should ordinarily use local legal remedies rather than demand intervention by their governments. It was not an assertion that foreigners had no rights; instead, it rejected a special diplomatic privilege that might transform an individual's commercial dispute into an excuse for coercion. The Drago Doctrine addressed a narrower problem, the use of armed intervention to recover public debt. Their differences help explain the variety of legal defenses constructed by Latin American intellectuals.

Arbitration promised an alternative to gunboat demands, but an arbitral decision was not the same as an enforceable guarantee of independence. Governments disagreed over when arbitration should be compulsory, how judges should be appointed, and whether stronger states would comply with unfavorable judgments. Delegates at the Hague peace conferences and Pan-American meetings debated these questions. The 1907 convention concerning contractual debt required recourse to arbitration in certain circumstances but preserved exceptions. Legal norms thus emerged through bargaining and partial compromise, not through a sudden consensus that force had become illegitimate.

In 1914 Argentina, Brazil, and Chile (the ABC powers) offered mediation in the Mexican crisis after the U.S. occupation of Veracruz, meeting at Niagara Falls, Ontario. The initiative showed regional diplomacy rather than a unified front.

Latin American states were not equally situated. Argentina, Brazil, and Chile possessed larger fiscal and military resources than many Caribbean republics and often enjoyed greater diplomatic freedom. Brazil sought wider international recognition and participated in the Hague conference; Argentina defended sovereign financial independence through Drago's note; Chile's diplomatic calculations reflected Pacific security and relations with neighbors. Mexico during its revolutionary turmoil faced direct American pressure. Smaller Central American republics attempted regional adjudication and criticized treaty concessions that could affect their own coasts. The diversity of positions prevents treating Latin America as a single diplomatic actor.

The mediation by Argentina, Brazil, and Chile during the 1914 Mexican crisis demonstrates an attempt to reduce confrontation between Washington and a revolutionary neighboring state. The ABC governments assembled at Niagara Falls and pursued negotiations, but they could not settle all Mexican questions or replace the agency of Mexican political factions. Mediation nevertheless signaled that regional powers wished to participate in managing hemispheric crises rather than leave decisions entirely to the United States. Across the period, diplomacy became a language through which Latin Americans defended political space: treaty interpretation, arbitration, formal protest, and intellectual critique were practical instruments of sovereignty.

Monroe Doctrine, Corollary, and Sovereignty

The small states of the Caribbean basin. Who experienced more continuity? Larger South American states, less exposed to intervention. Section 70.19

Woodrow Wilson, the First World War, and the Limits of American Hemispheric Leadership

Woodrow Wilson proclaimed a policy of respect for self-government and spoke of constitutional order, yet he authorized occupations of Haiti and the Dominican Republic and the occupation of Veracruz in April 1914. In 1916–1917 a Punitive Expedition under John J. Pershing entered northern Mexico after Pancho Villa's raid on Columbus, New Mexico. Chapters 71–74 treat the revolution itself.

Woodrow Wilson entered office criticizing the assumptions of dollar diplomacy and offering a rhetoric of constitutional government and moral responsibility. Yet American military operations continued, revealing the distance between declared principles and policy under pressure. Wilson's administration intervened in Mexican affairs during the revolution, occupying Veracruz in 1914 and sending a punitive expedition into northern Mexico in 1916–1917. He also presided over the occupations of Haiti and the Dominican Republic. These actions differed in causes, legal justifications, and duration, but together they made nonintervention a central topic of inter-American argument.

The Veracruz crisis arose from a naval incident and escalating disagreement with the government of Victoriano Huerta. U.S. forces occupied the port in April 1914, and casualties occurred among Mexican defenders and civilians. The intervention generated nationalist outrage beyond support for Huerta himself. Argentina, Brazil, and Chile attempted mediation at Niagara Falls, highlighting an alternative diplomatic role for Latin American powers. The later pursuit of Pancho Villa by U.S. troops brought another armed crossing of the frontier and extensive tensions with Carranza's government. The Mexican Revolution's domestic story should be reserved for the next volume, but these international episodes belong to this chapter's account of sovereignty.

Wartime responses differed. Brazil declared war on Germany in 1917, and Cuba, Panama, Guatemala, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Haiti, Honduras and others also declared war, mostly aligned with the United States. Argentina, Chile, Mexico, and others stayed neutral, and trade with both sides continued. The war disrupted European trade and capital flows, and shifted Latin American commerce and finance toward New York, reinforcing U.S. influence while also inspiring doubts about it.

World war changed the context. In 1914 many Latin American governments declared neutrality, and commercial disruption initially affected both exports and the availability of credit and imported manufactures. The entry of the United States in April 1917 increased pressure for diplomatic alignment, but regional responses remained divergent. Cuba and Panama declared war in April 1917; Brazil entered in October after German submarine attacks and diplomatic controversy. Guatemala, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Haiti, and Honduras declared war in 1918. Other states broke relations without formally entering the conflict, while Argentina, Chile, Mexico, Colombia, and several neighbors maintained neutrality. These distinctions should not be collapsed into a single statement that Latin America followed Washington.

The war strengthened the relative importance of U.S. finance and shipping while disrupting European connections, but effects varied by commodity and country. Brazil's wartime participation included maritime and supporting activities; most regional belligerents made diplomatic or logistical contributions rather than sending large armies to Europe. Neutral governments also made consequential choices concerning ports, trade, and foreign communities. After 1918 debates continued about the League of Nations, collective security, and the future of inter-American institutions. The chapter should end this section with the limits of Wilson's promise of self-determination: the language of sovereignty gained prominence internationally while direct occupations in the Caribbean still constrained several American republics.

Section 70.20

Historical Synthesis: The Panama Canal, American Power, and Latin American Sovereignty

The canal's opening on 15 August 1914 symbolized both international economic integration and unequal power. Between 1823 and 1914 U.S. policy had moved from opposing European colonization to claiming a supervisory role, aided by capital, naval strategy, treaties, and occupations. Panama's Canal Zone, Cuba's Platt Amendment, Puerto Rico's territorial status, Nicaragua's treaties and interventions, Haiti's long occupation, and the Dominican receivership and occupation illustrate different mechanisms: titular independence with divided jurisdiction, treaty tutelage, colonial incorporation without statehood, financial supervision, and direct military government.

The new hemispheric order was neither a single empire with identical institutions everywhere nor a collection of fully autonomous neighbors related only by commerce. Puerto Rico became an American territory, Cuba experienced formal independence under an intervention clause, Panama granted an exceptionally broad canal jurisdiction, and the Dominican Republic placed customs revenues under supervision before military occupation. Nicaragua faced a combination of political pressure, financial agreements, troops, and future canal privileges; Haiti underwent a long military occupation that reshaped its constitution and financial administration. Distinguishing these instruments is the starting point for comparing their political consequences.

The canal reveals the relationship among geography, power, and labor. The waters connecting the two oceans shortened routes for trade and warships, but the strategic value of passage depended on a territorial concession negotiated in the aftermath of Panamanian independence. Engineering success relied on organized workers, new sanitary systems, and the harsh division of Canal Zone society by employment category and race. The financial opportunities of the canal were real for many merchants and workers, while the denial of ordinary Panamanian governmental powers inside the Zone became a durable source of conflict. Economic infrastructure could not be separated from political authority.

Latin American agency appeared in negotiation, accommodation, litigation, writing, and armed struggle, and in doctrines of nonintervention that outlasted the occupations. The central tension remained: could sovereign states cooperate when one held overwhelming financial, naval, and diplomatic power? Volume IV takes up how revolutions and mass politics reshaped the answer.

Latin American governments and social groups helped shape the limits of intervention even when they could not prevent it. Colombian legislators asserted treaty ratification rights; Panamanians defended their new state while objecting to restrictions on it; Dominican and Haitian activists combined legal protest with political organization and, in some cases, armed resistance. Argentine jurists developed the Drago argument, Central American governments turned to regional courts, and ABC diplomats sought influence in Mexican mediation. These actions did not eliminate material inequality among states, but they ensured that the language of sovereignty and nonintervention remained contested rather than disappearing under American power.

A final comparison should separate immediate outcomes from longer trajectories. The 1914 canal opening and the wartime strengthening of New York finance suggested a durable U.S. strategic position, yet intervention could generate nationalist movements that later demanded treaty revision and withdrawal. Some occupation-created institutions survived military departure and contributed to internal authoritarian politics. Meanwhile Pan-American bodies offered genuine technical cooperation without resolving the balance-of-power problem. Volume III closes where Volume IV begins: national states had acquired stronger economies and wider diplomatic connections, but workers, reformers, nationalists, and revolutionary movements would challenge the distribution of power within countries and across the hemisphere.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of U.S. policy from the noncolonization principle of 1823 to a far more assertive hemispheric role by the 1910s. The war of 1898 removed Spain and gave the United States island bases, a protectorate-like relationship with Cuba, and a territorial relationship with Puerto Rico.

Panama's separation from Colombia in 1903 resulted from the interaction of Panamanian regional politics, Colombian weakness after civil war, and U.S. naval and diplomatic support. The Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty created a Canal Zone in which the United States exercised sovereign-like powers while Panama retained title, a divided arrangement that remained disputed.

Canal construction depended on engineering, sanitation, and the labor of West Indian workers organized in a racially stratified employment system. The canal opened in 1914 and reshaped shipping and strategy.

The Roosevelt Corollary of 1904 extended the Monroe Doctrine into a claim of police power. Customs receivership in the Dominican Republic and dollar diplomacy in Nicaragua used finance as a lever, followed by military occupation in Haiti and the Dominican Republic, each with its own legal basis and character.

Latin Americans responded through negotiation, litigation, nationalist publishing, armed resistance, and legal doctrines such as Calvo and Drago. Pan-American conferences produced institutions, notably the Pan American Union, but not equality of power. The First World War brought varied alignments and shifted finance toward New York.

The central lesson is that the new hemispheric order was built by distinct mechanisms operating unevenly, and that Latin American agency shaped its limits and its legal vocabulary. The questions of sovereignty and cooperation it raised continue into Volume IV.

Geography linked canal policy to a broader system of maritime strategy. A route across Panama offered speed and commercial opportunity, but American naval planners considered the surrounding passages, island stations, and port defenses essential. Canal construction therefore reshaped territorial diplomacy and helps explain why the Caribbean figured so prominently in official security calculations.

The legal history also shows that the recognition of sovereignty did not establish equal practical bargaining power. Colombia defended its constitutional ratification requirements; Panama acquired independence but ceded extraordinary Canal Zone jurisdiction; Cuba maintained republican institutions under a continuing intervention clause. These cases demonstrate different ways a state could be formally independent while unable to exercise unconstrained authority.

The human consequences were not incidental. West Indian canal builders and their families sustained one of the period’s principal engineering projects under segregated working arrangements. Haitians and Dominicans confronted restrictions on political participation, military coercion, and contested reform. Social and legal responses differed, requiring historians to study communities and local parties rather than using official U.S. explanations as complete accounts.

The emerging inter-American order also had cooperative possibilities. Scientific exchange, commercial information, diplomatic conferences, and arbitration were genuine innovations, even though the power to define and enforce regional rules remained unequal. Latin American governments defended sovereignty through legal doctrines, courts, mediation, and public protest. Their efforts did not end intervention, but helped establish principles that later generations would continue to invoke.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the twentieth-century relationship between Latin America and the United States was built through specific instruments, not a single act. Doctrine, treaty, loan, customs collector, and marine each carried distinct legal and political meanings, and each met distinct responses.

The canal links these themes: a global engineering achievement that depended on Caribbean labor, and a legal arrangement that divided sovereignty. Its memory shaped Panamanian nationalism for decades.

Volume IV turns to revolution and mass politics. The Mexican Revolution, nationalist movements, and later Cold War conflicts would reopen the questions of sovereignty, nonintervention, and economic independence raised here.
