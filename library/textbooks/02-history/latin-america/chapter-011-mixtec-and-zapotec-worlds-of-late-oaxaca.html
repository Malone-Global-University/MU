Mixtec and Zapotec Worlds of Late Oaxaca

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A World of Many Dynasties

Around 1084, a Mixtec lord named Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw established a new center of power at Tututepec on Oaxaca's Pacific coast. In 1097, the Toltec-affiliated lord Four Jaguar pierced Eight Deer's septum in a rite conferring the prestige title tecuhtli, incorporating him into a wider Tolteca-Chichimeca lineage tradition and helping legitimate his accession to rulership at Tilantongo the following year. Then, according to the traditional correlation, in Year 9 Reed (1099), Eight Deer undertook a further ritual journey across a watery boundary to a sacred solar realm remembered in the manuscripts as the House and Temple of the Sun, where he received a turquoise necklace strung with gold bell rattles and kindled a New Fire — a still later legitimating act layered atop his already-established rulership. His life, recorded across dozens of painted pages, was not the story of a single unified Mixtec nation. It was the story of one extraordinarily ambitious man operating within a densely fragmented, competitive, and interconnected political world that Chapter 7 left in the aftermath of Monte Albán's political collapse and that this chapter now reopens several centuries later, transformed considerably and yet still recognizably Oaxacan.

Chapter 7 traced the rise of Zapotec civilization at Monte Albán, a mountaintop city that was the Valley of Oaxaca's principal urban and political center for much of its long history. By the eighth century CE, Monte Albán's regional authority had begun to fragment, and by roughly 800 CE its central ruling institutions had collapsed and the city itself had sharply depopulated. It would be a serious mistake, however, to conclude that Oaxaca's history paused, declined into chaos, or waited passively for the arrival of the Mexica and, later, the Spanish. What followed Monte Albán's political fragmentation was one of the most historically well-documented, dynamically competitive, and culturally rich periods in all of ancient Mesoamerica — a world of dozens of dynastic states, painted historical books, ambitious rulers, marriage alliances, prolonged rivalries, migrations, and shifting coalitions that modern scholars generally divide, for convenience, into "Mixtec" and "Zapotec" worlds.

These two labels are useful shorthand, but students must resist the temptation to imagine them as two centralized nations facing off across a map, the way a modern atlas might depict two neighboring countries. The Mixteca — the highland, valley, and coastal regions where Mixtec-speaking peoples predominated — was never a single kingdom. It was a landscape of small, independent, hereditary states, called by later Spanish chroniclers cacicazgos, each ruled by its own lineage, each capable of allying with or warring against its neighbors, and each linked to others through an intricate web of royal marriages. The Zapotec-speaking world of the Central Valleys, the Sierra, and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec was organized in broadly similar ways, its own dynasties tracing descent from illustrious ancestors and asserting authority over particular valleys, hilltop centers, and market towns.

What makes this period unusually accessible to historians is a body of evidence unlike anything available for the Olmec, Zapotec of the Classic period, or many other Mesoamerican societies: a series of surviving painted books, made of deerskin or bark paper and folded into accordion-like screens, that preserve detailed Indigenous accounts of specific rulers, their births, their marriages, their wars, their alliances, and their deaths. These pictorial manuscripts, created by Mixtec scribes using a sophisticated system of place signs, calendar dates, and pictorial conventions, allow historians to reconstruct dynastic histories with a level of individual, political detail that is exceptionally rare for the pre-conquest Americas. The most celebrated of these figures, Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw, appears across many painted pages as a lord whose extraordinary political career — marked by ambition, warfare, strategic marriage, and eventual downfall — offers students a rare window into how power was actually built, contested, and lost in Postclassic Oaxaca.

This chapter also asks students to reconsider the ruins of Monte Albán itself. Political decline did not mean abandonment. Archaeological evidence shows that later Oaxacan peoples continued to visit the ancient hilltop city, to leave offerings among its ruined temples, and, in at least one especially famous case, to reuse one of its ancient tombs for new burials centuries after the tomb was first built. That tomb, known to archaeologists as Tomb 7, has become one of the most debated single archaeological findings in all of Mesoamerican studies, and it offers this chapter an ideal opportunity to examine how historians interpret contested physical evidence.

Finally, this chapter follows Oaxacan history outward from the highlands to the Pacific coast, where the conquest state of Tututepec built one of the most substantial Late Postclassic territorial powers in Mesoamerica, and southeast to the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, where Zapotec-speaking communities migrated, settled, and built new centers such as Guiengola among Chontal, Mixe, Zoque, and Huave neighbors who already inhabited that landscape. By the close of this chapter, students should understand Postclassic Oaxaca not as a simple two-sided rivalry between "the Mixtecs" and "the Zapotecs," but as a plural, competitive, and deeply interconnected political world with its own distinctive internal complexity.

This world remained politically active, plural, and dynamically interconnected, still expanding and contracting through war and alliance, when the first Spaniards entered Mesoamerica in the early sixteenth century. Understanding Oaxaca on its own terms, before conquest reshaped it permanently, is essential preparation for the chapters on conquest and colonization that follow in Volume II.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Monte Albán's political decline did not mean the disappearance or discontinuity of Zapotec civilization.
  2. Describe the major regions of Postclassic Oaxaca, including the Mixteca Alta, Mixteca Baja, Mixteca de la Costa, the Valley of Oaxaca, and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec.
  3. Analyze how hereditary dynasties, marriage alliances, and factional coalitions organized political life in the absence of a single centralized state.
  4. Interpret Mixtec pictorial manuscripts as historical sources, distinguishing genealogy, ritual legitimation, and political narrative from myth and cosmology.
  5. Trace the political career of Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw as an illustration of how power was assembled through ritual legitimacy, marriage, alliance, and warfare.
  6. Evaluate the archaeological and historical evidence for Tututepec as a substantial Late Postclassic coastal conquest state, without treating it as a unified "Mixtec Empire."
  7. Compare Zapotec political centers such as Zaachila, Mitla, Yagul, and Guiengola with Mixtec highland and coastal polities.
  8. Assess the significance of continued ritual activity, offerings, and tomb reuse at Monte Albán after its political decline.
  9. Describe craft production, market exchange, and household economies in Postclassic Oaxaca, including metalwork, textiles, and polychrome ceramics.
  10. Explain Zapotec migration and settlement in the Isthmus of Tehuantepec as a process of interaction with existing Chontal, Mixe, Zoque, and Huave communities.
  11. Evaluate why "Mixtec versus Zapotec" is frequently the wrong framework for understanding specific historical conflicts and alliances.
  12. Assess the strengths and limitations of pictorial manuscripts, archaeology, and later colonial-era testimony as evidence for reconstructing Postclassic Oaxacan history.

Historical Context

Chapter 7 examined the rise of Monte Albán as the political, religious, and urban center of the Valley of Oaxaca for well over a millennium, from its Formative-period founding through its Classic-period height as one of the largest and most enduring cities in ancient Mesoamerica. By the Late Classic period, roughly the eighth and ninth centuries CE, Monte Albán's authority over the Valley of Oaxaca had substantially diminished, part of a broader pattern of political transformation affecting many Classic Mesoamerican centers discussed elsewhere in this volume. This chapter takes up Oaxacan history from that point forward, tracing developments through the Postclassic period and down to the eve of Spanish contact in the early sixteenth century.

Two broad linguistic and cultural traditions dominate this chapter: Mixtec-speaking peoples of the highland Mixteca Alta, the lower Mixteca Baja, and the Mixteca de la Costa along the Pacific; and Zapotec-speaking peoples concentrated in the Valley of Oaxaca, the Sierra regions to the north and south, and, increasingly during this period, the Isthmus of Tehuantepec to the southeast. These were not the only peoples of the region — Chontal, Mixe, Zoque, Huave, Chatino, and other communities inhabited parts of Oaxaca and its coastal and isthmian margins — but Mixtec and Zapotec dynastic politics receive the most sustained documentary attention because of the survival of Mixtec pictorial manuscripts and the density of Zapotec archaeological evidence.

Students should understand from the outset that "Mixtec" and "Zapotec" describe language families and broad cultural traditions, not centralized political states. Political organization throughout this period rested on the hereditary rule of individual city-states, known in the colonial-era Spanish sources as cacicazgos, each governed by a ruling lineage that claimed descent from illustrious, sometimes semi-legendary, founding ancestors. These small states numbered in the dozens across the Mixteca alone, ranging from modest highland towns to substantial regional powers, and they related to one another through a constantly shifting combination of marriage, tribute, alliance, and warfare rather than through any single overarching government.

The historical record for this period is unusually rich because Mixtec scribes produced sophisticated pictorial manuscripts recording the genealogies, marriages, wars, and political careers of specific named rulers, extending back, in some cases, to the tenth century CE. These manuscripts must be read carefully and are not simple illustrated storybooks; they combine calendrical dates, place signs, genealogical registers, and narrative scenes according to conventions that modern scholars have only gradually learned to interpret, increasingly informed by Mixtec-language scholarship and Indigenous cultural knowledge. Archaeological evidence — including continued activity at Monte Albán, excavations at coastal Tututepec, and recent lidar mapping at the Isthmian city of Guiengola — provides an essential complement to, and occasional corrective for, the pictorial and later colonial-era textual record.

Section 11.1

Oaxaca after Monte Albán: Fragmentation without Disappearance

The fragmentation of Monte Albán's regional state, which began with decentralization in outlying communities around 700 CE and culminated in the collapse of its central ruling institutions and the sharp depopulation of the city itself by roughly 800 CE, did not produce an empty stage awaiting new actors. Zapotec-speaking communities throughout the Valley of Oaxaca continued to live, farm, trade, worship, and organize politically, but authority that had once been concentrated in a single mountaintop capital dispersed among a growing number of smaller centers, each governed by its own ruling lineage. This process resembles political transformations examined elsewhere in this volume — the end of Maya "big kingship" at some Classic centers, discussed in Chapter 9, or the reorganization of central Mexico after the decline of Teotihuacan, discussed in Chapter 10 — in that political decline at one dominant center opened space for a more plural political landscape rather than producing a historical vacuum.

Archaeological evidence indicates that Monte Albán itself remained an important place even after it ceased to function as a unified state capital. Later communities continued to visit its ruined temples and plazas, leaving offerings among structures that had, by then, stood for centuries. This continued ritual activity is central to understanding how later Oaxacan peoples related to their own deep past, a theme this chapter returns to in Section 11.8.

Students should understand "fragmentation" in this chapter as a description of political structure, not cultural decline. The centuries following Monte Albán's political weakening witnessed vigorous artistic production, sophisticated historical recordkeeping, ambitious architectural projects at multiple centers, extensive long-distance trade, and some of the most successful individual political careers in all of Mesoamerican history. Oaxaca's Postclassic world was busy, competitive, and creative — not a quiet coda to an earlier golden age.

Section 11.2

The Political Geography of Late Oaxaca

Understanding Postclassic Oaxaca requires a clear sense of its internal geographic diversity. The Mixteca Alta, a rugged highland zone of narrow valleys and eroded mountainsides, hosted numerous small but historically prominent dynastic centers, including Tilantongo, whose ruling lineage figures prominently in the pictorial manuscripts examined later in this chapter. The Mixteca Baja, at somewhat lower elevation to the northwest, and the Mixteca de la Costa, along the Pacific coastal plain to the south, each developed their own political centers and, in the case of the coast, eventually gave rise to the powerful conquest state of Tututepec.

The Valley of Oaxaca, home to Monte Albán and, later, to Zapotec centers such as Zaachila, Mitla, and Yagul, remained the demographic and political heartland of the Zapotec-speaking world. To the north and south, mountainous Sierra regions hosted smaller Zapotec, Mixe, and other communities whose histories are less thoroughly documented in surviving pictorial sources but whose participation in regional trade and political networks was nonetheless significant. To the southeast, the Isthmus of Tehuantepec — a lowland corridor connecting the Gulf of Mexico and the Pacific — was home to Chontal, Mixe, Zoque, and Huave communities before and during the period of Zapotec expansion examined in Section 11.10.

This geographic diversity matters historically, though it should not be read as a simple determinist explanation for Oaxaca's political fragmentation. Oaxaca's rugged terrain raised the costs of transportation and communication and helped shape strongly regional political networks, but geography did not by itself fix the scale of political organization achievable in the region — as the case of Tututepec, examined in Section 11.6, demonstrates. What geography did shape was a strong tendency toward regionally rooted political networks, within which both small dynastic states and, at times, more substantial territorial powers could develop.

Section 11.3

Dynasties, Marriage, Alliance, and the Small-State System

Political authority throughout Postclassic Oaxaca rested overwhelmingly on hereditary rulership. In Mixtec terms, the basic political unit was the ñuu, a local polity that could be ruled by a man or a woman in its own right; rulership was not automatically restricted to joint male-female pairs, though marriage between the ruling lines of two ñuu could, when it occurred, temporarily unite those polities in an alliance known as a yuhuitayu. Succession, marriage, and genealogy were not merely personal or domestic matters; they were the primary instruments of interstate politics. A marriage between the ruling houses of two dynastic centers could cement an alliance, combine claims to territory, or set the stage for future succession disputes among the children of that union. The colonial-era Spanish term cacicazgo, discussed further in this chapter's vocabulary feature, was applied retrospectively to these ñuu and their ruling lineages and should not be mistaken for an Indigenous political category.

Because no single Oaxacan polity possessed the resources to administer the entire region directly, prominent centers such as Tilantongo exercised influence through networks of allied and subordinate lineages rather than through direct territorial control. A dominant lord might not personally govern every allied town; instead, he might secure the loyalty, military support, or ritual deference of allied rulers through marriage ties, shared ancestry claims, tribute arrangements, or successful demonstrations of military and ritual power. This system allowed considerable flexibility — alliances could be renegotiated, marriages arranged and broken, and loyalties shifted — but it also meant that even the most successful rulers, as Eight Deer's career demonstrates, built power that remained vulnerable to rival coalitions.

Factional competition was a normal, expected feature of this system rather than a sign of political breakdown. Rival branches of the same royal lineage frequently competed for succession, and defeated factions did not necessarily disappear; they might regroup, seek alliance with a rival center, or wait for a future opportunity. Understanding this factional, alliance-based political culture is essential preparation for the individual political career examined in Sections 11.4 and 11.5.

Section 11.4

Painted Books as Indigenous Political History

The single most distinctive body of evidence for Postclassic Oaxacan history is a small corpus of surviving Mixtec pictorial manuscripts, commonly called codices, made of deerskin or bark paper coated with a fine plaster surface and painted with mineral and organic pigments. These books were folded accordion-style into long screenfolds that could be read continuously across many painted pages. Rather than alphabetic writing, Mixtec scribes recorded information through a structured system of place signs identifying specific locations, calendrical day-signs and numerical coefficients marking dates according to the Mesoamerican 260-day and 365-day calendar systems, personal name glyphs identifying individual rulers, and pictorial scenes depicting births, marriages, wars, sacrifices, and ritual events.

These manuscripts should not be read as neutral, objective chronicles in the modern sense, nor should they be dismissed as mere myth or decoration. They were commissioned and maintained by ruling lineages, and their content reflects the political interests of the dynasties that produced and preserved them — emphasizing legitimate descent, favorable alliances, military success, and ritual sanction, much as royal chronicles and dynastic histories elsewhere in the world often served the political interests of the ruling houses that sponsored them. At the same time, the level of specific, checkable detail these manuscripts contain — named individuals, calendar dates, marriages, and campaigns that can sometimes be cross-referenced across multiple surviving codices — provides historians with a genealogical and political record of a precision rarely available for other pre-conquest Mesoamerican societies.

Modern interpretation of these manuscripts has advanced considerably through sustained collaboration between art historians, archaeologists, and specialists in Mixtec (Ñuu Savi) language and cultural knowledge, whose understanding of place names, ritual practice, and narrative convention has substantially refined earlier, more speculative readings. Students encountering these manuscripts should learn to distinguish several layers of content within a single page or sequence: genealogical registers establishing descent; ritual scenes establishing legitimacy through connection to sacred places, ancestors, or deities; and narrative sequences recording specific political and military events. Confusing these layers — for example, treating a ritual legitimation scene as a literal historical journey without recognizing its ceremonial function — risks serious misinterpretation.

Historical Vocabulary
Codex / Pictorial Manuscript

A codex (plural codices) in the Mesoamerican context refers to a painted manuscript, typically a folded screenfold of deerskin or bark paper, recording historical, genealogical, ritual, or calendrical information through pictorial and glyphic conventions rather than alphabetic writing. Surviving Mixtec codices, including the Tonindeye discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, are among the most important sources for Postclassic Oaxacan political history.

Students should distinguish pre-conquest codices, produced within Indigenous scribal traditions before sustained European contact, from colonial-era pictorial manuscripts produced after conquest, which often blend Indigenous pictorial conventions with European artistic influence, alphabetic annotation, and, in some cases, content shaped by colonial administrative needs, a theme developed further in Volume II.

Section 11.5

Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw and the Politics of Ambition

No individual life is recorded in greater pictorial detail in Postclassic Oaxacan sources than that of Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw, whose personal name combines his calendrical birth-date name (Eight Deer) with a secondary name (Jaguar-Claw) associated with his political and martial identity. According to the traditional correlation used by most specialists, Eight Deer was born in the mid-eleventh century CE, a son of a Tilantongo lord, though not initially positioned as heir to Tilantongo's principal throne. His political career, spanning several decades, illustrates how ambitious lords in Postclassic Oaxaca could accumulate power that exceeded their formal hereditary position through a combination of ritual legitimation, strategic marriage, military conquest, and shifting alliance.

The traditional correlation used by most specialists places Eight Deer's establishment of a new center of power at Tututepec on the Pacific coast around 1083–1084, an early and decisive step in extending his influence well beyond the Mixteca Alta highlands of his birth. In 1097, the Toltec-affiliated lord Four Jaguar performed a nose-piercing investiture on Eight Deer, conferring the prestige title tecuhtli and incorporating him into a wider Tolteca-Chichimeca lineage tradition — a ceremony that helped legitimate his accession to rulership at Tilantongo the following year, in 1098, at a point when his position within his own lineage was not automatically dominant and required this kind of external ritual sanction.

A further, separate legitimating episode followed rather than preceded these events. According to the traditional correlation, in Year 9 Reed (1099) Eight Deer undertook a ritual journey, recorded at plates of the Tonindeye Codex, across a watery boundary to a place called, in translation, the House and Temple of the Sun. There he received a turquoise necklace strung with gold bell rattles and kindled a New Fire — ritual acts linking his already-established authority to cosmological renewal and sacred, ancestral sanction. This episode should not be read as a literal travel itinerary in the modern sense; it functions within the manuscript as a legitimation narrative layered atop rulership Eight Deer had already secured through his tecuhtli investiture and Tilantongo accession, rather than as the initial event that made his rise possible. Students should take particular care not to reverse this order: the House-of-the-Sun journey reinforced and elaborated Eight Deer's authority after Tilantongo, rather than launching his career.

Eight Deer's subsequent career combined marriage alliances with multiple politically significant women, ongoing military campaigns against rival lords, and the continued extension of his coastal and highland influence. His success generated rivals as well as allies; Postclassic Oaxacan politics rewarded ambition but rarely tolerated unchecked dominance for long. According to the traditional chronology, Eight Deer was captured and sacrificed by rival lords in 1115; the precise political coalition and mechanism behind his downfall are less securely established than the fact of his capture and death, and this chapter avoids presenting a single settled reconstruction of exactly who organized it. His downfall was recorded by later Oaxacan political memory with the same careful attention as his rise, since the same manuscripts that celebrated his ambition also recorded the consequences of the enmities it created.

Eight Deer's story offers students the clearest available case study in how Postclassic Oaxacan power actually functioned: not through inherited administrative office alone, but through an ongoing, high-stakes combination of ritual sanction, marriage diplomacy, military success, and the management, or mismanagement, of rival factions.

Section 11.6

Tututepec and the Making of a Coastal Power

Among the many dynastic states of Postclassic Oaxaca, Tututepec, known in Mixtec as Yucu Dzaa, stands out as an unusually successful and substantial territorial power. Located on the Pacific coastal plain in the Mixteca de la Costa, Tututepec's establishment as a new center of power is traditionally dated to around 1083–1084 and directly connected to Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw, whose broader career is examined in Section 11.5; archaeological and ethnohistorical research confirms its emergence as a major regional power early in the Late Postclassic period. Over subsequent generations, Tututepec's rulers extended their authority over a large, ethnically diverse coastal and near-coastal territory, incorporating Mixtec, Chatino, Amuzgo, and other communities under a shared political structure.

Tututepec's rise illustrates an important historical point: substantial territorial states could and did emerge within the fragmented Postclassic Oaxacan system without unifying that system as a whole. Tututepec was a genuinely large and militarily formidable polity by the standards of the region, capable of resisting later Mexica pressure more successfully than many highland Mixtec centers, but it remained one expansionist Mixtec-associated state among several competing powers rather than the seat of a single "Mixtec Empire." Students should firmly resist any characterization of Tututepec as ruling over all Mixtec-speaking peoples; highland centers such as Tilantongo, and numerous smaller Mixteca Alta and Baja polities, retained their own independent rulers throughout this period, regardless of Tututepec's coastal dominance.

Archaeological excavation at Tututepec has also illuminated the internal social structure of a large Postclassic Oaxacan state, revealing elite residences, evidence of tribute collection, and, as discussed further in Section 11.9, household-level craft production and market participation that persisted even under centralized coastal rule. Tututepec's history thus provides this chapter with its clearest example of successful large-scale state-building in the Mixtec world, while its limits — geographic, ethnic, and political — equally illustrate why no single Postclassic Oaxacan polity ever became a unified regional empire.

Section 11.7

Zapotec Political Worlds: Zaachila, Mitla, Yagul, and the Central Valleys

Zapotec political history did not end with Monte Albán's decline; it reorganized around new centers within and beyond the Valley of Oaxaca. Zaachila emerged as an important Postclassic Zapotec dynastic seat, associated with elite tombs and continued royal lineage tradition. Mitla, renowned for its elaborate stone mosaic architecture, functioned as a major religious and political center closely associated with elite burial and ancestor veneration, while Yagul combined defensible hilltop settlement with palace architecture reflecting continued lineage authority.

These centers should be understood, like their Mixtec counterparts, as independent or semi-independent dynastic states rather than provinces of a single unified Zapotec kingdom. Marriage alliances, tribute relationships, and periodic warfare connected Zapotec centers to one another and, importantly, to neighboring Mixtec polities; Zapotec and Mixtec ruling houses intermarried on numerous documented occasions, complicating any attempt to treat "Zapotec" and "Mixtec" as sealed, mutually exclusive political or even ethnic categories. A Zapotec lord might have a Mixtec mother, wife, or ally, and a Mixtec dynasty might claim descent connections to a Zapotec lineage, or vice versa.

This section should be read alongside Section 11.3: the same small-state, alliance-based political culture that shaped Mixtec highland politics operated equally among Zapotec centers, even though the specific dynastic names, sacred places, and calendrical traditions differed in detail between the two linguistic traditions.

Section 11.8

Monte Albán after Monte Albán: Ruins, Tombs, and Ancestral Authority

Perhaps no aspect of Postclassic Oaxacan history connects more directly back to Chapter 7 than the continued significance of Monte Albán after its political decline. Archaeological research has documented Postclassic-period offerings deposited among the ruined temples and plazas of the former Zapotec capital, indicating that later communities continued to regard the site as sacred even though it no longer functioned as a unified political capital. This pattern — the persistence of ritual meaning at a politically diminished center — recurs across Mesoamerican history and should not be treated as unique to Oaxaca; it resembles, for instance, later Mexica veneration of earlier ruined centers such as Teotihuacan, discussed in Chapter 10.

The clearest and most famous example of this continued engagement with Monte Albán's past is Tomb 7, an elite burial chamber constructed as early as the Terminal Formative period and later reentered during the Postclassic period for continued or renewed use in ancestor-veneration activity, resulting in a complex mortuary and ritual assemblage involving multiple individuals and hundreds of precious offerings, including finely worked gold, silver, turquoise, jade, and carved bone. Long characterized in earlier scholarship simply as "a Mixtec tomb," Tomb 7's precise depositional sequence, the affiliation of its later users, and the interpretation of its assemblage have become the subject of extensive and continuing scholarly debate, examined in detail in this chapter's Evidence and Interpretation feature below.

Students should draw two conclusions from this material. First, Monte Albán's political decline did not extinguish its religious and symbolic importance; later Oaxacan elites, whether identified as Zapotec, Mixtec, or some combination reflecting intermarriage and shared regional culture, could deliberately connect themselves to Monte Albán's ancient sacred authority through offerings and burial practice. Second, archaeological style — the artistic and material characteristics of objects found at a site — cannot automatically be equated with the ethnic or linguistic identity of the people who used them, a caution that applies directly to the debate over Tomb 7.

Evidence and Interpretation

Monte Albán's Tomb 7: Whose Tomb, and What Does It Mean?

The evidence: Tomb 7 at Monte Albán, first constructed as early as the Terminal Formative period and excavated by archaeologists in the early twentieth century, was reentered during the Postclassic period and used for what current scholarship increasingly describes as a complex, multi-episode mortuary and ritual assemblage, involving multiple individuals and hundreds of precious offerings, including gold ornaments, carved jade and turquoise mosaic work, and finely worked bone. The artistic style of many objects shows strong affinities with Mixtec pictorial and material traditions, leading early excavators to describe the tomb simply as "the Mixtec tomb" at a Zapotec site — a label current scholarship treats with considerable caution. The three dimensions below address different questions — who used the tomb, how its contents accumulated over time, and what ritual purpose its reuse served — and should be read as complementary rather than as mutually exclusive rival theories.

Interpretive Dimension A — Who used the tomb: later Mixtec-affiliated elites: One important question concerns the identity of those who reentered and used Tomb 7. On one reading, its later use reflects Mixtec elites, or a Mixtec-affiliated dynasty, deliberately appropriating the ancient prestige of Monte Albán by reopening and reusing one of its most important tombs, associating their own authority with the site's deep Zapotec past.
Interpretive Dimension B — How deposition occurred: continued or episodic mortuary use over time: A second, largely compatible question concerns process rather than identity: whether Tomb 7 was the product of a single reopening and reburial event or of ongoing, repeated mortuary activity across an extended period, in which the tomb functioned as an active, evolving assemblage built up through multiple episodes of interment and offering-deposition.
Interpretive Dimension C — What ritual purpose reuse served: ancestor veneration within a sacred landscape: A third question concerns meaning: the tomb's later use can also be understood as an act of ancestor veneration connecting later elites, whatever their specific linguistic or dynastic affiliation, to the sacred authority of Monte Albán's deep past — a ritual function that could operate regardless of exactly who used the tomb or how many episodes of use occurred.

Why it matters: The Tomb 7 evidence illustrates a broader methodological caution central to this chapter: archaeological style cannot be read as a direct, unambiguous marker of ethnic identity, and later reuse of an ancient sacred space can carry political and religious meaning independent of, and considerably more complex than, a simple story of one group reclaiming another group's monument. Construction in the Terminal Formative period and later Postclassic reuse are reasonably well established; what remains genuinely debated is the precise depositional sequence, the affiliation of the individuals involved, and how to interpret the assemblage as a whole.

Student task: Using the evidence and cautions described in this chapter, explain why historians resist declaring Tomb 7 definitively "Mixtec" or "Zapotec," and identify what kind of additional evidence might help clarify the depositional sequence and affiliation of its users.

Section 11.9

Craftsmanship, Markets, and Household Economies

Postclassic Oaxaca supported highly skilled craft production across multiple media. Metalworkers produced gold and silver ornaments through sophisticated casting techniques, exemplified by objects recovered from Tomb 7. Lapidaries worked turquoise, jade, and shell into ornaments and mosaic inlay. Textile production, painted ceramics, featherwork, and the manufacture of the painted books discussed in Section 11.4 all required specialized skill and, in many cases, long apprenticeship within particular communities or lineages.

Archaeological research at Tututepec has shown that craft production and market exchange were not confined to palace workshops serving only the ruling elite. Household-level excavations reveal that commoner households retained meaningful control over certain kinds of craft production and obtained valued goods, including copper objects and painted polychrome pottery, through participation in markets even under the authority of a powerful centralized coastal state. This evidence complicates any assumption that Postclassic Oaxacan economies were rigidly and exclusively controlled from the top down; commoners were market participants and, in some cases, producers of goods with wider circulation.

Polychrome ceramics decorated with imagery closely related to the pictorial conventions of the painted codices demonstrate that elite ideological and religious imagery was not confined to manuscripts housed within royal palaces; such imagery circulated on everyday household objects as well, suggesting that visual literacy in this symbolic system extended, to some degree, beyond a narrow ruling class. Long-distance exchange connected Oaxaca to obsidian sources, cacao-producing regions, and coastal shell resources, situating even modest highland households within networks of exchange stretching well beyond the immediate region.

Section 11.10

Migration, Zaachila, and the Zapotec Isthmus

Later historical tradition describes a movement of Zapotec-speaking populations from the Central Valleys southeast into the Isthmus of Tehuantepec during the Postclassic period. This movement should be understood as migration, alliance, and, at times, conquest into an already inhabited and culturally diverse landscape, not as settlement of empty territory. Chontal, Mixe, Zoque, and Huave communities had long inhabited the Isthmus, and Zapotec expansion into the region involved complex interaction — including conflict, negotiation, intermarriage, and the establishment of new political centers — with these existing populations.

Recent airborne lidar survey at the Isthmian site of Guiengola has substantially advanced understanding of this process, revealing a large, carefully planned Late Postclassic city with palace compounds, administrative architecture, elite residential zones, and evidence of a structured noble hierarchy. This mapping complicates older, simpler narratives that portrayed Isthmian Zapotec settlement as a modest frontier outpost; Guiengola appears instead to have functioned as a substantial capital in its own right, closely associated with the Zapotec dynastic center of Zaachila and Tehuantepec.

Whether this Isthmian Zapotec presence should be understood as a more integrated, unified state centered on Zaachila and Tehuantepec, or as a coalition of semi-autonomous local lords operating under a shared Zapotec cultural and dynastic umbrella, remains an active question addressed further in the Historical Debate feature below. Either way, students should understand Zapotec expansion into the Isthmus as a genuine historical process of migration, interaction, and state-building rather than a simple, uncontested extension of Valley of Oaxaca authority into an empty frontier.

Section 11.11

Warfare, Alliance, and the Limits of Ethnic Labels

Having traced Mixtec highland politics, coastal Tututepec, Zapotec valley centers, and the Isthmian Zapotec world separately, this section draws them together to make an essential interpretive point: Mixtec and Zapotec rulers related to one another through the same flexible, shifting combination of marriage, alliance, tribute, and warfare that characterized relations within each linguistic tradition. A Zapotec lord might war against another Zapotec center while allying by marriage with a Mixtec dynasty; a Mixtec lord might fight a Mixtec rival while receiving support from Zapotec allies. "Mixtec versus Zapotec" was rarely, if ever, the operative political framework on the ground; the operative framework was almost always which specific dynasty, allied with which other specific dynasty, against which rival coalition, at which particular moment.

This point connects directly to the ongoing scholarly discussion, introduced in Section 11.10, over whether later Zapotec Isthmian power under Zaachila and Tehuantepec functioned as a genuinely integrated state or as a looser coalition of semi-autonomous local rulers sharing dynastic and cultural ties — a question that historians studying Guiengola's lidar-mapped urban plan continue to debate.

Historical Debate

Was Late Postclassic Zapotec Power in the Isthmus a State or a Coalition of Petty Kingdoms?

The question: Did Zapotec authority in the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, centered on Zaachila and Tehuantepec and materially expressed at Guiengola, constitute a genuinely integrated territorial state, or a coalition of semi-autonomous local lords bound together by shared dynastic ties and cultural identity rather than by centralized administration?

Interpretation A — An integrated territorial state: Proponents point to Guiengola's carefully planned urban layout, palace and administrative architecture, and apparent structured noble hierarchy as evidence of centralized planning and coordinated authority extending across the Isthmian region under Zaachila-Tehuantepec leadership.
Interpretation B — A coalition of semi-autonomous "petty kingdoms": Proponents argue that the broader pattern of Postclassic Oaxacan politics — small hereditary states linked by marriage and alliance rather than direct administration — makes it more likely that Isthmian Zapotec communities retained substantial local autonomy under individual lords, joined together primarily through shared identity, mutual defense, and dynastic connection to Zaachila rather than through unified bureaucratic governance.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same lidar-based archaeological mapping of Guiengola's architecture and layout, but disagree about how much centralized planning that layout actually demonstrates, and how representative Guiengola is of the wider Isthmian region.

Student task: Using the evidence and reasoning developed throughout this chapter regarding the small-state, alliance-based character of Postclassic Oaxacan politics generally, argue for the interpretation you find more persuasive, or explain why the truth may combine elements of both.

Section 11.12

Oaxaca on the Eve of the Sixteenth Century

By the close of the fifteenth century, Oaxaca remained what it had been throughout the Postclassic period: a politically plural, densely interconnected, and highly competitive regional system. Tututepec dominated much of the Pacific coast; numerous independent Mixtec highland states continued to govern the Mixteca Alta and Baja; Zapotec dynasties held authority in the Central Valleys and, through Zaachila-Tehuantepec, extended influence into the Isthmus; and commercial and dynastic networks bound these many centers together even as rivalries and warfare continued among them.

During the fifteenth century, the expanding Mexica-led Triple Alliance, examined in Chapter 14, began pressing upon Oaxacan polities through tribute demands and periodic military campaigns, adding an external pressure to Oaxaca's already complex internal politics. Some Oaxacan centers negotiated accommodation with Mexica power; others, including Tututepec, successfully resisted more sustained incorporation. This external pressure did not, however, unify Oaxaca into a single resistance movement or erase its internal rivalries; local dynastic competition remained the dominant feature of Oaxacan political life down to the arrival of Spanish forces in the early sixteenth century.

Students should carry forward from this chapter a clear image of Oaxaca not as a region "awaiting conquest," passively positioned for later Spanish arrival, but as an actively functioning, sophisticated political world with its own histories, rivalries, and achievements, fully capable of shaping the terms on which conquest and colonization would later unfold, examined in Volume II.

Historical Vocabulary
Cacicazgo

A cacicazgo is a term adapted by Spanish colonial administrators, originally from a Caribbean Indigenous word for a chief, and later applied broadly to the small hereditary dynastic states of Mixtec and Zapotec Oaxaca. Each cacicazgo was governed by a ruling lineage claiming descent from illustrious ancestors, and dozens of such polities existed across the Mixteca and the Zapotec-speaking regions during the Postclassic period.

Students should use this term carefully: it is a colonial-era administrative label applied retrospectively to pre-conquest Indigenous political units, not a term Postclassic Oaxacans themselves would necessarily have used to describe their own polities.

Primary Source Window

The Tonindeye Codex, Plates 80 and 85: Eight Deer's Journey to the House of the Sun

Creator: Unknown Mixtec scribes, working within an established dynastic scribal tradition · Date: Broadly dated by the British Museum to approximately 1200–1521 CE; the episode depicted concerns events understood by the traditional correlation to have occurred in Year 9 Reed (1099 CE), after Eight Deer's 1098 accession at Tilantongo · Type: Two individual painted scenes from a screenfold pictorial manuscript, deerskin, painted on both sides

Context: The manuscript now generally referred to as the Tonindeye, and earlier known primarily by the collector-derived name Zouche-Nuttall, records dynastic histories associated with several Mixtec centers on one side, and the genealogy, marriages, warfare, and political career of Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw on the other. Plate 80 depicts Eight Deer crossing a watery boundary toward a sacred solar realm, and plate 85 depicts his arrival at the House and Temple of the Sun, where he receives insignia of rulership and kindles a New Fire — events that, according to the traditional correlation, followed rather than preceded his 1097 tecuhtli investiture and 1098 Tilantongo accession.

Rather than reproducing the painted scenes directly, students should understand their content in summary form: plate 80 shows Eight Deer's journey across a body of water toward the solar realm, rendered through place signs and a pictorial water convention; plate 85 shows him receiving a turquoise necklace strung with gold bell rattles and kindling a New Fire, ritual acts understood as reinforcing authority he had already secured through his earlier 1097 nose-piercing investiture and 1098 accession, discussed in Section 11.5.

Analysis: This sequence demonstrates that Postclassic Oaxacan elites maintained sophisticated, detailed historical and ritual recordkeeping according to conventions very different from European alphabetic chronicle-writing, and that a single ruler's legitimacy could be reinforced through more than one distinct ritual episode, recorded separately and at different points in a manuscript, even after his rulership had already been formally established. Modern interpretation increasingly draws on Mixtec-language scholarship and Indigenous cultural knowledge to distinguish these episodes and their correct sequence precisely, rather than collapsing them into a single generic "legitimation scene" or reversing their order, and current museum practice increasingly favors the Indigenous-derived name Tonindeye over the older collector-derived name as part of a broader effort to recover Mixtec perspectives on the manuscript's meaning and ownership.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a ruling lineage have commissioned a manuscript recording a ruler's specific ritual journeys and investitures in this much detail?
  2. What are the risks of treating this manuscript as a fully neutral, objective historical record?
  3. Why does it matter historically that the House-of-the-Sun episode reinforced authority Eight Deer had already secured, rather than launching his rise to power?
  4. Why does the choice between the names "Tonindeye" and "Zouche-Nuttall" matter for how museums and historians represent Indigenous perspectives on such objects?
Multiple Perspectives

Living in a Fragmented, Interconnected World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across evidence. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of a Mixteca Alta dynastic ruler: Political survival depended on carefully managing marriage alliances, maintaining ritual legitimacy, and balancing cooperation with rival lineages against the ever-present possibility of war, since no single center could guarantee lasting dominance over its neighbors.
From the perspective of a noblewoman married into a rival dynasty: Marriage was not merely a personal event but a political act binding two ruling houses together, carrying real consequences for territorial claims, succession, and the future alliances or rivalries of one's own children.
From the perspective of a skilled artisan at Tututepec: Producing fine metalwork, textiles, or painted ceramics offered both a livelihood and access to markets and valued goods, even under the authority of a powerful centralized ruler, connecting household life to wider regional networks of exchange.
From the perspective of a Chontal or Zoque community member in the Isthmus of Tehuantepec: The arrival of Zapotec-speaking migrants and the growth of new centers such as Guiengola represented a significant, sometimes disruptive, transformation of an already inhabited homeland, requiring negotiation, accommodation, or resistance rather than settlement into empty land.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Monte Albán's Height to Postclassic Plurality

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from a single dominant capital at Monte Albán to a dispersed network of dozens of independent dynastic states
  • New centers, including Zaachila, Mitla, Yagul, Tututepec, and Guiengola, rose to regional prominence during the Postclassic period
  • Zapotec political and demographic presence expanded southeast into the Isthmus of Tehuantepec
  • Detailed pictorial historical recordkeeping, especially among Mixtec scribes, produced an unusually rich documentary record of individual rulers and dynasties

What Stayed the Same

  • Monte Albán retained sacred and symbolic significance even after losing political dominance
  • Hereditary rulership, marriage alliance, and ritual legitimation remained the fundamental mechanisms of political authority throughout the period
  • Mixtec and Zapotec dynasties continued to interact through intermarriage, alliance, and warfare rather than existing as sealed, separate political worlds
  • No single Oaxacan polity ever achieved lasting unification of the entire region

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic political reorganization after Monte Albán and the underlying continuity of dynastic, alliance-based politics helps students avoid treating Postclassic Oaxaca as either a simple continuation of the Classic period or a complete historical rupture from it.

Cause and Effect

How Could Eight Deer Build Extraordinary Power in a Fragmented Political Landscape?

Long-term causes

The small-state, alliance-based political system of Postclassic Oaxaca rewarded ambitious individuals who could combine ritual legitimacy, marriage diplomacy, and military success, since no single hereditary claim automatically guaranteed dominance.

Short-term causes

Eight Deer's establishment of a new power base at Tututepec around 1083–1084 and his 1097 nose-piercing investiture and tecuhtli title, conferred by Lord Four Jaguar, allowed him to accumulate authority beyond his initial hereditary position and helped legitimate his 1098 accession at Tilantongo; his later 1099 journey to the House of the Sun then reinforced and elaborated authority he had already secured.

Effect

Eight Deer became one of the most powerful individual lords of his generation, associated with the founding traditions of the coastal power of Tututepec.

Longer-term consequence

His success generated rival enmities that eventually led to his capture and death, illustrating the structural limits on individual dominance within Oaxaca's competitive dynastic system.

Historical complexity: Eight Deer's career cannot be reduced to personal ambition alone; it depended on, and was ultimately constrained by, the broader alliance-based political structure examined throughout this chapter.

Material Source

Codex-Style Polychrome Pottery from a Tututepec Household

Object type: Painted polychrome ceramic vessel · Findspot: Excavated commoner household contexts at Tututepec (Yucu Dzaa) · Period: Late Postclassic

Description: Archaeologists excavating ordinary residential compounds at Tututepec have recovered painted polychrome ceramic vessels decorated with imagery — including warfare and sacrifice scenes — closely related to the pictorial conventions used in elite manuscripts such as the Tonindeye Codex. These vessels were not confined to palace contexts; they appear regularly in the households of commoners, apparently obtained through participation in markets that drew on multiple producers rather than through direct elite gift-giving alone.

Significance: This material evidence complicates any assumption that the symbolic and ideological content of Mixtec elite manuscripts was accessible only to a narrow ruling class. The circulation of codex-style imagery on everyday household pottery suggests that ordinary Tututepec residents possessed some degree of familiarity with, and access to, the same visual and symbolic vocabulary used to record dynastic history and legitimate rulership, even if they could not read a painted manuscript in the same specialized way a trained scribe or noble could. It also demonstrates that commoner households were active market participants with some independent control over what goods they acquired, a theme developed further in Section 11.9.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household at Tututepec

Household archaeology at Tututepec reveals that ordinary residents lived within a coastal state actively engaged in tribute collection, craft production, and long-distance exchange, yet retained meaningful economic agency of their own. Excavated house compounds include evidence of textile production, food preparation, and small-scale craft work, alongside painted polychrome pottery whose imagery echoes the symbolic conventions of the elite pictorial manuscripts discussed earlier in this chapter, suggesting that such imagery was recognized and valued well beyond palace walls.

Commoner households obtained goods such as copper ornaments and decorated ceramics through market exchange rather than exclusively through elite redistribution, indicating that Postclassic Oaxacan coastal society combined centralized political authority with a functioning, semi-independent market economy accessible to ordinary families. This evidence complicates any picture of Postclassic Oaxacan states as rigid top-down systems in which commoners possessed no independent economic life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of Oaxaca from the political decline of Monte Albán, examined in Chapter 7, through several centuries of Postclassic political reorganization down to the eve of Spanish contact in the early sixteenth century. Rather than a single successor civilization replacing Zapotec Monte Albán, this period produced a densely plural political world of dozens of independent Mixtec and Zapotec dynastic states, linked through marriage, alliance, tribute, and warfare rather than centralized administration.

Students have examined the political geography of Postclassic Oaxaca, including the Mixteca Alta, Mixteca Baja, and Mixteca de la Costa, the Valley of Oaxaca, and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, and have learned to understand the Mixtec ñuu, rulable by a man or a woman, as the basic unit of political organization, with a yuhuitayu marriage alliance capable of temporarily joining two ñuu together; the colonial-era Spanish term cacicazgo, applied retrospectively to these polities, is best understood as a later administrative label rather than an Indigenous political category. The chapter has introduced Mixtec pictorial manuscripts, especially the Tonindeye Codex, as an unusually rich body of Indigenous historical evidence, and has used the documented career of Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw — including his 1097 nose-piercing investiture and his later 1099 House-of-the-Sun journey — to illustrate how ritual legitimation, marriage alliance, and military conquest combined to build individual political power within this fragmented system, and how that same system ultimately constrained his career, ending with his capture and sacrifice by rival lords in 1115.

The chapter has examined Tututepec as a genuine large-scale Late Postclassic conquest state on the Pacific coast, while insisting that its power never amounted to a unified "Mixtec Empire." It has restored Zapotec political history to equal weight through examination of Zaachila, Mitla, and Yagul, and has traced Zapotec expansion into the Isthmus of Tehuantepec and the growth of Guiengola as a process of migration and interaction with existing Chontal, Mixe, Zoque, and Huave communities rather than settlement of empty land.

Students have also examined the continued ritual significance of Monte Albán after its political decline, most famously through the contested case of Tomb 7, and have practiced distinguishing archaeological style from ethnic identity as a matter of historical method. The chapter has surveyed craft production, market exchange, and household economies, showing that commoners as well as elites participated in Postclassic Oaxaca's economic and symbolic life.

Finally, the chapter has argued that "Mixtec versus Zapotec" is frequently the wrong framework for understanding specific historical conflicts and alliances, since dynastic competition and cooperation crossed linguistic lines throughout this period. By the early sixteenth century, Oaxaca remained a functioning, competitive regional political system, increasingly pressured but not unified by expanding Mexica power, fully prepared to encounter, resist, negotiate with, and eventually be transformed by the Spanish forces examined in Volume II.

Chapter 11 has asked how Oaxaca's Zapotec and Mixtec worlds reorganized after Monte Albán's political decline. Chapter 12 turns east to the equally fragmented, but differently organized, Postclassic Maya world of Chichén Itzá, Mayapán, and the Guatemalan highlands.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look past Monte Albán's political decline and discover a Postclassic Oaxacan world every bit as historically rich, dynamic, and consequential as the earlier Zapotec state examined in Chapter 7. Rather than a single successor civilization, Oaxaca produced dozens of competing dynastic states, an extraordinary body of Indigenous pictorial history, and individual political careers, epitomized by Eight Deer Jaguar-Claw, that historians can trace with rare specificity.

The chapter has also modeled several habits of historical reasoning that will recur throughout this textbook: resisting simple two-sided ethnic or political frameworks in favor of specific dynastic detail; distinguishing archaeological style from ethnic identity; reading Indigenous pictorial sources critically rather than either literally or dismissively; and recognizing that political fragmentation does not equal cultural decline or historical unimportance.

Oaxaca's Postclassic history connects directly forward to the conquest narratives of Volume II, where Spanish forces would encounter not a single Oaxacan nation but a landscape of competing dynasties, some of which would ally with, resist, or attempt to use Spanish intervention for their own purposes — continuing, in new form, the same alliance-based political culture examined throughout this chapter.

Understanding Postclassic Oaxaca on its own terms equips students to recognize that Mesoamerican political history was never a simple story of a few dominant empires; it was, in Oaxaca as elsewhere, a story of many peoples, many dynasties, and many overlapping historical experiences whose full complexity deserves careful, evidence-based attention.
