Napoleon, 1808, and the Collapse of Imperial Legitimacy

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Throne Without a King, an Empire Without a Center

In 1808 the two Iberian monarchies met the same French pressure and arrived at opposite outcomes. The Portuguese prince regent crossed the Atlantic and ruled from Rio de Janeiro. The Spanish king and his heir were drawn to Bayonne, forced to renounce their rights, and held in France. The constitutional question that followed in Spanish America was not "Shall we be independent?" but "Who now has the right to govern in the king's name?"

Between October 1807 and May 1808, events in Europe separated the Iberian crowns from the authority they had exercised over the Americas for three centuries. Napoleon Bonaparte, then at the height of his power, sought to close continental ports to British trade and to bring the Iberian peninsula within his system. Portugal, a long-standing British ally with a large Atlantic empire, was invaded in late 1807. Spain, nominally a French ally, was occupied by French troops that arrived as partners and stayed as masters. By the summer of 1808 the Spanish king had abdicated, his son had abdicated, and Napoleon's brother Joseph had been proclaimed king of Spain and the Indies.

This chapter treats those events as the opening of a crisis of legitimacy rather than as the start of a war for independence. The distinction matters. Chapters 48 through 50 described the strains within the Iberian empires: Bourbon and Pombaline reform, the rebellions of the Andes and Minas Gerais, and the Haitian Revolution, which showed elites across the Americas how swiftly a colonial order could be overturned from below. Those strains made the empires vulnerable. They did not by themselves produce the events of 1808, and they did not point to a single outcome. The collapse of accepted royal authority, the appearance of competing governments, and the eventual choice by some movements to seek independence were three related but separate developments, and this chapter keeps them apart.

The central argument is that Napoleon destabilized the monarchies but did not cause the Americas' political fragmentation by himself, and that the people of Spanish and Portuguese America responded in ways that varied by region, social position, and moment. In Brazil, royal presence relocated the center of the empire and preserved dynastic continuity while altering colonial relations. In Spanish America, the dynasty's displacement opened a dispute over where sovereignty resided and who could exercise it. Many of the first American governing bodies swore loyalty to the imprisoned Ferdinand VII, and many of their members meant it. Loyalty to the king, however, did not imply obedience to whoever claimed to rule in Spain, and that gap between the monarch and his officials became the space in which new politics took shape.

The chapter also takes Cádiz seriously. Between 1810 and 1814, Spanish and American deputies meeting in the one Spanish city the French did not capture wrote a constitution that declared sovereignty to reside in the nation, limited royal power, extended representation to the Americas, and reformed colonial institutions. It was a real alternative vision of imperial government, supported by many Americans, and it was undone in 1814 by the restored Ferdinand VII, not by a failure of American commitment. Its limits were also real: it left slavery intact, excluded women from citizenship, restricted the civic standing of people of African descent, and established Catholicism as the only permitted religion.

The evidence is as scattered as the crisis. Historians use cabildo minutes, viceregal and captaincy-general correspondence, trial records, the proceedings of the Cortes, government decrees, newspapers such as the Gazeta de Caracas and the Gazeta de Buenos Ayres, political catechisms and pamphlets, merchants' letters, and Portuguese royal decrees. Nearly every one of these sources was produced by a person or an institution with something to prove, and almost all of them were written by literate, urban, male officeholders. Indigenous communities, enslaved people, free people of African descent, and the poor appear mostly as objects of other people's debate, though their actions shaped what the debate could achieve.

The chapter's boundaries follow the outline. It begins with the Napoleonic pressure on Portugal and Spain in 1807 and ends with the restoration of Ferdinand VII in 1814. It does not narrate the insurgency of Hidalgo and Morelos, the campaigns of Bolívar and San Martín, the final Andean wars, or the separation of Brazil, which belong to Chapters 52 through 57. Its purpose is to show how an imperial crisis became a crisis of government across two continents and to prepare the ground for the wars that follow.

Learning Objectives

  3. Trace the sequence from the Aranjuez mutiny through the abdications of Bayonne to the installation of Joseph Bonaparte.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What did it mean to declare loyalty to Ferdinand VII while refusing obedience to officials in Spain?
  3. On what legal and intellectual foundations could American cabildos claim authority when the monarch was absent?

Historical Context

By 1800 the Spanish monarchy governed territory from California to Tierra del Fuego, together with Cuba, Puerto Rico, Santo Domingo, and the Philippines. It was organized into four viceroyalties (New Spain, Peru, New Granada, and the Río de la Plata) and several captaincies-general and presidencies, including Venezuela, Chile, and Guatemala. As Chapter 48 explained, the Bourbon reforms of the eighteenth century had increased fiscal extraction, expanded military forces, created intendancies, and given peninsular-born officials a larger share of senior posts. These measures strengthened royal power on paper and generated resentment among American-born elites, merchants, corporations, and Indigenous communities.

Portuguese America was governed differently. Brazil was a single large territory under a governor-general or viceroy, with captaincies, municipal councils, and a planter and merchant elite closely tied to Atlantic slaving and the export of sugar, gold, cotton, and other goods. The Pombaline reforms described in Chapters 41 and 48 had strengthened the crown's hand but had not provoked the wide corporate resistance that Bourbon reform met in parts of Spanish America. Brazilian society rested heavily on slavery and the Atlantic trade in enslaved Africans, which gave planters and merchants strong reasons to value order and to fear social upheaval.

Between 1780 and 1804 the Atlantic world had shaken. Chapter 49 described the great Andean rebellions led by Túpac Amaru II and Túpac Katari and the comunero revolt in New Granada. Chapter 50 described how an enslaved population in Saint-Domingue, taking advantage of the French Revolution's disruption of metropolitan authority, overthrew slavery and founded Haiti. American creole elites drew an important lesson from these events: political experiments could slip beyond the control of those who began them. After 1804, fear of Haiti, and of Indigenous and enslaved insurgency generally, made many property-holding Americans cautious about challenging royal authority in ways that might unleash popular mobilization.

War and trade also mattered. From 1796 Spain was allied with revolutionary and then Napoleonic France, and it suffered at sea: the British destroyed much of the combined Franco-Spanish fleet at Trafalgar in 1805. Naval blockades repeatedly cut Spanish America off from the peninsula, and Spain sometimes allowed neutral shipping to trade with its colonies as a wartime necessity. The royal treasury, strained by war and by the issuance of paper debt, drew on American funds, including the 1804 consolidation decree (Consolidación de Vales Reales), which forced the repayment of church loans and hurt many American borrowers, notably in New Spain. British merchants, meanwhile, had developed an appetite for Spanish American markets, and British forces had attacked Buenos Aires and Montevideo in 1806 and 1807. Those invasions, resisted largely by local militias, gave Río de la Plata an unusual experience of self-defence and armed colonial politics.

The reader should keep one caution in mind throughout. It is tempting to read these developments as a gathering road to independence. They were not. In 1807 almost no one in Spanish or Portuguese America was working toward the end of monarchy, and the historical question is how, within seven years, so much changed. The answer lies less in a hidden nationalist current than in the abrupt removal of the political center around which loyalty and obedience had been organized.

Section 51.1 · Division I — The Crisis of the Iberian Monarchies, 1807–1808

The Iberian Monarchies under Napoleonic Pressure

By 1807 Napoleon controlled most of continental Europe and was waging economic war on Britain. His Berlin Decree of 1806 declared the British Isles under blockade and forbade French-controlled and allied states from trading with Britain. Historians call the resulting arrangement the Continental System. It was only as effective as its enforcement, and the Iberian peninsula was a conspicuous gap: Portugal had traded with Britain for more than a century and was bound to it by treaty, and Spain, though allied to France, had an empire whose commerce could not easily be cut off from British goods and capital.

The two states stood in different positions. Portugal's wealth depended on Brazil, and Brazil's trade on the Atlantic, which the British navy dominated. A Portuguese government that closed its ports to Britain risked losing control of the sea and the colony; one that did not risked invasion. Spain was a weaker partner than its size suggested. Its navy had never recovered from Trafalgar, its treasury was heavily indebted, and its government, headed in practice by the royal favorite Manuel Godoy, had bound itself to France without securing protection from French demands. Spanish decisions were also complicated by tension within the royal family, since the heir to the throne, Ferdinand, resented Godoy and drew a circle of aristocratic and clerical supporters around himself.

The chapter's later arguments depend on one point established here. The Iberian monarchies did not fall because American subjects had decided to reject them. They were damaged by European coercion and by weaknesses in their own courts, and those events then raised a question the American territories had to answer without any prior plan. Chapter 48 described how the reforms of the previous decades had altered the relationship between the American territories and their crowns. This section does not retell them. It notes only that by 1807 royal authority in the Americas rested on habits of obedience, networks of officials and corporations, and the symbolic standing of the crown, and that all three depended on a functioning monarch in Europe.

Section 51.2

The French Invasion of Portugal, 1807

In the summer of 1807 Napoleon demanded that Portugal close its ports to British ships and confiscate British property. The Portuguese regent, Prince João, governing on behalf of his mother, Queen Maria I, who had been judged unfit to rule, tried to delay. He attempted to meet French demands partially while retaining British goodwill, a policy that kept both powers uncertain. In October 1807 France and Spain signed the Treaty of Fontainebleau, which agreed to divide Portugal among French, Spanish, and Godoy-related claims and authorized French troops to cross Spain. A French army under General Jean-Andoche Junot began its march toward Lisbon in the same month.

Portugal's decision to depart rather than fight grew out of an older Portuguese strategic idea: that the monarchy, if threatened in Europe, might be preserved in Brazil. The idea had been discussed in Portuguese circles for generations. In the autumn of 1807 the regent also negotiated with Britain, whose government, anxious to keep Portugal's fleet and Atlantic ports out of French hands, agreed to escort the Portuguese ships and to support the transfer. A secret convention between Lisbon and London, signed in October, provided for British protection of the royal family's voyage and, in return, for commercial concessions that would follow.

The departure took place on 27 to 29 November 1807, as French troops approached the capital. Contemporary and later estimates of the number who sailed with the royal family range between roughly ten and fifteen thousand: members of the court, ministers, judges, military officers, clergy, servants, and a sizable portion of the state's archives, treasury, and library. The sailing was chaotic. Some people and goods were left on the docks. Junot entered Lisbon on 30 November, only days after the fleet had cleared the Tagus. The French occupied the country, dissolved the regency council the prince had left behind, and imposed heavy contributions. Portugal then entered a period of occupation and, after the British landed forces in 1808, of war.

Students should notice what the decision accomplished. The prince regent had not abdicated, had not been captured, and had not recognized French rule. The Portuguese monarchy therefore remained a functioning source of authority, now located in the Americas. Whatever the court's critics said then and later about flight, it kept the legitimate government intact and out of French hands, a result with no counterpart in Spain.

Section 51.3

The Portuguese Court in Brazil, 1808

The royal fleet reached Salvador, Bahia, on 22 January 1808, after a voyage disrupted by storms that scattered the ships. Within a week the prince regent issued a decree, dated 28 January, opening Brazilian ports to friendly nations. Until then, colonial trade had been channeled through Portugal. After the decree, foreign vessels, and in practice chiefly British ones, could trade directly with Brazilian ports. The decree responded to an immediate necessity: with Portugal occupied, Brazilian commodities could no longer reach the Portuguese market and Brazilian consumers could no longer receive imports through it. It also reflected the pressure and influence of Britain and of Brazilian merchants and planters who had long chafed at the commercial monopoly.

The court moved on to Rio de Janeiro, where it arrived in March 1808, and the city began a rapid transformation. Rio became the seat of an empire's government. The prince regent established the institutions that a European court required: ministries, a high court, a treasury, a royal printing press, a library, a military academy, and a bank. A decree of April 1808 lifted the prohibition on manufactures in Brazil, and in the following years new schools, a botanical garden, and a newspaper, the Gazeta do Rio de Janeiro, appeared. In 1810 Portugal signed commercial treaties with Britain, granting British goods preferential and low import duties, a concession that deepened British economic influence and drew criticism within Portugal and Brazil. In December 1815 the prince regent declared Brazil to be a kingdom, and the realm became the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil, and the Algarves, with Rio de Janeiro as its capital.

These changes preserved royal continuity while altering the colonial relationship. Brazil's elites, notably planters and merchants in Rio and the southeast, gained access to the court, to offices, and to titles. The arrival of thousands of courtiers also created new burdens: housing was requisitioned, prices rose, and many Brazilians resented the privileges of the newcomers. The court remained dependent on slavery and the Atlantic slave trade. Rio became one of the largest slave-trading ports in the Atlantic world in the decades that followed, and the social order the court protected was a slave society. The monarchy's presence thus reassured Brazilian slaveholders who had reason to dread disorder, and it gave them a stake in the crown's survival.

Portugal itself felt abandoned. After the French were expelled, the country was governed under British military influence, and a growing sense that Portugal had become a colony of its colony helped to feed the liberal revolution that began in Porto in 1820. That revolution demanded the king's return and, in time, challenged the new standing of Brazil. Chapter 56 follows those events to Pedro I's declaration of independence. For present purposes, the point is the contrast. In 1808 the Portuguese crown moved; in the same year the Spanish crown disappeared from view. The Spanish Americans therefore faced a question that Brazilians, in the first years, largely did not: who was now the sovereign?

One qualification applies. Carlota Joaquina, the Spanish-born wife of the prince regent and sister of Ferdinand VII, tried to turn the dynastic crisis to her advantage by claiming the right to act as regent over Spanish America while her brother was held in France. Her project, known as carlotismo, found some support among Río de la Plata elites in 1808 and 1809, and it tied the two Iberian crises together in the person of one woman. It failed, because the British opposed it, because Spanish officials suspected it, and because the prince regent's own interests did not align with hers. It nevertheless shows that some Americans were looking for any legitimate dynastic solution before they considered abandoning monarchy altogether.

Section 51.4

Godoy, the Mutiny of Aranjuez, and the Spanish Succession Crisis

Spain's crisis grew out of its alliance with France and its internal rivalries. Manuel Godoy, a guards officer who had risen to be chief minister and was widely believed to be the queen's lover, had long been unpopular among nobles, clergy, and the public. Ferdinand, Prince of Asturias, had conspired against him and had been tried in 1807 in the so-called El Escorial affair, in which the prince was accused of plotting against his parents and was pardoned after he implored his father's forgiveness. These events weakened the monarchy's dignity and made Ferdinand a symbol for opponents of Godoy.

French troops had entered Spain under the terms of Fontainebleau, nominally to cross to Portugal, and in the following months they occupied Pamplona, Barcelona, and other key places. Their numbers exceeded what the treaty justified. In March 1808 the court, alarmed, prepared to leave Madrid for Andalusia, perhaps for the Americas, in imitation of the Portuguese example. The prospect of a royal flight angered many Spaniards, who feared abandonment and associated flight with Godoy's influence. On the night of 17 March a crowd, with the participation of guards and aristocratic partisans of Ferdinand, rose at the royal residence of Aranjuez, sacked Godoy's house, and seized him. On 19 March Charles IV, under pressure, abdicated in favor of his son. Ferdinand was proclaimed king, and the news was greeted with public celebration.

The crisis did not end there. Napoleon's marshal, Joachim Murat, entered Madrid on 23 March at the head of French troops. Charles IV soon declared that his abdication had been coerced and was invalid, and appealed to Napoleon. Ferdinand entered Madrid on 24 March, but Napoleon refused to recognize him as king. Thus, within a few days, two Spanish monarchs claimed the throne, and the French emperor asserted the right to arbitrate. In effect, the French had converted a Spanish dynastic quarrel into an opportunity.

The consequences for the Americas were not immediate, since news took weeks or months to cross the ocean. What mattered was the precedent. A king had abdicated under duress, his successor was uncertain, and a foreign sovereign claimed to decide the matter. Each of these facts would later furnish an argument. Those who held that Ferdinand was the true king could claim that Charles's abdication of 19 March was valid and Bayonne's later abdications forced. Those who saw only confusion could claim that no legitimate sovereign remained, and so authority had reverted to the people. These arguments came from the same set of events.

Section 51.5

The Abdications of Bayonne and Joseph Bonaparte

In April 1808 Napoleon summoned the Spanish royal family to Bayonne, in southwestern France, and encouraged both Charles IV and Ferdinand to come and settle their dispute before him. Ferdinand, believing that Napoleon would recognize him, crossed the frontier. The royal family, along with Godoy, soon found themselves in a situation from which there was no easy exit. During the negotiations of 5–10 May, Ferdinand was pressed to relinquish the crown in favor of his father, Charles IV, while Charles transferred his dynastic rights to Napoleon. These coerced transfers and renunciations are known as the abdications of Bayonne. The exact sequence of the formal instruments requires care, but the outcome is clear: Napoleon claimed possession of the Spanish crown through renunciations obtained from both father and son.

Napoleon then named his brother Joseph, formerly king of Naples, as king of Spain and the Indies. A group of Spanish notables, summoned to Bayonne, accepted the arrangement, and in July 1808 an assembly there approved a constitutional text, the Bayonne Statute. It was a royal charter drafted under French direction rather than the product of a representative Spanish process, and it adopted a Napoleonic model: a strong monarch, a Senate, a Cortes that was limited in power, the abolition of some privileges, and, notably, a place for American deputies and a declaration that the American territories would enjoy equal commercial and representative rights with Spain. The inclusion of the Americas is important, because it shows that both sides in the coming struggle would bid for American support.

Students should be careful to distinguish two things that are easily confused. The Bayonne Statute contained reforms that some Spaniards and Americans might have found attractive, including provisions favoring uniform law and the weakening of corporate privilege. The statute nevertheless lacked legitimacy for most Spaniards and Americans because it was imposed by a foreign power on the basis of renunciations that most of them regarded as coerced. A constitution is not made legitimate by its content alone. Whether its authority is accepted depends on who issues it, by what process, and with whose consent. A minority of Spaniards, later called afrancesados, supported Joseph's regime in the belief that it offered reform and order. Most Spaniards did not, and the statute's American clauses did little to persuade the American cabildos, whose officials had in most cases sworn allegiance to Ferdinand.

By the end of the spring, then, the Spanish monarchy faced a situation without clear precedent. The reigning king and his heir were in French custody. A foreign-imposed king claimed the throne. The institutions of the peninsula, from the Council of Castile to the provincial audiencias, were either cooperating with the French or under their control. The question of where authority lay was now urgent in Spain and, once news arrived, in the Americas. Spanish resistance supplied an answer that would shape everything that followed.

Section 51.6 · Division II — Government without an Accepted Sovereign

The Dos de Mayo Uprising and the Peninsular War

On 2 May 1808 a crowd gathered in Madrid as members of the royal family were taken from the palace toward France. A confrontation with French troops grew into a street uprising. French forces under Murat suppressed the rising on 2 May, and executions of captured Spaniards began that afternoon and continued during the night of 2–3 May, reprisals later made famous by Francisco de Goya's paintings. Accounts of the uprising were soon overlaid with legend, including narratives about Manuela Malasaña in Madrid; Agustina de Aragón, another celebrated heroine, became associated instead with the later defense of Zaragoza. The surviving accounts of women's participation require historians to distinguish documented action from subsequent patriotic embellishment. Historians therefore treat the Dos de Mayo as both a real event, in which many ordinary Madrileños took part, and as a founding symbol of Spanish national resistance.

The uprising was suppressed, but the news spread. Beginning in late May 1808, towns and provinces across Spain rose against the French and formed local juntas to organize resistance. The war that followed, known in Spanish as the War of Independence and in English as the Peninsular War, lasted until 1814. It was fought by regular Spanish armies, British and Portuguese forces under Sir Arthur Wellesley, later Duke of Wellington, and irregular bands that gave the world the word guerrilla, "little war." In July 1808 a Spanish army forced a French corps to surrender at Bailén in Andalusia, a victory that astonished Europe and prompted Joseph Bonaparte to leave Madrid. Napoleon himself came to Spain in November with a large army and retook the capital in December. The war then settled into a long and destructive struggle in which French forces controlled much of the country but could not secure it.

The war's importance for this chapter lies in its political effects rather than its military course, which this textbook does not follow in detail. It destroyed the central authority of the Spanish state. Royal councils, the army command, and the bureaucracy were divided or occupied, and in the territories not under French control authority was exercised by locally formed bodies. The war also generated a vocabulary of national resistance, in which the Spanish people, not the court, were the defenders of the monarchy and the religion. Spaniards were told that the nation was defending its king against a usurper, and that the nation's will was therefore the source of the king's authority. That language carried political implications that the American cabildos did not overlook.

The war also hurt the American economy and administration. Spain's naval weakness meant that communications and trade across the Atlantic became less reliable. The British, now Spain's allies, gained still more access to American markets, a development that benefited British merchants and many American consumers and producers, and provoked dissatisfaction among Spanish merchants of Cádiz. The costs of the war were partly passed to the Americas, which were asked to send money, in the form of donations and loans, to support the struggle. Many Americans responded generously, a sign that early American loyalty to the monarchy was real and not merely formal.

Section 51.7

Provincial Juntas and the Reconstitution of Political Authority

The juntas that formed in Spain in the spring and summer of 1808 were not designed in advance. In Asturias, Galicia, Valencia, Seville, and other cities and provinces, local notables, officials, clergy, military officers, and sometimes crowds assembled to govern in the name of Ferdinand VII. Each junta assumed powers that the monarchy had never delegated to it: raising troops, collecting taxes, appointing officials, and negotiating with foreign powers. Some, like the junta of Asturias, sent envoys to London to seek aid. The junta of Seville, the most powerful in the south, styled itself a supreme junta of Spain and the Indies, and for a time claimed authority over the American territories.

These bodies were not uniform. Some were dominated by traditional elites, while others included merchants, lawyers, and army officers. Their legitimacy rested on an unwritten argument: that in the absence of the king, the community had the right to govern itself in his name. They did not agree on much else. Their competing claims to supremacy were a problem, because a government that each province refused to obey could not coordinate a war. Rivalries among juntas, such as that between Seville and Madrid-area authorities, or between Valencia and Murcia, meant that the unity of the resistance was fragile.

For the Americas, the Spanish juntas established a precedent that the American cabildos would study carefully. If Spanish towns and provinces could form juntas to govern in the king's name, why could American cities not do the same? The question was legal and political. The Spanish juntas were responding to foreign occupation, and Spanish officials could argue that American conditions, with no French soldiers on American soil, did not require such bodies. American advocates responded that the vacancy of the throne, not the physical presence of the French, created the need. The American juntas that followed in 1810 would copy the form of the Spanish ones, often using the same language, and their supporters were frequently mystified when Spanish authorities treated the same action as treason in America and patriotism in Spain.

Section 51.8

The Supreme Central Junta and the Spanish Empire

To end the rivalry of the provincial juntas, representatives of the provinces met at Aranjuez and on 25 September 1808 established the Supreme Central and Governing Junta of Spain and the Indies. Its first president was the elderly statesman the Count of Floridablanca, who died in December, and its members included figures of the Enlightenment reform tradition, among them Gaspar Melchor de Jovellanos, who argued for the convocation of a Cortes and for constitutional reform. The Central Junta was a coalition of provincial delegates rather than an elected government. It took the style of "Majesty," issued decrees, and claimed to exercise the full authority of the crown in Ferdinand's absence.

Its record was mixed. It coordinated the Spanish war effort with Britain and held together the provincial armies, but it also suffered military defeats and the sharp criticism of a public that expected victories. It was forced to flee from Aranjuez to Seville in December 1808, when Napoleon's army advanced on Madrid, and it faced factional dispute over whether to convene a Cortes. Its legitimacy, already derivative, depended on continued success, and success was not forthcoming.

The Central Junta's treatment of the Americas was its most consequential policy for this chapter. On 22 January 1809 it issued a decree declaring that the American dominions were not colonies or factories of the kind that other European empires possessed but essential and integral parts of the Spanish monarchy. To give effect to this status, the decree called for the viceroyalties of New Spain, Peru, New Granada, and the Río de la Plata, and the independent captaincies-general of Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guatemala, Chile, and Venezuela, together with the Philippines, to elect representatives to the Junta. Each of these territories was to elect one deputy, selected through a complicated process in which the cabildos of the capital cities of provinces proposed candidates and the final choice was made by lot, or by the viceroy or captain-general acting with others. Peninsular provinces, by comparison, had each sent two members.

The decree was a significant concession in language and a modest one in practice. It formally recognized the Americas as part of the nation and invited American participation in the central government for the first time. It also installed an unequal system, since the Americas, with a far larger population than Spain on most contemporary estimates, would have about nine deputies against more than thirty from the peninsula. Moreover, the electoral mechanism concentrated power in a few capital cities and in the hands of officials. Some Americans received the decree with gratitude. Others, like the New Granadan lawyer Camilo Torres, whose Memorial de Agravios of 1809 protested the inequality, took it as evidence that peninsular authorities still regarded America as subordinate. The text of the decree is examined in the Primary Source Window later in this chapter.

Section 51.9

Sovereignty, Representation, and the Meaning of the Nation

The central intellectual problem of the crisis was the location of sovereignty. In the political tradition of the Spanish monarchy, the king was the head of a body politic made up of kingdoms, cities, and corporations, and he governed under law and custom. The Americas had been incorporated into the crown of Castile, not as colonies in the later sense but as kingdoms, a point that American jurists, drawing on legal traditions that went back to the sixteenth century, emphasized. The king's authority was held in trust, and neo-scholastic thinkers, among them Francisco Suárez and Francisco de Vitoria, had taught that political power came from God through the community, which transferred it to the ruler.

From this tradition came the doctrine that is central to the period, retroversión de la soberanía: if the king could not rule, sovereignty reverted to the people from whom it had come. The doctrine had long roots, including in the medieval Castilian law code known as the Siete Partidas, and it had been used on the peninsula in earlier disputes. In 1808 both Spanish juntas and American cabildos invoked it, often without citing it by name. A related set of ideas, by contrast, drew on eighteenth-century Enlightenment and revolutionary sources: that sovereignty rested in the nation, understood as a body of citizens, and that a constitution expressed the nation's will. These two traditions overlapped but did not coincide, and the debates of the period contain constant slippage between them.

A second problem was who the "people" were. In the Hispanic usage of the period, pueblo could mean the people in general, a town or community, or a political body with legal personality, and pueblos in the plural referred to the cities and towns that made up the kingdom. When American cabildos claimed that sovereignty had reverted to the pueblos, they often meant that each city or province, as a corporate body, could constitute its own government. This was a conception that favored local autonomy and, in time, produced sharp disputes between capitals and provinces, as when provincial towns refused to accept the authority of the cabildo of Caracas, Buenos Aires, or Bogotá. A conception of sovereignty that began with the corporate pueblos could not easily be reconciled with one that began with a single national people.

Third, the cabildos' powers were uncertain. The cabildo, or municipal council, was a body of local property owners and officeholders that supervised city affairs. In ordinary times its authority was limited, and viceroys and audiencias exercised far larger powers. But the cabildo was also the traditional voice of the community, and in a crisis it could summon an open council, a cabildo abierto, of leading residents to deliberate on questions of public safety. This institution had precedent in the medieval Spanish tradition and was invoked in 1808 and 1810 to give a veneer of popular legitimacy to decisions about government. Participation in a cabildo abierto was restricted to what contemporaries called vecinos, respectable householders, so the claims made in the name of the people were made by a small segment of society.

Finally, loyalty to Ferdinand VII and obedience to authorities in Spain had to be separated. A person could say, without hypocrisy, that they were loyal to the king and that the Central Junta, or later the Regency, had no right to govern them. Declarations of fidelity to "Ferdinand VII" accompanied the earliest American juntas, and historians have debated whether this was a mask for independence or a sincere position. The evidence suggests that both occurred. For some leaders, the invocation of Ferdinand was a convenient screen. For others, it was a conviction, and the real question was how a monarchy of kingdoms should be governed in the absence of the monarch. The language of loyalty, in other words, could shelter very different aims, and its meaning shifted as events unfolded.

Historical Vocabulary
Retroversión de la soberanía

The doctrine of retroversión de la soberanía (the "reversion" or "return" of sovereignty) held that political authority came from the community and was entrusted to the ruler, and that when the ruler was absent, incapacitated, or unable to govern, authority returned to the community or its representatives. It drew on neo-scholastic political thought and on Castilian legal tradition.

The doctrine allowed both Spanish juntas and American cabildos to claim authority while professing loyalty to the king. Modern readers should not equate it with the later idea that "the people" are sovereign in a democratic republic. In 1808 it was often applied to corporate bodies such as cities and provinces, and it did not necessarily imply universal participation or an end to monarchy. Its ambiguity is what made it useful and what made it dangerous.

Historical Vocabulary
Junta

A junta (from juntar, "to join") was a governing committee or council. In the Hispanic world, juntas had long existed as advisory or administrative bodies. In 1808 the term acquired a new meaning: an emergency government formed by local or provincial notables to exercise authority when regular government had failed.

The word should not be confused with the twentieth-century meaning of a military junta, a ruling committee of officers, which is how Chapters 84 through 87 will use it. The juntas of 1808 to 1810 were civilian or mixed bodies, usually claiming to rule in the name of the absent king. Whether a particular junta was treated as loyal or seditious depended less on its form than on how royal officials and rival juntas judged its claims.

Section 51.10 · Division III — The Crisis Crosses the Atlantic, 1808–1810

News, Rumor, and Imperial Loyalty in Spanish America

News of the European crisis reached Spanish America slowly, unevenly, and in contradictory forms. A ship from Cádiz or Britain might take six to ten weeks to reach Veracruz, Cartagena, La Guaira, or Montevideo, and longer to reach Lima or Mexico City's interior. Information moved by ship to the ports, by courier and mule train inland, and by word of mouth from merchants, sailors, clergy, and officials. Printed material was limited: the Americas had few newspapers in 1808, though the Gazeta de Caracas began publication that year and the Gazeta de Buenos Ayres in 1810, and the Gazeta de México, an official paper, circulated in New Spain. Official correspondence was supplemented by private letters and by rumor, and officials sometimes suppressed or delayed information they judged dangerous.

The result was an unusual condition of uncertainty. In the summer of 1808, American officials and elites learned in rapid succession of the Aranjuez mutiny, the proclamation of Ferdinand VII, the Bayonne abdications, Joseph Bonaparte's accession, and the Spanish uprising. Competing messengers arrived with competing claims. Emissaries of Napoleon, bearing news of Joseph's accession, reached Montevideo and Caracas in mid-1808 and were rejected. Envoys of the Seville junta, notably the Peruvian-born officer José Manuel de Goyeneche, arrived in the Río de la Plata and then traveled to Lima and Charcas, urging recognition of Seville's authority. Meanwhile British ships and officers reported the Spanish resistance and offered the alliance of their government. A viceroy or captain-general had to decide whom to believe, which authority to recognize, and what to tell the public.

Nearly every American official and corporation reacted first by declaring loyalty to Ferdinand VII and rejecting Joseph, and by organizing oaths, public ceremonies, and subscriptions to support the Spanish war. These were not empty gestures. Ferdinand, imprisoned and idealized, became a focus of popular affection, and the crown had centuries of symbolic authority behind it. Public ceremonies in his honor were conducted across the Americas, in Indigenous villages as well as in cities. Historians who have studied these ceremonies find that many participants were sincere, and that the language of loyalty was widely shared across class and ethnic lines.

Uncertainty about which authority was legitimate created openings. Viceroys had been appointed by the king, but which king? The audiencias, courts that also had administrative functions, claimed authority in the absence of a viceroy. Cabildos claimed to speak for their communities. Seville's junta claimed the whole empire, and then the Central Junta did. A well-informed American might ask whether obedience was owed to any of them. The problem was not abstract, since taxes, military commands, and judicial appointments all depended on resolving it. And the question was complicated by social fears, in particular the fear that open disputes among whites would give Indigenous, mixed-race, and enslaved populations room to act on grievances of their own.

Section 51.11

The Mexico City Crisis and the Coup of 1808

New Spain was the richest and most populous of the Spanish American territories, the source of much of the silver on which the crown relied, and the home of a large and varied population that included Indigenous communities in the majority. When news of the Spanish events arrived in the summer of 1808, Viceroy José de Iturrigaray, in office since 1803, was already unpopular with many peninsulars, who suspected him of corruption and of ambitions of his own. He responded to the crisis by calling a series of meetings, in August and September, that brought together the audiencia, the cabildo, the archbishop, and other corporate bodies to deliberate on how the viceroyalty should be governed.

The Mexico City cabildo, dominated by American-born members, took the lead in arguing for a form of representative government. Its syndic, Francisco Primo de Verdad, and a lawyer-councilor, Juan Francisco Azcárate, argued that with the king absent, sovereignty had returned to the people, represented by their corporations, and that the viceroy should govern with a body drawn from them. The Mercedarian friar Melchor de Talamantes, a friend of the viceroy, wrote tracts that went further and discussed the possibility of a national congress. The viceroy's position was ambiguous. He appeared willing to work with the cabildo's proposals and to avoid recognizing the Seville junta's claim to authority, a stance that peninsulars read as a bid to make himself the head of an autonomous regime.

The audiencia, dominated by peninsular judges, and the wealthy peninsular merchants of Mexico City, who feared both political change and the loss of their commercial position, opposed these ideas. They argued that the cabildo had no authority to claim sovereignty, that the viceroy was bound to obey the juntas of Spain, and that any change would invite disorder. On the night of 15 to 16 September 1808, a group of armed peninsulars led by the wealthy landowner Gabriel de Yermo entered the viceregal palace, arrested Iturrigaray, and installed an elderly military officer, Pedro de Garibay, as viceroy with the audiencia's support. The viceroy was sent to Spain.

This was a coup in the proper sense: an unconstitutional seizure of government by a faction, with the participation of armed civilians and the acquiescence of the court. The consequences were severe for those who had favored the cabildo's proposals. Primo de Verdad died in prison in October, in circumstances that contemporaries and later historians have disputed, and Talamantes died in 1809, of yellow fever, as a prisoner in the fortress of San Juan de Ulúa. Azcárate and others were imprisoned. The coup also established a precedent: in New Spain, the peninsular establishment would defend its authority by force, and an American-led political movement would not be able to proceed through institutional channels. The conspiracy of Valladolid in 1809 and, ultimately, the insurgency that began in 1810 under Miguel Hidalgo (Chapter 52) arose in a political setting shaped by the 1808 coup.

Historians have long debated the meaning of the 1808 events. Some have seen the cabildo's proposals as an early step toward independence. Others stress that the American councilors did not call for separation and that their objective was an autonomous government under Ferdinand VII. The weight of the evidence favors the second view. The Mexico City cabildo wanted a share of authority within the monarchy, not an end to it, and its conflict with the peninsular merchants was about who would control government in a time of uncertainty. That the conflict ended in a coup made later movements more radical, but the 1808 proposals themselves should be read on their own terms.

Section 51.12

The First American Junta Projects, 1808

In 1808 and early 1809, similar proposals arose in several places, and they generally failed. In Caracas, the Captaincy General of Venezuela, a wealthy landowning and commercial elite known as the mantuanos, whose families dominated the cabildo, had long chafed against peninsular officials and Bourbon policy. In late 1808 a group of them petitioned the captain-general, Juan de Casas, to create a junta modeled on those of Spain. The captain-general refused, and authorities arrested some of the petitioners or dispersed their meetings. The mantuanos' concern was not simply political: they feared that a divided leadership would endanger the order on which their position as owners of cacao plantations and enslaved workers depended, and they were suspicious of the free population of color, who outnumbered them.

In the Río de la Plata, the situation was complicated by the aftermath of the British invasions of 1806 and 1807. Viceroy Santiago de Liniers, a French-born naval officer who had led the reconquest of Buenos Aires in 1806, was suspected by some peninsulars of French sympathies, and he faced a rival in Montevideo, where the governor, Francisco Javier de Elío, established a junta in September 1808 that recognized the authority of Seville and defied the viceroy. On 1 January 1809 the cabildo of Buenos Aires, led by the Spanish merchant Martín de Álzaga, attempted to depose Liniers and form a junta. The attempt failed, largely because the American-born militia commanders, notably Cornelio Saavedra and the Patricios regiment, supported the viceroy. Here the lines were drawn differently from Mexico: a peninsular merchant faction sought a junta, and American-born military officers defended the existing viceroy. It is a reminder that "peninsular" and "American" were not always the decisive dividing lines.

In New Granada and elsewhere, similar discussions took place in cabildos and among officials, and similar caution prevailed. Colonial authorities generally succeeded in preventing the formation of governing bodies before 1810, and they did so by a combination of persuasion, arrests, and force. Several things distinguish these early efforts from the movements of 1810. They were generally limited to small groups of notables. They were typically presented as defenses of Ferdinand VII against a possible French takeover. And most did not question the monarchy's existence. A historian who treats them all as early independence movements will misread them. They were attempts to settle who should govern in the king's absence, and the people who made them held a range of views about how much change they wanted.

Section 51.13

Charcas and La Paz: The Andean Crisis of 1809

The Audiencia of Charcas, with its seat in Chuquisaca (modern Sucre), governed a mining region that included Potosí and a large Indigenous population, and it was a center of legal learning, home to the University of San Francisco Xavier, whose graduates included many of the lawyers who later played political roles. The audiencia's president, Ramón García de León y Pizarro, quarreled with the judges, and the dispute became entangled with the larger question of whom to obey: the Seville junta, whose envoy Goyeneche had arrived, or the claims of Carlota Joaquina, which some of the judges and academics favored. In May 1809 a crowd and a group of judges and university men, in an episode known as the revolution of Chuquisaca, deposed the president and assumed government. The motives were mixed, partly a power struggle among officials, partly a response to suspicions that the president favored recognition of Joseph or of Carlota, and partly an expression of the legalist political thinking of the university. Participants insisted that they acted to preserve the rights of Ferdinand VII.

In La Paz, on 16 July 1809, a group of local notables, militia officers, and others, led by the militia officer Pedro Domingo Murillo, took control of the city, arrested the bishop and the governor, and established a Junta Tuitiva, a "protective junta." Its proclamation spoke of defending the rights of the king and of the people against the threat of foreign domination and the failures of Spanish rule, and it contained language critical of the Spanish government's treatment of Americans. Historians have debated whether the junta's aim was autonomy within the monarchy or separation from it, and its documents can be read both ways. The movement had a social dimension as well. Mestizo and Indigenous participants joined, and the junta adopted measures that drew on resentments against peninsular officials and the economic burdens of colonial rule. Its leaders, however, were unable to organize a stable government or to win the support of the surrounding provinces.

Royalist reaction was swift. The viceroy of Peru, José Fernando de Abascal, a determined defender of royal authority, sent a force under Goyeneche from Lima, while the Río de la Plata viceroy, Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros, dispatched troops from Buenos Aires under the command of Vicente Nieto. The forces suppressed the La Paz junta in October and reoccupied Chuquisaca. Murillo and other leaders were tried and, in January 1810, executed. Violence accompanied the repression, and Indigenous communities, who had been courted by both sides, suffered. Abascal's intervention is significant: Lima became the military base of royalism in South America, and Upper Peru, though it later experienced years of guerrilla warfare, remained under royal control until the 1820s.

The Charcas and La Paz movements are sometimes called the first cry of independence in Spanish America. That label belongs to the later nationalist tradition, which treated them as ancestors of the Bolivian republic. The contemporary evidence suggests a more complicated story. The movements arose from local disputes, from uncertainty about the legitimacy of competing Spanish authorities, from resentment of peninsular domination, and from the presence of social grievances that exceeded elite goals. Their leaders professed loyalty to Ferdinand, and the extent to which that loyalty was sincere cannot be settled. What the episodes demonstrate is that the same crisis produced different outcomes in different places, and that royalist military power in the Andes was strong enough to crush early movements.

Section 51.14

Quito, 1809, and the Limits of Early Autonomy

Quito, the seat of an audiencia that belonged to the Viceroyalty of New Granada, had a long record of conflict with Bourbon fiscal policy, notably the rebellion of the barrios in 1765, and an educated elite that included reform-minded intellectuals. Its political class was divided between aristocratic families, the cabildo and audiencia officials, and the leaders of Quito's artisan and Indigenous populations. In the summer of 1809, a group of aristocrats and professionals, many of whom had met in the household of the Marquis of Selva Alegre, Juan Pío Montúfar, began to conspire to create a junta. Tradition, which cannot be fully verified, holds that Manuela Cañizares hosted a key meeting on 9 August and urged the hesitant to act; the story has been important in Ecuadorian memory.

On 10 August 1809 the conspirators arrested the president of the audiencia, Manuel Urriez, Count Ruiz de Castilla, and proclaimed a Junta Suprema with Montúfar as president. The junta declared that it governed in the name of Ferdinand VII, in response to the danger that the peninsular authorities would hand the realm to France. Its members included nobles, clergy, and officials, and the aristocratic character of the movement was pronounced. The junta announced reforms, including cuts in taxes on some goods, and offered a measure of reconciliation to peninsular residents.

It faced almost immediate isolation. The neighboring authorities in Popayán, Cuenca, and Guayaquil refused to recognize it, and Abascal, in Lima, and the viceroy in Santafé de Bogotá, sent troops. The junta's own base of support was thin, and it lacked resources, a military force, and the allegiance of the surrounding provinces. In October 1809, under pressure from the approaching royalist forces and from divisions within its ranks, the junta dissolved and restored Ruiz de Castilla to office. Its leaders had been promised amnesty, but they were arrested in the following months, and the royalist authorities initiated prosecutions.

The violence of 2 August 1810 gave the story its tragic ending. On that day, an attempted rescue of imprisoned leaders became a violent confrontation; soldiers killed prisoners in their cells, and fighting and reprisals in the city left many other residents dead. The number of dead is uncertain, with estimates in the hundreds, and the episode, which remains controversial, embittered the city's politics. A second junta formed in September 1810 and eventually declared independence, but Quito was reconquered by royalist forces in 1812. The Quito case shows how limited early autonomy movements could be when they lacked regional support and military power, and it shows that suppression could be bloody and could radicalize survivors.

Section 51.15

The Collapse of the Central Junta and the Regency, 1810

While the American juntas of 1809 were being suppressed, the military position in Spain deteriorated. In January 1810, French armies overran Andalusia, and the Central Junta, discredited by defeat and by accusations of incompetence and corruption, was dissolved under popular and political pressure. Before it did so, it delegated its authority to a five-member Regency, which was established in the Isla de León and then in Cádiz, the port city that became the last stronghold of unoccupied Spain. The Regency was a small body of notables, initially presided over by General Francisco Javier Castaños; the bishop of Orense, Pedro de Quevedo y Quintano, joined it later and assumed its presidency on 1 August 1810. It faced the same questions of legitimacy as its predecessor, with the added difficulty that the Central Junta had transferred its powers without any authority to do so, as its critics at once noted.

The Regency issued a proclamation to the Americas in February 1810, asserting that the overseas territories were entitled to the rights of Spaniards and telling their inhabitants that their political destiny would be placed in the hands of deputies they elected, rather than ministers, viceroys, or governors. It called for the election of American representatives to a forthcoming Cortes. The proclamation was an appeal for American support and conveyed in strong language the Regency's recognition that the overseas territories were essential to the survival of Spanish resistance. Some advocates of American self-government could draw on that rhetoric, but the proclamation itself called for representation within a common Cortes, not for the creation of independent American governments. The Regency also did not match its language with institutions: its decree provided for American deputies to the Cortes through a procedure that, as the next sections show, left the Americas underrepresented, and it named substitute deputies chosen from among Americans present in Spain.

When news of the Central Junta's fall and the Regency's establishment reached the Americas in the spring of 1810, it had an explosive effect. Many Americans concluded that the legitimate government of Spain, in the form of the Central Junta, had ceased to exist. The Regency, in their view, had been created in a way that they had not been consulted about and that was of doubtful legality. Others accepted it, judging it the best available authority. This division, between those who recognized the Regency and those who did not, was the immediate political issue in 1810, and it was resolved differently in different places.

That the French occupation of Andalusia had destroyed the last central authority created a practical argument for local government as well. If Spain had fallen, or was about to, the cabildos and provinces of America might need to decide how to govern themselves and defend the territory in the king's name. Many Americans offered this rationale for the juntas of 1810, and it was a reasonable reading of an uncertain situation. A reading that treats the juntas as a pretext for independence overlooks that for many participants the principal alternatives were a French conquest and local self-rule, not independence and Spanish loyalty.

Section 51.16

The American Junta Movements of 1810

Between April and September 1810, juntas formed in several of the principal cities of Spanish America. Many emerged through cabildos or cabildos abiertos, limited or displaced existing royal officials, and declared that they governed in the name of Ferdinand VII. Their positions toward the Regency differed: Caracas and Buenos Aires rejected its authority, whereas Chile’s founding act expressly recognized it. All rejected Napoleon’s imposition of a new dynasty, but they differed greatly in leadership, aims, and results.

In Caracas, on 19 April 1810, which was Holy Thursday, the cabildo met and, after confrontation with the captain-general, Vicente Emparan, who was forced to resign, formed the Supreme Junta for the Preservation of the Rights of Ferdinand VII. Its leaders came from the mantuano elite, and its early measures included the expulsion of Emparan and other officials, the opening of trade to foreign ships, and the prohibition of the slave trade. The junta invited the other provinces of Venezuela to join. Coro, Maracaibo, and Guayana refused, and the junta's reach was therefore limited from the start. The Caracas junta called a congress, which in July 1811 declared independence, a step that Chapter 53 examines.

In Buenos Aires, after a cabildo abierto on 22 May, the cabildo deposed Viceroy Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros and on 25 May established a Primera Junta, with Cornelio Saavedra as president and the lawyer Mariano Moreno as secretary. The junta justified its actions as an exercise of the people's rights in the absence of the legitimate government, and it professed loyalty to Ferdinand VII. It then invited the other provinces of the viceroyalty to send deputies. Montevideo, Paraguay, Córdoba, and much of Upper Peru did not comply. In Córdoba, the former viceroy Liniers organized resistance and was captured and shot in August. The Buenos Aires junta thus entered a civil conflict with regions that it regarded as part of its jurisdiction and that regarded themselves as separate communities with rights of their own.

In New Granada, Cartagena formed a junta on 22 May, and a number of other towns, among them Cali, Pamplona, and Socorro, formed their own in the following weeks. In Santafé de Bogotá, a quarrel with a Spanish merchant over the loan of a flower vase on 20 July became the pretext for a riot, and the cabildo, in an extraordinary session, set up a Supreme Junta, which included the viceroy as nominal president and soon deposed him. The New Granadan juntas quarreled with each other about whether to form a single government or a federation of provinces. The disagreement was an early example of the conflict between centralist and federalist conceptions of the new polities that would shape much of the nineteenth century.

In Chile, a junta was formed in Santiago on 18 September 1810, under the elderly Count of La Conquista, Mateo de Toro y Zambrano, as president. It pledged loyalty to Ferdinand and the Regency, at least initially, and its first acts were moderate, though it soon opened Chilean ports to foreign trade and called a congress. A second junta in Quito in September, as noted above, also appeared. In Mexico, the mid-September uprising led by Miguel Hidalgo took a very different form, as a mass rural insurgency, and is the subject of Chapter 52.

Equally important is where juntas did not form. Lima, under Abascal, remained firmly royalist and became a counterrevolutionary base. In Mexico City, the audiencia and peninsular merchants kept control. Cuba and Puerto Rico, with expanding plantation economies built on slavery, stayed loyal, in part because their elites feared that political experiment might endanger slavery, as the Haitian example had done. Guatemala also remained loyal. The geography of 1810 shows that the choice of a junta depended on local factors: the strength of royal military forces, the cohesion of the local elite, the presence of a threatening social majority, the weight of peninsular merchants, and the proximity of royalist power. It does not support the idea of a continent-wide movement with a single aim. Who made these choices also mattered: the juntas were governments of notables, and their relationship to Indigenous communities, free people of color, and enslaved people, who were the majority of the population in many regions, was uncertain and became a central problem in the wars to come.

Section 51.17 · Division IV — Constitutionalism and the Road to Independence

The Cortes of Cádiz and American Representation

The Cortes, the traditional assembly of the Castilian kingdom, had not been convened for decades. In 1810, a general Cortes was summoned by the Regency, in response to reformers' demands and the need for a legitimating authority. It opened on 24 September 1810 on the Isla de León, near Cádiz, and moved to the city of Cádiz in February 1811. Because the French occupied most of Spain, many provinces could not elect deputies, and the Cortes was composed in part of substitutes, chosen from among people from those provinces who happened to be in Cádiz. Because American news and travel were slow, American territories were represented initially by about thirty suplentes, Americans who were present in the city and who were selected by their compatriots there, and only later by elected deputies.

In its first session the Cortes declared that sovereignty resided in the nation, represented by the assembled deputies, and that the Cortes was the legitimate government in the absence of the king. This was a break with older conceptions of the monarchy, and it led the deputies to claim authority above the Regency. On 15 October 1810 they decreed that the American and Spanish dominions were equal and that Americans had the same rights as Spaniards, and in the following months they enacted reforms, including the freedom of the press in November 1810, which gave Americans and Spaniards a new way to debate politics.

The deputies from the Americas, among them José Mejía Lequerica of Quito, who became famous as an orator, Miguel Ramos Arizpe of Coahuila, José Miguel Guridi y Alcocer of Tlaxcala, Vicente Morales Duárez of Peru, who presided over the Cortes in early 1812, and José Joaquín de Olmedo of Guayaquil, were among the most vocal members. They advanced demands for representation proportional to population, for free trade, for equal access to office, for the end of the mita and other forced Indigenous labor, and, in some cases, for the end of the slave trade. Dionisio Inca Yupanqui, a Peruvian deputy who claimed descent from the Inca, denounced the exploitation of Indigenous people in the Cortes, and his intervention is an example of Indigenous concerns being voiced in that forum, though by a member of the educated elite rather than by Indigenous communities themselves.

The problem of representation was the issue that most divided the Cortes. The Americans argued that, since the Americas contained a majority of the monarchy's population, they should be represented in proportion to it. The Spanish deputies, who feared losing control of the assembly, resisted. The outcome, written into the Constitution, was a compromise that counted Spaniards and Indigenous people toward population, but excluded most people of African descent, a measure that substantially reduced American representation. The final text gave the Americas fewer deputies than their population justified, and it generated resentment that fed both loyalist disappointment and separatist argument. The Cortes's deliberations also exposed a difference in conceptions of the monarchy's future. Many Spanish liberals envisioned a unified nation with equal citizens. Many American deputies envisioned a monarchy of kingdoms with local self-government. These were not the same project, and the gap would widen.

Section 51.18

The Constitution of 1812 and Liberal Reform

The Cortes completed its constitution after prolonged debate and promulgated it on 19 March 1812, the feast of Saint Joseph, which gave the text its popular name, La Pepa. With 384 articles, it was one of the longest constitutions of its time. It declared that sovereignty resided essentially in the nation, and that the nation had the exclusive right to establish its fundamental laws. It created a constitutional monarchy: the king remained the head of state, but his power was limited. Legislative authority was shared between the Cortes and the king, who had a suspensive veto, while the courts were independent of the executive. The king was required to govern through ministers, and the Cortes had powers over taxation and the armed forces.

Elections were indirect, passing through parish, district, and provincial stages, with deputies chosen by electors who were themselves chosen by citizens in several steps. A single chamber, the Cortes, would meet annually. A permanent deputation of the Cortes would serve between sessions. Provincial deputations were established as local bodies with administrative powers, including in the Americas, and constitutional town councils, ayuntamientos constitucionales, were to be elected in any community of a thousand or more inhabitants. The latter provision proved especially significant. In New Spain and elsewhere, the creation of elected town councils allowed many Indigenous communities to establish municipal governments with some independence from the traditional cabildos and from Spanish officials, and recent scholarship has stressed how widely these bodies were adopted.

Alongside the Constitution, the Cortes passed reforms that affected the Americas directly. It abolished Indigenous tribute and the forced labor service of the mita and related obligations in 1811 and 1812, though enforcement was uneven and royalist officials in Peru, short of revenue, restored tribute when the Constitution was suspended. It abolished seigneurial jurisdictions in Spain, curtailed the guilds, and, in February 1813, abolished the Inquisition, a measure that conservatives, clergy, and many ordinary Catholics opposed. It also granted freedom of the press, which led to a proliferation of newspapers and pamphlets in the territories where the Constitution was proclaimed. These reforms were significant: they dismantled institutions that had defined colonial rule.

The application of the Constitution in the Americas depended on royalist officials and military circumstances. It was proclaimed in Mexico City in September 1812 and in many other places, but viceroys and captains-general often moved to limit its effects, in particular the freedom of the press and the elected councils, when these threatened the order they sought to maintain. In New Spain, Viceroy Francisco Javier Venegas, then Félix Calleja, who was fighting an insurgency, suspended press freedom and restricted the Constitution's application. In Peru, Abascal proclaimed it but ruled in ways that curtailed it. The Constitution thus operated within real constraints, and where it was in force it was in force for a limited time, since it was annulled in 1814. During those years, though, it shaped American politics profoundly: elections took place, elected officials took office, and ordinary people participated in a constitutional process that left lasting memories.

Section 51.19

Citizenship, Race, Religion, and the Limits of Equality

The Constitution's treatment of citizenship reveals its ambitions and its limits. It defined Spaniards as all free men born and resident in the Spanish dominions, and their children, and it defined citizens, a narrower category with political rights, as Spaniards whose ancestry derived from the Spanish dominions of both hemispheres. This formula treated Indigenous people, who were counted as Spaniards, as citizens, a far-reaching departure from the colonial system, in which Indigenous people had belonged to a separate legal "republic" with its own obligations and protections. For many Indigenous communities, citizenship carried the benefits of equal political rights and the abolition of tribute, but also the loss of some of the protections of the old regime, including special courts and communal safeguards, and these tradeoffs were debated in the communities themselves.

The Constitution excluded people of African descent from citizenship by ancestry. Free people of African descent were Spaniards but not citizens, and the Constitution provided only that individuals could petition the Cortes for citizenship in recognition of service or merit. The exclusion reflected the fear of many Spanish and American deputies that granting citizenship to people of African descent, who made up a large proportion of the population in places like Venezuela, Cuba, and parts of New Granada, would upset the racial and social order and, in the American case, would reduce the weight of white elites in representation. Americans like Guridi y Alcocer and Mejía Lequerica argued for including castas in the count and in citizenship, but their efforts failed. Many American elites supported the exclusion for the same reasons the Spaniards did. The result was a distinction that fell hardest on free people of color, who had served in militias and in many places formed a substantial part of the artisan and military population. The exclusion would be remembered, and when independence movements later sought the support of free people of color and enslaved people, the memory of Cádiz's refusal played a part.

Slavery survived. The Cortes debated proposals to abolish the slave trade and slavery in 1811, prompted by an American deputy's motion and by British pressure and Spanish liberal conscience, and it tabled them, in part because the economic interests of Cuba and the sensitivities of slaveholding delegates stood in the way. The enslaved population thus received no benefit from Cádiz. The Haitian experience cast a long shadow, and Cuban planters, for example, were among the most committed opponents of change. Some American juntas took steps against the slave trade, as Caracas did in 1810, but none abolished slavery at this stage.

Women were excluded from formal political citizenship, as they were in almost every constitutional system of the time. This did not mean that women were absent from political life. They took part in the ceremonies, the donations, the public debates, and the networks of correspondence that underpinned politics, and in some places, women were active in the insurgencies that followed. The Constitution's silence about women's political rights reflects a broader assumption, shared across the political spectrum, that politics was a male sphere. Also excluded from the franchise were domestic servants, and, in theory from 1830, those who could not read or write, which in Indigenous and poor communities meant most adults. The Constitution established Catholicism as the religion of the nation, to the exclusion of any other, and the Inquisition's later abolition did not change that.

For these reasons the Constitution is best seen as both a serious attempt at reform and a reflection of the limits of its drafters. It offered Americans more than any earlier Spanish government had, including a share in making law, an equal formal status, and local government. It also left in place much of the hierarchy of race, religion, and gender, and it did so under a monarchy whose king was away. Americans who had hoped that Cádiz would settle their grievances found that it settled some, left others, and created a few new ones.

Section 51.20

From Imperial Legitimacy Crisis to Independence

By 1812 Spanish America contained several political positions, and individuals could move among them. Loyalists, sometimes called royalists, supported the established authority of Spain, whether in the form of the Regency, the Cortes, or later Ferdinand, and they included peninsular officials and merchants, many American clergy and landowners, and in many places Indigenous communities and free people of color who saw the crown as a protector against local elites. Autonomists wanted American self-government within the monarchy and often sat in the juntas and at Cádiz. Constitutional monarchists, who overlapped with autonomists, embraced the Constitution of 1812 as the way to reform the empire. A fourth group, whose numbers grew as events unfolded, favored full independence. These categories were not fixed. A person might begin as an autonomist, become an independentist after royalist violence, or become a loyalist when social conflict seemed to threaten order. The categories help to organize the discussion, but historians should not assign people to them too early.

The movement from autonomy to independence depended on contingency. In Venezuela, the Caracas congress declared independence in July 1811, a step encouraged by the junta's dispute with the Regency and by radical societies in the capital. In the Río de la Plata, the Buenos Aires junta moved gradually, avoiding a formal declaration until 1816 while governing in practice as an independent state. In Mexico, the insurgency began in 1810 in Ferdinand's name and only later adopted a separatist program. In Paraguay, a movement in 1811 deposed the Spanish governor and rejected both Spain and Buenos Aires. In other places, royalist forces regained control. These divergences underline the argument of this chapter: the collapse of royal authority produced a wide range of political outcomes, and independence was one of them rather than the preordained result.

The restoration of Ferdinand VII in 1814 was the decisive change. After Napoleon's defeat in Spain, Ferdinand returned to his country in March 1814 under the Treaty of Valençay of December 1813. Many Spaniards expected him to accept the Constitution. Instead, backed by conservative deputies, army officers, and the Church, he issued a decree at Valencia on 4 May 1814 that annulled the Constitution and the acts of the Cortes, restored absolutism, and began the persecution of liberals. In 1815 he sent an expeditionary force of about ten thousand soldiers under General Pablo Morillo to reconquer Venezuela and New Granada. The restoration closed the constitutional alternative. It meant that Americans who had hoped for an autonomous place within a reformed monarchy faced an unreformed king, and it converted many autonomists into independentists, though not all, and not at once. In 1820 a liberal revolt in Spain would restore the Constitution briefly and reopen the question, an episode that Chapters 52 and 56 take up.

Students should end the chapter with a clear view of what had and had not happened by 1814. The accepted royal authority had collapsed, and Spain's own government had been recomposed several times. New governing institutions, from cabildos and juntas to the Cortes and constitutional town councils, had appeared, and they had given many people, including some outside the elite, experience of politics. Some places had declared independence, others had resisted the idea, and most were still fighting over it. The wars that Part XII narrates arose out of this setting, and they were fought over questions of legitimacy, social order, and sovereignty that had been opened in 1808 and had not been settled.

The Supreme Central Junta's Decree on the American Dominions, 22 January 1809

Creator: The Supreme Central and Governing Junta of Spain and the Indies · Date: 22 January 1809 · Place: Seville, Spain · Type: Government decree

Context: The Central Junta, newly formed and needing American money and loyalty as well as legitimacy, issued this decree to the viceroyalties and captaincies-general. It reached Americans in the following months, and was read in cabildos and printed in official gazettes.

Paraphrase of the decree's central claims: The vast dominions of the Indies are not colonies or factories like those of other nations but an essential and integral part of the Spanish monarchy. For this reason, and to express the Junta's gratitude for American loyalty, the territories are invited to send deputies to sit in the Central Junta. Each viceroyalty and each independent captaincy-general is to elect one representative, chosen by a process centered on the cabildos of the principal cities.

Analysis: The decree is important for what it declares and for what it provides. Its declaration of integral status gave Americans an argument that they were equal members of the monarchy, and it was cited repeatedly in later debates. Its provisions, however, gave the Americas one deputy for each large territory while peninsular provinces had two each, so that an American population larger than Spain's would have had a small fraction of the seats. The selection process also narrowed power to cabildos and officials. The source reflects the aims of its authors, who sought American support without surrendering control. It does not record the views of those who received it, who differed widely in their responses.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Napoleon Cause Latin American Independence, or Expose an Existing Imperial Crisis?

The question: Was the French invasion of Iberia the decisive cause of the Spanish American independence movements, or did it merely trigger the collapse of an empire already weakened by long-term strains?

Interpretation A — A crisis made in Europe: Historians such as François-Xavier Guerra and Jaime E. Rodríguez O. emphasize the political rupture of 1808. They argue that Spanish Americans had no coherent separatist movement before the crown fell and that the crisis arose from the dispute over sovereignty and representation across the whole Hispanic world. On this view, the juntas and the Cortes show that Americans were engaged in a common constitutional struggle and that independence emerged gradually from the failure to settle it.
Interpretation B — A crisis long in preparation: Other historians, including John Lynch and, in different ways, Tulio Halperín Donghi, stress long-term processes: Bourbon fiscal and administrative reform, the rivalry between American-born and peninsular elites, the growth of regional economies, Enlightenment ideas, and the rebellions of earlier decades. On this view, 1808 revealed and accelerated tensions that were already shaping American politics, and some form of separation might have come even without Napoleon.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same archive: cabildo minutes, official correspondence, pamphlets, constitutional debates, and the record of the juntas' declarations. Interpretation A points to the language of loyalty and the lack of separatist programs before 1808. Interpretation B points to grievances recorded in earlier petitions and to the sharp divisions between peninsulars and Americans. Many scholars combine elements of both, and the difference lies largely in emphasis.

Why it matters: If the rupture of 1808 was decisive, independence appears contingent, a result of European events and imperial failure to accommodate American demands. If long-term strains dominate, it looks more structural. The question also affects how historians view the loyalty of 1808 to 1810, as either genuine or tactical, and how they judge the choices of Cádiz and Ferdinand VII.

Student task: Write a short case for each interpretation using evidence from this chapter, and identify one kind of evidence that would help decide how much weight each deserves.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Spanish and Portuguese Imperial Government before and after 1808

What Changed

  • The Portuguese court relocated to Rio de Janeiro, and Brazil became the seat of an empire and, in 1815, a kingdom
  • The Spanish crown was displaced, and authority passed to juntas, a Regency, and the Cortes
  • Americans acquired new political institutions: cabildos acting as governments, junta congresses, constitutional town councils
  • Brazilian ports opened to foreign trade; Spanish American commerce increasingly escaped peninsular control
  • A public sphere of newspapers, pamphlets, and debate expanded

What Stayed the Same

  • Slavery remained intact in Brazil, Cuba, and under the Constitution of 1812
  • Elite men, in the cabildos and juntas, led most political initiatives
  • Catholicism remained the established religion
  • Monarchy remained the dominant form of legitimate government
  • Racial and social hierarchies persisted, though they were challenged

Who experienced change most directly: Urban elites and officials in the capitals, and Indigenous communities affected by new tribute and municipal rules. Who experienced greater continuity: Enslaved people, women, and many rural communities. Why the pattern matters: The crisis redistributed political authority among a narrow set of actors while leaving the social order largely in place. That combination explains both the speed of political change and the conflicts that followed when other groups asked for more.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Dynastic Displacement to Rival Governments and Separation

Long-term causes

Bourbon and Pombaline reform; fiscal strain and war debt; rivalry between American-born and peninsular elites; the rebellions of the Andes and Minas; the example and fear of Haiti; growing trade with Britain.

Short-term causes

Napoleon's Continental System; the Treaty of Fontainebleau; the Aranjuez mutiny; the Bayonne abdications; the collapse of the Central Junta in January 1810.

Event or transformation

The Spanish dynasty was displaced and authority passed among competing bodies, while the Portuguese dynasty relocated to Brazil.

Immediate effects

Juntas and cabildo movements; the Mexico City coup; royalist repression in the Andes; the Cortes of Cádiz; Rio de Janeiro as an imperial capital.

Long-term consequences

Constitutional experience across the Hispanic world; regional conflicts between capitals and provinces; the wars of independence; a different path to independence in Brazil.

Historical complexity

The crisis did not make independence inevitable. It opened a dispute about sovereignty, to which regions, classes, and individuals responded differently, and which Ferdinand's restoration and military events shaped further.
Everyday Life and Social History

Oaths, Donations, Rumors, and the Burdens of Crisis

For most people in the Americas, the crisis arrived as a public ceremony, a new tax request, or a rumor. Communities gathered to swear oaths to Ferdinand VII, to hear proclamations, and to celebrate his name days. Priests preached about the usurper. Indigenous villages, parish congregations, and militia companies took part, and many families made donations of money, jewels, or goods for the Spanish war effort. These donations were sometimes voluntary and sometimes encouraged by pressure from officials and clergy.

Rumor traveled faster than newspapers. Stories that Napoleon planned to abolish religion, that French agents were landing, or that the king had been killed circulated in markets and churches. In slave societies, rumors that the quarrels among whites might lead to freedom for the enslaved could spread through households and plantations, and masters took them seriously, strengthening patrols and discipline. In Rio de Janeiro, the arrival of thousands of courtiers meant the requisition of houses, higher prices, and a growing market for skilled labor, with enslaved Africans and free people of color working as artisans, porters, and domestic workers in a swelling city. In Spain and in parts of America, women contributed through donations, household labor, correspondence, and in some cases direct participation in resistance, though their names are rarely recorded. The surviving documents mostly record the actions of officials and notables, and the experience of ordinary households must be reconstructed from fragments.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Napoleon's pressure on Portugal and Spain produced a crisis of legitimate government across the Iberian Atlantic and has argued that this crisis, not an existing plan for independence, opened the way to the wars that follow. It has distinguished three developments: the collapse of accepted royal authority, the appearance of competing governing institutions, and the eventual decision by some movements to seek independence.

In 1807 the Continental System and the Treaty of Fontainebleau placed both Iberian monarchies under French pressure. Portugal's prince regent, advised by a long-standing strategic idea and supported by Britain, took the court across the Atlantic and ruled from Brazil. The decision preserved the dynasty and its government, opened Brazilian ports to foreign trade in January 1808, and made Rio de Janeiro the capital of an empire. Royal presence altered colonial relations without breaking them, and it gave Brazil's slaveholding elites a stake in the crown's survival.

The Spanish monarchy met a different fate. The mutiny of Aranjuez toppled Godoy and brought Ferdinand VII to the throne in March 1808, but Napoleon refused to recognize him. At Bayonne in May the king and his heir were forced to renounce their rights, and Joseph Bonaparte was installed. The Bayonne Statute offered reforms and American representation, but it was imposed by a foreign power on the basis of coerced abdications, and most Spaniards and Americans rejected it.

The Dos de Mayo uprising and the Peninsular War created a vocabulary of national resistance and a patchwork of provincial juntas, and the Supreme Central Junta of September 1808 tried to unify them. Its decree of January 1809 declared the Americas integral parts of the monarchy but offered unequal representation. The doctrine of retroversión de la soberanía and the Hispanic meanings of pueblo and cabildo explain how American cities could profess loyalty to Ferdinand VII while claiming authority to govern themselves.

American responses varied. News arrived slowly and in contradictory forms. In Mexico City, a peninsular coup in September 1808 ended a proposal for representative government. In the Andes in 1809, juntas in Chuquisaca, La Paz, and Quito were suppressed by royalist forces from Lima and elsewhere, and the repression at La Paz and Quito was violent. In 1810 the fall of the Central Junta and the creation of the Regency divided America over whether to recognize the new authority, and juntas formed in Caracas, Buenos Aires, Bogotá, Santiago, and other cities, while Lima, Mexico City, Havana, and Guatemala stayed under royal control. The juntas quarreled with their own provinces, anticipating the conflicts between centralism and federalism that would shape the century.

The Cortes of Cádiz, which opened in September 1810, and the Constitution of 1812 offered a serious alternative to independence. They declared sovereignty to reside in the nation, created a constitutional monarchy, extended representation to the Americas, established elected town councils, and abolished tribute, the mita, and the Inquisition. The reforms mattered to Indigenous communities and to many Americans who took part in elections.

The Constitution also retained serious inequalities. It treated Indigenous people as citizens but denied citizenship by ancestry to people of African descent, excluded women from political rights, left slavery intact, and made Catholicism the only permitted religion. Its representation provisions reduced American weight in the Cortes, which fed grievances among Americans who had expected equality. Royalist officials in America often limited its application.

By 1812, loyalists, autonomists, constitutional monarchists, and independentists occupied different positions, and people moved among them as circumstances changed. Some places declared independence, as Venezuela did in 1811; others did so later or resisted. The return of Ferdinand VII in 1814, his annulment of the Constitution, and his dispatch of an expeditionary force closed the constitutional path and converted many autonomists into supporters of independence, though not all and not immediately.

The comparison with Brazil remains central. Spanish America faced a vacancy of sovereignty and a dispute over who could fill it, while Brazil received the monarchy itself. That difference helps explain why Spanish American independence took the form of multiple wars and republics, while Brazil's separation, examined in Chapter 56, was negotiated within a monarchy. The reader should leave with the sense that 1808 opened a set of possibilities, and that independence, though a real outcome, was not the only one and not the inevitable one.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the independence era began as a dispute about who could rightfully govern. A French emperor's ambitions removed or relocated two monarchs, and the people of two continents had to decide whom to obey. Their answers varied with their position, their fears, and their information. The same crisis produced a court in Rio de Janeiro, a constitution at Cádiz, juntas in the Andes and the River Plate, and a royalist fortress in Lima.

It also shows how much of the later history rested on unsettled questions. Who were the sovereign people? Did sovereignty belong to the nation, to the cities, or to the provinces? Could the new governments include Indigenous communities, free people of color, and the enslaved? How would capitals relate to the territories they claimed to represent? The juntas and the Cortes raised these questions without answering them, and they would return in constitutions, civil wars, and caudillo politics across Volume III.

The chapter also illuminates the difference between Spanish and Portuguese America. In Brazil, the monarchy's arrival preserved continuity and slavery and gave the colony a new standing, so that independence in 1822 would be a negotiated separation under a Braganza prince. In Spanish America, the vacancy of the throne made every political order a matter of dispute, and independence would come through many wars and many republics.

Chapter 52 takes up the first of those wars, beginning with the insurgency of Miguel Hidalgo in Mexico in September 1810. Students should carry forward the understanding that the movements they will study did not begin with a common program of separation. They began in a crisis of legitimacy, and the choices made in the following years, under pressure from royalist armies, social conflict, and a restored king, shaped what independence would mean.
