Agriculture, Maize, and the First Mesoamerican Villages

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Toward the First Villages

No single morning marks the invention of farming in Mesoamerica. Instead, across many centuries and across many valleys, coastlines, and river bottoms, small communities of people who already knew their local plants intimately began, gradually and unevenly, to favor certain grasses, gourds, and legumes over others — replanting, selecting, tending, and eventually reshaping them, and in the process slowly reshaping their own way of life as well.

In a dry rock-shelter called Guilá Naquitz, set into the volcanic tuff cliffs above Oaxaca's Valley of Oaxaca, archaeologists have recovered thousands of desiccated plant fragments — squash rinds, bean pods, chili pepper seeds, and wild plant remains — preserved for thousands of years by the cave's arid conditions. Layer after layer of the cave's floor tells a story not of sudden transformation but of small bands of people returning to the same seasonal shelter across many generations, gradually shifting the balance of what they gathered, what they planted, and what they carried with them. Elsewhere, in the highland basin of the Tehuacán Valley in Puebla, decades of excavation recovered small, primitive maize cobs, scarcely longer than a thumbnail, embedded among the remains of wild plants that people continued to collect long after they had begun cultivating a domesticated crop. These fragile plant remains, unglamorous as they may seem beside the temples and inscriptions examined later in this volume, document one of the most consequential transformations in the human history of this hemisphere.

This chapter is the first fully sustained historical chapter of this textbook. Chapters 1 through 4 established what the region is, how it was peopled, what environments shaped it, and how historians and archaeologists reconstruct a past that left no writing behind. This chapter now puts those methods to work on a specific, richly documented, and enormously important problem: how did mobile foraging communities in Mesoamerica gradually give rise to villages, agriculture, exchange networks, and the beginnings of social differentiation — developments that, over further centuries, created the conditions within which the Olmec, the Zapotec, the peoples of Teotihuacan, the Maya, and the Mexica would eventually build the civilizations examined in the chapters that follow?

It is essential to state plainly, from the first page of this chapter, what this transformation was not. It was not an overnight invention. It was not a single "Neolithic Revolution" imported wholesale from elsewhere. It was not a story of primitive hunters suddenly discovering a superior way of life and abandoning the old one entirely. And it was not an inevitable staircase leading directly and automatically from wild plants to pyramids. Instead, the evidence recovered from caves, riverbanks, and buried village floors across Mesoamerica describes a long, regionally varied, and often reversible process, in which communities combined hunting, gathering, fishing, and cultivation in different proportions for thousands of years, and in which agriculture and settled village life, though closely related, developed along their own separate and sometimes surprising timelines.

Maize will occupy the center of this chapter, and rightly so: no single domesticated plant did more to shape the subsequent history of Mesoamerica, or of the wider hemisphere, than the grass known today as maize, or corn. But this chapter resists letting the story of maize become the entire story. Beans, squash, chili peppers, avocados, agave, and other plants each had their own separate histories of management and domestication, sometimes developing in tandem with maize and sometimes independently of it. The eventual "maize-beans-squash complex" that would nourish later Mesoamerican civilizations was not present from the beginning; it was itself a historical development, assembled gradually as different communities in different regions brought together crops whose domestication histories did not necessarily start together or proceed at the same pace.

This chapter also asks students to watch a second transformation unfold alongside the domestication of plants: the gradual shift from highly mobile foraging bands toward more settled village communities. These two processes — domestication and sedentism — are related, but, as this chapter will explain repeatedly, they are not identical. Some communities cultivated plants while remaining highly mobile for centuries. Others became substantially more settled before agriculture supplied more than a modest share of their diet. Only by keeping these two historical threads distinct can students avoid the common but misleading assumption that farming and village life arrived together, as a single package, the moment someone planted the first seed.

Finally, this chapter asks students to watch what else changed as communities settled into more permanent relationships with particular landscapes: how households organized labor, how communities exchanged goods across considerable distances, how people buried their dead and remembered their ancestors, and how early forms of social differentiation began to appear in the archaeological record. None of these developments yet produced kings, states, or empires — larger regional political and ceremonial formations belong to later chapters, beginning with the Olmec in Chapter 6. But the villages examined in this chapter were where many of the social, economic, and ritual foundations of later Mesoamerican civilization first took shape, long before any ruler's name was recorded anywhere in the region.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the shift from foraging to food production in Mesoamerica should be understood as a long, uneven transition rather than a single revolutionary event.
  2. Distinguish plant management, cultivation, and domestication as related but analytically separate concepts.
  3. Distinguish agricultural dependence from sedentism, and explain why the two did not necessarily develop together or at the same pace.
  4. Trace the domestication of maize from wild teosinte populations in southwestern Mexico, including the archaeological and genetic evidence involved and the uncertainties that remain.
  5. Identify other domesticated or managed plants important to early Mesoamerican subsistence, including squash, beans, chili peppers, avocados, agave, and gourds.
  6. Analyze the archaeological evidence — architecture, storage facilities, grinding tools, middens, and burials — used to identify increasingly settled village life.
  7. Describe how household organization, labor, cooperation, and early social differentiation developed within emerging village communities.
  8. Explain the significance of long-distance exchange networks, particularly obsidian, for understanding regional interaction before the rise of states.
  9. Assess the role of ritual, burial practice, and symbolic material culture in early village communities without projecting later state-level religious institutions backward onto them.
  10. Apply the evidentiary reasoning introduced in Chapter 4 — including phytoliths, pollen, starch grains, macrobotanical remains, and radiocarbon context — to specific questions about domestication and settlement.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for why agriculture developed in Mesoamerica, recognizing that no single cause is likely to account for developments across every region.
  12. Connect the developments examined in this chapter to the emergence of more complex ceremonial and political formations examined in Chapter 6, without treating those later developments as an inevitable outcome.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 traced the peopling of the Americas and the broad transition, discussed briefly in its final sections, from small Paleoindian foraging bands to the more regionally diverse societies of the Archaic period. Chapter 3 examined the environmental diversity of Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia, the Caribbean, and other regions that shaped how those Archaic-period communities lived. Chapter 4 explained the evidentiary methods — archaeology, genetics, linguistics, ethnohistory, and Indigenous oral tradition — that allow historians to reconstruct societies that left no written records of their own. This chapter now applies those methods directly to Mesoamerica's Archaic period, covering the long span from the early Archaic period through the Initial Formative developments preceding San Lorenzo's rise, examined in Chapter 6.

Mesoamerica, as Chapter 3 explained, is not environmentally uniform. It includes semi-arid highland valleys such as Oaxaca and Tehuacán, tropical Gulf and Pacific coastal lowlands, temperate central highlands, and a range of intermediate environments in between. This environmental diversity matters enormously for the history examined in this chapter, because communities in different regions had access to different wild plants, different growing seasons, and different constraints on mobility and storage. As a result, the domestication of maize, beans, squash, and other plants did not proceed as a single unified process spreading outward from one point of origin, but as a set of related, overlapping, and regionally distinct historical trajectories.

Historians and archaeologists refer to the long period this chapter examines, extending from roughly 8000 to 2000 BCE, with considerable regional variation around the transition, as the Archaic period, a term introduced briefly in Chapter 2. During this period, communities across Mesoamerica gradually intensified their use of particular plant and animal resources, experimented with cultivation, and, in some regions, began occupying settlements for longer portions of the year. By the period's end, some communities had become substantially more sedentary, more dependent on cultivated plants, and more socially differentiated than their Paleoindian predecessors, even though many communities elsewhere in the Americas continued to live by hunting, gathering, and fishing for millennia afterward, and were not thereby any less historically significant.

Understanding this chapter also requires some familiarity with basic concepts in plant biology and archaeobotany. A cultivated plant is one that people deliberately plant, tend, or harvest, whether or not it has undergone genetic change as a result. A domesticated plant is one that has become genetically and morphologically distinct from its wild ancestor as a result of generations of human selection, typically losing traits that favored survival in the wild — such as the ability of seeds to disperse naturally — in favor of traits favored by human harvesters, such as larger seeds, non-shattering seed heads, or reduced natural defenses. Cultivation can occur without domestication, particularly in a plant's early history of management, and, as this chapter's opening emphasized, domestication does not automatically require full sedentism. Holding these distinctions clearly in mind is essential to understanding the evidence examined throughout this chapter.

Section 5.1

Mesoamerica after the Ice Age

By the beginning of the Archaic period, roughly 8000 BCE, the megafauna hunting economies described briefly at the end of Chapter 2 were disappearing across most of Mesoamerica, as mammoths, horses, and other large Ice Age mammals became extinct or retreated from the region. Communities responded by broadening their subsistence base, relying increasingly on smaller game, fish and shellfish where available, and an expanding range of wild plant foods, including various grasses, cactus fruits, tree pods, tubers, and seeds, whose seasonal availability varied considerably across Mesoamerica's highland valleys, coastal lowlands, and semi-arid basins described in Chapter 3.

This broadening of the diet, sometimes called a "broad-spectrum" subsistence strategy, is important for understanding what came next, because it meant that Archaic-period communities already possessed detailed, generations-deep knowledge of the growth cycles, harvesting seasons, and preparation requirements of dozens of wild plant species well before any of those plants became domesticated crops. Domestication, in other words, did not require inventing an entirely new relationship with plants; it built upon an already sophisticated body of ecological knowledge that Archaic-period foragers had developed through direct, sustained engagement with their local environments.

Mesoamerica's environmental diversity meant that this process unfolded differently in different regions. In semi-arid highland valleys such as Tehuacán and Oaxaca, seasonal scarcity encouraged intensive management of drought-tolerant plants such as squash and, eventually, maize. Along the Gulf Coast and the Pacific coast of Chiapas and Guatemala, rich estuarine, riverine, and marine resources provided a more reliable year-round food supply, which, as later sections will discuss, appears to have supported earlier and more substantial sedentism in some coastal communities even before agriculture became a dominant part of the diet. No single environment or regional sequence should be treated as representative of the whole of Mesoamerica during this period.

Section 5.2

From Gathering to Cultivation

The transition from wild plant gathering to cultivation did not require a single moment of invention. Archaeobotanists generally describe several overlapping stages: wild harvesting, in which people simply gather naturally occurring plants; incidental or deliberate plant management, in which people alter the local environment to favor useful plants, for example by clearing competing vegetation, protecting seedlings, or transplanting favored specimens; and cultivation proper, in which people deliberately plant seeds or cuttings, whether or not the plant has yet undergone genetic change as a result. Only after generations of cultivation, involving repeated human selection of particular traits, does a plant typically become domesticated in the fuller genetic sense discussed in Section 5.3.

This staged process helps explain why cultivation and domestication should not be treated as synonyms. A community could cultivate a wild-type plant for generations, replanting its seeds each season, without that plant's genetic makeup changing very much, if the plant's biology did not lend itself readily to rapid selection or if human harvesting practices did not consistently favor particular heritable traits. Conversely, once a plant had become substantially domesticated, communities might continue managing it using techniques developed originally for wild plant harvesting, blending old and new practices rather than replacing one system with another overnight.

It is equally important to emphasize that cultivation, and even domestication, did not require communities to abandon mobility. Ethnographic and archaeological evidence from many parts of the world, including Mesoamerica, indicates that mobile or semi-mobile groups frequently planted crops at favored locations, then moved on to other subsistence activities, returning later in the season to harvest what they had planted. Such "seasonal round" cultivation could persist for centuries before, if ever, a community settled into full-time residence at a single location. This is the first of the chapter's central distinctions: agriculture and sedentary village life are related processes, but they are not the same process, and one did not automatically produce the other.

Historical Vocabulary
Sedentism

Sedentism refers to the pattern of residing in one location for extended or year-round periods, rather than moving seasonally between multiple camps or settlements. Archaeologists identify increasing sedentism through a convergence of evidence — substantial and durable house construction, storage facilities capable of supporting residence across a full annual cycle, thick and continuously accumulated midden deposits, and burials associated with a specific settlement over time — rather than through any single indicator considered alone.

Sedentism should be distinguished carefully from agricultural dependence, discussed in Section 5.2. A community could cultivate crops for generations while remaining highly mobile, planting at favored locations during a seasonal round and returning later to harvest. Conversely, some communities, particularly those with access to rich and reliable wild resources such as the estuarine and marine environments of the Pacific coast discussed in Section 5.5, became substantially sedentary before agriculture supplied more than a modest share of their diet. Sedentism and agriculture are related historical developments in Mesoamerica, but neither automatically produced the other, and each followed its own regional and chronological trajectory.

Section 5.3

The Domestication of Maize

No plant matters more to the subsequent history of this textbook than maize, the grass known in English as corn. Understanding its domestication requires combining several distinct kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations, a theme this chapter returns to directly in Section 5.10.

Genetic research comparing modern maize varieties with populations of teosinte, a group of wild grasses native to Mexico and Central America, has established with a high degree of confidence that maize descends from a specific teosinte subspecies native to the seasonally dry tropical forests of the Balsas River watershed in what is today the Mexican state of Guerrero. This genetic evidence has identified a number of genes associated with major differences between teosinte and maize — including the number and arrangement of kernel rows, the hardness of the kernel casing, and the branching structure of the plant — some of which have unusually large individual effects on plant form. Modern genomic research, however, indicates that maize's transformation from teosinte cannot be reduced to a small handful of genes acting in isolation; it involved a broader, more polygenic reorganization of the plant's genome, and it was further complicated by substantial later gene flow between early domesticated maize and wild teosinte populations, including Zea mays subspecies mexicana, as domesticated maize spread into new environments. Domestication, in other words, was not simply a single early genetic bottleneck, but a continuing evolutionary process involving repeated interaction between cultivated and wild populations.

Students should distinguish two different kinds of archaeological evidence for early maize, since they document different things and appear at different times in the record. The first is microbotanical evidence — starch grains and phytoliths, both discussed in Section 5.10, that are microscopic and require laboratory analysis to detect but that can survive in contexts where visible plant material does not. The earliest currently reported and widely cited microbotanical evidence for maize under cultivation anywhere in Mesoamerica consists of maize starch grains and phytoliths recovered from Xihuatoxtla Shelter, a site in the Central Balsas watershed, from contexts dated to roughly 8,700 years before the present, or approximately 6750 BCE — a date very close to genetic estimates for the region and period of initial domestication. It is worth noting that this date derives from the dated archaeological context in which the microremains were recovered rather than from direct radiocarbon dating of the starch grains and phytoliths themselves, since attempts to date the microremains directly have not succeeded; the evidence nonetheless remains squarely archaeological, based on excavated material and its stratigraphic context, rather than a projection from genetic data alone.

The second kind of evidence is macrobotanical evidence — visible cob remains large enough to be excavated, described, and directly radiocarbon dated. This evidence appears considerably later than the microbotanical record. The famous small maize cobs recovered from Guilá Naquitz, Oaxaca, once assumed to date to roughly 5000 BCE, have since been directly AMS radiocarbon dated to approximately 4350 to 4050 BCE — more than two thousand years after the Xihuatoxtla microbotanical evidence, and roughly six thousand years before the present. Similarly, the long-celebrated early maize sequence from the Tehuacán Valley, once presented as some of the oldest maize evidence in the Americas, has been substantially revised following direct dating: securely dated cob material from the Tehuacán sequence, including specimens from the San Marcos rock-shelter, dates to roughly 3600 BCE or, in some reassessed cases, to approximately 5,100 years before the present — younger than the Guilá Naquitz series, not older, reversing the sequence long assumed in earlier scholarship.

This distinction matters for two reasons. First, it shows that the gap between the earliest archaeological evidence for maize domestication and the earliest surviving visible cobs is not a gap between "genetic estimate" and "physical evidence," as earlier textbooks sometimes suggested, but a gap between two different kinds of physical, archaeologically recovered evidence — microscopic and macroscopic — each of which survives under different conditions and was recovered through different methods. Second, it illustrates, in a very concrete way, the methodological lesson developed further in Section 5.10: the absence of visible macrobotanical remains at an early date does not mean domestication had not yet begun, since much earlier evidence may survive only in microscopic form.

The macrobotanical evidence that does survive tells an important story of gradual change rather than sudden transformation. The earliest directly dated maize cobs recovered from Guilá Naquitz are tiny by modern standards — often only a few centimeters long, with a handful of small, hard kernels arranged along a fragile cob quite different from the large, many-kernaled ears familiar today. Successive layers of cave deposits across multiple sites show cobs gradually increasing in size and changing in structure over the following several thousand years, consistent with continued human selection for larger, more productive, and more easily processed plants. This gradual sequence should caution students against imagining that early Mesoamerican farmers cultivated maize resembling the crop grown in the region — or purchased in a modern market — today; for a very long stretch of its early history, domesticated maize would have provided a comparatively modest contribution to the overall diet, one additional resource among the many wild and cultivated plants that Archaic-period communities continued to rely upon.

Maize's eventual historical importance rests on several interlocking qualities that became more pronounced as domestication proceeded: high potential productivity in favorable growing conditions; kernels that store well over long periods when properly dried; portability, which made maize valuable for trade, tribute, and travel; and a genetic flexibility that allowed distinct local varieties to be developed for different elevations, rainfall patterns, and soil types across Mesoamerica's varied environments. These same qualities help explain maize's later integration into the intensive agricultural systems, tribute economies, and religious symbolism of the civilizations examined in subsequent chapters — but those later developments should be understood as building upon, rather than already present within, the modest, still-developing crop discussed in this chapter.

Section 5.4

More Than Maize

A chapter that focused solely on maize would misrepresent the actual historical record. Archaeobotanical evidence indicates that several other plants underwent management and domestication in Mesoamerica during the Archaic period, in some cases earlier than maize itself, and that these crops developed along their own regional and chronological trajectories rather than as an automatic package alongside maize.

Squash species, including several distinct types cultivated for their flesh, seeds, and, in earlier stages, their hard rinds — which may initially have been valued as containers as much as for food — appear among the earliest domesticated plants in the Americas, with archaeobotanical evidence from sites including Guilá Naquitz and locations in the Balsas watershed suggesting management and domestication beginning by roughly 8000 to 6000 BCE, potentially predating maize domestication by a significant margin. Beans present a more complicated picture, and one that illustrates especially well how different kinds of evidence can produce different chronological pictures of the same underlying process. Genomic research on modern common bean populations infers that the independent Mesoamerican domestication of the common bean, Phaseolus vulgaris, began roughly 8,000 years ago — a timeline broadly comparable to squash. The directly dated archaeological macrofossil record, however, tells a very different story. Early bean remains recovered from cave sites such as Guilá Naquitz, once assumed to be very early domesticated specimens, are now understood, following direct AMS radiocarbon dating, to represent the continued gathering and management of wild bean populations rather than an already-domesticated crop; this direct dating substantially overturned an older chronology that had relied on indirect dating of associated deposits rather than the bean specimens themselves. On current direct dating, the oldest Mexican bean macroremains that securely document the domesticated common bean are surprisingly late — roughly 2,500 years before the present in the Tehuacán Valley and roughly 2,100 years before the present in Oaxaca. This should not be read as evidence that bean domestication itself began only that recently; rather, it most likely reflects the poor preservation of bean remains relative to the genuinely much earlier domestication process indicated by genomic evidence, together with the small number of specimens available for direct dating. Students should treat this as an instructive case in which genomic and archaeobotanical evidence currently point toward different chronologies for the same underlying process, rather than assuming that one line of evidence has simply proven the other wrong.

Chili peppers present a similarly protracted and still incompletely resolved history. Chili remains recovered from early cave deposits, including Guilá Naquitz, document long-standing human use of wild and semi-wild capsicum plants, valued for flavoring, preservation, and likely ritual and medicinal purposes as well as nutrition. However, current research describes the morphological domestication of chili pepper in Mexico as a protracted and geographically complex process rather than an event securely datable to any single early period; specimens recovered from early contexts are frequently closer in form to the wild progenitor than to a fully domesticated plant, and researchers caution against classifying early preceramic chili macroremains as proof of completed domestication. This chapter therefore describes early chili pepper history in terms of sustained use and management, with domestication understood as an ongoing and only partially resolved process rather than an established early achievement. Avocados were exploited from an early period as well — evidence from highland sites, including Tehuacán, documents avocado use by roughly 7,000 to 6,000 years before the present — but clearer morphological evidence of avocado domestication, reflected in changing seed and rind dimensions, appears considerably later, generally placed around 3,000 to 2,000 years before the present. Early avocado use and avocado domestication should therefore be treated as two distinct claims rather than a single early development. Agave, a spiny, drought-tolerant plant well suited to Mesoamerica's semi-arid highlands, was managed and eventually cultivated for its fibers, its sap, and, in some regions and periods, as a significant caloric resource, while various gourd species served practical functions as containers, tools, and fishing-net floats long before, and alongside, their use as food.

This diversity of crops matters historically for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Mesoamerican domestication was not a single event but a broader, ongoing system of plant management involving numerous species, several of which were domesticated independently of one another and in different regions. Second, it means that the later, usually maize-centered Mesoamerican cultivation system known in Nahuatl-speaking communities as the milpa — which often, though not invariably, involved intercropping maize with beans, squash, chilies, and other locally important plants, with its precise composition and management varying considerably by region and period — was itself a historical assemblage, brought together gradually as different crops with different domestication histories were eventually cultivated together in complementary arrangements. Students should resist projecting this later, integrated agricultural system, or any single fixed version of it, backward onto the earliest stages of Mesoamerican cultivation discussed in this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Domestication

Domestication refers to the long-term process by which sustained human selection transforms a wild plant or animal species into a form genetically and often morphologically distinct from its wild ancestor, typically favoring traits useful to human harvesters — larger seeds, non-shattering seed heads that keep grain from scattering before harvest, reduced natural toxins, or larger edible portions — over traits that favored the plant's survival without human assistance.

Domestication should be distinguished from cultivation, which refers simply to the deliberate planting, tending, or harvesting of a plant, whether or not it has undergone genetic change as a result. A plant can be cultivated for many generations while remaining genetically close to its wild ancestor, particularly if its biology does not readily permit rapid selection or if human management practices do not consistently favor particular heritable traits. Domestication is best understood as a gradual, cumulative outcome of sustained cultivation and selection over an extended period, rather than a single discrete event that either has or has not occurred.

Section 5.5

From Seasonal Camps to Settled Villages

Alongside the gradual domestication of plants discussed in the preceding sections, Mesoamerican communities underwent a second, related but distinct transformation: a gradual shift from highly mobile foraging camps toward more substantial, more frequently reoccupied, and eventually more permanent settlements. This section examines the archaeological evidence for that shift and the reasoning historians and archaeologists use to interpret it.

Early in the Archaic period, most Mesoamerican communities appear to have lived in small, mobile bands that moved seasonally across defined territories, occupying temporary camps, rock shelters, and cave sites for days, weeks, or a single season before moving on in pursuit of seasonally available plant and animal resources. Archaeological evidence for these early camps tends to be relatively sparse and ephemeral: scattered hearths, discarded stone tools, and modest quantities of processed plant remains, without substantial architecture or evidence of significant investment in a particular location.

Over the following several thousand years, evidence from multiple regions indicates a gradual increase in the size, durability, and frequency of reoccupation of settlement sites. Archaeologists look for several converging kinds of evidence to identify this shift: more substantial house structures, built with post-and-thatch construction or, in some regions, with foundations of packed earth or stone, replacing the more ephemeral shelters of earlier periods; storage facilities such as subterranean pits, sometimes lined with plaster or fired clay, capable of preserving surplus grain, seeds, or other foods across a full annual cycle; increasingly substantial midden deposits, or accumulated household refuse, whose depth and density can indicate how long and how continuously a location was occupied; and formal or semi-formal burials placed within or near settlements, suggesting an emerging relationship between specific communities and specific places, a theme developed further in Section 5.8.

The timing of this shift toward genuine year-round village settlement varied considerably by region, and current chronologies place it later, and more unevenly, than older textbooks sometimes suggested. On the Pacific coast of Chiapas, well-documented ceramic-using sedentary village communities, supported by rich and relatively stable estuarine and marine food sources, appear securely by roughly 1550 BCE. On the Gulf Coast, the site of San Lorenzo — examined at length in Chapter 6 — began as a small village settlement by roughly 1800 BCE, well before its later development into a major regional ceremonial and political center beginning around 1400 BCE. In the Maya lowlands, by contrast, a comparable shift toward ceramics and settled community life did not become widespread until roughly 1000 BCE, considerably later than on the Pacific and Gulf coasts. Archaeologists look for a convergence of evidence to identify genuine year-round settlement at any of these sites: clusters of substantial houses, evidence of continuous multi-generational occupation, thick and continuously accumulated midden deposits, and burials associated with the settlement over time.

One inference that must be handled with particular care involves ceramics. The appearance of pottery at a site is sometimes treated as automatic proof of sedentism, on the assumption that fired clay vessels are too fragile, heavy, or labor-intensive to be worth making unless a community intends to remain in one place. Archaeologists caution against this inference: ethnographic and archaeological evidence from multiple world regions demonstrates that semi-sedentary or even seasonally mobile communities can and do make, use, and transport ceramics, so that pottery may correlate with changing patterns of mobility without independently proving year-round residence at any given site. Ceramics should therefore be treated as one useful piece of evidence among several, not as conclusive proof of sedentism on its own — another illustration of the evidentiary caution developed further in Section 5.10. This shift toward greater sedentism did not occur simultaneously or identically across Mesoamerica, and communities in different regions, following different local resource strategies, arrived at settled village life along their own distinct timelines, illustrating once again that sedentism and agricultural dependence, while related, followed their own distinct regional trajectories.

Section 5.6

Household, Labor, and Community

As communities spent longer periods at particular settlements, the archaeological record begins to reveal changes in social organization that extended well beyond subsistence alone. Household archaeology — the study of individual house structures and their surrounding activity areas — allows historians to reconstruct, in a general way, how labor, resources, and daily life were organized within these emerging village communities.

Evidence from house floors, associated tool assemblages, and food-processing debris suggests that households functioned as basic units of production and consumption, likely organized around kinship, though the precise nature of that kinship — whether nuclear families, extended families, or larger lineage groups shared individual structures or clusters of structures — is difficult to establish with confidence from material remains alone and probably varied across regions and periods. Grinding stones, found in large numbers at village sites, indicate that processing plant foods, particularly maize once it became more prominent in the diet, required substantial and probably recurring labor, likely performed within household settings.

Archaeologists have also identified evidence for cooperative labor extending beyond individual households, including communal storage and processing facilities, and, at some sites, evidence for coordinated construction projects exceeding what a single household could easily accomplish alone. Such cooperation likely extended to agricultural labor itself: clearing land, planting, weeding, and harvesting on a schedule tied to seasonal cycles probably required cooperation among multiple households, whether organized through kinship obligation, reciprocal labor exchange, or emerging forms of community leadership.

It is at this point in the archaeological record that historians begin to identify tentative evidence for social differentiation — that is, differences in wealth, status, or access to resources among individuals or households within the same community. This evidence includes variation in house size and construction quality, differential access to exotic or labor-intensive goods discussed in Section 5.7, and, in some cases, differences in burial treatment discussed in Section 5.8. This evidence must be interpreted cautiously. It does not indicate the presence of hereditary elites, formal political offices, or class systems comparable to those examined in later chapters on Formative-period civilizations; it indicates, more modestly, that some village communities of this period were not perfectly egalitarian and that certain individuals, households, or lineages may have enjoyed greater prestige, wealth, or influence than others, for reasons that the archaeological evidence often cannot fully specify — successful leadership, ritual knowledge, productive land, favorable kinship ties, or simple chance.

Gender is a particularly difficult dimension of household organization to reconstruct from this period's material evidence. Ethnographic analogy, skeletal evidence regarding patterns of physical activity, and the association of certain tool types with particular activity areas have led some archaeologists to propose likely, though not certain, divisions of labor — for example, an association between grinding tools and food processing tasks that later ethnohistoric sources often associate with women, and an association between hunting tools and activities more often associated with men in later documented societies. Such inferences should be treated as informed hypotheses rather than established fact, since they risk projecting patterns documented in much later, textually recorded societies backward onto communities that left no comparable testimony about their own social arrangements.

Section 5.7

Exchange Networks before States

Village communities of the Archaic and Early Formative periods were not isolated. Archaeological evidence demonstrates that materials, and presumably the people, ideas, and social relationships that accompanied them, moved across considerable distances well before the rise of any state-level political organization in Mesoamerica.

Obsidian, a volcanic glass prized for its ability to be worked into extremely sharp cutting edges, provides the clearest evidence for this early exchange. Because obsidian forms only at specific volcanic sources, and because each source has a distinctive chemical signature, archaeologists can use geochemical sourcing techniques to trace obsidian artifacts recovered at a given site back to the particular volcanic outcrop from which the material originated, sometimes hundreds of kilometers away. The recovery of obsidian at village sites far from any known source demonstrates that some combination of long-distance travel, trade, and inter-community exchange relationships connected communities across substantial portions of Mesoamerica well before the Formative period covered in Chapter 6.

Other materials provide corroborating evidence for these early networks. Marine shell, recovered at inland sites far from any coastline, indicates connections between coastal and interior communities. Certain mineral pigments, used for decoration, ritual, or craft production, likewise appear at sites distant from their geological sources. Where ceramics appear later in this period, stylistic similarities across considerable distances have sometimes been interpreted as evidence of shared technological knowledge, exchange, or social interaction, though archaeologists are generally cautious about assuming that stylistic similarity alone proves direct contact rather than independent development or more indirect, multi-step transmission.

The existence of these exchange networks carries significant historical implications. It demonstrates that regional interaction, and the social relationships that such interaction implies — alliance, kinship, ritual partnership, or simple economic exchange — long predated the large states and empires examined later in this volume. It also suggests that the ceremonial and political developments of the Formative period, beginning with the Olmec in Chapter 6, drew upon exchange relationships, shared symbolic vocabularies, and inter-regional connections that had already been developing for centuries, rather than emerging from a single isolated source. Students should understand these networks as evidence of a genuinely interconnected pre-state Mesoamerican world, not as a prelude to a single point of civilizational origin.

Section 5.8

Ritual, Burial, and Community Identity

As communities became more settled, they also developed increasingly elaborate ways of marking, remembering, and organizing their relationships to particular places, ancestors, and to one another. Archaeological evidence for ritual and symbolic life during this period is necessarily incomplete, since much of what likely mattered most to these communities — spoken traditions, songs, and beliefs — left no direct physical trace. What survives, however, is suggestive.

Burials associated with village sites, sometimes accompanied by modest grave goods such as tools, ornaments, or specific arrangements of the body, indicate that communities were developing formal practices for marking death and, quite possibly, for maintaining relationships between the living and their deceased kin. The placement of burials within or near residential areas, rather than in separate, distant locations, may reflect an emerging sense of ancestral connection to a particular settlement — a theme that would become considerably more elaborate in the ancestor-focused religious practices of later Mesoamerican civilizations examined in subsequent chapters, though this chapter's evidence supports only a modest and tentative version of that later pattern.

Figurines, typically small hand-modeled clay or stone objects, often depicting human forms, appear at a number of village sites dating to the later Archaic and Early Formative periods. Their precise function remains debated among archaeologists: proposed interpretations include roles in fertility ritual, ancestor commemoration, healing practices, teaching or play, and social or status display, and different figurines from different contexts may well have served different purposes. Students should resist the temptation to assign a single, confident explanation to this varied and still incompletely understood body of evidence.

At a small number of later Archaic and Early Formative sites, archaeologists have identified evidence for spaces that appear to have served communal or ceremonial functions distinct from ordinary household space — cleared plazas, small earthen platforms, or specially prepared surfaces — though these features remain modest in scale compared to the monumental ceremonial architecture examined in Chapter 6. It is important to state clearly what this chapter's evidence does not support: there is little basis in the archaeological record of this period for assuming that ritual practice was primarily a mechanism through which emerging elites manipulated agricultural surplus or extracted labor from the wider community. Such interpretations, sometimes advanced for later, better-documented state societies, would be an anachronistic projection if applied uncritically to the village communities examined in this chapter. The more defensible conclusion is that subsistence, household organization, community identity, and ritual life during this period were closely interconnected, developing together rather than one straightforwardly causing or controlling the others.

Section 5.9

Environmental Modification and Agricultural Intensification

As communities became more dependent on cultivated plants and more settled in particular locations, they also began modifying their local environments more deliberately and more permanently than the more mobile foraging strategies of earlier centuries had required. This section briefly surveys the beginnings of that process, while deliberately avoiding the more elaborate agricultural systems — including the raised-field chinampa agriculture of the Basin of Mexico and the administered terrace systems of the later Andes — that belong to much later chapters.

Clearing vegetation to prepare fields for planting, sometimes through the use of fire, represents one of the earliest and most widespread forms of environmental modification associated with early cultivation, and evidence for increased charcoal deposition in sediment cores from several Mesoamerican regions during the Archaic period has been interpreted by some researchers as indirect evidence of this practice, although natural fire regimes complicate this interpretation and require careful, site-specific analysis. In some lowland and riverine environments, communities appear to have taken advantage of natural flooding cycles to plant crops in fertile, periodically inundated soils, a comparatively low-investment strategy that did not require the elaborate water-management infrastructure of later periods.

By the later part of the period examined in this chapter, roughly 2000 to 1200 BCE, evidence from several regions suggests increasing investment in particular cultivated landscapes, including soil modification and other localized evidence of sustained land use, and a generally increasing reliance on cultivated plants relative to wild resources in the overall diet, as indicated by carbon stable isotope analysis of human skeletal remains, a method introduced in Chapter 4 that allows researchers to estimate the relative dietary contribution of plants using different photosynthetic pathways — broadly, so-called C3 plants, which include most wild Mesoamerican plant foods, versus C4 plants, a category that importantly includes maize. Because some other plants also follow the C4 pathway and isotopic values for maize, other C4 plants, and certain CAM plants can overlap, this method can help track increasing maize consumption only where ecological, archaeobotanical, and other dietary evidence supports that specific interpretation, rather than by isotopic values alone. This intensification should be understood as a gradual and regionally uneven trend rather than a uniform transformation completed by a fixed date across all of Mesoamerica.

Section 5.10

What Archaeologists Can Actually Know

This chapter has drawn throughout on the evidentiary methods introduced in Chapter 4, and this section makes that methodology explicit, since the reasoning involved is essential for evaluating the claims made throughout this chapter and, indeed, throughout the rest of this textbook.

Establishing that a particular plant was cultivated, let alone domesticated, at a particular time and place requires combining several independent lines of evidence, each of which carries its own strengths and limitations. Macrobotanical remains — visible plant parts such as seeds, cobs, or rinds, preserved through desiccation in dry environments like the caves of Oaxaca and Tehuacán, through charring, or occasionally through waterlogging — provide the most direct physical evidence, but they survive unevenly across different environments and are often fragmentary. Phytoliths, microscopic silica structures that form within plant cells and survive long after the rest of the plant has decayed, can sometimes distinguish domesticated from wild varieties of certain crops even when no macrobotanical remains survive at all. Starch grain analysis, which examines microscopic starch residues preserved on the surfaces of stone tools such as grinding stones, can identify which plants a particular tool was used to process, sometimes revealing evidence of maize processing at sites or in contexts where no maize remains themselves have survived. Pollen analysis, or palynology, examines pollen preserved in lake and wetland sediment cores to track changes in regional vegetation over time, including the expansion of maize cultivation, though pollen can travel considerable distances and requires careful interpretation to distinguish cultivated fields from more distant sources. Radiocarbon dating, discussed in Chapter 2, remains essential for establishing the age of organic plant remains, though, as that chapter explained, radiocarbon dates carry margins of error and depend on secure archaeological context. Finally, ancient DNA and comparative genetics, discussed in Section 5.3, allow researchers to trace domestication events and identify the wild ancestors of domesticated crops, though genetic evidence on its own generally cannot specify an exact date, location, or the particular lived experience of domestication without being combined with archaeological evidence.

Broad reconstructions of domestication and settlement are strongest when multiple independent lines of evidence converge; a single line of evidence may establish a narrower claim quite securely — a directly dated diagnostic specimen, for example, can firmly establish that a particular plant was present and used at a particular time and place — but usually cannot, on its own, reconstruct the fuller process of domestication or settlement. Historians and archaeologists therefore look for convergence: do macrobotanical remains, phytolith evidence, starch residues, pollen records, and genetic data point toward a broadly consistent conclusion? When they do, as with the broad outline of maize domestication discussed in Section 5.3, confidence in the larger reconstruction increases considerably, even as important chronological and regional details remain actively debated. When different lines of evidence conflict, or when only one kind of evidence is available, historians must present broader conclusions more cautiously, exactly as Chapter 2 modeled in its treatment of the Monte Verde controversy.

Evidence & Interpretation

How Do Archaeologists Know When a Plant Became Domesticated?

Consider the case of maize. Researchers have available: (1) the size and structural characteristics of surviving cobs; (2) phytoliths recovered from soil and tool surfaces; (3) pollen grains preserved in lake sediments; (4) starch residues on grinding stones; (5) radiocarbon dates on the plant remains themselves or on securely associated organic material; and (6) genetic comparisons between modern maize and wild teosinte populations.

Each source of evidence, considered alone, has a significant limitation. Cob morphology changes gradually, so a "transitional" cob may be difficult to classify confidently as wild, managed, or domesticated. Phytoliths and starch grains can sometimes be produced by more than one closely related plant species, complicating identification. Pollen can travel long distances on the wind, so its presence does not prove cultivation at the exact location where it was recovered. Radiocarbon dates depend entirely on secure association between the dated material and the human activity in question. Genetic evidence can estimate roughly when two populations diverged but generally cannot specify an exact calendar date or geographic location with precision.

Student task: Explain why historians consider a domestication claim strongest when multiple independent lines of evidence, gathered using different methods and, ideally, from different sites, converge on a similar conclusion, and explain what a responsible historian should do when the available evidence does not converge.

Evidence & Interpretation

Does Maize Cultivation Prove Permanent Settlement?

A student might assume that once a community begins cultivating maize, it must have become a settled, year-round village. This chapter's evidence does not support that assumption. Communities documented in the Tehuacán Valley sequence, for example, continued to occupy seasonal cave shelters and campsites for a very long period after maize cultivation began, moving between different resource zones across the year even as they planted, tended, and harvested small maize plots at particular locations.

To establish genuine sedentism, historians look beyond the presence of a cultivated crop toward a broader combination of evidence: substantial, well-built house architecture; storage facilities capable of supporting year-round residence; thick, continuously accumulated midden deposits indicating long and repeated occupation; burials associated with a specific settlement, suggesting a lasting relationship between a community and a place; and, where available, seasonal indicators from plant and animal remains that can reveal whether a site was occupied across multiple seasons or only briefly each year.

Student task: Using the categories of evidence described above, explain why a single cultivated maize cob recovered from a site would not, by itself, be sufficient evidence that the people who grew it lived there year-round, and identify what additional evidence would be needed to support that stronger claim.

Section 5.11

From Villages toward the Formative World

By roughly 2000 to 1200 BCE, a span archaeologists often call the Initial Formative period, the developments traced throughout this chapter had produced, in several regions of Mesoamerica, communities that were considerably larger, more sedentary, more dependent on cultivated plants, more socially differentiated, and more thoroughly connected to distant communities through exchange networks than their Archaic-period predecessors had been many centuries earlier. These developments created new possibilities: for expanded population, for more elaborate ceremonial life, for emerging forms of leadership, and for the concentration of people, resources, and symbolic authority at particular locations.

It is essential, however, that students not read this outcome backward into a false inevitability. Not every Mesoamerican community followed the same trajectory, at the same pace, or toward the same outcome. Many communities across Mesoamerica, and across the wider Americas discussed in Chapter 2, continued to rely substantially on hunting, gathering, and fishing for centuries or millennia after the developments described in this chapter, without this making their societies any less historically significant, sophisticated, or successful on their own terms. Even among communities that did become more sedentary and agriculturally dependent, the particular combination of population growth, exchange, ritual elaboration, and social differentiation that eventually contributed to the rise of large ceremonial centers occurred in some regions and not others, for reasons that remain the subject of ongoing scholarly investigation, discussed further in the Historical Debate feature below.

The Gulf Coast lowlands of southeastern Mexico, examined in detail in Chapter 6, represent one region where these long-term Archaic and Initial Formative developments — sedentary village life, agricultural intensification, exchange networks, and increasing social differentiation — eventually contributed to the emergence of a large-scale ceremonial and political center at San Lorenzo, generally regarded as the earliest major Olmec center. It is important to keep three dates distinct: San Lorenzo began as a modest village settlement by roughly 1800 BCE, part of the same broad Initial Formative pattern of increasing sedentism examined throughout this chapter; the site began developing into a substantially larger and more elaborate regional center starting around 1400 BCE; and it reached its height, or florescence, as the first major Olmec center — the subject of Chapter 6 — during the period of roughly 1200 to 1000 BCE. Some four centuries, in other words, separate San Lorenzo's beginning as an ordinary village from the start of its development into a major regional center, and that span of time was filled with exactly the kind of gradual village-level development this chapter has examined. This chapter's final task is to make clear that San Lorenzo's later prominence did not appear from nothing, and did not appear because Olmec communities possessed some unique cultural genius absent elsewhere. It appeared because centuries of village-level historical development — the subject of this chapter — had, in that particular region, produced the demographic, economic, and social conditions within which a larger and more elaborate form of political and ceremonial organization eventually became possible. Chapter 6 examines what happened next.

The Guilá Naquitz Plant Remains

Site: Guilá Naquitz rock-shelter, Valley of Oaxaca, Mexico · Excavation: Principally conducted by archaeologist Kent V. Flannery and collaborators beginning in the 1960s, with later reanalysis incorporating direct radiocarbon dating of individual plant specimens · Type: Archaeobotanical assemblage recovered from a dry cave deposit

Context: Guilá Naquitz is a small, dry rock-shelter set into the region's volcanic tuff cliffs whose arid interior preserved an exceptional quantity of desiccated plant remains across many centuries of intermittent human use. Excavators recovered squash rinds and seeds, bean pods, chili pepper remains, and small maize cob fragments, alongside stone tools and other evidence of periodic occupation, allowing researchers to reconstruct a long sequence of changing plant use at a single, well-documented location.

Rather than reproducing technical laboratory data, students should understand the assemblage's significance in summary form: repeated, direct radiocarbon dating of individual squash rind and seed fragments recovered from the site's stratified layers has provided some of the most securely dated early evidence for squash domestication anywhere in the Americas, while associated maize cob fragments, though from somewhat later layers, have contributed to the broader archaeobotanical sequence documenting maize's gradual increase in cob size and structural change over time.

Analysis: The Guilá Naquitz assemblage is valuable precisely because it allows researchers to date individual plant specimens directly, rather than relying solely on the age of associated charcoal or other material that might not be securely linked to the plant remains in question. This kind of direct dating substantially strengthens confidence in the resulting chronology, illustrating the general evidentiary principle, introduced in Section 5.10, that dating the object of interest directly is preferable to dating only materials assumed to be associated with it.

Historical Debate

Why Did Agriculture Develop in Mesoamerica?

The question: Given that Archaic-period foraging strategies were often successful and required less sustained labor than cultivation eventually demanded, why did communities in Mesoamerica gradually shift toward increasing reliance on domesticated plants?

Interpretation A — Environmental and climatic change: Some researchers emphasize changing climatic conditions during the early and middle Holocene, including shifts in rainfall patterns and seasonal variability, which may have made certain wild resources less reliable and encouraged experimentation with cultivation as a risk-reducing strategy in resource-scarce environments such as the semi-arid Tehuacán and Oaxaca valleys.
Interpretation B — Population growth and resource pressure: Other researchers argue that gradual population growth increased pressure on locally available wild resources, making the more labor-intensive but potentially more productive strategy of cultivation increasingly attractive or necessary as foraging alone became less able to reliably support a growing number of people in a given territory.
Interpretation C — Deliberate experimentation and social or ritual incentives: A third line of interpretation emphasizes that some early cultivated plants, particularly squash and bottle gourd, may have been valued initially for reasons other than caloric need — as containers, ritual objects, or items of curiosity — and that continued management of already-familiar wild plants, undertaken as part of ordinary seasonal scheduling and risk management rather than out of scarcity or necessity, gradually produced domestication as an incidental long-term outcome rather than a deliberate strategic goal.

Evidence: Each interpretation draws upon overlapping archaeobotanical, paleoclimatic, and settlement-pattern evidence, but assigns different relative weight to environmental pressure, demographic pressure, and cultural or ritual motivation.

Why it matters: Most current specialists regard these explanations as complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and caution against assuming that a single universal cause can explain agricultural origins across every Mesoamerican region equally. Different communities, in different environments, likely experienced different combinations of these pressures and incentives.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 5.1 through 5.4, explain why historians increasingly favor multi-causal explanations for the origins of agriculture over single-cause theories, and identify what kind of additional evidence might help historians weigh the relative importance of environmental, demographic, and cultural factors in a specific region.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Transition: Perspectives on Village Life

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across many sites. They are not quotations from any named individual, since no written records survive from this period.

From the perspective of a household elder managing stored maize and squash: Careful storage and rationing of cultivated food across the dry season could mean the difference between security and hardship, making decisions about how much to plant, harvest, and preserve a matter of considerable daily importance to the household.
From the perspective of a still highly mobile forager in a neighboring region: Wild plant and animal resources continued to provide a reliable, well-understood livelihood without requiring the sustained labor of clearing fields and tending crops, and there was no obvious reason to abandon a successful way of life simply because neighboring communities were experimenting with cultivation.
From the perspective of a toolmaker producing obsidian blades for exchange: A skillfully worked obsidian blade, made from material obtained through exchange relationships reaching far beyond one's own community, connected daily craft work to a much wider world of trade, travel, and social relationship extending well past the boundaries of any single village.
From the perspective of a person responsible for burying a deceased kinsperson near the settlement: Placing the dead close to where the living continued to work and live created an enduring bond between a particular family or community and a particular place, a bond that later generations would inherit along with the land, the stored knowledge of the crops, and the responsibilities of the household.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Forager to Villager

What Changed

  • Subsistence shifted gradually from broad-spectrum foraging toward increasing reliance on cultivated and eventually domesticated plants, at different rates in different regions
  • Settlement patterns shifted from small, highly mobile seasonal camps toward larger, more durable, and eventually year-round villages
  • Household and community organization grew more complex, with evidence for cooperative labor and, in some communities, emerging social differentiation
  • Exchange networks expanded in scale and regularity, connecting communities across considerable distances
  • Ritual and burial practices became more elaborate and more closely tied to specific settlements

What Stayed the Same

  • Communities throughout the period continued to rely on wild plant and animal resources alongside cultivated crops, rather than abandoning foraging entirely
  • Households and kin-based communities remained the basic organizing units of daily life and labor
  • Mesoamerica's underlying environmental diversity, established in Chapter 3, continued to shape regional variation throughout this period
  • No single region or community's experience can be taken as representative of the whole of Mesoamerica

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the substantial changes and the important continuities of this period prevents students from imagining a clean, sudden break between "foraging Mesoamerica" and "farming Mesoamerica," when the actual historical record documents a long, gradual, and regionally varied transformation.

Gradual domestication of maize and squash, alongside continued management and incorporation of other plants, including beans and chili peppers, whose domestication histories are less securely resolved in the surviving archaeological record; increasing storability of cultivated foods; growing familiarity with specific landscapes across generations.

Immediate developments

More durable housing; construction of storage facilities; longer and more frequent reoccupation of favored settlement locations; increased population concentration at particular sites.

Possible further consequences

Expanded need for cooperative labor; growth of exchange relationships to obtain non-local materials; opportunities for emerging status differences; development of more elaborate and place-based ritual and burial practices.

Historical complexity

None of these outcomes was automatic or uniform. Some communities intensified cultivation without becoming fully sedentary; others became sedentary while continuing to rely heavily on wild resources.

Historical complexity: Increasing food production and increasing sedentism reinforced one another in many communities, but the relationship was not a fixed formula, and historians must examine each region's evidence individually rather than assuming a single universal sequence.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Day in an Early Mesoamerican Village

The following passage is a historically informed reconstruction based on archaeological evidence discussed throughout this chapter. It is not an eyewitness account, since no written testimony survives from this period, and it should be read as an illustration of plausible daily activity rather than a documented record of specific individuals.

Before dawn, members of a household might already be at work grinding maize kernels, softened overnight, into meal using a stone quern and handstone recovered in similar form at many Archaic and Formative village sites. Others would tend to stored squash and other preserved plant foods, alongside gathered and cultivated foods whose importance varied by region, checking for spoilage and rationing supplies against the coming season. Through the morning, some household members might travel to nearby fields to weed, water, or watch over ripening crops, while others gathered wild plant foods still important to the diet, collected firewood, or repaired tools and structures using materials obtained through both local resources and long-distance exchange, including obsidian blades whose origin lay in volcanic sources far beyond the village itself.

Midday activities might include food preparation, craft production such as basketry or textile work using plant fibers, and the supervision of children, who likely learned essential ecological and craft knowledge through direct participation in household tasks from an early age. Toward evening, households might gather to share a meal, discuss the allocation of labor for coming agricultural tasks, or participate in modest ritual observances tied to the burial grounds or small ceremonial spaces increasingly associated with the settlement. Archaeological evidence cannot recover the details of conversation, belief, or individual experience from this period, but the material record — grinding stones worn smooth from use, storage pits carefully maintained, burials thoughtfully placed, and obsidian tools carried from distant sources — testifies to lives organized around sustained labor, careful planning, and enduring relationships to both people and places.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined one of the most consequential long-term transformations in Mesoamerican history: the gradual shift from mobile, broad-spectrum foraging toward cultivated agriculture, more sedentary village life, expanding exchange networks, and early forms of social differentiation. Rather than presenting this transformation as a sudden "agricultural revolution," the chapter has emphasized that domestication, sedentism, and social change each developed gradually, unevenly, and along partly independent timelines across Mesoamerica's environmentally diverse regions.

Students have traced the domestication of maize from wild teosinte populations in the Balsas River watershed, examining both the earliest microbotanical evidence — starch grains and phytoliths recovered from Xihuatoxtla Shelter, dated to roughly 6750 BCE — and the later macrobotanical evidence, including directly AMS-dated cob remains from Guilá Naquitz and the Tehuacán Valley, documenting maize's gradual increase in size and structural complexity over thousands of years. The chapter has placed maize within a broader domestication system that also included squash, beans, chili peppers, avocados, agave, and gourds, emphasizing that beans and chili peppers have more complicated domestication histories — directly dated Mexican bean macroremains are surprisingly late despite genomic evidence for a much earlier domestication process, while the chronology and geography of chili domestication remain incompletely resolved — and resisting the temptation to let maize alone stand for the entire process.

The chapter has also traced the gradual emergence of settled village life, examining the archaeological evidence — architecture, storage facilities, middens, and burials — that allows historians to identify increasing sedentism, while insisting throughout that agricultural dependence and sedentism, though related, did not necessarily develop together or at the same pace in every region. It has examined how household organization, cooperative labor, and modest but genuine social differentiation developed within these emerging village communities, and how long-distance exchange networks, especially those documented through obsidian sourcing, connected these communities across considerable distances well before the rise of any Mesoamerican state.

Students have also examined the beginnings of ritual and ceremonial life in this period, including burial practices, figurines, and modest communal spaces, while being cautioned against projecting later, more elaborate state-level religious and political institutions backward onto communities whose evidence supports only a much more modest interpretation. The chapter has made explicit the evidentiary reasoning — combining macrobotanical remains, phytoliths, starch grains, pollen analysis, radiocarbon dating, and genetic evidence — that allows historians to respons­ibly evaluate claims about domestication and settlement, directly applying the methodological principles introduced in Chapter 4.

Finally, the chapter has positioned the developments it examines as a foundation for, but not an inevitable path toward, the emergence of the Olmec ceremonial centers examined in Chapter 6. Village-level developments in agriculture, settlement, exchange, and social organization created new historical possibilities in certain regions, particularly the Gulf Coast lowlands, but these possibilities were realized unevenly across Mesoamerica, and many communities continued to live by other means for centuries afterward, without this making their histories any less significant.

Chapter 5 has asked how communities in Mesoamerica began transforming plants, landscapes, subsistence, and settlement into the first enduring village worlds. Chapter 6 turns to what happened when some of these long-term processes contributed to a larger and more elaborate ceremonial and political formation on the Gulf Coast: the emergence of the Olmec center of San Lorenzo.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Domestication
The long-term process by which sustained human selection genetically and often morphologically differentiates a species from its wild ancestor, typically favoring traits useful to human harvesters.
Cultivation
The deliberate planting, tending, or harvesting of a plant, whether or not it has undergone genetic change as a result.
Plant management
Deliberate human alteration of a local environment to favor useful wild plants, an early stage often preceding formal cultivation.
Sedentism
The pattern of residing in one location for extended or year-round periods, related to but distinct from agricultural dependence.
Archaic period
The long period, roughly 8000 to 2000 BCE in Mesoamerica, marked by gradual plant management, domestication, and increasing sedentism, giving way thereafter to the Initial and Early Formative periods.
Teosinte
A group of wild grasses native to Mexico and Central America, genetically ancestral to domesticated maize.
Macrobotanical remains
Visible plant parts, such as seeds or cobs, preserved through desiccation, charring, or waterlogging and used as direct archaeobotanical evidence.
Phytolith
A microscopic silica structure formed within plant cells, sometimes distinguishing wild from domesticated plant varieties.
Starch grain analysis
A method examining microscopic starch residues on tool surfaces to identify which plants a tool was used to process.
Palynology
The study of pollen preserved in sediment cores, used to track changes in regional vegetation over time.
Midden
An accumulated deposit of household or settlement refuse, used by archaeologists to assess diet, tool use, and duration of occupation.
Grinding stone
A stone tool used for processing plant foods, particularly maize, into meal or flour.
Storage pit
A subterranean facility used to preserve surplus food across an annual cycle, one line of evidence that can contribute to an inference of increasing sedentism.
Household archaeology
The study of individual house structures and associated activity areas to reconstruct social and economic organization.
Division of labor
The distribution of different tasks among different members of a household or community, difficult to reconstruct with certainty for this period.
Social differentiation
Differences in wealth, status, or access to resources among individuals or households within a community.
Exchange network
A system of long-distance movement of goods, and likely people and ideas, connecting communities before the rise of states.
Obsidian sourcing
Geochemical analysis tracing obsidian artifacts to their specific volcanic source, used as evidence of long-distance exchange.
Ancestor veneration
Possible ancestor-related practices inferred cautiously from burial placement and other evidence in this period; direct beliefs cannot be reconstructed securely from material remains alone.
Ceremonial architecture
Constructed spaces set apart from ordinary household use for communal or ritual purposes, appearing modestly in this period.
Agricultural intensification
Increasing investment of labor and resources in food production, including land modification and increasing dietary reliance on cultivated plants.
Formative period
The era following the Archaic period, often divided into an Initial Formative phase (roughly 2000–1200 BCE) and an Early Formative phase (roughly 1200–900 BCE), associated with increasing sedentism, agricultural dependence, and, by its later phase, the emergence of larger ceremonial centers such as the Olmec sites examined in Chapter 6.
Milpa
A Mesoamerican cultivation system, usually maize-centered and often involving intercropping with beans, squash, chilies, and other locally important plants; its crop composition and management practices vary by region and period, discussed in Section 5.4.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept or institution to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Broad-spectrum subsistence
A foraging strategy relying on a wide range of plant and animal resources rather than specialization in a few species.
Stable isotope analysis
A method examining human skeletal remains to estimate the relative dietary contribution of C3 versus C4 plants; because maize is a C4 plant, this can help track increasing maize consumption where ecological, archaeobotanical, and other dietary evidence supports that interpretation, though isotopic values for maize can overlap with those of other C4 and CAM plants.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a transformation almost entirely invisible in the monumental architecture, inscriptions, and royal dynasties that dominate popular images of ancient Mesoamerica, yet without which none of those later achievements would have been possible. The patient, generations-long work of managing wild plants, selecting favorable traits, building more durable homes, storing food against future need, and maintaining relationships with distant communities through exchange laid the essential economic and social foundation upon which later Mesoamerican civilizations would build.

The chapter has modeled, directly and explicitly, the evidentiary reasoning introduced in Chapter 4: a single piece of evidence, whether a maize cob, a phytolith, a genetic sequence, or a piece of obsidian, can often establish a narrow, specific claim quite firmly, but broad reconstructions of domestication or settlement are strongest only when multiple independent lines of evidence converge. Only through that careful convergence can historians respons­ibly reconstruct the larger processes examined in this chapter, in a period that left no writing behind, and even then, as this chapter has repeatedly emphasized, significant chronological and regional uncertainties remain.

This chapter has also insisted, throughout, that the developments it examines were neither inevitable nor uniform. Different Mesoamerican communities followed different paths, at different speeds, toward different outcomes, and many communities continued to live by hunting, gathering, and fishing for centuries after their neighbors had become settled farmers, without this making their histories any less significant. Readers should carry this lesson in historical complexity forward into the chapters that follow, resisting the temptation to read later civilizational achievements backward as the predetermined destination of every Mesoamerican community's history.

The story this textbook tells now turns, in Chapter 6, to what happened when the long-term developments examined in this chapter — sedentary village life, increasing food production, diverse subsistence strategies, exchange networks, and social differentiation — contributed, in one particular region of the Gulf Coast, to the emergence of a larger, more elaborate, and more politically significant form of community organization: the Olmec center of San Lorenzo, and the beginning of the Formative-period civilizational history that occupies the remainder of Part II.
