Governing Spanish America

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Monarchy Across an Ocean

A royal order issued in Madrid might take many months to reach Lima or Mexico City, and a reply could take a year or more. Governing Spanish America therefore meant something different from transmitting commands: it meant building institutions that could act, argue, delay, and compromise in the king's name.

Earlier chapters of this volume followed expeditions, sieges, alliances, and frontier wars. This chapter asks what came next. Once Spanish captains had claimed territory, how did a monarchy based in Castile organize authority over peoples who spoke hundreds of languages, lived in cities, villages, forests, and mining camps, and had been brought into the empire by very different routes? The answer takes in the institutions of the Crown, viceroys and judges, town councils, Indigenous officeholders, tax collectors, and the law itself, along with the people who used or evaded all of them.

The central argument is that the Spanish colonial state grew into an extensive and often effective system of royal officials, courts, municipalities, laws, and fiscal institutions, yet its authority was neither uniform nor absolute. Madrid legislated, appointed officials, and collected revenue. But rule across the Atlantic depended on local elites, Indigenous communities, ecclesiastical institutions, and a continual process of petition, litigation, bargaining, and sometimes open refusal. Spanish America was neither a tightly centralized administration run from Madrid nor a loose set of autonomous colonies. It was a system of overlapping jurisdictions, in which rival authorities checked one another and in which ordinary people, including Indigenous and Afro-descended people, could sometimes make that rivalry work for them. Participants did not hold equal power, and negotiation was frequently conducted under the shadow of coercion.

The chapter's geography and chronology are broad but deliberately bounded. Its main emphasis is the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when the basic institutions were created and took on the forms recorded in the great compilation of 1680. The systematic reforms of the eighteenth century, including new viceroyalties, intendants, and fiscal centralization, belong to Chapter 48 and are foreshadowed only where needed. The Portuguese system is reserved for Chapter 41, the mining and labor economies for Chapter 42, and cities, estates, and commerce for Chapter 43. The question here is how authority was organized, exercised, and contested.

A reader of Chapter 31 already has one example of how this worked. The Tlaxcalans who allied with Cortés later presented their service as a claim on the Crown. In 1535 the Spanish monarch granted Tlaxcala the title of city, a recognition that fitted the community into the legal vocabulary of the empire while preserving a council that mixed Indigenous and Spanish forms of government. Such arrangements were not exceptional. They show a pattern in which communities learned the language of royal law and used it to obtain privileges, and in which the Crown accepted such arrangements because they made governance cheaper and more stable.

Evidence deserves attention from the start. Royal legislation, such as the New Laws of 1542 and the Recopilación de leyes de los reynos de las Indias of 1680, shows what the monarchy intended. It does not show what happened. For that, historians use municipal minutes, court records, tax accounts, investigations of officials, petitions, letters to the Crown, wills, and notarial records, including thousands of documents written by Indigenous scribes in Nahuatl, Mixtec, Maya, and Quechua. The Andean noble Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala wrote a long illustrated letter to Philip III around 1615 denouncing corrupt officials, clergy, and Indigenous lords alike, and it survives as one of the most remarkable commentaries on colonial government ever written by a colonized person. Each source has its own purpose, and the gap between law and practice is itself a central historical problem.

Women and people of African descent are part of this story even though most formal offices were closed to them. Indigenous noblewomen held cacicazgos and defended them in court. Spanish widows inherited grants and managed households that were units of political power; Beatriz de la Cueva, widow of Pedro de Alvarado, was named governor of Guatemala in 1541 after her husband's death, though she died within days in the flood that destroyed the capital. Free and enslaved Afro-descended people served in militias, formed confraternities, paid tribute, and negotiated with officials. The chapter keeps these participants in view while explaining the formal institutions.

Historical Context

Chapters 27 through 39 described how Spanish claims were made and enforced: Columbus and the Caribbean, the destruction of the Mexica imperial system, the wars in the Andes, and the prolonged frontier conflicts in Chile and the Río de la Plata. Chapter 33 showed how early New Spain was assembled from conquest, encomienda, and Indigenous accommodation. Chapter 37 traced colonial formation in the northern Andes. This chapter assumes those narratives and asks how the resulting patchwork was converted into a system of rule.

The institutional materials came from Castile. The Catholic Monarchs had strengthened royal courts, appointed corregidores to supervise Castilian towns, and expanded the practice of investigating officials at the end of their service. Castilian municipal government, with its councils, elected judges, and corporate privileges, provided the model for the new cities founded in America. The papal bulls of 1493 and later concessions linked the Crown to the evangelization of the Indies and gave it extraordinary influence over the Church there. The legal theory was that the Indies were possessions of the Crown of Castile, to be governed by the king through councils and officials, though contemporaries often spoke of the "kingdoms of the Indies," a phrase that appears in the title of the 1680 compilation.

American conditions strained these inherited models. Distances were immense, and the demographic collapse described in Chapter 29 transformed the Indigenous populations on which tribute and labor depended. The early conquerors, including encomenderos who held grants of Indigenous tribute and labor, formed local powers that could resist the Crown, as the Peruvian civil wars of the 1540s would show. Indigenous societies, meanwhile, had their own political structures, from the Nahua altepetl to the Andean ayllu and kuraka lordships, and these remained the basic units through which most Indigenous people encountered the state.

Interpretation of this system has changed. Older writing emphasized an authoritarian Spanish bureaucracy and a passive Indigenous population, or alternatively a "Black Legend" of unmitigated cruelty. Modern scholarship has emphasized legal pluralism, negotiation, Indigenous agency, and the sheer weight of paper that tied communities to the courts. It has also been criticized for sometimes making the system sound benign, and this chapter tries to hold both points in view: Spanish rule was built on conquest and extraction, and it was also a legal order that colonized people learned to use.

I — Constructing Imperial Government
Section 40.1

From Conquest to Colonial Government

The first Spanish presence in the Americas rested on contracts between the Crown and private entrepreneurs. A capitulación granted an expedition leader a title such as adelantado, the right to explore and settle, and a share of future profits, while the Crown retained sovereignty and the royal fifth of treasure. The leader paid for the enterprise and recruited followers with promises of rewards. Columbus's own privileges, awarded in 1492, proved too sweeping for a monarchy that did not want hereditary rivals across the Atlantic, and the Crown gradually replaced him with royal governors, beginning with Nicolás de Ovando in 1502.

The conquests described in earlier chapters reproduced the problem on a larger scale. The men who seized Mexico and Peru expected to hold the land and its people as their reward, and some of them acted as semi-sovereign lords. The Crown's response was to assert its jurisdiction step by step: sending judges, regulating encomienda, limiting private grants, and installing officials who owed their positions to the king. The first audiencia of Mexico (1527), dominated by Nuño de Guzmán, so abused its power that the Crown replaced it in 1530 with a second audiencia led by Sebastián Ramírez de Fuenleal, which attempted to stabilize relations with Indigenous communities. The creation of the viceroyalties in the following decade completed the shift from conquest to government.

Sovereignty carried legal consequences for Indigenous people. The Crown claimed them as free vassals, which meant, in theory, that they could not be enslaved except under narrow conditions and that they owed tribute and loyalty to the king rather than to conquerors. Spanish law also classed Indigenous people as miserables, a category that invoked protection for those regarded as vulnerable, and which allowed lawyers to argue for special treatment in court. These categories were paternalistic and often served the interests of the Crown, but they gave Indigenous litigants a legal vocabulary they could use.

It was never possible to govern all populations in the same way. Dense sedentary societies such as the Nahua and Andean polities could be incorporated through existing tribute structures and local lords. Semi-nomadic peoples on the northern frontier of New Spain, the Mapuche in Chile, and the peoples of Amazonia and the Chaco were governed through missions, forts, or not at all. The Caribbean islands, whose Indigenous populations had collapsed, were governed largely as military outposts and plantation zones. This chapter therefore treats Spanish government as a family of regional systems under a single legal framework.

The evidence for this transition is unusually rich. Royal correspondence with the governors of Hispaniola, the texts of the capitulaciones, and the Laws of Burgos of 1512–13 show the Crown trying to regulate conquest after the fact. Complaints by clergy, notably the sermon preached on Hispaniola in 1511 by the Dominican Antonio de Montesinos, forced the monarchy to treat the treatment of Indigenous people as a question of royal conscience and royal jurisdiction. Conquerors for their part wrote probanzas de méritos y servicios, formal dossiers of witnesses and testimony, to argue that their services deserved reward. These sources were written to persuade, and each side described the conquest in the way that best supported its claim, so historians read them against one another.

The transition was also uneven in time. In central Mexico, royal government took hold within about a decade of the fall of Tenochtitlan, while in parts of Chile, the Chaco, and northern New Spain, Spaniards continued for generations to govern through fortresses, missions, and military commanders. Government was therefore a process, and a settled institutional map should not be read back into the 1520s or 1530s.

Section 40.2

The Crown, the Council of the Indies, and the Casa de Contratación

Spanish monarchs ruled through councils, each responsible for a territory or subject, an arrangement historians call the polysynodial system. The Council of the Indies, formally established in 1524 and later settled in Madrid, became the central body for American affairs. It proposed and drafted legislation, nominated candidates for major offices, heard appeals in important civil cases from the audiencias, reviewed investigations of officials, and advised the king on virtually every aspect of colonial policy. The king decided, but he did so on the basis of consultas, the reasoned recommendations of councillors, who relied on reports sent from America.

The Casa de Contratación, created in Seville in 1503, was a different institution. It regulated the fleets that carried people, goods, and silver across the Atlantic, registered ships and passengers, collected certain revenues, trained pilots, and judged commercial disputes. It was the point at which American treasure entered Spain and at which the Crown tried to enforce its trade monopoly. The Casa and the Council worked together but belonged to different parts of the system: one governed commerce and navigation, the other government and law. Later chapters examine the commercial system; here the Casa matters as an instrument for controlling movement and revenue.

The institutions in Spain depended on information, and information moved slowly. Letters, petitions, and reports crossed the Atlantic in fleets that sailed on fixed schedules. A viceroy's request might receive an answer after a year or more, and the answer might be outdated by the time it arrived. This delay shaped the entire system. Local officials necessarily exercised discretion, and the Crown relied on cross-checking: it encouraged rival officials to report on one another and used visits and investigations to correct abuses. The system also generated enormous quantities of paper, from which historians reconstruct much of colonial life.

Jurists played an influential role. Juan de Solórzano Pereira, who served as a judge in Lima before joining the Council, wrote a treatise on the law of the Indies and, in 1647, his Política indiana, which became a standard guide for colonial officials. His career shows how people moved between America and the Council, carrying knowledge of local conditions into metropolitan decisions, though the Council also drew heavily on Castilian lawyers who had never crossed the ocean.

The Council's procedures reveal how the system worked. It was headed by a president and staffed mainly by university-trained jurists (letrados), with a royal attorney (fiscal) and secretaries who prepared the paperwork. Its work was organized around written reports. Viceroys, audiencias, bishops, city councils, and private petitioners wrote to the king; their letters were summarized, debated in the Council, and returned to the monarch with a recommendation. The Council also compiled short lists of candidates for major offices, judged the residencias of viceroys and other senior officials, and served as the highest court of appeal in important cases. After the inspection of the Council led by Juan de Ovando in the late 1560s, new ordinances in 1571 clarified its duties and strengthened its capacity to gather information.

The Casa de Contratación had its own judges, treasury officers, and pilots, and it kept the registers that recorded which ships sailed, what they carried, and who traveled. Merchant interests organized alongside it in a guild-like consulado at Seville, and later merchants in Mexico City (1592) and Lima (1613) established their own consulados, with some judicial authority over commercial disputes. The Casa remained in Seville until the early eighteenth century and then moved to Cádiz, a change that belongs to the Bourbon period. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that two metropolitan institutions with different jurisdictions divided the supervision of the Indies, so that no single office controlled all of it.

Section 40.3

The Viceroyalties of New Spain and Peru

The Crown created the Viceroyalty of New Spain in 1535 and appointed Antonio de Mendoza as its first viceroy. The Viceroyalty of Peru followed in 1542, in the same legislation that issued the New Laws, though its first viceroy, Blasco Núñez Vela, did not arrive until 1544. The two jurisdictions were huge. New Spain at first included central and southern Mexico and extended in practice over Central America, the Caribbean, and later the Philippines, with the audiencias of Santo Domingo, Guatemala, and later Manila retaining their own functions. Peru's jurisdiction at first embraced almost all of Spanish South America.

Students should be careful about the eighteenth-century map. Spanish America did not always have four viceroyalties. For the period studied here, New Spain and Peru dominated. The Viceroyalty of New Granada was created in 1717, suppressed, and restored in 1739, and the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata was created in 1776. Those changes belong to Chapter 48 and reflect the Bourbon reorganization. Before them, regions that later became their own viceroyalties, such as Bogotá, Quito, and Buenos Aires, were governed through audiencias, governors, and captains general under the nominal authority of one of the two original viceroys.

Peru's first decade was violent. Núñez Vela attempted to enforce the New Laws and provoked a rebellion of encomenderos led by Gonzalo Pizarro, who defeated and killed the viceroy in 1546. The Crown's response was to send Pedro de la Gasca, a cleric and royal judge with extraordinary powers, who defeated Pizarro in 1548 through a mixture of negotiation, pardon, and force. The Crown then revised the New Laws and withdrew its most contested provision on the inheritance of encomiendas. The episode demonstrates that the first viceregal government in Peru was not able to impose royal policy, and that royal authority depended on bargaining with colonial elites.

The two viceroyalties faced different conditions. New Spain was built on a densely populated Nahua and Mixtec core, with tribute systems that Spaniards could adapt and a short route to the Atlantic coast. Peru's geography was vertical and extremely long: coast, highlands, and eastern slopes were connected by roads that climbed through some of the world's highest terrain. After the discovery of the silver of Potosí in 1545, the center of economic gravity in the Andes moved toward the southern highlands, far from Lima. The Crown responded in 1559 by creating the Audiencia of Charcas, which gave Upper Peru a court of its own. Viceroys also traveled or sent inspectors to the provinces. Toledo spent several years in the 1570s on a personal general inspection (visita general) of the Andes, which is why his decrees and the surveys that accompanied them are among the most informative sources for sixteenth-century Andean society.

Viceregal jurisdiction was real but variable. A viceroy of New Spain had formal responsibility for the Caribbean, Central America, and the Philippines, yet the audiencias of Santo Domingo, Guatemala, and Manila handled much of the day-to-day government of those regions and corresponded directly with the Council. The same was true of the audiencias of Quito, Bogotá, and Chile in relation to Lima. Students should therefore picture the viceroyalties as frameworks of supervision and precedence rather than as chains of command that reached every village.

Historical Vocabulary
Viceroyalty and Viceroy

A viceroyalty (virreinato) was a large territorial jurisdiction governed by a viceroy (virrey), literally "vice-king," who represented the monarch. The viceroy was normally governor, captain general, and president of the audiencia in his capital, and he also acted as vicepatrón, the king's deputy in matters of ecclesiastical patronage.

The word suggests more authority than the office carried. Viceroys depended on the courts, the treasury officials, local councils, and the Church, and the Crown deliberately gave other officials the right to write to the king independently. The term should not be read as implying a smaller copy of the monarchy. Jurisdiction varied by region, and the boundaries of the viceroyalties changed over time.

Section 40.4

Viceroys: Royal Representation and the Limits of Power

Viceroys were drawn mostly from the Castilian nobility and selected by the king on the advice of the Council. Their duties included supervising finances, commanding defense, presiding over the audiencia, enforcing royal law, organizing public order, and representing the king in ceremony. The ceremonial role mattered. Viceroys entered their capitals in elaborate processions, presided at religious festivals, and lived in palaces that signaled the presence of royal majesty. Historians such as Alejandro Cañeque have argued that this symbolic dimension was central to how royal authority was understood.

The practical powers of a viceroy were broad but hemmed in. The audiencia could advise and check him, and in certain circumstances its judges could write to the Crown against him. The treasury officials reported independently on revenues. The archbishop and the cathedral chapter represented an institution that the viceroy could not command. Municipal councils and powerful local families had their own links to the court. Officials of the viceroy's own household sometimes competed with local interests, and a viceroy's term, often around three to six years, ended with an investigation, the residencia. These checks were a deliberate strategy: the Crown preferred rivals who informed on one another to a single, unobserved authority.

Strong viceroys could nonetheless reshape their territories. Mendoza pacified the Mixtón War on the northwestern frontier (1540–41), organized the mint of Mexico City, and established patterns of consultation with Indigenous leaders. Toledo, in Peru, conducted a general inspection of the Andes, ordered the resettlement of Indigenous communities into new towns, reorganized the mit'a labor draft for Potosí, and presided over the execution of the last Neo-Inca ruler, Túpac Amaru I, in 1572, discussed in Chapter 36. His government was one of the most ambitious exercises of colonial power and one of the most criticized, including by Spaniards.

The limits of viceregal power appeared in conflict. In 1624 Viceroy Gelves of New Spain quarreled with the audiencia and the archbishop of Mexico over corruption, the grain trade, and jurisdiction. After the viceroy ordered the archbishop exiled, a crowd attacked and burned part of the viceregal palace. Gelves fled, and the Crown ultimately replaced him. The episode illustrates the instability of a system in which officials were encouraged to contest each other, and it also shows how urban crowds could intervene in elite conflicts. The Crown did not abandon the viceroy's office, but it learned to treat conflict reports as the normal currency of government.

Procedures for succession illustrate the logic of the system. Before leaving Spain, a viceroy might carry sealed instructions, known as a pliego de mortaja, naming who should govern temporarily if he died in office. When no such instructions existed, authority passed to the audiencia, which governed collectively. Departing viceroys also wrote reports for their successors, the relaciones or instructions of government, which survive in large numbers and give a viceroy's own account of finances, defense, Indigenous policy, and the people he regarded as allies or obstacles. Like all government documents, these are partial: they show what outgoing officials wished to emphasize, and they were written with an eye to their own reputations at the residencia.

Viceroys also had to manage a court of their own. The household of a viceroy included relatives, clients, and servants who expected appointments and favors, and the pursuit of patronage was part of what made the office valuable. The Crown knew this and tried to limit it by forbidding viceroys to marry locally or to appoint relatives to offices, but enforcement was weak. In effect, a viceroy governed by combining legal authority with the careful distribution of favors to local families and institutions, and a viceroy who alienated the audiencia, the archbishop, and the city council together could be undone, as Gelves discovered.

II — Regional and Local Government
Section 40.5

The Audiencias: Justice, Administration, and Royal Oversight

The audiencia was the characteristic institution of Spanish American government. It was a court of judges, called oidores, who heard civil and criminal appeals from lower officials, but it also carried administrative and governmental responsibilities. In the absence of a viceroy or governor the audiencia could govern, as it did in New Spain and Peru when a viceroy died. It advised executive officials, reviewed legislation, supervised treasury officials, and heard complaints from Indigenous communities. It was, in effect, a collective body at the center of regional power.

The Crown established audiencias progressively: Santo Domingo (1511), Mexico (1527), Panama (1538), Lima (1543), Guatemala or "Los Confines" (1543), Guadalajara (1548), Santa Fe de Bogotá (1549), Charcas (1559), Quito (1563), Chile (with interruptions, restored in Santiago in 1609), and Manila (1583). Historians often distinguish types. Viceregal audiencias, in Mexico and Lima, were presided over by the viceroy. Praetorial audiencias, in places such as Santo Domingo and Chile, were presided over by a governor-captain general. The others were headed by a president and subordinate to a viceroy in practice. These categories help students avoid the mistaken assumption that every audiencia was the same.

The Crown took steps to keep judges independent of local society. Oidores were generally peninsular Spaniards, prohibited from owning property, engaging in trade, serving as godparents, or marrying within their jurisdiction without special license, rules discussed in the Primary Source Window below. These restrictions reveal an effort to prevent judges from becoming entangled in local families and factions. In practice, dispensations were sold or granted, judges formed relationships anyway, and the Crown, after earlier sales of other offices, began the commercial sale of audiencia judgeships in 1683, according to Ángel Sanz Tapia's study of the process, which opened the courts to American-born men with local fortunes. (Some older accounts give later dates because they treat particular stages of the sales rather than the first commercial offerings.) The tension between independence and integration became a major theme of the seventeenth century.

In practice, an audiencia worked through a series of formal procedures. The judges met in a chamber of the court to hear civil cases on appeal, to review criminal cases (in Mexico and Lima, separate criminal judges, alcaldes del crimen, formed their own chamber), and to deliberate on matters of government in a session called the real acuerdo. A royal attorney, the fiscal, defended the interests of the Crown, and in many audiencias a fiscal or other official was designated protector of the Indigenous people. Oidores also traveled their districts on inspection, and one of the titles of the 1680 compilation is devoted to judges serving as district inspectors. The court could take direct complaints from Indigenous communities, which turned it into a forum in which villages in distant provinces could contest the decisions of corregidores, encomenderos, and city councils.

Because audiencias combined judicial and governmental roles, they were also arenas of politics. A court could side with a viceroy against a bishop, or with a province against a governor. In seventeenth-century Quito, for example, studies by John Leddy Phelan showed how the audiencia was embedded in rivalries among the president, the bishop, the city council, and local families, with each side appealing to the Council of the Indies. This evidence supports the view, discussed in section 40.16, that the system worked through managed conflict.

Historical Vocabulary
Audiencia, Jurisdiction, and Oidor

An audiencia was a royal high court with governmental functions; its judges were oidores (literally "hearers"). Jurisdiction (jurisdicción) is the legal authority to declare law, judge, and command within a defined sphere. In Spanish political thought, government was largely understood as the administration of justice, so the exercise of jurisdiction was central to what governing meant.

The English word "court" understates the institution, since audiencias supervised other officials and sometimes ruled. Jurisdictional disputes, over who had the right to decide a case or command a body, were therefore among the most common political conflicts in colonial government. Students should read such disputes as struggles over power, not procedural quibbles.

Section 40.6

Governors, Captaincies General, Corregidores, and Alcaldes Mayores

Below the viceroys and audiencias, provincial government was exercised by a range of officials with varying titles. Governors administered provinces such as Venezuela, Cuba, Chile, and Tucumán. Where defense was urgent or distances were great, the governor was also captain general, with command of military forces; the captaincies general of Guatemala, Chile, and Cuba, among others, had significant autonomy from the viceroys in practice. These officials were responsible for fortifications, militias, and relations with unconquered peoples, and they were often more powerful locally than the viceroy.

Within provinces, local jurisdictions were administered by corregidores in the Andes and by corregidores and alcaldes mayores in New Spain. These officials, appointed by the Crown or viceroy, served as judges of first instance, supervised Indigenous tribute and local councils, and kept order. In Peru the corregidor of Indians was established in the 1560s and became central to the Toledan reorganization. Their salaries were low and their duties large. Many used their offices to profit, in particular through forced sales of goods to Indigenous communities, the repartos de mercancías, which became a major source of grievance, protested in Guaman Poma's letter.

Overlapping jurisdiction was constant. A corregidor might clash with the parish priest over control of an Indigenous town, with the local cabildo over water or markets, with a hacendado over labor, or with the audiencia over an appeal. The titles, powers, and salaries of these officials changed over time and varied by region, so students should read titles as functional descriptions rather than a fixed organizational chart. The overall pattern is more important: provincial officials were the point at which royal government most visibly touched ordinary people, and also the point where the gap between royal intention and local reality was widest.

Because corregidores were meant to supervise Indigenous councils and tribute collection, they stood at the junction of Spanish and Indigenous government. In the Andes they confirmed or supervised the annual selection of Indigenous alcaldes and other council officers, kept the tribute rolls, heard minor cases, and were expected to prevent abuses by encomenderos and clergy. In practice, corregimientos were often sold or granted as rewards for service, and officeholders arrived in debt and with brief tenure, a combination that encouraged them to extract income quickly. Guaman Poma's letter lists a catalogue of such abuses, from forced purchases to exploitation of Indigenous labor, and although he wrote as an aggrieved critic, many of his complaints match what lawsuits and investigations record.

Governors presented a different problem, because they combined civil, judicial, and military functions. In Chile the governor was both chief administrator and commander of the army fighting the Mapuche, and Peru's viceroys intervened regularly to supply money and men. In the Caribbean, governors of Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Santo Domingo commanded garrisons whose upkeep came from other treasuries. These officers could be effective rulers or abusive ones, and the Crown attempted to restrain them through audiencias, treasury officials, and residencias. In New Spain, Spanish officers of lower rank, including lieutenants who deputized for district officials, also entered the picture, which meant that the person who actually confronted a village might be far down a chain of delegated authority.

Section 40.7

Cabildos and the Government of Colonial Municipalities

Spanish settlement was organized around cities. Founding a town meant naming a council, the cabildo or ayuntamiento, which governed the town and its surrounding territory. A typical cabildo included two alcaldes ordinarios, who served as judges of first instance, and a number of regidores, who deliberated and administered the city. Other officers included a royal standard-bearer (alférez real), a chief constable (alguacil mayor), and a procurator who represented the city. The cabildo met regularly, kept minutes, and could correspond directly with the king.

The cabildo's responsibilities were practical: regulating markets and weights, setting prices for staples, ensuring the supply of food, maintaining roads, bridges, and water systems, organizing festivals, supervising guilds, and allocating town lots and, in the early period, lands. In emergencies, it could convene an open meeting, the cabildo abierto, of leading residents. The council was also a political forum in which the city's most powerful households negotiated with viceroys and governors and with each other. Cabildos sent agents to the royal court to press claims for privileges.

Membership was restricted to vecinos, the property-holding householders recognized as full members of the community. In the early decades, encomenderos and conquerors dominated. Over time, municipal offices became a prize for local elites. In the late sixteenth century, particularly after 1591, the Crown increasingly sold council offices to raise revenue, and these offices could be inherited or resold. The result was that municipal government often became the property of a small group of families, who used it to protect their interests in land, labor, and trade. At the same time, the sale of offices created a powerful constituency with a stake in the monarchy.

Women, Indigenous people, and Afro-descended people were generally excluded from council membership, but they were not absent from municipal life. Women ran households and businesses, owned property, and appeared in municipal courts. Indigenous residents came into Spanish cities as workers and market traders, and their separate councils interacted with the Spanish cabildo. Free and enslaved Afro-descended residents formed confraternities and served in militias, which gave them standing in civic life even without a vote.

Elections and appointments varied. In many cities the outgoing council chose new alcaldes each year at the beginning of January, subject to confirmation by a governor, viceroy, or audiencia. Regimientos, by contrast, began as appointments or elected posts and later became, in many cities, perpetual offices that could be purchased, inherited, or resold. A cabildo therefore tended to become a corporation of leading families that regulated access to land and trade for others. Sources such as the minutes of the Mexico City and Lima councils record debates about water, markets, the price of bread and meat, and festival expenses, giving a vivid picture of municipal priorities.

The physical shape of Spanish cities also expressed government. Royal ordinances on discovery and settlement issued in 1573 prescribed the layout of new towns around a main plaza, with the church, council house, and residences of leading citizens clustered nearby. Cities were intended to be the places where Spanish authority could be seen, and this model was applied in Mexico City, built over the ruins of Tenochtitlan, and in newly founded towns on the frontiers. The principles of order and hierarchy were thus embedded in streets and buildings as well as in law, though many cities developed more irregularly than the ordinances envisioned.

Section 40.8

The Republics of Spaniards and Indians

Spanish legal and religious thought divided the population into two corporate bodies: the república de españoles and the república de indios. The idea was that Indigenous communities should be governed through their own officials, protected from abuse, instructed in Christianity, and required to pay tribute, while Spaniards lived separately in cities and held land and offices. The legislation attempted to restrict Spaniards, Africans, and mixed-descent people from living in Indigenous towns, and to protect Indigenous land. The concept was also a tool of fiscal organization, since it defined who owed tribute and labor.

To enforce the separation, the Crown sponsored resettlement. In New Spain, officials carried out congregaciones in the mid-sixteenth century and again around 1600, concentrating dispersed populations into new towns. In Peru, Viceroy Toledo's reducciones of the 1570s resettled hundreds of thousands of people into planned towns, with a plaza, church, and council. The aims were Christian instruction, tribute collection, and control. The effects were severe: communities lost access to dispersed lands, families were separated from ancestral places, and mortality sometimes rose. Many people left their new towns, and some communities resisted or reestablished older settlement patterns.

The reality was always more complex than the legal ideal. Indigenous people moved to Spanish cities to work, trade, and escape tribute, forming barrios of Indigenous residents. Spaniards, mestizos, and Africans lived in Indigenous towns, rented land, and married into the community. Mixed populations expanded through the seventeenth century. Modern scholarship has therefore challenged the idea of a rigid segregation. The república de indios was a legal and political framework that gave communities a corporate identity and a legal standing, which they used to defend land and autonomy, and which also constrained them. It was enforced unevenly and changed over time.

Two Andean categories show how the legal framework could be bent by social reality. Yanaconas were Indigenous people who served Spaniards or other masters outside their home communities, while forasteros were people who had left their communities of origin and thereby evaded tribute and mit'a duties tied to their ayllu. Both groups grew during the seventeenth century as populations declined and families sought better conditions, and both challenged the neat division between Spaniards and Indians because they lived in an ambiguous legal position. In Mexico City, meanwhile, Indigenous districts such as San Juan Tenochtitlan and Santiago Tlatelolco retained their own Indigenous governors and councils within the Spanish capital, a reminder that the two republics overlapped even in the viceregal seat.

Historical Vocabulary
República de indios and Cabildo de indios

The república de indios ("republic of Indians") was the legal category for Indigenous communities as corporate bodies with their own officials, lands, and obligations. A cabildo de indios was the Indigenous town council, typically headed by a governor and including alcaldes and regidores, modeled on Spanish municipal institutions but selected and operated in Indigenous communities.

The word república here means a political community, not a modern republic. The "Indian" category was also a colonial creation that grouped diverse peoples under a single label; communities mostly identified by their local polity, such as an altepetl or an ayllu. Using the term helps students understand how colonial law imposed a framework on Indigenous societies that they then adapted for their own purposes.

Section 40.9

Indigenous Government: Caciques, Curacas, Cabildos, and Communities

Indigenous leaders were not marginal to Spanish government. Because the Crown lacked the personnel to administer thousands of communities directly, it relied on Indigenous elites to organize tribute collection, labor drafts, and local order. In central Mexico, hereditary rulers (tlatoque) were gradually replaced by governors and councils on the Spanish pattern, though many communities continued to choose from the same noble lineages, and the altepetl, the basic Nahua political community, persisted as the unit of local identity. Scholarship by Charles Gibson and James Lockhart documented how Nahua communities adapted Spanish forms while maintaining their own organization, including in Nahuatl-language records.

In the Andes, kurakas (Spanish curacas) led ayllus and larger groupings, mobilized labor, and mediated with colonial officials. Their authority drew on kinship, reciprocity, and ritual obligations that predated Spanish rule, and the colonial system gave them new roles and new pressures. A curaca could protect a community by negotiating exemptions, or exploit it by collecting more than was owed. The Crown gave caciques privileges, including exemption from tribute and labor drafts, and the right to ride horses and carry arms in some cases, which created tension with the people they governed. Some of the most striking colonial documents, including Guaman Poma's, were written by members of this intermediate elite.

Communities defended their interests through the courts. The Crown appointed protectors of Indians and, in Mexico, created in 1592 a General Indian Court (Juzgado General de Indios) funded by a small levy on Indigenous communities, which heard cases and offered legal assistance. Litigation over land, water, tribute, labor, and abusive officials was common, and communities hired Indigenous and Spanish intermediaries, including interpreters, notaries, and lawyers. Nahuatl and Quechua speakers produced petitions, wills, land titles, and council records that show how deeply Indigenous people had absorbed the legal system. Indigenous women appear in these records as cacicas who inherited authority, as litigants defending property, and as market traders and household heads, although men dominated formal office.

Two features of Indigenous government deserve emphasis. First, status within the community could itself be a matter of dispute. In central Mexico, Spanish officials used the terms cabecera and sujeto for head towns and their subject communities, and the question of which settlement ranked as a head town could determine who paid tribute where and who sat on which council. Communities litigated to gain or to avoid subordination, and Nahuatl records show that these struggles were part of ordinary local politics. Second, leadership carried risks as well as privileges. Caciques and governors were responsible for deliveries of tribute and labor, and if a community fell short, they could be fined or imprisoned. They therefore had incentives to protect their communities and to protect themselves, which did not always coincide.

In the Andes, the authority of the kuraka rested on a structure of dual divisions, upper and lower halves of a community (hanan and hurin), and on a hierarchy of lesser leaders. Kurakas who were successful at delivering workers and tribute could receive recognition and sometimes exemptions, while those who resisted could be deposed. Succession disputes were common and often reached the courts, in part because Spanish law required proof of hereditary right. Scholars working in Nahua, Mixtec, Maya, and Andean communities, such as Yanna Yannakakis for Oaxaca, have shown that Indigenous intermediaries, including scribes, interpreters, and local officers, were central to how colonial government actually functioned, since the state could not communicate with most villages without them.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Government Was Experienced in an Indigenous Town

For most Indigenous residents, the state appeared as a rhythm of obligations. Tribute was assessed per household or adult tributary and paid in installments in goods, cloth, and increasingly coin, which required families to sell crops or labor for money. Community officials assembled the payment, kept records, and answered to the corregidor. Households also owed rotating labor for public works, the church, and sometimes for Spanish employers, and communities maintained a communal treasury, the caja de comunidad, to cover local expenses and fiestas.

Women's work sustained these obligations, spinning and weaving cloth, grinding maize, raising animals, and trading in markets, and when men were drafted or migrated, women managed fields and households. Children took part in catechism and community labor. Town life revolved around the church, the council house, and the market, and the cofradías (religious confraternities) provided mutual aid and organized festivals. When an official abused his position, communities might send a delegation to the audiencia, a costly and risky undertaking that required collecting funds, finding a scribe, and traveling for weeks. Such petitions survive in great numbers and are among the best evidence of how people understood their rights.

Section 40.10

The Laws of the Indies and Colonial Legal Practice

Spanish rule was a legal enterprise. The body of law governing the Indies, known as derecho indiano, combined royal decrees, ordinances, local regulations, and the general principles of Castilian law, which applied when no specific law existed. The Crown issued orders in the form of reales cédulas and provisiones, and viceroys and audiencias issued local ordinances. Indigenous customary law was also recognized, within limits: communities could continue to use customs not contrary to Christianity or royal law. The result was legal pluralism, in which different laws, courts, and jurisdictions coexisted and sometimes competed.

A landmark was the New Laws of 1542, issued after Bartolomé de las Casas and others had pressed the Crown on the abuses of conquest. They forbade the enslavement of Indigenous people, restricted personal service, and provided that encomiendas would not be inherited, ending them over time. The reaction among colonists was fierce, and the Peruvian rebellion forced the Crown to withdraw the inheritance clause in 1545. The episode shows both the Crown's reforming ambition and the constraints on its power. Many provisions survived, including the principle that Indigenous people were free vassals, and later legislation built on them.

The mass of legislation grew unwieldy, and by the seventeenth century jurists were attempting to order it. After decades of work in which jurists including Antonio de León Pinelo and Juan de Solórzano Pereira played a role, the Recopilación de leyes de los reynos de las Indias was issued under Charles II in 1680. It is organized in nine books, covering the Church, the Council and audiencias, viceroys and military affairs, settlement and cities, governors and justice, Indigenous people, criminal law and social order, the treasury, and trade with the Casa de Contratación. It was a monument to the Crown's legal ambitions and to its habit of governing through writing.

A phrase associated with the system captures the gap between law and practice: obedezco pero no cumplo, "I obey but do not execute." An official who received a royal order believed to be based on false information or harmful to the king's service could acknowledge the order's authority while suspending its execution and writing back to explain. The doctrine was not a license to disobey, but it provided a legal mechanism for delay and negotiation, and it was frequently used. It illustrates the point that law in Spanish America was a continuing conversation between the center and local officials, not a one-way command.

The mechanics of circulation shaped the law's reach. Royal orders were usually handwritten, copied into official registers, and forwarded to the officials concerned, who were expected to acknowledge them. Because the number of orders grew so quickly, officials and jurists compiled collections to find and apply them. Vasco de Puga published a collection of royal orders for New Spain in 1563, and Diego de Encinas compiled a larger one in 1596. These works show that legal knowledge was a form of power: an official or litigant who could cite the right cédula gained an advantage, and this encouraged the growth of a profession of lawyers, notaries, and solicitors in the viceregal capitals.

Law also gave Spanish rule its moral language. The monarchy presented itself as the source of justice and as the protector of vulnerable subjects, and a long tradition of jurists argued about the legitimacy of conquest and the rights of Indigenous people. Solórzano's Política indiana synthesized this tradition for colonial officials. Such arguments could justify rule and criticize its abuses at the same time, which helps explain why Indigenous litigants and clerical critics so often invoked royal law against local officials.

Section 40.11

Encomienda, Repartimiento, and the Government of Indigenous Labor

Spanish rule in America rested on access to Indigenous labor and tribute, and the Crown's chief challenge was to regulate that access. The encomienda entrusted a group of Indigenous people to a Spanish holder, the encomendero, who was entitled to tribute and, early on, labor, in return for protecting them and providing Christian instruction. It originated in the Caribbean and spread to the mainland. The Crown tolerated it as a reward for conquerors and as a way to defend and govern territory, while attempting to prevent it from becoming a hereditary lordship that would rival royal power.

The term repartimiento was used in several senses, and students should keep them apart. In the earliest period it meant the distribution of Indigenous people to Spaniards. Later, in New Spain, it described a system of rotating labor drafts, in which Indigenous communities were required to supply a share of their workers, regulated by officials and paid at fixed wages. The Andean mit'a, adapted from an Inca system of rotational labor service, was reorganized by Toledo in the 1570s to supply workers for mines, notably Potosí, with a draft of roughly one-seventh of adult men from designated provinces. Other regions, such as Chile, Paraguay, and Yucatán, had their own variants, with encomienda persisting in Yucatán into the eighteenth century.

Government's role was legal and administrative: the Crown assessed tribute (tasaciones), regulated who could demand labor, set wages and limits, and tried to enforce rules through officials and courts. The detail of the labor economy, including how mines and estates actually used workers, is reserved for Chapter 42. For this chapter, the key point is that these were instruments of state policy as well as private arrangements, and that the Crown steadily moved toward direct control. Encomiendas lapsed or reverted to the Crown, and communities' tribute was increasingly collected by royal officials and paid in coin, making the Crown, rather than the encomendero, the principal beneficiary.

Regulation proceeded by inspection and assessment. Royal officials counted tributaries and assigned what each community owed in a tasación, which fixed the amounts and sometimes forbade certain kinds of service. Mid-century legislation, including measures of 1549, restricted personal service as a form of tribute, and Toledo's assessments in the 1570s created a standard framework for the Andes. These decisions mattered because they defined what an encomendero could legally demand and what a community could legally refuse, and communities frequently went to court to enforce the assessed amounts. Women could also hold encomiendas: widows and daughters inherited them for the lives allowed by law, and some managed their holdings actively.

In New Spain, rotating draft labor, known in Nahuatl-influenced usage as coatequitl and in Spanish as repartimiento, drew a small percentage of each community's workers at any time for public works, agriculture, and mining, with wages fixed by officials. The draft for agricultural work was largely ended in 1632, though it continued for some mining and public works. These institutions reflect an effort by the Crown to supervise the allocation of labor, and also the way that private employers and Indigenous communities bargained over it. The full economic story belongs to Chapter 42.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Spanish Authorities Transformed Systems of Labor and Tribute

Long-term causes

The Crown's reluctance to allow a hereditary New World aristocracy; religious and legal criticism of Indigenous servitude; and the fiscal ambitions of a monarchy at war in Europe.

Short-term causes

Abuses documented by clergy and officials, the demographic collapse of Indigenous populations, the Peruvian rebellion of the 1540s, and rising demand for labor in mines and estates.

Event or transformation

The shift from encomienda toward Crown-regulated tribute and rotating labor drafts, together with growing wage labor.

Immediate effects

New assessments and regulations, conflict with encomenderos, increased use of royal officials in tribute collection, and new legal grounds for Indigenous complaints.

Long-term consequences

Greater royal fiscal control, the spread of coerced and wage labor on estates and in mines, and a long record of Indigenous litigation over labor and tribute.

Historical complexity

The changes reflected Crown interest, colonial demand, and demographic crisis together. Reform did not end coercion; it often redirected it.
Section 40.12

Royal Patronage and the Church in Colonial Government

The Church was a partner of the state. Through a series of papal concessions beginning in the late fifteenth century, the monarchs gained the patronato real, the right of royal patronage, which gave them power over ecclesiastical appointments, the creation of dioceses, the collection and distribution of tithes, and the supervision of missionary activity. The Council of the Indies nominated bishops, whom the pope formally confirmed, and the viceroy acted as the king's deputy in patronage matters. Churchmen were thus partly royal officials, and the Crown relied on them for education, record-keeping, poor relief, and moral supervision.

Religious orders, including Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians, and, from the 1560s and 1570s, Jesuits, held much of the early responsibility for Indigenous parishes and missions, and they were often the Crown's allies in restraining colonists. Over time, the Crown encouraged the replacement of friars by secular clergy under bishops, which produced conflicts. Church courts, with their own jurisdiction over clergy and certain matters, coexisted with royal courts. The Inquisition established tribunals in Lima and Mexico in the early 1570s; Indigenous people were excluded from its jurisdiction. The fuller history of conversion, missions, and religious culture belongs to Chapter 46.

The partnership produced conflict. Bishops and viceroys quarreled over appointments and jurisdiction, as in the Gelves affair of 1624. In the 1640s Bishop Juan de Palafox of Puebla, who had been sent as a royal inspector, clashed with the Jesuits and briefly served as acting viceroy. Such disputes reveal that the Church was a competing center of authority with its own resources, networks, and loyalties. Local communities benefited from the rivalry, appealing to bishops against civil officials and to civil courts against clergy, in a pattern that was characteristic of the whole system.

The Church was also a financial and administrative institution. Tithes were collected by ecclesiastical officials, but the Crown received a share, commonly summarized as two-ninths of tithe revenue, and it supported the clergy in other ways. Parish priests kept baptismal, marriage, and burial registers that serve as some of the best demographic sources historians have, and they often served as interpreters, notaries, and mediators for communities. At the same time, they could be agents of extraction, collecting fees for sacraments and organizing community labor for church building.

Councils of the Church helped to standardize practice. The Third Council of Lima (1582–83), associated with Archbishop Toribio de Mogrovejo, and the Third Mexican Council (1585) issued rules on catechism, clergy conduct, and Indigenous parishes, and these texts needed royal approval. The relationship between bishops and the Crown was therefore one of cooperation within limits, and of competition when the two disagreed about jurisdiction or revenue.

Section 40.13

Royal Finance: Tribute, Taxation, and the Colonial Treasury

Government depended on revenue, and the royal treasury, the real hacienda, was organized through local treasuries (cajas reales) in capitals, ports, and mining centers. Each was staffed by royal treasury officials, typically an accountant, a treasurer, and sometimes a factor and inspector, who recorded revenues and disbursements and answered to the Crown. Treasury officials were independent of viceroys and governors in important respects and could report on them directly, another example of the system's design.

Revenue came from many sources. Indigenous tribute, assessed on communities and households, was the main source of income in many regions, as the decline of encomienda moved tribute into Crown hands. The royal fifth (quinto real) was a levy on mined silver and gold, with rates adjusted over time. Customs duties applied to goods entering and leaving American ports. Sales taxes, the alcabala, were introduced in New Spain in the 1570s and in Peru in the early 1590s, and caused local resistance, such as the Quito rebellion of the alcabalas in 1592–93. The Crown also earned money from the sale of offices, the monopoly on mercury essential to silver production, church revenues such as the crusade bulls, and the regularization of land titles through payments.

Revenue did not simply flow to Spain. Large portions remained in America to pay salaries, finance defense, and subsidize poorer regions. The situado, a system of transfers from richer treasuries to defended outposts, sent silver from Mexico to Caribbean garrisons in Havana, Puerto Rico, and Florida and to Manila, and from Peru to Chile to support the war against the Mapuche. The Crown created tribunals of accounts in Lima, Mexico City, and Bogotá in 1605 to audit the treasuries. In the seventeenth century, as Spain's European wars multiplied, the Crown sought more American revenue, which increased pressure for sales of offices and special levies, and encouraged local elites to bargain for concessions. Production and commerce are examined in Chapters 42 and 43; here, finance matters as the link that connected local government to imperial obligations.

Tribute assessments rested on counts. Officials conducted periodic enumerations of tributaries, adjusting the rolls to reflect deaths, migrations, and exemptions. Some groups, such as Indigenous nobles and certain officeholders, were exempt, and in some regions people of African descent who were free were assessed tribute of their own. Communities paid in installments through the year, and the proceeds were divided among the encomendero, the Crown, and local expenses such as clergy support and community funds. As encomiendas lapsed, a growing share went directly to royal treasuries. Because the rolls lagged behind population decline, communities often paid for people who had died or fled, and petitions to adjust the counts form a significant category of litigation.

Modern studies of the treasury accounts, notably the work of John TePaske and Herbert Klein, have shown how revenue patterns changed over time. The accounts suggest that over the seventeenth century a larger share of American revenue remained in the Americas for local administration and defense, while remittances to Spain became less predictable. Historians still debate how to interpret these patterns, but they agree that the Crown's finances were bound up with colonial conditions and that the colonial treasuries had considerable autonomy in practice.

Section 40.14

Inspecting the Empire: Visitas, Residencias, Petitions, and Bureaucratic Records

Because the Crown could not supervise officials continuously, it relied on periodic scrutiny. At the end of an official's term, a residencia opened a public inquiry into his conduct, in which witnesses could testify and complaints be filed; the results could produce fines or dismissal. A visita was an inspection ordered by the Crown or the Council, sometimes of a single official, sometimes of an entire audiencia or province. Notable visitas included those that carried the New Laws to New Spain in the 1540s and the inspection of the Council of the Indies itself, led by Juan de Ovando in the late 1560s, which led to a reorganization of colonial government.

The Crown also gathered information. In 1577 the monarchy circulated a printed questionnaire, the Relaciones geográficas, to local officials in the Indies, asking them to describe the history, population, economy, and geography of their districts. Responses, including maps painted by Indigenous artists, survive for parts of New Spain and elsewhere and are invaluable sources. Petitions (memoriales) from individuals and communities, and probanzas de méritos y servicios in which people documented service to the Crown to claim rewards, filled the archives.

The reliance on paper had consequences. Notaries (escribanos) were essential intermediaries, drafting wills, contracts, and petitions; Indigenous communities also trained their own scribes. Documents allowed the state to track obligations and let subjects press claims. But litigation was costly, written procedures privileged those with access to lawyers and scribes, fees were high, and officials sometimes turned inspections into instruments of faction. Residencias could be manipulated by friends and patrons, and visitadores could become tools of rival interests. The bureaucracy could restrict abuses, reproduce inequalities, and become entangled in local political struggles, sometimes at once. These records, later preserved in archives such as the Archivo General de Indias in Seville, form the basis of much of what historians know about colonial government.

The process of a residencia gives a sense of how scrutiny worked. At the end of an official's term, a judge of residencia was appointed, public notice was given that complaints could be filed, and witnesses were examined; the official could not leave the district until the process was complete. The results could lead to fines, suspension, or nothing at all. Even great figures were not exempt: the residencia of Hernán Cortés began in 1526. A major visita of New Spain was conducted by Jerónimo de Valderrama in the 1560s, and such investigations produced large dossiers that historians still mine for information about corruption, Indigenous conditions, and factional conflict.

The records that resulted created their own politics. Access to a notary, a lawyer, or an interpreter could determine whether a complaint was ever heard. Fees, travel, and the long delays of appeals favored wealthy litigants, and Indigenous communities often pooled community funds to pay for a case. At the same time, the sheer volume of paper meant that officials could not easily ignore petitions, and the practice of citing precedent from earlier decisions gave communities a way of holding authorities to their own rules. The Relaciones geográficas of the 1580s, with their detailed local descriptions and painted maps, are a rare example of the Crown asking communities themselves to describe their world, even if the responses were shaped by local officials and Indigenous painters.

IV — Governing in Practice
Section 40.15

Regional Variation: Mexico, the Andes, the Caribbean, and the Frontiers

The two viceregal capitals, Mexico City and Lima, were the main centers of government. Mexico City was built on the ruins of Tenochtitlan and drew on Nahua tribute networks and labor; Lima, founded in 1535 near the Pacific coast, was the administrative and commercial hub of South America, connected to Potosí and the highlands. Both housed viceroys, audiencias, archbishops, universities, and the headquarters of treasury and mercantile institutions. Their authority extended to peripheral regions with diminishing effect, and communication over mountains, deserts, and oceans shaped how orders traveled and how officials acted.

Other regions differed sharply. The Caribbean islands had lost most of their Indigenous populations, and Santo Domingo, Havana, and San Juan became fortified ports and fleet stops governed by military governors and funded by the situado, with large enslaved African populations in some areas. Central America was governed through the audiencia and captaincy general of Guatemala, with Indigenous communities, especially in the highlands, administered through local lords and friars, and with frontier regions in the Caribbean lowlands only partly controlled. Northern South America was governed through audiencias in Bogotá and Quito, and through governors in Venezuela and the Caribbean coast, where Cartagena was a major port and slave market.

In the far south and the frontiers, government was shaped by war and distance. In Chile, a governor and captain general presided over a prolonged struggle with the Mapuche, financed by transfers from Peru and sustained by a standing army. The Río de la Plata had a small population and a precarious position; Buenos Aires was refounded in 1580, and it briefly had its own audiencia from 1661 to 1671. Northern New Spain was governed through presidios (forts) and missions, with Indigenous allies from central Mexico, including Tlaxcalans, settled as colonists. Frontier government depended heavily on military leaders, missionaries, and negotiation with Indigenous peoples who were not under Spanish control. Across all these regions, the same legal framework meant very different things.

Several frontiers deserve separate mention. In the Philippines, Spanish government was organized from Mexico, with the Audiencia of Manila established in 1583, and the Manila galleon carried silver from Acapulco to the Philippines. In northern New Spain, settlement by miners, ranchers, and missionaries expanded into Nueva Vizcaya and, from 1598, New Mexico, where governors and friars ruled a small Spanish population among Pueblo communities and the system collapsed in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. In Chile, a permanent army was established at the beginning of the seventeenth century, supported by transfers from Peru. In the Río de la Plata region, governors answered to the audiencia of Charcas, Buenos Aires developed as a port with a large contraband trade, and the Jesuit missions among the Guaraní formed an alternative mode of governance in the interior.

These differences affected the daily experience of government. A Nahua town in the Valley of Mexico lived under a Spanish judge, an Indigenous council, a parish priest, and the scrutiny of the audiencia. A village in the Chaco or on the Mapuche frontier might see an official only occasionally, if at all, and relations were negotiated with military commanders and missionaries. In Cartagena or Havana, the most visible state presence was the garrison and the customs house. Comparing these settings reminds students that Spanish America was governed through many local arrangements, and sets up the comparison with the Portuguese system in Chapter 41.

Section 40.16

Negotiated Imperial Authority: Local Elites, Indigenous Communities, and the Crown

Negotiation was fundamental to the system, but it was not conducted between equals. Local elites negotiated through council petitions, purchases of office, and family ties to judges and viceroys. The Quito rebellion of 1592–93 against the alcabala, resolved by compromise and pardons after the Crown refused to back down on principle, shows how a colonial elite could resist a tax without rejecting royal authority. In many places, the sale of offices allowed local families to buy influence, so that the Crown's fiscal needs and the elite's political ambitions were mutually reinforcing.

Indigenous communities negotiated through litigation, petitions, delegations, and sometimes flight or open resistance. Tlaxcala's special status, including exemption from encomienda and certain privileges, was built on its claims of service. In 1591 the Crown and the viceroy negotiated an agreement under which hundreds of Tlaxcalan families settled in the northern frontier of New Spain with privileges, as part of a strategy to secure territory. Andean communities sued over land, tribute assessments, and mit'a obligations, and kurakas traveled to Lima and even Spain with petitions. Their success was uneven, and the vast majority of cases were slow and expensive. Negotiation also took place under the threat of force: the execution of Túpac Amaru I in 1572 was a reminder of what the state could do.

Afro-descended people negotiated within narrower limits. Free people of African descent in New Spain were required to pay tribute from the 1570s, and many served in militias, which gave them standing and exemptions in practice. Enslaved people appealed to courts and church authorities regarding mistreatment, family separation, and manumission. Maroon communities negotiated for freedom, and their chronologies are disputed. Near Veracruz, a viceregal expedition against the maroon leader Yanga in 1609 failed to subdue his community, and negotiations followed. In her study of San Lorenzo Cerralvo (Ulúa, 2012), Adriana Naveda Chávez-Hita distinguishes a settlement that developed earlier, which one authority dates to 1608 and another to 1618, from the viceregal legitimation of the town of San Lorenzo de los Negros under the Marquis of Cerralvo, associated with 1630. The community therefore emerged through a long process of conflict and negotiation, and no single date should be treated as an undisputed founding. Near Cartagena, the governor offered peace to the maroon leader Benkos Biohó in 1605, though the Spanish later seized and executed him in 1621. The settlement of San Basilio de Palenque belongs to a sequence of 1713–1714: a pardon and permission for commerce were issued in December 1713, and in January 1714 Bishop Antonio María Cassiani and the community leader Nicolás de Santa Rosa negotiated the agreement associated with the name San Basilio Magno and the community's church. A royal charter of 1691 recognizing the freedom of runaway communities in the Montes de María is widely cited, but its significance is disputed and it should not be treated as the settled founding act of San Basilio. These cases, further discussed in Chapter 47, show negotiation as an alternative to endless warfare, with the state conceding limited autonomy in exchange for loyalty, and they show how uncertain the surviving documentation can be.

Negotiation could also be institutionalized. Free Black and mulatto militias in Mexico, Lima, Cartagena, Havana, and elsewhere offered the Crown defense at low cost and gave their members privileges and recognized corporate status. Militia officers petitioned the Crown, and their service was cited in claims for exemption from tribute or labor. Even so, discrimination limited what such service could win, and free people of African descent remained excluded from most offices and from many privileges open to Spaniards.

The limits of negotiation need emphasis. A community that won a favorable ruling might wait years for enforcement, and victory in court did not always prevent the loss of land. Communities that resisted by force, such as the Neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba, were destroyed. The existence of legal channels did not make participants equal, and the poorest and most remote people often had the least access to them. Negotiation was thus a feature of a system built on unequal power, not evidence that power was evenly shared.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was Spanish Colonial Government Centralized or Negotiated?

The question: Did the Spanish monarchy exercise real, centralized control over its American empire, or did government depend so heavily on local actors and compromise that it is better described as negotiated?

Interpretation A — A strong and legally ambitious state: Some historians emphasize the extent of royal institutions: councils, courts, treasuries, written law, inspections, and ceremonial representation. On this view, the Crown created an extensive and durable administrative and legal order, in which officials were conscious of acting as representatives of the king, and in which law structured conflict.
Interpretation B — A system of negotiation and compromise: Others, building on the work of John Leddy Phelan and later scholars of local and Indigenous politics, emphasize that officials adapted, delayed, and compromised, that rival authorities were encouraged to check one another, and that colonial subjects influenced outcomes through petitions and litigation. On this view, the system functioned because it was flexible, not because it was rigidly centralized.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same records: royal legislation and compilations, correspondence between officials and the Crown, council minutes, court cases, and petitions. They differ in what they emphasize and in how they interpret procedures such as "obedezco pero no cumplo."

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians understand the origins of later Latin American politics, including regionalism, strong local elites, and the role of law. It also affects how Indigenous agency is understood. A purely negotiated model risks understating coercion and inequality, while a purely centralized model risks ignoring the roles of colonized people. Many scholars combine the two, describing a state that was ambitious in law, constrained in practice, and effective because it was willing to bargain.

Student task: Choose a case from this chapter, such as the 1624 Mexico City riot or the New Laws, and argue which interpretation it best supports. Identify evidence that could challenge your argument.

Section 40.17

The Spanish Colonial State by 1700: Institutional Strength and Structural Limits

By 1700, the Spanish colonial state had accomplished a great deal. It held together a territory of unprecedented extent for nearly two centuries, with viceroys, audiencias, treasuries, and councils that functioned across vast distances. It created a legal order that incorporated Indigenous communities as corporate bodies with recognized rights, and which many colonized people used. It moved silver, tribute, and information across oceans, and it endured rebellions and conflicts without losing control of the core territories. This durability was real.

It also had contradictions and limits. The system relied on coercion of Indigenous and enslaved labor. Officials routinely enriched themselves, and the sale of offices tied the state to local elites. Law was repeatedly issued but unevenly enforced. Frontiers, such as those in Chile, northern New Spain, and parts of Amazonia and the Chaco, remained beyond effective control. Fiscal strain from Spain's European wars led to pressures that undermined the Crown's ability to monitor its officials. In the seventeenth century, American-born elites gained power in councils and courts, and revenue increasingly stayed in the Americas. These changes did not make the empire disloyal, but they altered the balance between center and periphery, and set the stage for the eighteenth-century reforms.

The next chapters build on this foundation. Chapter 41 examines the Portuguese colonial system, which also relied on local elites but developed different institutions and a different relationship to the Crown. Chapters 42 and 43 show how colonial institutions interacted with mining, labor, agriculture, commerce, and cities. The death of Charles II in 1700 and the arrival of the Bourbon dynasty opened a new era, which Chapter 48 will examine. Here the chapter ends by noting that the Spanish colonial state of 1700 was both an extensive imperial machine and a set of negotiated arrangements, and that its history cannot be reduced to either.

The effects of the sale of offices were far-reaching. By the late seventeenth century, American-born men held a larger proportion of seats in city councils and, after 1683, in some audiencias, and local families had acquired a lasting stake in colonial institutions. This process, sometimes called the creolization of office, did not produce a movement for independence, but it changed the political culture of the colonies. It also left the monarchy dependent on the goodwill of the families it had sold offices to, a dependence that Bourbon reformers would later try to reverse.

Historians disagree about how to characterize the seventeenth century as a whole. Older accounts described a period of decline and "crisis," while more recent work emphasizes economic growth in some regions, increasing local autonomy, and the consolidation of colonial societies. Both views identify real changes. What the evidence supports is a shift in the balance of power from the Crown toward colonial elites, coupled with continuity in the core legal and fiscal structures. The death of Charles II on 1 November 1700 and the accession of the Bourbon Philip V opened a new era in which the monarchy sought to recover control, a story told in Chapter 48.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Colonial Governing Institutions, Sixteenth to Seventeenth Centuries

What Changed

  • Government moved from conquerors' grants to royal officials and courts
  • Encomienda declined and tribute moved toward direct royal collection and coin
  • Sale of municipal and, later, judicial offices brought local elites into the institutions
  • Law was increasingly codified, culminating in 1680

What Stayed the Same

  • Overlapping jurisdictions and rival authorities remained the basic design
  • Indigenous communities continued to govern locally and litigate
  • Distance and slow communication continued to require local discretion
  • Coerced labor and tribute remained foundations of the system

Who experienced change most directly: Encomenderos lost power to the Crown and later to officeholders, and Indigenous communities experienced resettlement and new tribute arrangements. Greater continuity: Indigenous town government and local legal practices adapted but endured. Why the pattern matters: It shows a state that changed through accommodation rather than replacement, which shaped the political culture inherited by independent republics.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Spain built an extensive system of government across its American territories and that this system depended on negotiation. The Crown moved from conquest by private entrepreneurs to rule through royal officials, courts, councils, and treasuries, in part to prevent conquerors from becoming hereditary lords. The process was shaped by crisis, including abuses, rebellions, and demographic collapse.

In Spain, the Council of the Indies and the Casa de Contratación supervised the empire. The Council advised on law, appointments, and appeals; the Casa regulated trade and navigation. Slow communication across the Atlantic meant that local officials exercised wide discretion, and the Crown relied on cross-checking and on information gathered through letters, questionnaires, and inspections.

The Viceroyalties of New Spain (1535) and Peru (1542) were the principal jurisdictions for the period studied. Viceroys represented the king but were constrained by audiencias, treasury officials, the Church, and local elites. The eighteenth-century viceroyalties of New Granada and the Río de la Plata belong to the later Bourbon reorganization. Conflicts such as the Peruvian rebellion of the 1540s and the Mexico City riot of 1624 show the limits of viceregal authority.

Audiencias combined judicial and governmental functions and varied by type. They were intended to act as checks on viceroys and governors and to provide justice, and the Crown tried to keep judges independent of local society, though those efforts weakened, especially after audiencia judgeships began to be sold commercially in 1683. Provincial officials such as governors, corregidores, and alcaldes mayores brought government to local communities, often with low pay and high temptation, and jurisdictional overlap produced constant conflict.

Cabildos governed cities and their surroundings, controlling markets, public works, and land. They were dominated by property-holding vecinos, and the sale of offices increased the influence of local families. Women, Indigenous people, and Afro-descended people were mostly excluded from formal office but participated in municipal life through work, confraternities, and militias.

The república de españoles and república de indios were legal categories that structured Spanish rule, supported by resettlement policies such as congregaciones and reducciones. In practice, the boundary was porous and unevenly enforced. Indigenous leaders and communities were integral to government: caciques and curacas, Indigenous cabildos, and community officials collected tribute, supplied labor, and defended collective interests through petitions and litigation, with institutions such as the Juzgado General de Indios in Mexico supporting their access to the courts.

The law of the Indies combined Castilian law, royal decrees, local ordinances, and recognized Indigenous custom. The New Laws of 1542 and the Recopilación of 1680 show the Crown's ambitions, while "obedezco pero no cumplo" illustrates the gap between law and enforcement. Labor and tribute institutions, including encomienda, repartimiento, and mit'a, were regulated by royal policy, which gradually moved toward direct control and coin payment, shaped by demographic collapse and fiscal need.

The Church was a partner and rival through the patronato real, as the Crown controlled appointments while bishops and orders retained their own authority. The royal treasury drew revenue from tribute, mining levies, customs, sales taxes, and office sales, and redistributed part of it through the situado to defended frontiers. Residencias, visitas, questionnaires, and petitions provided oversight and information, and they could restrain abuse, reproduce inequality, or serve factional interests.

Regional variation was great: Mexico City and Lima were centers of government, while the Caribbean, Central America, northern South America, Chile, and the Río de la Plata were governed under different military, demographic, and Indigenous conditions. Negotiation was fundamental, involving local elites, Indigenous communities, and Afro-descended people, though without equal power. By 1700 the Spanish colonial state was durable and legally ambitious but dependent on coercion, local elites, and compromise, and these features shaped the world that Bourbon reformers and later republics inherited.

Law and Practice: A Royal Provision and a Local Dispute

This project asks you to compare what the Crown prescribed with what happened in a particular place, and to evaluate what each kind of source can reveal.

  1. Select a theme. Choose one: Indigenous town government, judges and local society, taxation, or labor drafts.
  2. Locate the law. In a digital edition of the 1680 Recopilación, find the relevant provisions, record their book, title, and law numbers, and note the original dates and issuing monarchs.
  3. Locate evidence of practice. Using translated documents, scholarly studies, or published collections, find a petition, lawsuit, municipal record, or investigation concerning the same theme in a specific region.
  4. Compare. Identify where law and practice agree, differ, or are silent, considering each source's author, date, and purpose.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what can be reconstructed with confidence, what remains uncertain, and what additional evidence would help.

Library resources to use: Digital editions of colonial legal compilations, translated primary-source collections on colonial Spanish America, scholarly studies of Indigenous litigation and colonial administration, and the MGU History Library's reference materials.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Spanish America was governed by a state of unusual legal ambition and equally unusual dependence on others. Councils in Spain wrote laws, viceroys and judges applied them, treasuries counted tribute, and communities petitioned. Yet the system worked because officials delayed, adapted, and bargained, and because Indigenous leaders, municipal elites, clergy, and ordinary people learned how to operate within it.

The chapter also shows how conquest became colonial society. The destruction of the Mexica and Inca imperial systems, described in earlier chapters, did not immediately produce stable government. Stability came through institutions that gave conquered peoples a defined place, often an unequal and coercive one, and that gave them tools to defend themselves. Indigenous communities that appear in earlier chapters as allies, rivals, and victims reappear here as litigants, officeholders, and taxpayers whose collective institutions endured.

Many features of this system survived into the republican era: strong local elites, regionalism, a legal culture based on petition and patronage, and tensions between central authority and local power. Independence, examined in Volume III, would not erase them. Students should also notice the limits of the chapter's frame: law and government were only part of colonial life, and the economies, social hierarchies, religion, and resistance that filled it are the subjects of the chapters ahead.

Chapter 41 turns to Portuguese Brazil, where a different Crown, a different geography, and a different relationship between settlers and officials produced a different kind of colonial government. Comparing the two will help students see which features of Spanish rule were distinctive and which belonged to the wider early modern Iberian world.
