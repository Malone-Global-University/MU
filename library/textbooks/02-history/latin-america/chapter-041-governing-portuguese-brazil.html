Governing Portuguese Brazil

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Monarchy That Governed by Layers

When Tomé de Sousa sailed for the Bay of All Saints in 1549, he carried a set of royal instructions, a fleet of more than a thousand people, and a problem that no Portuguese king had yet solved in America: how to turn a claim to a long and thinly settled coast into a working government.

Chapter 40 examined how Spain built viceroyalties, audiencias, and Indigenous municipal institutions in a world of dense tribute-paying populations. Portugal's task in Brazil was different. The Portuguese Crown held a coast that stretched for thousands of kilometers, with peoples who were not organized into centralized states, no early silver, and a monarchy whose wealth and attention lay in Asia and Africa. The government that emerged was not a smaller copy of Spanish America. It was assembled in layers: private captaincies, then a royal governor-general, then courts, treasuries, municipal councils, religious institutions, separate territorial governments, and, by 1642, an Overseas Council in Lisbon.

The central argument of this chapter is that Portuguese government in Brazil developed through successive additions and not through a comprehensive plan. The monarchy first delegated substantial powers to hereditary donatários, then established a governor-general without abolishing the captaincies, expanded royal institutions, depended on câmaras and private interests, and created a separate northern government where geography required it. By the seventeenth century Brazil was part of a connected Atlantic administrative system whose institutions relied on local cooperation, coercion, and negotiation.

The chapter concentrates on roughly 1530 to 1700. It does not retell the conquests and frontier wars narrated in Chapter 39, the Spanish system of Chapter 40, the slave trade and Afro-Brazilian society of Chapter 44, the religious history of Chapter 46, or the eighteenth-century reforms of Chapter 48. It asks how authority was organized and exercised, and how Indigenous peoples, Africans, settlers, missionaries, and officials experienced it. Indigenous allies and enemies, enslaved Africans and their descendants, and women such as Dona Ana Pimentel, Dona Brites de Albuquerque, and Catarina Paraguaçu appear in this story, though most formal offices were closed to them.

Evidence for government comes from several kinds of sources. Royal instructions, such as the 1548 Regimento, show what the Crown intended. Municipal records, treasury accounts, court cases, and the correspondence preserved in the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino in Lisbon show how institutions operated. Jesuit letters, travel accounts such as Gabriel Soares de Sousa's description of Brazil (1587), and early histories such as Frei Vicente do Salvador's (1627) describe the colony from particular points of view. Inquisition denunciations and parish registers reach people who rarely appear elsewhere. All of these were produced by literate elites or institutions, and Indigenous and African perspectives must usually be reconstructed from them.

The chapter's sections move from the founding institutions, through the offices and courts of colonial administration, to the governance of Indigenous peoples and settlers and the effects of foreign war, and finally to territorial expansion and the Atlantic framework. The closing synthesis compares the Portuguese and Spanish systems without treating either as the standard for the other.

Historical Context

Earlier chapters laid the foundations. Chapters 23 and 24 described the Indigenous societies of the Brazilian coast and interior, including Tupi-speaking peoples, and Chapter 28 explained the Treaty of Tordesillas and the first Portuguese presence. Chapter 39 narrated the Portuguese coast, the captaincies, the bandeirantes, and the frontier wars. Chapter 40 described Spanish administration. This chapter assumes those accounts and asks how Portuguese claims were turned into institutions.

The monarchy that created them was an Atlantic and Asian maritime empire with experience of delegation. Islands such as Madeira and the Azores had been settled under donatary captaincies, and trading posts in Africa and India were governed by a mixture of royal officials, contractors, and military orders. The Crown's legal framework came from Portuguese law, and its local institutions followed Portuguese models: the câmara, the ouvidor, the provedor. The Order of Christ and the padroado gave the monarchy a unique tie to the Church overseas.

Conditions in Brazil differed from those in Mexico or Peru. There were no centralized Indigenous states to inherit, so the Portuguese dealt with many separate societies through alliance, trade, war, and enslavement. Sugar, introduced in the 1530s and 1540s, would soon become the basis of settlement, and African slavery grew alongside it. Distances along the coast were enormous, and communication with Lisbon depended on winds and fleets. These conditions help explain why government developed through layers, regional variation, and negotiation.

Historians interpret this process in different ways. Older accounts stressed the failure of the captaincies and the rescue by royal centralization. More recent scholarship stresses the continuing importance of local elites and corporate bodies, and the plural, overlapping character of authority. The chapter reflects this scholarship while acknowledging the real power of royal institutions.

I — Establishing Portuguese Government in Brazil
Section 41.1

The Portuguese Monarchy and Its American Possessions

Brazil entered Portuguese history as one part of a maritime empire whose center of gravity lay elsewhere. When Pedro Álvares Cabral's fleet reached the coast in April 1500, Portugal already held fortified trading posts along the West African coast, island colonies in the Atlantic, and, within a decade, Goa (1510) and Malacca (1511) in the Indian Ocean. The Crown's income and prestige rested on the spice trade of Asia and the gold and slave trade of Africa. A long, thinly known coastline with no obvious cities, no gold, and Indigenous societies that did not fit the tribute model Spaniards were then finding in Mexico, seemed a secondary prospect. Chapter 28 explained how the Treaty of Tordesillas assigned Portugal its claim; this chapter asks how that claim became a government.

The first Portuguese economic activity was brazilwood, a dye wood cut and carried to ships by Indigenous workers, mostly Tupi-speaking peoples, who exchanged labor for metal tools and other goods. Traders built small fortified depots (feitorias), and the Crown leased its monopoly to merchant contractors, while French ships competed for the same trade. A claim maintained only by occasional trading voyages was vulnerable. By the 1530s the monarchy of João III decided that permanent settlement was needed to hold the coast, and it chose a method with a long Portuguese pedigree: delegating authority to private lords who would settle and defend land at their own expense, as had been done in Madeira and the Azores.

Three features of the Portuguese system frame everything that follows. First, the Crown claimed sovereignty but governed by delegation, through donatários, municipal councils, religious orders, and contractors, and it kept adding royal institutions over time rather than replacing the earlier ones. Second, the law was Portuguese: the Ordenações Manuelinas of the early sixteenth century and, from 1603, the Ordenações Filipinas supplied the basic civil, criminal, and municipal rules. Third, the Crown's relationship with the Church was close. Royal ecclesiastical patronage (padroado) had developed through fifteenth-century papal concessions to the Crown and to the Order of Christ, whose masters were members of the royal family, and it gave the monarchy influence over the overseas Church and the revenue of its tithes. In 1551 the papal bull Praeclara charissimi permanently joined the masterships of the Orders of Christ, Santiago, and Avis to the Portuguese Crown, consolidating these earlier rights and making the king the permanent head of the orders. The chapter therefore explains government in Brazil as layers added over time and not as one design.

Portuguese practice in Africa and Asia shaped expectations. There the Crown had generally secured fortified trading posts and coastal strongholds, backed by naval power and treaties with local rulers, and had not tried to administer large inland populations. The same logic first applied in Brazil: the monarchy sought harbors, supplies, and trade, and treated the interior as a zone of Indigenous power to be approached by negotiation. Settlement was also limited by the size of Portugal itself, a small kingdom whose population was needed in several oceans. The people who went to Brazil in the first decades included officials, soldiers, merchants, artisans, New Christians seeking opportunity, and convicts sentenced to exile (degredados). Government therefore had to be built with few settlers, little money, and Indigenous peoples who held the land and shaped the terms of contact.

Section 41.2

The Hereditary Captaincies and the Donatários

Between 1534 and 1536 João III divided the Brazilian coast into hereditary captaincies, each running inland from the sea. The conventional count is fourteen captaincies, distributed as fifteen territorial parcels among twelve donatários, since some captaincies were made up of geographically separate parcels and some donatários received more than one grant. The donatários were mostly lesser nobles, military men, and officials with experience in Asia or Africa. Each captaincy (capitania) was created by a grant, the carta de doação, and regulated by a charter, the foral, which set out the donatário's rights and the Crown's reserved rights. The grants were hereditary and could not be divided. They gave the donatário broad powers: to found towns (vilas), to distribute land to settlers as sesmarias, to appoint judges and notaries, to exercise civil and criminal jurisdiction, and to hold monopolies over certain mills and fisheries.

The Crown kept important rights for itself: the royal fifth of any precious metals, the tithes, which belonged to the Order of Christ and therefore to the Crown, the monopoly on brazilwood and spices, customs duties, and a right of appeal in serious cases. The donatário's obligations were as important as his powers. He was to bring settlers, build defenses, and pay the costs from his own resources. The system was thus a contract in which the monarchy transferred risk and expense to private individuals in return for possible expansion of its claim. Women took part: Dona Ana Pimentel administered São Vicente while her husband, Martim Afonso de Sousa, was away, and Dona Brites de Albuquerque directed Pernambuco after the death of her husband, Duarte Coelho, in the 1550s.

Results were uneven. Pernambuco, under Duarte Coelho, and São Vicente flourished, in large part because of sugar mills and alliances with some Indigenous groups. Others failed or stagnated: donatários lacked money, settlers were few, Indigenous resistance was serious, and some grants were never occupied. In Bahia the donatário was killed in the 1540s, which created the legal opening the Crown used in 1548–49. The captaincies also illustrate the gap between claimed and occupied territory. On paper each grant extended inland to the Tordesillas line, but in practice a captaincy might consist of a few coastal settlements, with the interior controlled by Indigenous peoples. Chapter 39 describes the conquest of these regions; the point here is that the captaincy was an instrument of sovereignty and colonization, with limited actual power.

The donatários used their powers in different ways. Duarte Coelho of Pernambuco founded Olinda in 1535, brought settlers and capital, built sugar mills, and sought alliances with some Indigenous groups while fighting others, and his captaincy became the richest in the colony. Martim Afonso de Sousa had already founded São Vicente in 1532 and encouraged an early mill, but he returned to Asia and left the captaincy in the hands of agents. The Crown did not withdraw entirely. It kept its own officers in each captaincy to collect royal revenues, defended its right to hear serious appeals, and mediated disputes between donatários and settlers, who often complained of abuses. The donatary system thus combined private lordship with a continuing royal presence, a mixture that explains both its early successes and its later redefinition.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitania, Donatário, Foral, and Carta de Doação

A capitania (captaincy) was a territorial grant that gave its holder, the donatário, hereditary rights of jurisdiction and settlement. The carta de doação was the deed of grant, and the foral was the charter that set out the donatário's powers, the settlers' obligations, and the taxes owed to the Crown.

The English word "captain" is misleading, since a donatário was less a military officer than a hereditary lord with delegated royal powers. The captaincy was also not a modern province: its boundaries were mostly theoretical, and its real extent depended on who actually settled and defended it.

Section 41.3

The Creation of the Governor-General, 1548–1549

By the late 1540s the weaknesses of the captaincy system were clear. Several grants had failed, the French were trading and settling along the coast, and Indigenous resistance made isolated settlements precarious. João III decided to add royal government to the donatary system. In 1548 the Crown acquired the captaincy of Bahia, whose donatário had died, and made it the seat of a governor-general, with authority to coordinate defense, justice, and revenue across all the captaincies. Tomé de Sousa, a nobleman with military experience in North Africa and India, was appointed and received his instructions, the Regimento, dated 17 December 1548.

Tomé de Sousa left Portugal in early 1549 with a fleet of six ships and more than a thousand people, including soldiers, convicts sent into exile (degredados), artisans, officials, and six Jesuits led by Manuel da Nóbrega. They reached the Bay of All Saints on 29 March 1549 and began building the fortified city of Salvador, with help from Diogo Álvares, called Caramuru, a Portuguese who had lived for decades among the Tupinambá, and from his Tupinambá connections, including Catarina Paraguaçu, whose role as intermediary is recorded in Portuguese sources. The expedition brought with it the officers who would staff the new government: a chief treasury official (provedor-mor), a chief judge (ouvidor-geral), and a coastal defense commander. This was a state apparatus built in the field.

The Regimento became a model for later instructions to governors. Historians who have translated it, notably in the documentary collection Early Brazil edited by Stuart Schwartz and Alida Metcalf, note that it begins with the king's declaration that supporting and ennobling the captaincies served both God and the Crown. It directs the governor to build a fortified city on the Bay of All Saints, chosen for its harbor, rivers, and healthy land, and to be captain of that fortress and territory. It also addresses relations with Indigenous peoples, allotment of land, encouragement of sugar mills, the work of judges and treasury officers, and the visiting of the other captaincies. Its details on Indigenous policy matter, because the Crown wished to secure allies and trade while allowing force against groups that attacked settlers. The Regimento tells us what the Crown wanted, not what the governor achieved.

The first years of the governor-general show how royal government was built in practice. Salvador's walls and buildings were raised with timber and earth, with labor drawn from the fleet's artisans, from Indigenous allies, and from enslaved people. A town council was created in 1549, and the Jesuits founded a school and began to organize Indigenous villages around the city. The Crown encouraged settlement by sending more colonists, and in the early 1550s it dispatched orphan girls to marry settlers. Tomé de Sousa also traveled along the coast to inspect other captaincies, judge disputes, and assert royal rights, though donatários did not always welcome the visit. His term ended in 1553, and his successors found, as he had, that a governor's authority depended on cooperation from the people he was supposed to command.

Historical Vocabulary
Governo-Geral and Regimento

The governo-geral (general government) was the royal office, created in 1548–49, of a governor-general with overall responsibility for the Estado do Brasil. A regimento was a formal set of royal instructions defining an officeholder's duties, jurisdiction, and procedures; each major office had its own.

A regimento was not a modern constitution or job description. It combined law, advice, and aspiration, and it could be revised or ignored in practice. For historians, regimentos are among the best evidence of what the Crown expected of its officials.

Primary Source Window

The Regimento of Tomé de Sousa, 17 December 1548

Creator: King João III of Portugal · Date: 17 December 1548 · Type: A royal instruction (regimento) to an appointed governor. An English translation appears in Schwartz and Metcalf, Early Brazil: A Documentary Collection to 1700 (Cambridge), under "Royal Government."

Context: The document was written in Lisbon, by the king's officials, for a commander about to sail. The text was also used later as a pattern for instructions to senior officials in Brazil.

Paraphrase of the opening and core provisions: the king states that, in the service of God and his own interest, he wishes to maintain and develop the captaincies and settlements of Brazil. He names the Bay of All Saints as the most suitable place on the coast for a principal town, citing the harbor, the rivers that flow into it, and the healthy and productive land. The governor is ordered to build a fortress there and to serve as captain of the fortress and of the surrounding territory of Bahia. Further provisions concern coastal defense, the settlement and land grants, the regulation of dealings with Indigenous peoples, and the work of the officials who accompany him. (For exact wording and the full list of provisions, read the translation in the collection cited above.)

Analysis: The Regimento shows a monarchy trying to convert a claim into administration by creating a fortified capital, a chain of command, and a model of duties. It also reveals Crown priorities: defense, revenue, settlement, and an agreeable relationship with Indigenous allies. It cannot tell us how the governor interpreted these orders, how Indigenous peoples experienced them, what the donatários thought, or how much of it was carried out. Evidence of implementation comes from other sources, including Jesuit letters, later correspondence, and records of the officials who served under him. Instructions written in Lisbon also reflect limited knowledge of the territory.

Section 41.4

Central Authority and the Continuing Captaincy System

The creation of the governor-general did not abolish the captaincies. It placed royal coordination on top of existing grants. Donatários retained their hereditary rights, and governors-general frequently quarreled with them over jurisdiction, taxation, and appointments. At the same time the Crown created new royal captaincies directly. Rio de Janeiro became a royal captaincy after its founding in 1565, and Paraíba was established as royal land in the 1580s. For two centuries donatary and royal captaincies existed side by side, and the Crown acquired the remaining private grants gradually, mostly through purchase in the early eighteenth century and a final abolition under Pombal that belongs to Chapter 48.

The early governors-general show how the system developed. Tomé de Sousa (1549–53) built Salvador and surveyed the coast. Duarte da Costa (1553–57) faced conflicts with the first bishop and with Indigenous groups, and French settlers established themselves in Guanabara Bay. Mem de Sá (1558–72) is usually credited with consolidating the government: he defeated the French, supported the founding of Rio in 1565, and worked with the Jesuits to resettle Indigenous people into villages. In 1572 the Crown tried a different arrangement, dividing Brazil into a northern government at Salvador and a southern government at Rio. The Arquivo Nacional dates the royal decision to reunify the government to April 1577, and the single governor-general is generally described as taking effect in 1578, so the reunification should be understood as a decision in 1577 and a transition completed in 1578. The experiment shows the monarchy testing ways to govern a coast that stretched for thousands of kilometers.

The result was layered government. A settler in São Vicente answered to a donatário's officers, local councils, church officials, and the governor-general's representatives, each with different claims. Layered authority created opportunities for conflict and also for people who could play one authority against another. It also meant that orders issued at Salvador were interpreted and applied differently from one captaincy to another.

Contemporaries understood the governor-general's authority as higher than a donatário's but not unlimited. Captains in royal captaincies could correspond directly with the Crown, and Lisbon often heard complaints from local officials, councils, and bishops without passing through Salvador. This allowed the monarchy to cross-check its governors, yet it also meant that orders could be delayed or contradicted. In regions far from Bahia, such as Pernambuco, Rio, and later Maranhão, governors-general found it difficult to enforce their wishes, and Dutch occupation in the north and French intrusion in the south repeatedly showed the limits of Salvador's reach. The monarchy's gradual purchase of captaincies and its creation of royal ones therefore expressed a long-term effort to increase direct control, not a single act of centralization.

Distance also shaped how royal authority worked in practice. Letters between Salvador and Lisbon could take months, and communications along the coast depended on winds and the availability of ships. A governor-general who needed an answer from the king might wait most of a year, so he acted first and reported afterward. Colonial officials therefore developed habits of improvisation, and the Crown learned to judge them by results and by the complaints of their rivals. These conditions encouraged officials to cultivate local allies, since a governor without the cooperation of councils, militia captains, and planters could do little. The coexistence of private and royal captaincies, in this sense, mirrored the broader pattern of shared and negotiated power.

II — Institutions of Colonial Administration
Section 41.5

Governors, Captains-Major, and Royal Officials

Below the governor-general, government was organized through officers whose titles reveal their functions. Each royal captaincy had a governor or capitão-mor, who combined military command with civil authority. In donatary captaincies, the donatário or his appointee performed these roles. Judges (ouvidores) handled legal matters, the first ouvidor-geral having accompanied Tomé de Sousa, and in 1628 a regimento for the ouvidor-geral of the Estado do Brasil defined his duties. Treasury officials (provedores and their clerks) collected and recorded revenue under the provedor-mor. Students should distinguish four kinds of authority that the sources often blur: military command, civil government, justice, and tax collection.

Military organization depended heavily on part-time forces. A royal law of 1570 organized all able-bodied free men into militia companies (ordenanças), commanded locally, and after the Dutch invasions the Crown also maintained paid regiments (terços) and fortifications. The result was a militarized society in which local leaders held both civil and military rank. The examples differ by region. Bahia was the seat of the governor-general and the Relação. Pernambuco remained under a donatary family for most of this period, was occupied by the Dutch from 1630 to 1654, and developed powerful local elites. Rio de Janeiro, a royal captaincy, grew as a naval and commercial port, and its governors, such as Salvador Correia de Sá e Benevides, were influential figures who also led the reconquest of Angola in 1648.

Officeholders were often rewarded for service rather than trained for administration. Offices were granted as royal favors (mercês), often to men who had served in Asia, Africa, or against the Dutch, and could be held through deputies or sold on in various forms. Salaries were modest, and officials relied on fees and favors. Conflicts among officers were routine, and the governor-general's authority over captaincies and officials in other parts of Brazil was frequently contested. The Crown encouraged this indirectly by hearing complaints from all sides.

Rules for the governor-general's own conduct became more detailed over time. Terms were generally limited to about three years, though they could be extended. In 1677 the Crown issued a long regimento to Governor-General Roque da Costa Barreto, which repeated many earlier provisions and added new ones on defense, justice, finance, and the treatment of Indigenous people, and which later governors treated as a standard text. Conflicts among officials were common. The first bishop quarreled with Governor-General Duarte da Costa, governors disputed jurisdiction with ouvidores, and treasury officers resisted interference in their accounts. The Crown tolerated some friction because rival officials reported on one another, but it also tried to clarify powers through new instructions, which is why regimentos survive in such number.

Section 41.6

Câmaras Municipais and Local Political Power

The municipal council, or câmara, was the central institution of local government and one of the most important political bodies in colonial Brazil. A typical câmara consisted of councillors (vereadores), two ordinary judges (juízes ordinários), a procurator, a clerk, and market inspectors (almotacés) who served short terms. Only certain men, the homens bons ("good men"), were eligible: free property-holding householders of good reputation, usually excluded if they worked at manual trades or were thought to have impure ancestry. Elections followed a system in which electors drew up lists of eligible men, names were sealed in wax balls (pelouros), and officers were drawn each year.

The câmara regulated markets and weights, set prices for staple foods, maintained roads, bridges, wells, and jails, organized public festivals such as Corpus Christi, levied local assessments, and raised militia or labor for defense. It could write directly to the king, send agents to Lisbon, and petition for privileges. Because the Crown was thin on the ground, the câmara also became the voice of local property owners in negotiations with governors and ouvidores. Late in the seventeenth century the Crown began to appoint royal judges (juízes de fora) to some towns to supervise councils, a sign that it saw them as both indispensable and difficult to control.

The influence of councils appears in conflict. In Rio de Janeiro in 1660–61, the câmara and townspeople rose against Governor Salvador Correia de Sá e Benevides amid disputes over taxation, and a local faction controlled the town for several months before the governor returned. In Bahia, the council supported the petition that led the Crown to restore the Relação in 1652. In São Paulo, the council expelled the Jesuits in 1640. These cases show that câmaras could resist royal officials and act as bases for factions, and also that the Crown usually restored its authority afterward through negotiation, pardon, or selective punishment.

Exclusion defined the councils as much as inclusion did. Rules about purity of blood, which disqualified descendants of Jews, Muslims, Africans, and sometimes Indigenous people from certain offices and honors, and the exclusion of men who worked in manual trades, kept most residents out of the formal political community, and the boundaries were contested and varied by town. Women could not vote or hold office, yet widows often managed households, estates, and sugar mills, and they appear in petitions and legal cases as landholders. The councils also saw themselves as spokesmen for a wider community. When they wrote to the king, they spoke for "the people" of the town, a formula that claimed legitimacy while excluding most of the population from the discussion.

Historical Vocabulary
Câmara Municipal, Homem Bom, and Vereador

The câmara municipal was the town council, the vereadores its councillors, and the homens bons the eligible local men who could vote and hold office. Together they made up a community that was legally and socially defined as the political body of a town.

A homem bom was not simply "a good man" in a moral sense but a person of recognized standing: free, usually white or accepted as such, a property holder, and a Christian of acceptable ancestry. The category excluded most of the population, including enslaved people, many Indigenous people, free people of African descent in many cases, and poorer artisans.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Government Touched Daily Life in a Colonial Town

In a town such as Salvador, much of what people experienced as government came from the câmara. Its inspectors checked weights and measures and the quality of bread and meat, its ordinances fixed where animals could be slaughtered and where water could be drawn, and it required householders to maintain the street in front of their houses. It organized the Corpus Christi procession, in which guilds, brotherhoods, and officeholders marched in a prescribed order that expressed rank. A charitable brotherhood, the Misericórdia, founded in Salvador in 1549, ran a hospital and cared for orphans, widows, and prisoners.

Enslaved Africans and Indigenous people did much of the physical work, carrying water and goods, building, and tending animals. Free and enslaved women sold food in the streets and ran households, though they could not hold office. Black confraternities, such as those devoted to Our Lady of the Rosary, appear by the seventeenth century and gave people of African descent institutions of their own. These details come largely from council records, ecclesiastical records, and the correspondence of officials, which tend to register people when they were regulated, taxed, or punished.

Section 41.7

Royal Justice, Ouvidores, and the Relação da Bahia

Justice moved upward through several levels. Ordinary judges elected by the câmara handled local cases, ouvidores heard appeals and supervised justice in a captaincy or district, and the ouvidor-geral had jurisdiction over the Estado do Brasil as a whole. Above them, in principle, stood courts in Lisbon, especially the Casa da Suplicação and the Desembargo do Paço, which meant that appeals from Brazil could take years. Other jurisdictions existed alongside royal justice: church courts for clergy and some matters, judges of orphans, private justice exercised by landowners over their dependents, and, in donatary captaincies, the donatário's own judges.

To bring a high court closer, the Crown established the Relação da Bahia. It was planned in the 1580s and formally regulated by a regimento of 7 March 1609. The court was presided over by the governor-general and staffed by university-trained judges (desembargadores) sent from Portugal. It heard appeals, supervised lower judges, and handled certain administrative matters. In 1626 the Crown suppressed it, with cost, quarrels with governors, and the Dutch invasion among the reasons commonly cited, and appeals again went to Lisbon. After the Restoration, a royal letter of 12 September 1652 reestablished the court, with support from Bahian authorities, and it remained the only high court in Brazil until the mid-eighteenth century.

Stuart Schwartz's study of the court, Sovereignty and Society in Colonial Brazil (1973), shows how the Relação embodied royal sovereignty yet was drawn into local society: judges married into local families, owned property, and built relationships with planters and merchants despite rules against doing so. The comparison with Spanish audiencias is instructive but should not be pressed too far. Both were high courts with administrative duties that tied a governor to the judiciary. But Spanish America had more than a dozen audiencias by 1600, whereas Brazil had one, and the Relação's position under the governor-general differed from the many audiencias outside viceregal capitals.

The ordinary operation of royal justice also relied on inspection. Ouvidores made periodic circuits (correições) through the towns of their district, hearing complaints, examining local officers, and conducting judicial inquiries (devassas). These visits gave residents a chance to appeal beyond the local judge and gave the Crown information about its officials. Litigation was slow and expensive: a case that reached Lisbon could take years, and parties often relied on patrons, relatives, or lawyers with connections. Court records are valuable because they preserve the voices of litigants, witnesses, and clerks, including some Indigenous and enslaved people who sought freedom or redress, although most surviving petitions by Indigenous litigants date from the eighteenth century. The records also show how justice could be bent by influence.

Section 41.8

The Royal Treasury and Colonial Finance

Colonial finance was administered through the Real Fazenda, the royal treasury, headed in Brazil by the provedor-mor and supported by provedores in each royal captaincy, clerks, and customs officers. The Crown's revenues came from several sources. Customs duties (dízima and others) were collected at ports such as Salvador. Tithes belonged to the Crown through the Order of Christ and were generally collected by private contractors who bid for the right. The Crown also leased monopolies, such as brazilwood, and collected the royal fifth on metals, which was minor until the discovery of gold in the 1690s.

Most spending was military. Salaries for soldiers and officials, fortifications, ships, and supplies consumed much of the revenue, and arrears were chronic. The Dutch wars sharply increased costs and pressed the Crown to rely on local contributions: levies approved by councils, loans from planters and merchants, and extraordinary taxes. After the 1661 settlement with the Dutch Republic, in which Portugal agreed to pay a large indemnity, part of the burden was raised from the colonies. Each new tax required negotiation, which gave councils and elites leverage, as the Rio revolt of 1660 suggests.

Treasury institutions also show how Brazil fit into a larger Portuguese system. Overseas revenue was recorded and reviewed in Lisbon, and the Crown moved resources among territories as needs arose. The monarchy initially depended more on Asian trade than on Brazil, but over the seventeenth century, as Asian possessions were lost or reduced to Dutch and English competition, Brazil's customs and tithes became central to royal finance. The commercial and plantation economy behind that revenue is the subject of Chapter 43. The point here is that taxation tied local elites to the monarchy while giving them reasons to bargain.

Contracting was a central device of colonial finance. Instead of collecting taxes directly, the treasury often auctioned the right to collect tithes, customs, or monopoly revenues to private contractors, who advanced money to the Crown and kept the margin. This made contractors important financiers and political figures, and their interests often differed from those of planters. In the 1660s the Crown also imposed extraordinary levies on Brazil, associated with the dowry of Catherine of Braganza on her marriage to Charles II of England and with the Dutch indemnity, and it asked câmaras to organize collection. Local councils negotiated the amounts and the methods, and collection depended on cooperation, so taxation became another arena of bargaining as much as command.

The treasury's capacity was uneven. Soldiers' pay and officials' salaries were frequently in arrears, and the provedores' accounts depended on contractors' honesty and on local officials' willingness to enforce collection. Compared with the network of audited cajas reales that Spain maintained across its American territories, the Portuguese treasury in Brazil was smaller and less closely inspected, so the Crown often learned of problems through complaints. Revenue also varied with the sugar harvest and with shipping, since customs and tithes rose and fell with the volume of goods. The Crown therefore combined fiscal demands with attempts to secure the cooperation of the groups who paid, in the knowledge that heavy-handed collection could provoke resistance.

Section 41.9

Sesmarias, Land Grants, and Territorial Jurisdiction

Land policy was a major instrument of government. The Portuguese system of land grants, sesmarias, derived from a law of 1375 that required landowners in Portugal to cultivate their land or forfeit it. The Crown extended the principle overseas. Donatários and governors granted land to settlers on the condition that they cultivate it within a set time, commonly about five years, pay tithes, and respect boundaries. A grant that was not developed could be taken back. Because land was abundant and labor scarce, grants in Brazil were far larger than in Portugal, and the Crown required confirmation of important titles, which made land ownership a continuing relationship with royal government.

The system concentrated large holdings in the hands of a few families, the Church, and religious orders. Garcia d'Ávila, who came with Tomé de Sousa, founded the Casa da Torre in the Bahian interior and became one of the largest landholders in the colony, an example of how a grant combined landholding, military service, and local power. Land law also worked as a means of defining jurisdiction: who held land determined who owed tithes, who served in the militia, and which officials had authority over them.

The system rested on an assumption that land was empty or underused, which ignored Indigenous settlement, hunting, farming, and ritual use. The Crown sometimes acknowledged Indigenous claims. A royal decree of 1 April 1680 for Indigenous people recognized them as original lords of their lands and said that grants should not harm them. But such statements were hard to enforce, and Indigenous communities often lost land through settler expansion, migration, and resettlement. The gap between legal recognition and practice is a central theme of the next section.

Many settlers occupied land without waiting for a formal grant. In the sertão, ranchers pushed cattle into the interior and claimed land by occupation, often in advance of any official survey, and Indigenous communities were pushed aside or incorporated as workers. Religious orders also built up large estates through grants and donations, and Jesuit properties supported schools and mission villages. Boundaries were frequently vague, and disputes over land were among the most common reasons for litigation. The Crown periodically required registration or confirmation, but systematic regulation of land titles would not come until much later, with the Land Law of 1850. For the period of this chapter, land policy shows a state that distributed property to create loyal and productive settlers while lacking the surveyors, courts, and force to administer it consistently.

III — Governing a Contested Colonial Society
Section 41.10

Indigenous Communities, Royal Legislation, and Colonial Labor Control

Indigenous peoples were participants in the making of colonial government, as allies, enemies, laborers, and residents of mission villages. In São Vicente the Tupiniquim were early partners of the Portuguese. In Rio de Janeiro the Temiminó leader Arariboia allied with the Portuguese against the French and their Tupinambá allies, and was rewarded with membership in the Order of Christ and land for a village. In the northeast the Potiguar leader Antônio Filipe Camarão fought with the Portuguese against the Dutch, received a knighthood and the title Dom, and commanded Indigenous troops until his death in 1648. Other communities resisted: the Confederation of the Tamoios (1554–67) fought the Portuguese and allied with the French in the south. These alliances and conflicts shaped Portuguese government as much as royal decrees did.

The Crown's legislation on Indigenous freedom and slavery changed repeatedly, because it tried to balance missionary pressure, settler demand for labor, military needs, and royal revenue. A law of 1570 restricted enslavement to captives taken in wars authorized by the Crown or its governor and to groups regarded as habitual attackers. Later measures declared Indigenous people free (1609) and then allowed enslavement under just-war and ransom provisions (1611). In the Estado do Maranhão, a law of 1655 regulated enslavement and the missions. A law of 1 April 1680, applying to the Estado do Maranhão, prohibited new enslavement of Indigenous people, including the exceptions previously recognized, such as ransom (resgate), and directed that people captured in authorized wars were to be held as prisoners without losing their legal freedom. The historian José Ribamar Bessa Freire argues that the law did not itself emancipate people who had been enslaved before it was enacted, an interpretation of the text's effects and not an express statutory provision. A Regimento das Missões of 1686 placed the administration of mission villages largely in the hands of religious orders. After intense settler opposition, an alvará of 28 April 1688 restored enslavement after eight years, specifying the conditions of "just war" and of the ransom of captives said to be held by other Indigenous groups.

Three institutions governed Indigenous labor. Mission villages (aldeamentos or aldeias), first organized by Jesuits in the 1550s and concentrated by Governor Mem de Sá, brought people down from the interior (descimentos) to live under missionary supervision and provide labor for both the community and colonists. Slavery by war or ransom supplied captives to settlers. Wage and rotational labor drew on aldeia residents. Legislation often had little effect where enforcement was weak, since settlers could present captives as lawfully taken, and missionaries or officials could be bribed or pressed. Historians therefore read these laws as evidence of policy debates and of the demands of different groups, not as accounts of what happened to Indigenous people. Indigenous leaders (principais) served as intermediaries and sometimes obtained honors and titles, but they were also used to enforce labor demands on their own communities.

Africans and their descendants were also governed through law, courts, and military authority, though in different ways. There was no single comprehensive slave code in Portuguese America. Instead, the Ordenações, local ordinances, church teaching, and the power of owners shaped daily conditions, and royal officials seldom intervened in the relationship between master and enslaved person. Manumission was usually recorded before notaries or in church records, and some free people of African descent served in militia companies, in separate units of Black and mixed-race soldiers, or in brotherhoods that provided mutual aid and burial. Officers charged with capturing runaways, the capitães-do-mato, enforced the owners' claims. Henrique Dias's regiment shows the Crown's willingness to use Black soldiers and to reward their leaders, even as it protected the institution of slavery. The history of slavery and Afro-Brazilian society belongs to Chapters 44 and 47.

Section 41.11

The Padroado Real, Jesuits, and Ecclesiastical Government

The Crown governed the Church in Brazil through the padroado real. Because the king, after 1551, was permanent grand master of the Order of Christ (consolidating patronage rights that had developed earlier), he controlled the creation of dioceses, the presentation of bishops, the payment of clergy, and the collection of tithes, and he was responsible for supporting church building and missions. A bishopric of Brazil was created at Salvador in 1551, and its first bishop, Pero Fernandes Sardinha, was killed by the Caeté in 1556. In 1676 the Church raised Salvador to an archdiocese and created new dioceses at Olinda and Rio de Janeiro, with Maranhão following in 1677. Clergy were thus both church officers and royal clients, and church and state shared a vocabulary of obedience, jurisdiction, and service.

The Jesuits, who arrived with Tomé de Sousa in 1549, held an especially important position. They founded colleges in Salvador and São Paulo, wrote the earliest detailed accounts of Indigenous societies, ran aldeias, and became the leading advocates of Indigenous freedom against settler demands. Their position made them political actors. In 1640 the council of São Paulo expelled them, and in 1661 settlers drove them from Maranhão and Pará after the preaching of António Vieira against enslavement. In 1684 the revolt of Manuel Beckman in São Luís, directed against a monopoly company and Jesuit control of Indigenous labor, again expelled them temporarily. The Crown responded each time by reasserting authority while adjusting policy.

Other institutions also exerted ecclesiastical government. Benedictines, Carmelites, Franciscans, and other orders established houses in the major towns. Parish priests kept registers of baptisms, marriages, and burials, which are essential demographic sources, and the Misericórdias provided hospitals and charity. There was no Inquisition tribunal in Brazil, but the Lisbon tribunal sent visitors who held investigations in Bahia and Pernambuco in the 1590s, and its familiars and denunciations reached into colonial life, especially affecting New Christians. The fuller religious and cultural history belongs to Chapter 46.

The Church's administrative functions extended beyond religion. The Crown paid part of the clergy's incomes, parish priests recorded births, marriages, and deaths, and bishops conducted visitations of their dioceses, examining clergy, hearing complaints, and enforcing marriage rules. Charitable brotherhoods, particularly the Misericórdias, ran hospitals and distributed relief, and lay confraternities of Europeans, Africans, and mixed-race people organized burial, festivals, and mutual aid. Jesuit colleges educated the sons of settlers as well as Indigenous children, and the order's estates, including mills and ranches, made it a major economic actor and therefore a rival of planters. Bishops and governors also quarreled over precedence and jurisdiction, a reminder that the padroado made the Church part of royal government and also a body with interests of its own.

Section 41.12

Settler Elites, Local Interests, and Negotiated Royal Authority

Royal government in Brazil rested on a local elite of landowners, merchants, militia officers, and officeholders, who came to dominate the câmaras, the militia, and the confraternities. Sugar planters (senhores de engenho) held great social and political influence, supported by family networks, dependent workers, and enslaved laborers. Merchants, many of them New Christians or Portuguese-born, connected Brazil to Lisbon and Atlantic markets. The Crown relied on these groups to finance and defend the colony and in turn conferred offices, honors, and titles on them. Elites also used the courts and the Crown's own procedures, writing petitions, litigating, and dispatching agents to Lisbon.

Negotiation involved more than the wealthy. The Crown rewarded Indigenous and Black soldiers who served against the Dutch: Camarão received a knighthood and title, and Henrique Dias, commander of a regiment of Black soldiers, received honors and a pension. Black brotherhoods, mission villages, and individuals petitioned for rights, and enslaved people used the courts and the Church. People of limited standing had little access to formal institutions, and negotiation did not remove coercion: those who could not offer military service or money had few options. The system also left spaces in which less powerful groups could press claims against particular officials, for example by appealing to a bishop, a missionary, or a judge.

Officials could be both agents of the Crown and members of local society. Governors married into local families, ouvidores acquired land, and treasury officers took part in trade. The Crown tried to impose rules, periodic investigations, and rotation, and it used the conflicts among its officials to learn what was happening, but it could not wholly prevent entanglement. The result was a government that depended on local cooperation, rewarded loyalty with status, and punished open defiance. This is the central tension of the chapter, and historians interpret it in different ways.

The careers of individuals illustrate how service turned into standing. João Fernandes Vieira, a Madeiran-born sugar planter and merchant in Pernambuco, helped lead the insurrection against the Dutch in 1645, received honors and offices from the Crown, and later served as governor of Paraíba and of Angola. The Restoration created a group of war veterans and landowners who claimed rewards (mercês) for service, and the Crown responded with titles, military posts, and pensions. Such rewards linked local elites to the monarchy and encouraged them to seek recognition through service and petition rather than rebellion. They also created new rivalries over who deserved reward, as veterans competed with older families and with Portuguese-born officials.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Crown Centralization Versus Local Colonial Authority

The question: How far did the Portuguese Crown control its American colony, and how far did local elites determine how government worked?

Interpretation A: A strong and bureaucratic monarchy. Some historians stress the extent of royal institutions: regimentos, governors-general, the Relação, treasury officers, and the Crown's power of appointment. Raymundo Faoro's classic interpretation, in Os donos do poder (1958), described a patrimonial state in which the Crown and its officials dominated society. Stuart Schwartz's study of the Relação da Bahia showed how consciously the Crown built a judicial bureaucracy to embody sovereignty.
Interpretation B: A polycentric, negotiated monarchy. Others, including António Manuel Hespanha and, in studies of colonial Brazil, historians such as João Fragoso, Maria Fernanda Bicalho, and Maria de Fátima Gouvêa (O Antigo Regime nos trópicos, 2001), emphasize that authority was shared among corporate bodies, officials, and elites, and that câmaras and local families bargained with the Crown on terms of mutual obligation.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same sources: regimentos and royal letters, minutes of câmaras, court cases, treasury records, correspondence preserved in the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino, and accounts of revolts. Schwartz's own findings suggest that even a royal court became embedded in local society, which supports parts of both interpretations.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians understand the origins of later Brazilian political life, including patronage, regional elites, and the relationship between state and landed power. It also affects how much agency is attributed to Indigenous, Black, and poor people, who appear differently in a story of royal command than in a story of negotiation.

Section 41.13

The Iberian Union, Foreign Invasions, and the Portuguese Restoration

When King Henry of Portugal died without an heir in 1580, Philip II of Spain claimed the throne and in 1581 was acclaimed at Tomar as Philip I of Portugal. He promised to preserve Portuguese laws, offices, and the separate administration of its overseas possessions, and the Iberian Union (1580–1640) left Brazil under Portuguese institutions. In practice the union brought Portugal into Spain's wars. The Dutch, fighting Spain, attacked Portuguese holdings. The Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621, captured Salvador in May 1624, which the Crown recovered in 1625, and conquered Pernambuco from 1630. Under Johan Maurits of Nassau (1637–44) the Dutch built a government at Recife with its own councils, and they held much of the northeast until 1654.

A rebellion in 1640 brought the Duke of Braganza to the throne as João IV, ending the union, and the colonial elites of Brazil accepted the Restoration. Resistance to the Dutch combined local planters, Indigenous troops under Camarão, Black soldiers under Dias, and Portuguese and Brazilian officers. An insurrection began in Pernambuco in 1645, battles at Guararapes in 1648–49 turned the war, and the Dutch surrendered in 1654. In 1648 a fleet led by Salvador Correia de Sá from Rio retook Luanda from the Dutch, an event showing the integration of the South Atlantic. A treaty of 1661 settled the dispute with the Dutch Republic, with an indemnity that Portugal had to raise.

The governmental consequences were large. The Crown relied more on colonial militia and on regiments of paid troops. It rewarded local leaders with honors and offices, gave new weight to the câmaras, and created institutions to manage the Atlantic empire: the Conselho Ultramarino in 1642 and, in 1649, a monopoly trading company with convoys, followed by restoration of the Relação in 1652. Brazil became central to the Portuguese monarchy at a time when its Asian possessions were being lost or reduced. Chapter 39 describes some of the campaigns. This section focuses on how the wars changed government.

Dutch Brazil functioned as a rival colonial government. The Dutch West India Company ruled through a governor and councils in Recife, reorganized taxation, and allowed Catholics and Jews to practice their religion more openly than Portuguese authorities did, which attracted some residents and alienated others. The Portuguese Crown's response to the 1645 insurrection was cautious at first, since it did not want to provoke a new war, though it sent aid and eventually recognized the rising as a national cause. Colonial militias, supported by Indigenous and Black regiments, became a model of how to defend the colony cheaply, and their officers demanded recognition. The wars thus left Brazil with experienced local commanders and a sense that colonists had saved the colony for the Crown.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: War, Imperial Rivalry, and Administrative Transformation

Long-term causes

Portuguese delegation of authority to private and local actors, thin royal presence, and competition among European powers for Atlantic sugar and slaves.

Short-term causes

The Iberian Union, the Dutch war with Spain, the founding of the Dutch West India Company, and the Dutch occupation of Bahia and Pernambuco.

Event or transformation

The Dutch wars and the Restoration of 1640 forced mobilization of colonial resources and a reorganization of imperial administration.

Immediate effects

Militia and paid regiments expanded, new taxes were levied, honors went to local and Indigenous and Black leaders, and the Conselho Ultramarino and a trading company were created.

Long-term consequences

A more militarized and self-confident colonial elite, greater royal reliance on Brazilian revenue, and a closer link between Brazil and Angola.

Historical complexity

Foreign invasion strengthened both the Crown, which reorganized, and local elites, which gained leverage. The wars did not produce a single outcome.
IV — Territorial Expansion and Atlantic Administration
Section 41.14

São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and the Government of the Interior

Government beyond the coast was weak and often delegated. São Paulo de Piratininga, founded around a Jesuit school in 1554 and recognized as a town in 1560, developed a distinctive local society whose men organized expeditions into the interior. Its council acted with considerable independence, as when it expelled the Jesuits in 1640. The Crown tolerated this autonomy because it needed the expeditions to explore, capture Indigenous laborers, and extend claims. Chapter 39 treats the expeditions themselves. For the history of government, the point is that frontier governance often meant contracts, titles, and rewards for private leaders, not direct administration.

Rio de Janeiro, a royal captaincy from 1565, grew into the main port of the south and a base for expansion toward the Río de la Plata. In 1680 Governor Manuel Lobo founded Colônia do Sacramento on the river opposite Buenos Aires, a claim that Spain challenged almost at once; Spanish forces seized it the same year, and a provisional agreement returned it in 1681. The episode shows the Crown using the governor of Rio as an instrument of territorial policy. In the northeastern interior, cattle ranchers extended settlement, and a long series of conflicts with Indigenous peoples is sometimes called the Guerra dos Bárbaros. Sertão society was supervised by local militia captains and large landholding families.

The Crown also resorted to private contractors for pacification. In the 1690s the governor of Pernambuco hired the paulista leader Domingos Jorge Velho to destroy the large community of Palmares, a case treated further in Chapter 47. The discovery of gold in the 1690s, whose administrative consequences belong to Chapter 48, would soon bring the Crown into the interior in force. By 1700 the interior was governed by a patchwork of militia captains, landholders, missionaries, and private commanders under the loose supervision of governors.

Founding a town was an act of government. The governor or Crown authorized the creation of a vila, which gave it a council, judges, and a pillory (pelourinho) as a symbol of municipal jurisdiction. Settlements on the frontier were often elevated to the status of vila to bring them under royal law, and Indigenous mission villages could later be converted into towns. Captains of the militia and of field patrols (capitães-mores of districts) enforced order, collected men for expeditions, and reported to governors. The Crown also sent letters of appointment and honors to expedition leaders. These arrangements gave the monarchy a formal claim to the interior long before it could administer it.

Section 41.15

The Estado do Maranhão and the Northern Amazonian Administration

In 1621 the Crown created the Estado do Maranhão, a government separate from the Estado do Brasil, with its capital at São Luís, and its first governor took office in 1626. Both states answered directly to the Crown. The main reason was geography. Winds and currents along the northern coast made the passage from São Luís to Lisbon easier than the passage between São Luís and Salvador, so the north was more closely connected to Portugal than to Bahia. The Crown also wanted to defend the northern coast and the Amazon against French, Dutch, English, and Irish intruders. The French had founded São Luís in 1612, the Portuguese expelled them in 1615, and in 1616 they founded Belém at the mouth of the Amazon.

The Estado was renamed Maranhão e Grão-Pará in 1654, and its government shared the structures of the south: governors, câmaras, a treasury, and religious orders. Its economy rested on forest products (drogas do sertão), cacao, tobacco, and some sugar, and it depended on Indigenous labor in a way that the older sugar zones did not. The Dutch occupied São Luís in 1641–44 before settlers expelled them. This was an area where Portuguese control was thin, settlers were few, and the question of Indigenous labor dominated politics. The Jesuit António Vieira preached against enslavement in the 1650s, settlers expelled the Jesuits in 1661, and the laws of 1680 and 1688 described in section 41.10 were issued specifically for this Estado.

The conflicts continued. In 1682 the Crown granted a monopoly company the right to supply the region with goods and African slaves, which angered settlers, and in 1684 the Beckman revolt in São Luís overthrew local authority. A new governor suppressed the rising in 1685, and its leader was executed. The northern Estado shows that Portuguese Brazil was never a single administrative unit, and it illustrates the importance of geography, Indigenous labor, and religious politics in shaping government.

Indigenous labor policy in the north was administered through special bodies. A council of missionaries and officials, the Junta das Missões, met in the 1680s to examine accusations of just war and decide on the legality of captives, and the Regimento das Missões of 1686 gave religious orders authority to govern mission villages and to allot workers among settlers, missionaries, and the Crown. Expeditions to the interior (tropas de resgate) set out from Belém and São Luís to bring back captives and forest products, and the governors themselves often took part in the trade. Indigenous leaders served as intermediaries and officers in the villages. The result was a labor regime in which royal law, missionary power, and settler demand were constantly in tension.

Section 41.16

The Conselho Ultramarino and the Portuguese Atlantic Empire

The Conselho Ultramarino, the Overseas Council, was established in 1642 under João IV, after the Restoration. It took over the supervision of most of Portugal's overseas territories, including Brazil, Angola, other African holdings, and the Atlantic islands, and also the Asian possessions in this early period. Its job was to advise the king on appointments, finance, defense, and legal questions, to examine petitions from colonial councils and individuals, and to correspond with governors. Its consultas, the written opinions it submitted to the king, and the letters and petitions it received are preserved in the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino in Lisbon and form one of the main sources for the history of colonial Brazil.

The Council worked within a larger metropolitan framework that also included the Desembargo do Paço, the Casa da Suplicação, the Mesa da Consciência e Ordens, which oversaw matters of the Order of Christ and the Church, and the royal treasury councils. As in Spain, no single institution had full control. The Council's records reveal the patterns of colonial petitioning: câmaras asked for tax relief, individuals sought offices and honors, governors requested troops and funds, and each claimant tried to reach the Council or the king directly.

Brazil's institutional links with Africa were close. Governors, troops, and ships moved among Salvador, Rio, Recife, and Luanda, and officials from Brazil took part in the reconquest of Angola in 1648 and held office there. The Atlantic islands and Cape Verde were also part of this network. The Atlantic slave trade that bound these regions together is the subject of Chapter 44, and here the interest is in the administrative ties: the shared officeholders, the circulation of governors, and the regular dispatch of fleets that carried orders, petitions, and revenue.

The Overseas Council's reach showed how personnel and resources circulated. Salvador Correia de Sá, who had governed Rio, governed Angola from 1648 to 1652 after retaking Luanda. João Fernandes Vieira, who had fought in Pernambuco, later held office in Angola. Fleets of merchant ships carried orders, petitions, and revenue between Brazil and Portugal, and in 1649 the Crown organized a monopoly trading company, the Companhia Geral do Comércio do Brasil, that supplied convoys with naval protection. Letters moved slowly, and the Council often waited for reports from governors, councils, and bishops before advising the king, so decisions could take years. This was a system of continual correspondence, in which the periphery wrote constantly to the center and the center responded selectively.

Historians owe much of their knowledge of colonial government to this paper trail. The Council's consultas and the incoming letters, petitions, and accounts preserved in the Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino, a large portion of which has been catalogued and digitized in recent decades, allow researchers to follow disputes from the local council to the royal decision. They must be read with care, since they show the version of events that each correspondent wished the Council to hear, and they overrepresent literate officials, clergy, and property owners. Indigenous and African voices appear indirectly, in testimony recorded by clerks, in petitions drafted by intermediaries, or in the complaints of officials about disorder.

Section 41.17

Portuguese Brazil by 1700: Royal Authority, Local Autonomy, and Imperial Limits

By 1700 the Portuguese system in Brazil was a set of layered authorities: hereditary and royal captaincies, a governor-general at Salvador, a separate Estado in the north, a high court, municipal councils, a treasury, a Church bound to the Crown by patronage, militias, and mission villages, all supervised from Lisbon by councils and the Overseas Council. This structure had developed in stages. The monarchy had delegated authority to donatários, added royal institutions, accepted the power of local councils and elites, and adjusted its administrative geography when needed. The system worked well enough to defend the colony against the Dutch and the French and to extract growing revenue from its trade.

A comparison with Chapter 40 clarifies what was distinctive. Spain created two large viceroyalties and more than a dozen audiencias; Portugal governed through a governor-general, one high court, and captaincies, and used the title of viceroy only intermittently for Brazil before the eighteenth century. It was granted to Jorge de Mascarenhas, Marquis of Montalvão, in 1640–41 and to Vasco de Mascarenhas, Count of Óbidos, in 1663–67, and again in 1714–18, and it became the regular title of the governor of the Estado do Brasil from 1720. The adoption of the title did not by itself create a unified viceroyalty covering all Portuguese territories in America. Spain organized Indigenous communities into legal republics with their own councils, built on dense tribute-paying populations in Mexico and the Andes; in Brazil, Indigenous societies were more dispersed, and the main instruments were mission villages, wars, alliances, and slavery. Spain founded universities in Mexico and Lima in the sixteenth century, while Brazil had none, and young Brazilians studied at Coimbra. Yet the two systems also shared features: overlapping jurisdictions, the sale or reward of office, local elites in municipal councils, and a Church bound to the Crown.

Neither monarchy controlled everything. The Portuguese Crown could not prevent settlers from enslaving Indigenous people, could not fully supervise its judges, depended on local contributions for defense, and could not govern the interior. Yet it could reassert authority after revolts, grant or withhold honors, and move officials. The next chapters show how this system interacted with the economy: Chapter 42 turns to Spanish silver and Chapter 43 to estates, sugar, trade, and cities. The reforms of the eighteenth century, including Pombal's abolition of the remaining donatary captaincies and the expulsion of the Jesuits, are reserved for Chapter 48.

Neither monarchy restrained slavery of Africans, and both legal systems recognized property in enslaved people. The Church sometimes protected Indigenous people more than it protected Africans, and Portuguese and Spanish jurists justified enslavement of Africans on grounds that were applied inconsistently to others. Both monarchies promoted missions, both profited from taxes on slaving and slave-grown goods, and both depended on local elites who owned enslaved workers. In this sense the Portuguese and Spanish systems were more alike than their institutions suggest, and the differences lie in the form of government and in the position of Indigenous peoples. Students should therefore compare institutions carefully while remembering the shared foundation of coerced labor, a theme developed in Chapters 42 through 47.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From the Captaincy System to the Portuguese Colonial State

What Changed

  • Government moved from private hereditary lords to a royal governor-general, courts, and treasuries
  • A single Estado became two (Brasil and Maranhão) in 1621
  • Royal institutions in Lisbon multiplied, culminating in the Conselho Ultramarino in 1642
  • Local councils and militias gained weight through the Dutch wars

What Stayed the Same

  • Layered, overlapping authority remained the basic structure
  • The Crown continued to rely on local elites, contractors, and religious orders
  • Indigenous labor and enslavement remained contested and weakly regulated
  • Lisbon-based law and patronage continued to frame all government

Who experienced change most directly: Donatários lost relative power as royal institutions grew, and Indigenous communities experienced resettlement, war, and shifting labor laws. Greater continuity: The dependence of the Crown on local cooperation persisted. Why the pattern matters: It shows a state built by accretion, which shaped the inheritances of the eighteenth-century reforms and, later, of independent Brazil.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Portuguese government in Brazil developed in layers. A monarchy centered on Atlantic and Asian trade claimed a coast it could not easily occupy and, in the 1530s, delegated settlement and jurisdiction to hereditary donatários under cartas de doação and forais. Some captaincies, notably Pernambuco and São Vicente, took root, and others failed.

In 1548–49 the Crown created the governor-general and sent Tomé de Sousa to found Salvador on the royal captaincy of Bahia, with a Regimento that defined defense, justice, revenue, settlement, and relations with Indigenous peoples. This did not abolish the captaincies. Royal and donatary captaincies coexisted for about two centuries, and the Crown acquired private grants gradually.

Below the governor-general, officers with distinct functions, including capitães-mores, ouvidores, and provedores, handled military, civil, judicial, and fiscal tasks. Militia companies and paid regiments organized defense. Offices were often rewards for service and were exercised amid conflicts over jurisdiction.

Municipal câmaras governed towns through vereadores, ordinary judges, and market inspectors chosen from homens bons. They regulated daily life, raised local assessments, petitioned the Crown, and sometimes resisted royal officials, as in Rio in 1660–61.

The Relação da Bahia, regulated in 1609, suppressed in 1626, and restored in 1652, was the single high court of the Estado do Brasil. It symbolized royal sovereignty but became embedded in local society, and it can be compared with Spanish audiencias without being identical to them.

The Real Fazenda collected customs, tithes, contracts, and later other revenues, mostly spent on defense. Sesmarias, derived from a Portuguese law of 1375, tied land to cultivation and royal confirmation, while ignoring Indigenous land use despite occasional legal recognition such as the decree of 1680.

Indigenous peoples were allies, enemies, mission residents, and laborers. Laws of 1570, 1609, 1611, 1655, 1680, and 1688 swung between regulation and prohibition of enslavement, and legislation cannot be read as a record of treatment. Mission villages, wars, and ransom provided labor, and Indigenous leaders such as Arariboia and Camarão gained honors while their communities bore the costs.

The padroado real tied the Church to the Crown, and the Jesuits were influential and often controversial actors. Settler elites negotiated with the Crown through petitions, litigation, and service, as did some Indigenous and Black leaders, but the system rested on coercion and limited access for most people. Historians debate whether to call it centralized or negotiated.

The Iberian Union, Dutch invasions, and the Restoration forced mobilization, enlarged colonial militias, and led to new institutions, including the Conselho Ultramarino in 1642. Separate governments emerged where geography demanded, notably the Estado do Maranhão from 1621. The interior was governed through private commanders, local captains, and missionaries.

By 1700 Portuguese Brazil was a layered system that had defended itself, raised revenue, and remained dependent on local elites. Compared with Spanish America, it had fewer high courts, no formal Indigenous republics, and a stronger reliance on mission villages and slavery, though both monarchies governed through overlapping authorities and negotiation. The economic institutions of Chapters 42 and 43 and the reforms of Chapter 48 build on this foundation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Portugal governed Brazil by accumulation. Each institution, from the captaincy to the Overseas Council, answered a particular problem, and none replaced what came before. The result was a system that could defend a long coast, tax a growing trade, and survive foreign invasion, but that depended throughout on local councils, landholders, soldiers, missionaries, and Indigenous and African allies and subjects.

The comparison with Spanish America is the chapter's second lesson. Both monarchies claimed extensive authority and relied on overlapping institutions, local elites, and negotiation. They differed in the number of courts, in the legal treatment of Indigenous communities, in the role of missions and slavery, and in how they organized the interior. These differences were not signs that one system was more advanced than the other. They reflected different geographies, populations, and imperial priorities.

Many features of this system outlasted the colonial period: strong regional elites, municipal councils with real political weight, patronage as a means of government, and the tension between a distant center and local power. Chapter 42 turns to the silver empires of Spanish America, and Chapter 43 returns to Brazil's estates, sugar, trade, and cities, where the institutions described here met the economy that sustained them. The eighteenth-century reforms of Chapter 48 would try to reshape the system, but they built on the layers explained here.
