The Fall of Tenochtitlan, 1519–1521

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A City That Won, and Then Was Besieged

In the summer of 1520 the people of Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco drove the Spanish-Indigenous coalition out of their city. In the summer of 1521 a larger, rebuilt, and overwhelmingly Indigenous coalition returned, and this time it did not leave until the city was destroyed. The fall of Tenochtitlan is the story of that reversal.

Chapter 31 ended with the expedition lodged in the Axayácatl palace and Moctezuma II held under guard. That arrangement was a standoff, and it lasted barely six months. This chapter explains how it collapsed, how the Spaniards and their allies were expelled in a night of catastrophic losses, and how, over the following year, the survivors rebuilt a far stronger force. The chapter follows the conflict from the winter of 1519–1520 to the surrender of 13 August 1521, and its purpose is to examine the Spanish-Mexica conflict, the retreat traditionally called the Noche Triste, the smallpox epidemic, siege warfare, Indigenous armies, naval operations, destruction, and surrender.

The conventional version of this story gives the main roles to Hernán Cortés and a few hundred Spaniards whose steel, horses, and guns overcame an empire. That version cannot explain the evidence. The Mexica won the decisive fighting of 1520. The coalition survived the defeat only because Tlaxcalan leaders decided, after the disaster, to keep the alliance alive. The coalition returned only after months of campaigns that peeled communities away from the Triple Alliance, after Spanish reinforcements arrived from the Caribbean and from the defeated rival expedition of Pánfilo de Narváez, after an epidemic killed many inhabitants of the capital and severely disrupted its households, government, and military capacity, and after Indigenous workers built a lake fleet. The siege that followed was a land-and-water war that destroyed the urban and logistical systems sustaining the island city. By most estimates the force that besieged it was overwhelmingly Indigenous in manpower, and the human cost fell overwhelmingly on Indigenous people on both sides.

A second lesson concerns who counted as an actor. The Spaniards were not a bloc: Narváez came to arrest Cortés, and the two Spanish forces fought each other before they merged. The Indigenous world was not a bloc either. Texcoco was divided by a succession struggle, Mexica rulers died in quick succession, and communities around the lake chose sides according to their own tribute grievances, rivalries, and calculations of survival. The Mexica too practiced diplomacy and tried to hold or rebuild alliances while the coalition worked to detach their subjects. The fall of the city was thus a political war as well as a military one.

Evidence again demands care. Cortés's Second Letter (1520) and Third Letter (1522) are advocacy addressed to a king and written by a commander who needed to turn a defeat into a justified campaign. Bernal Díaz del Castillo wrote decades later, partly to correct the credit given to Cortés by Francisco López de Gómara. Nahua accounts, especially Book 12 of the Florentine Codex and the Annals of Tlatelolco, were composed under colonial conditions, with the memory of catastrophe, and with their own local loyalties, including tension between Tenochca and Tlatelolca narrators. The Lienzo de Tlaxcala and the history of Diego Muñoz Camargo emphasize Tlaxcalan service. Texcocan descendants such as Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxóchitl wrote to magnify Texcoco's role. Each author had reasons to shape the story, and every number in these sources, from troop strengths to the dead, must be treated as an estimate.

This chapter also observes strict boundaries. Chapter 29 explained smallpox as a demographic and biological event, so this chapter treats the 1520 epidemic only in its political and military effects. Chapter 33 will examine how military victory was converted, slowly and unevenly, into colonial government, encomienda, evangelization, Indigenous elite accommodation, and the reconstruction of the city as Mexico City. Here the narrative stops at 13 August 1521 and at the meaning of that date. The surrender of the city ended organized Mexica resistance in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco. It was not the instantaneous conquest of Mexico.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the occupation of Tenochtitlan with a captive Moctezuma could not remain stable in the winter and spring of 1520.
  2. Analyze the expedition of Pánfilo de Narváez as a Spanish conflict within the conquest and assess its effect on Spanish manpower and on Indigenous perceptions.
  3. Evaluate competing accounts of the Tóxcatl massacre without treating Pedro de Alvarado's motives as established.
  4. Interpret the evidence for Moctezuma's death and explain why the political consequence is clearer than the cause.
  5. Describe the retreat from Tenochtitlan and the Battle of Otumba without attributing either outcome to miracle or technology alone.
  6. Assess why Tlaxcalan leaders preserved the alliance after the retreat and how the Tepeaca campaigns prepared the return to the Basin.
  7. Explain the political and military effects of the 1520 smallpox epidemic while rejecting monocausal accounts.
  8. Trace how Texcoco and the surrounding communities became the base of the coalition and why Texcocan politics were divided.
  9. Analyze the brigantine fleet as a combined Indigenous and Spanish enterprise and explain why control of the lake mattered.
  10. Describe the causeway-and-canal siege and the human reality of famine, thirst, and urban destruction.
  11. Distinguish the defeat of organized resistance on 13 August 1521 from the broader conquest of Mexico.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part VII's claim that the conquest was a multinational conflict shaped by Indigenous diplomacy and rivalry.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the arrangement of governing through a captive tlatoani fail, and what does its failure reveal about Mexica political structure?
  2. How did the arrival of a second Spanish force change the balance of the conflict, and how did Indigenous actors read the division among the Spaniards?
  3. Why do the sources disagree so sharply about Tóxcatl and about the death of Moctezuma, and what can still be established?
  4. If the coalition was defeated in 1520, why did it survive, and why did Tlaxcala not abandon it?
  5. How should historians weigh the 1520 epidemic against military, political, and logistical causes of the city's fall?
  6. How did the political geography around Lake Texcoco change between July 1520 and May 1521, and who drove that change?
  7. What did the lake fleet contribute, and why is "ships against canoes" a misleading description?
  8. What did siege warfare mean for the civilians of Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco?
  9. In what sense was 13 August 1521 an end, and in what sense was it only a turning point?

Historical Context

Chapters 13 through 15 described the Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan as a tribute-collecting alliance dominated by the Mexica of Tenochtitlan. It was not a centralized state. Subject communities commonly retained their rulers and local institutions, but their relationships to the Triple Alliance varied. Tribute provinces regularly supplied goods, while subject states and strategic provinces could have different political, military, or defensive obligations. These relationships were maintained through warfare, asymmetric alliances, marriage, diplomacy, and the threat of force. That structure made the empire formidable and also brittle. Communities that resented tribute could change sides if the price of rebellion fell, a fact that Chapters 30 and 31 showed in the Totonac and Tlaxcalan decisions.

Tenochtitlan itself was an island city connected to the mainland by causeways, with the twin community of Tlatelolco on the northern part of the island. The causeways carried people, goods, and, in the case of the Chapultepec line, fresh water by aqueduct. The lake system around the island was a managed landscape of canals, chinampas, and fisheries, and the lake served as the city's highway and its food source. The same geography that made the city defensible also made it dependent on controlled access to water, food, and canoe traffic. The Mexica understood that dependence and had designed defenses around bridges and removable sections of causeway.

Chapter 31 established the condition in which this chapter begins. Cortés's force, strengthened by Totonac and Tlaxcalan allies, had entered the capital on 8 November 1519 and seized Moctezuma around 14 November. Chapter 29 treated the epidemic disease that was spreading across the Americas. Chapter 30 described the Spanish authorization problem: Cortés had broken with the governor of Cuba, Diego Velázquez, and founded Villa Rica in part to place himself under the Crown directly. Velázquez therefore regarded Cortés as a rebel, and that conflict, not the Mexica, supplies the first crisis of this chapter.

Students should also guard against hindsight. Neither the Mexica, the Tlaxcalans, the Texcocans, nor the Spaniards knew in 1520 that the city would fall. The Mexica had good reason to believe that they had defeated an invasion. The Tlaxcalans had reason to fear that they had backed a loser. Spanish survivors believed they might be destroyed. This chapter treats every turning point as a moment of open decision.

Section 32.1

From Captivity to Crisis: Tenochtitlan, Late 1519–Spring 1520

The situation Chapter 31 left behind contained a built-in contradiction. A few hundred Spaniards and a few thousand allies were lodged in a city of very large population, and they were trying to govern or influence an empire through a ruler they held hostage. Spanish accounts say Moctezuma continued to receive visitors, give orders, and perform parts of rulership, and the Spaniards used that authority to gather tribute, learn about gold sources, and send out scouting parties. Yet a tlatoani under guard was a compromised ruler. The institution of the tlatoani operated within a council of nobles, military officers, and priests, and those groups could and did consider their own options.

Spanish accounts record the next steps. Quauhpopoca, the lord blamed for the attack near Villa Rica, was brought to the city and burned, and Spanish sources say Cortés had Moctezuma put in shackles during the execution. The Spaniards also collected gold and other valuables, and Díaz and Cortés both describe a large treasure that was melted into bars and divided, with the king's fifth set aside. Cortés reports that Moctezuma and other lords swore vassalage to the Spanish king. Historians treat this report cautiously, for the same reason they treated the speech on the causeway: it served Cortés's legal case, and the Mexica ruler's coerced words may have meant something quite different to him and his court.

Religious conflict deepened the strain. Spanish accounts describe the installation of a cross and an image of the Virgin in a shrine within the sacred precinct, attempts to stop human sacrifice, and Moctezuma's reported warnings that his priests and people would not tolerate the removal of the gods. Whatever the precise details, the Spaniards were pressing on the center of Mexica religious life from a position of vulnerability. They also depended on a food supply controlled by hosts who might withdraw it, and on causeways that could be cut. Cortés, by his own account, ordered the construction of boats on the lake as a precaution, a detail that anticipates the later naval war.

Under these pressures, the Mexica court was debating. Cuitláhuac, who had hosted the expedition at Iztapalapa, is described in several sources as hostile to accommodation. Cacama of Texcoco, who had favored receiving the strangers, was reportedly drawn into a plot, arrested by the Spaniards, and replaced on the Texcocan throne by another member of the ruling family, a move that would complicate Texcocan politics for the rest of the war. Whether Moctezuma sincerely cooperated or tried to stall is not recoverable. What is clear is that the arrangement rested on the hope that the Mexica would not act, and that this hope depended on a ruler whose authority was draining away. In the spring of 1520 a message from the coast changed the situation.

Section 32.2

Pánfilo de Narváez and a Spanish War within the Conquest

Diego Velázquez, governor of Cuba, regarded Cortés as a subordinate who had exceeded his commission and kept the proceeds of his expedition. In 1520 he sent Pánfilo de Narváez with a much larger expedition to arrest or supersede Cortés. Estimates of its strength vary substantially, from roughly eight or nine hundred Spaniards in some reconstructions to more than a thousand in others, along with Indigenous servants, auxiliaries, sailors, horses, artillery, and numerous ships. Narváez landed on the Gulf coast in April 1520 and established himself at Cempoala. His arrival introduced into Mesoamerica a second armed Spanish group that was hostile to the first.

Indigenous leaders observed this. Spanish narratives report that Indigenous leaders closely observed the newcomers, that Mexica envoys made contact with Narváez, and that Moctezuma sent messages or gifts. Cortés had reason to fear that Narváez might ally with the Mexica against him, and Narváez had reason to treat the Cempoalans, who had backed Cortés, as enemies. For Indigenous politicians the dispute among Spaniards was information: it showed that the strangers had rivals and a ruler across the sea who might not endorse Cortés. It also offered the Mexica a possible way to play one Spanish group against the other.

Cortés made a decision that shaped the chapter. He left Pedro de Alvarado in command in Tenochtitlan with a garrison of roughly a hundred Spaniards, together with the allies who remained, and marched toward the coast with a small force, reinforced along the way by additional Spaniards from other garrisons, and he sought to negotiate with Narváez and also to win away his men with gold. In the night of 27–28 May 1520, in rain and darkness, Cortés's force attacked Narváez's camp at Cempoala, captured him after a wound that cost him an eye, and took the camp. Most of Narváez's men agreed to join Cortés, a result that Spanish accounts attribute to promises of treasure and to the fact that the city, with its supposed wealth, was waiting.

The victory solved Cortés's problem with Velázquez in the short term and greatly strengthened him: he now commanded more Spaniards, more horses, and more weapons than before. It also had consequences that the participants did not foresee. The men from Cuba were new arrivals who knew nothing of the country, had not shared the hardships of the earlier campaign, and expected riches. And the enlarged Spanish presence in Mesoamerica coincided with the arrival of a disease that would transform the war, a subject taken up in Section 32.7. Before Cortés could return, events in Tenochtitlan escaped control.

Section 32.3

The Tóxcatl Massacre and the Mexica Uprising

Tóxcatl was a major festival of the Mexica ritual calendar, celebrated in the spring in honor of Tezcatlipoca and associated with Huitzilopochtli's cult in the sacred precinct. In May 1520, during the festival, with Cortés absent, Alvarado's men attacked the assembled participants. Nahua accounts, including Book 12 of the Florentine Codex, describe dancers and singers, many of them nobles and warriors, gathered in the courtyard of the temple precinct, who were set upon by Spaniards who closed the entrances and killed large numbers. The Spanish accounts differ. Alvarado, and Cortés following him, said that the Spaniards had learned of a plot to attack them and acted to forestall it. Díaz and others relay competing explanations, and some Spanish writers suggest the Spaniards were motivated by the gold and ornaments worn by the dancers.

Historians do not agree about Alvarado's motives, and this chapter does not decide them. A preemptive attack based on rumor, a panic among an isolated garrison, an act of calculated terror, and a desire for plunder are all possibilities, and they are not mutually exclusive. The sources are also uneven in their authors' positions. Alvarado had every reason to justify himself, and Nahua survivors were recalling a catastrophe years later. The date is likely around 20 May, though the sources do not agree to the day.

The consequence is clearer than the cause. The attack killed a large number of Mexica nobles and warriors in a sacred setting, and it turned an uneasy truce into open war. The city rose. Nahua accounts describe the fighting as beginning immediately and as drawing on the whole population, with warriors attacking the palace in which the Spaniards were lodged and cutting off food and water. The Spaniards and their allies, besieged in the Axayácatl palace, held out with Moctezuma still in their custody. Alvarado sent word to Cortés, and the Mexica, while pressing the attack, also negotiated at points: Spanish accounts say that Moctezuma was brought out to urge calm and that fighting briefly lessened.

Tóxcatl deserves attention as a turning point, not simply an episode before the retreat. It destroyed whatever remained of the diplomatic equilibrium of November 1519. It also shifted the Mexica leadership. Nobles and priests killed in the courtyard were replaced by war leaders, and the voices urging accommodation lost authority. From this point forward the Mexica were fighting a war, and Moctezuma's usefulness to the Spaniards as a means of control declined with each day of fighting.

Section 32.4

Cortés Returns: Uprising, Cuitláhuac, and the Death of Moctezuma

Cortés returned to the Basin in late June 1520 with his enlarged force, including the men of Narváez and a number of Tlaxcalan and other allied warriors, and he entered Tenochtitlan on about 24 June. By Spanish accounts the city's markets were closed, the streets were quiet, and the Spaniards in the palace were exhausted. The reinforcement did not restore control. The Mexica, who had allowed the larger column to enter, resumed the attack with renewed force, and the combined garrison found itself besieged by a population that had decided to fight.

Cuitláhuac emerged as a principal organizer. The sources say he had been among those seized or restrained in the earlier months, and Spanish accounts report that Cortés released him to restore the supply of food to the garrison, an act that proved a decisive mistake. Whatever the precise path, Cuitláhuac became an effective war leader and was soon the leading figure among the Mexica. Fighting centered on the palace, on the temple precinct, and on the streets and canals around them. Spanish attempts to sortie met with heavy resistance, and the Mexica removed bridges and gaps in the causeways and filled the approaches with obstacles. Cortés, by his own account, tried again to use Moctezuma to calm the crowd.

What happened to Moctezuma is an evidence problem treated in the Historical Debate that follows this section. Spanish accounts say he was hit by stones thrown by his own people when he appeared on a rooftop to urge them to stop, and that he died a few days later, refusing treatment. Nahua accounts, including some in Book 12 of the Florentine Codex and the Annals of Tlatelolco, say the Spaniards killed him and the nobles held with him. Historians have not settled the question, and the surviving evidence may not allow it.

The political result does not depend on how he died. Whether he was killed by the Mexica, by the Spaniards, or by wounds of uncertain origin, his death ended the strategy of governing through a captive tlatoani. The Spaniards lost their principal source of leverage, and the Mexica were free to choose a successor, which they did, installing Cuitláhuac. Cortés and his men now had no reason to remain except to retrieve their treasure, and every reason to leave. They decided to retreat.

Historical Vocabulary
Noche Triste

The Noche Triste ("sad night" or "night of sorrows") is the traditional Spanish name for the night retreat from Tenochtitlan, conventionally dated 30 June–1 July 1520. The name enshrines the Spanish perspective, reportedly arising from Cortés's grief at the losses of his men.

This chapter calls the event the retreat from Tenochtitlan, traditionally called the Noche Triste, because the Spanish label should not decide the viewpoint. For the Mexica and Tlatelolca it was a victory in which an invading force was expelled. For the Spaniards' Indigenous allies it was a catastrophe in which they bore very heavy losses, and the name leaves them out.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Did Moctezuma II Die?

The question: Was Moctezuma fatally injured by his own people, as the leading Spanish accounts say, or killed by the Spaniards, as several Indigenous traditions maintain?

Interpretation A — Stoned by his own people: Cortés and Díaz say that Moctezuma went up on a roof to speak to the crowd and was struck by stones thrown by Mexica warriors, who no longer regarded him as their ruler. On this view the event reflects a collapse of his authority, and a ruler in Spanish custody who urged his people to stop fighting might plausibly have been attacked.
Interpretation B — Killed by the Spaniards: Several Nahua accounts, including a passage in the Florentine Codex tradition and the Annals of Tlatelolco, say the Spaniards killed Moctezuma and other noble captives before leaving the palace. On this view the Spanish story was convenient, because it removed responsibility from the party that held him and had begun to lose the war, and Indigenous memory preserved the killing.

Evidence: Both interpretations depend on narratives written after the event by people with strong motives. No neutral eyewitness survives. Cortés wrote to defend himself to the Crown; the Nahua authors wrote under colonial rule and may have shaped the memory of a ruler who had cooperated with the invaders. Some scholars find a Spanish role plausible because Moctezuma had become a liability, while others treat the stoning account as credible because it fits the breakdown of his authority.

Why it matters: The question affects the moral picture of the occupation and the status of the Mexica ruler in historical memory, whether as victim of his own people or of the invaders. It also illustrates how conquest sources contend with one another. Yet the strategic consequence is the same under either interpretation, and the cause of death remains disputed.

Student task: Write a paragraph for each interpretation, then identify what kind of additional evidence, if any, could change the balance.

Section 32.5

The Retreat from Tenochtitlan: The “Noche Triste” and Otumba

The retreat was planned for the night of 30 June–1 July 1520. The Spaniards chose the western route along the Tlacopan causeway, the shortest crossing to the mainland, and they prepared a portable bridge to cross the gaps where the Mexica had removed sections. They also loaded a large quantity of gold. Cortés reports distributing the treasure, and Díaz says that many Narváez men, who were eager for riches, overloaded themselves while experienced soldiers took less. Spanish accounts say a woman drawing water raised the alarm, and Nahua accounts describe the Mexica discovering the withdrawal and sounding the alert from the temple.

The causeway made the column vulnerable. Narrow and flanked by water, it forced the retreat into a long line, and Mexica canoes struck from both sides while warriors pressed from the city and the shore. The portable bridge was lost or wedged at the first gap, so that later crossings were over the bodies of the fallen and through the water. Horses and artillery were abandoned, and Spanish narratives say that some men drowned while carrying heavy loads of gold. Spanish accounts give losses in the hundreds, and some give far more than that, with figures for Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous allies in the thousands. These figures are estimates by participants, but the order of magnitude is not in doubt: the occupation was destroyed in a single night, and the surviving coalition emerged as a badly shattered but still functioning military force.

Indigenous allies bore a large share of the disaster, though the sources record their losses unevenly because Spanish authors cared more about Spanish dead. Malintzin, according to Spanish accounts, survived the retreat, and so did Aguilar. Noble hostages held by the Spaniards, including children of Moctezuma, were reportedly killed in the confusion, and the details are debated. The survivors gathered at Tlacopan and then moved on, and the rest of the column made its way around the lake, harassed by the Mexica and their allies, in the direction of Tlaxcala.

On about 7 July, at the plain near Otumba, the survivors met a large force of Mexica and allied warriors who had assembled to cut off their escape. Spanish accounts treat the battle as a near-miraculous victory. Cortés is said to have killed or struck down a commander carrying a major standard, whose fall disheartened the attackers. That account may contain truth, but it is the account of the victors' own storytellers. Historians point to other factors: the open ground favored the remaining horsemen, the Mexica forces were exhausted and not fully coordinated, and Tlaxcalan and other allied warriors fought on the Spanish side. The Mexica also did not press the pursuit into Tlaxcalan territory. The survivors reached Tlaxcalan lands a few days later, wounded and starving, and uncertain of their reception.

Primary Source Window

Nahua and Spanish Accounts of the Tóxcatl Massacre and the Retreat

Creators: Hernán Cortés (Second Letter); Bernal Díaz del Castillo; Nahua authors and painters of the Florentine Codex, Book 12 · Dates: Cortés wrote in 1520; Díaz wrote decades later; the Florentine Codex was compiled in the 1550s–1570s · Type: A legal-political report, a soldier's memoir, and an Indigenous-authored colonial-era history

Context: Cortés wrote to Charles V to justify his actions during and after a defeat. Díaz wrote to correct what he regarded as unfair credit given to Cortés and to honor common soldiers. The Florentine Codex was compiled by Nahua scholars working with the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún, decades after the fall of the city, under Spanish supervision.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: The Spanish accounts say Alvarado acted to forestall a plot and describe the retreat as a hard-fought escape in which a few Spaniards survived against enormous odds. The Nahua account describes a festival gathering that was surrounded and cut down in the sacred precinct, a population rising in anger, and, on the night of the retreat, an alarm and a pursuit along the causeway in which the invaders and their allies were killed in the water.

Analysis: Both traditions were composed after the events and in service of later purposes. The Spanish accounts try to show that violence was justified and that survival was providential. The Nahua account preserves the experience of those attacked, and it reflects retrospective understanding of the catastrophe that followed. Neither gives a neutral record. The Nahua tradition also contains internal variation, since Tlatelolca and Tenochca recollections differ in emphasis. Historians can establish that a massacre took place in the precinct, that it precipitated an uprising, and that the retreat was a disaster for the coalition. They cannot recover Alvarado's real motives or the precise numbers of the dead.

  2. Who created each source, when, and for what likely purposes?

Section 32.6

Defeat Did Not End the Alliance: Tlaxcala and the Campaign of Tepeaca

The survivors reached Tlaxcala in July 1520 in desperate condition, and the Tlaxcalan leaders faced a choice. They could have killed or expelled the Spaniards, handed them to the Mexica to buy peace, or simply withdrawn their support. The expedition had lost most of its artillery, many horses, a large part of its force, and the treasure that had motivated many of its members. A political calculation based only on the immediate balance of power would have counted the Spaniards as finished.

Tlaxcalan leaders chose differently, and the sources suggest that the decision was debated. Spanish accounts say Xicotencatl the Younger argued for abandoning the Spaniards, and that Maxixcatzin and Xicotencatl the Elder insisted on honoring the alliance. This is the same pattern of a "war faction" and a "peace faction" that Chapter 31 warned against taking at face value, and here too the evidence is partisan. What can be said is that Tlaxcalan rulers reasoned from their own interests. A Mexica victory over the Spaniards would not make peace with Tlaxcala, which had resisted Triple Alliance expansion for decades. Tlaxcalan leaders had reason to fear that a restored and victorious Mexica government might next renew pressure on Tlaxcala. The Spaniards, though badly damaged, remained a source of weapons, a connection to coastal ports and reinforcements, and a force that had demonstrated it could fight. And the alliance had already generated obligations, including kinship ties through the women given to Spanish captains.

The Tlaxcalans therefore sheltered and fed the expedition for weeks while the wounded recovered. Cortés, for his part, used the time to reorganize and to resolve an internal mutiny: some of the Narváez men wanted to return to the coast and Cuba, and he persuaded or pressured them to stay. The coalition after the retreat was smaller than before, and its leadership had to rebuild trust. Mexica envoys, according to Spanish accounts, also approached Tlaxcala urging it to break with the Spaniards, offering peace and an end to the blockade. The Tlaxcalan decision to refuse was a political choice made with full knowledge of the alternative.

The coalition's strategy in the following months aimed at cutting away the surrounding supports before attacking the city. In the Puebla corridor, around Tepeaca and neighboring towns, communities that were tribute-paying subjects of the Triple Alliance had killed Spaniards or blocked routes, and the Mexica had garrisons and allies there. Between August and October 1520 the coalition, including Tlaxcalan and other allied warriors, campaigned against these places. Spanish accounts describe the capture and branding of enslaved captives, which the sources treat as lawful war, and the founding of a Spanish settlement at Tepeaca, called Segura de la Frontera. The campaigns secured the road between Tlaxcala and the coast, brought new communities into the alliance, and detached the region from the Triple Alliance. They also showed how Spanish and Indigenous aims fit together unevenly: Tlaxcalans and others took plunder, captives, and revenge on old enemies, while the Spaniards pursued route security and manpower.

This section is essential because otherwise the book would leap from the retreat to the siege. The year between them was filled with campaigns that rebuilt the coalition on a larger basis. Spanish reinforcements also began to arrive: ships from Cuba and Jamaica, drawn by news of the conquest and unaware of the retreat, landed at Veracruz, and their men and horses were redirected to Cortés. By the end of 1520 the coalition was in a position to return to the Basin, and the capital was struggling with an epidemic.

Section 32.7

Smallpox, Cuitláhuac, and a Second Political Transition

Chapter 29 explained smallpox as a biological and demographic event, and this section does not repeat it. The focus here is the 1520 epidemic in central Mexico and its political and military effects. Smallpox reached the Gulf coast in 1520, and by the second half of the year it had spread into the Basin. Nahua accounts in Book 12 of the Florentine Codex describe the disease in terms of painful sores, blindness, inability to move, and the collapse of households in which too many people were sick to bring water or prepare food. They say it lasted roughly two months in Tenochtitlan, and that very many people died.

The traditional account attributes the introduction of smallpox to an enslaved African with Narváez's force. That attribution rests on later chronicles and is less secure than the broader conclusion that the epidemic was associated with the enlarged Spanish presence that followed Narváez's arrival. This chapter does not name a single carrier as established fact.

The epidemic had political effects, not only demographic ones. It struck the island city in the months when the Mexica needed to organize defense and to maintain alliances. It killed warriors, officials, and ordinary workers, and it disrupted markets, ritual life, and the labor that fed the city. It also killed Cuitláhuac, the leader of the resistance, who died after a reign of about eighty days according to later tradition. His death left the Mexica without their most effective war leader at the moment they needed one. They chose Cuauhtémoc, a younger noble, as tlatoani, and he led the final defense. The succession shows that the Mexica political system could still act, installing a new ruler in wartime, but it also reveals how many leaders had died or been killed within a year.

Historians avoid the old claim that smallpox conquered the Aztecs. The epidemic severely weakened a society already at war, and it struck Indigenous communities generally, including those in the coalition, whose population also fell. Spanish accounts note that the disease spared many Spaniards, who had prior exposure, and Indigenous allies were not spared. Yet the final defeat of the city required another military campaign, enormous Indigenous armies, control of the Basin, and a siege. The epidemic changed the conditions of that siege without deciding its outcome.

Section 32.8

Rebuilding the Coalition: Texcoco and the War for the Basin

The coalition left Tlaxcala in late December 1520 and entered the Basin of Mexico by the eastern side, reaching Texcoco around the end of the year. Cortés reports that his force consisted of several hundred Spaniards, including the reinforcements from the Caribbean, and a very large Indigenous army drawn from Tlaxcala and neighboring communities. The numbers in the sources are estimates and probably exaggerated, but the proportion of Indigenous to Spanish fighters was very large. Texcoco became the coalition's main operational base at the beginning of 1521.

Texcoco was not a single polity that simply switched sides. It was the second city of the Triple Alliance and the capital of the Acolhua, and its ruling house was divided. Cacama had been displaced in 1520, and after his removal the succession became contested among members of the family. Ixtlilxóchitl, a Texcocan prince with a claim and a grievance against the Mexica-aligned faction, sided with the coalition, and Cortés recognized a Texcocan claimant favorable to himself. Other Acolhua nobles and communities remained loyal to the Mexica or wavered. Later stages of the siege still involved Acolhua forces fighting for other rulers. Texcocan accounts, such as those preserved by Ixtlilxóchitl's descendant, emphasize Texcoco's role in the victory, and they must be read as advocacy by a community seeking recognition in the colonial order.

The months from January to April 1521 were taken up with a war to reshape the political geography around the lake. The coalition attacked and negotiated with communities on the eastern, southern, and western shores. Chalco, Tlalmanalco, and other towns in the southern Basin joined or were drawn in, partly through Mexica pressure that had alienated them and partly through their own grievances against Tenochtitlan. Huexotzinco and Cholula, whose people had suffered or taken part in the earlier violence, contributed warriors. In the south the lake towns of Xochimilco and its neighbors resisted, and the coalition, after hard fighting, took or neutralized them. Spanish accounts describe each campaign as a victory, but they also record setbacks, including a hard-fought engagement at Xochimilco in which Cortés was nearly captured and rescued by allied warriors.

The Mexica were conducting diplomacy too, and the sources show it. They sent envoys to towns around the lake, offered tribute relief or alliance, and tried to preserve relations with communities that were wavering. Cuauhtémoc's government reportedly reduced or canceled tribute obligations in an effort to retain support. They also fortified the island and prepared the canoe fleet. In some cases they punished or attacked towns that defected. The result was a political war in which both sides competed for the allegiance of lakeshore communities, and in which the coalition's success depended on the fact that many of these communities had little reason to defend Tenochtitlan's empire and some reason to fear its retaliation.

Historical Vocabulary
Brigantine

A brigantine was a small, shallow-draft vessel with sails and oars, suited to coastal and river work. In the conquest of Mexico the term refers to the thirteen vessels built at Texcoco in 1521 for use on Lake Texcoco, each armed with artillery or carrying crossbowmen and soldiers.

The word can mislead students into picturing an ocean fleet. These were small lake boats, built with Spanish shipbuilding knowledge but with Indigenous labor, timber, and transport. Their effect came from combination with canoes, causeway armies, and canal engineering, not from any isolated technological advantage.

Section 32.9

The Brigantines and the Battle for Lake Texcoco

The retreat of 1520 had shown that the lake and causeways were the key to the city. A coalition that could move only along narrow causeways, under attack from canoes, could not press an assault. The coalition therefore resolved to control the water. Spanish accounts describe the decision to build vessels, and Cortés's letters give prominence to it, in part because it showed foresight and in part because it was his own contribution. Martín López, a Spanish carpenter and shipwright, directed the construction.

The enterprise depended on Indigenous labor and logistics at every stage. Timbers were cut and shaped near Tlaxcala, and components, including planks, masts, and rigging, were carried across the mountains to Texcoco by thousands of Tlaxcalan porters, reportedly under escort. At Texcoco, Indigenous workers dug and prepared a canal to launch the finished vessels and assisted the assembly. Cortés records that thirteen brigantines were completed by 28 April 1521. The scale of the carrying and building effort was a mark of Tlaxcalan commitment, and it shows that the fleet was a coalition project.

The fleet's value lay in its integration with other forces. The brigantines carried artillery and crossbowmen and could break up canoe formations, protect causeway columns from flank attack, and ferry troops. But canoes remained important, and Indigenous crews, many from lakeside communities allied with the coalition, operated large numbers of them. The Mexica adapted. They drove stakes into the lake bed to trap or foil the brigantines, dug and flooded traps in shallow water, and attempted ambushes in which canoes lured vessels into concealed hazards, an episode the Spanish accounts record. The Mexica also continued to use canoes to move supplies and warriors and, at times, to launch raids on the coalition's camps. Control of the lake was therefore contested and gradual.

Cortés also acted against the city's water supply. In late May 1521 the coalition cut the aqueduct from Chapultepec that supplied the island with fresh water, and the Mexica, according to Spanish accounts, repaired it at least once. The attack on the aqueduct, the launching of the fleet, and the advance on the causeways were parts of a single strategy: to isolate the city from food, water, and reinforcement, and to bring coalition forces into the urban area along multiple lines.

Section 32.10

The Siege Begins: Causeways, Water, Canoes, and Indigenous Armies

The final siege began in late May or early June 1521, and the sources do not agree on a single first day. The coalition divided into divisions placed at the causeway approaches. Alvarado took the western approach at Tlacopan, Olid the southwestern approach from Coyoacán, and Sandoval the Iztapalapa line, with Cortés commanding the brigantines at first. Each division included a small number of Spaniards and a much larger Indigenous force, the latter drawn from Tlaxcala, Huexotzinco, Cholula, Chalco, Texcoco, and other communities. Spanish figures for the Indigenous armies run to the tens of thousands per division and perhaps over a hundred thousand in total. Some modern syntheses suggest a final allied force of hundreds of thousands, supported by roughly 1,500 Spaniards, though the sources are uncertain, and all numbers in this chapter are estimates.

The four Spanish captains were not the protagonists of the siege. The Indigenous contingents had their own commanders, whose names and actions appear in Tlaxcalan and Texcocan sources and in the Spanish accounts only intermittently. Indigenous workers built and maintained the camps, carried food and water, dug and filled channels, repaired damaged causeway sections, and supported the fleet. Women prepared food for the armies and tended the wounded. The siege was a mass enterprise whose labor was largely Indigenous.

It was not an effortless encirclement. The causeways were narrow, and the Mexica had removed sections and built barricades at the breaches. Coalition forces fought to cross each gap, filled it with rubble and adobe from demolished houses, and held it against counterattack. Every night the Mexica returned and reopened the gap or rebuilt the defenses. Defenders from Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco counterattacked repeatedly, struck from canoes, and used their knowledge of the canals to isolate and capture enemy detachments. At least once, Spanish accounts record a major setback in which a division's advance was cut off and many men were captured and sacrificed; Cortés had to withdraw in disorder and the Mexica displayed the heads of captured Spaniards to the other camps. These incidents show that the defenders retained the initiative at times and that the outcome was not predetermined.

The war also had a psychological and political dimension. After the setback, some allied contingents reportedly lost heart, and Mexica envoys tried to convince them to leave. Cortés had to restore confidence and keep the coalition together. Some allies did withdraw for a time. The siege therefore depended on the continuing commitment of Indigenous leaders who judged that destroying Mexica power served their own interests.

Section 32.11

Famine, Water, Tlatelolco, and the Destruction of the City

The siege was a campaign against a city as a system, and the burden on its population was immense. The coalition progressively took control of the lake and causeways, cutting fishing, canoe traffic, food deliveries, and reinforcement. The aqueduct was broken, and the defenders resorted to wells and brackish water, which Spanish and Nahua accounts both link to illness. Food ran out. Nahua accounts in Book 12 describe people eating whatever they could find, including lizards, leather, and the bark of trees, and Spanish accounts note the same.

The coalition destroyed the city as it advanced. Each time they took a district, the attackers demolished houses and used the debris to fill canals, creating a land route for their armies and removing cover for defenders. Barriers were torn down, neighborhoods were burned or dismantled, and the population was compressed into a shrinking area, increasingly toward Tlatelolco. Some historians stress that the destruction was a tactic, and some stress that Indigenous allies, with their own long grievances, took part in it with enthusiasm. Both statements can be true.

Civilians suffered enormously. The sources describe streets and canals filled with the dead, bodies lying unburied, and survivors, weakened by hunger and by the epidemic that had struck the previous year, moving through the ruins. Women, children, and the elderly were among those who died, and many fled by water or on foot, sometimes through coalition lines, where they were killed, robbed, or enslaved. Spanish accounts describe masses of refugees leaving the city in the final days and note the allies' killing of them, and Cortés says he tried to restrain his allies without success. How far he tried is difficult to establish, because his letter has an obvious interest in distancing himself from the slaughter.

Around the beginning of August the coalition took the Tlatelolco market area, the island's great commercial and food-distribution center. Nearby stood Tlatelolco's ceremonial precinct, and the loss of this sector compressed the defenders into an increasingly small area. Resistance nevertheless continued. Defenders fought from rooftops and from the remaining barricades, and Cuauhtémoc rejected offers of negotiation. Spanish accounts record continuing refusals and a belief among the defenders that they could still hold out. The siege turned on starvation, thirst, and the destruction of a physical city, and these were as important to its outcome as any battle.

Everyday Life and Social History

Thirst, Hunger, and the Households Inside and Outside the City

Inside the city, households broke down under hunger and illness. Women who had managed the grinding of maize, the preparation of food, and the care of children now searched for water and roots, and nursed the wounded and the sick. Fishers and canoe operators who had supplied the city lost access to the lake. Craftspeople, merchants, and farmers of the chinampas, whose work had fed the island, were displaced or drawn into the fighting. Nahua accounts record the use of whatever could be eaten and the sight of bodies in the streets, and they leave open the number who died.

Outside the city, the coalition's armies depended on the labor of porters and cooks, and on the households that supplied them with maize and tortillas. Tlaxcalan and other allied communities lost workers to the war and to disease, and women prepared food and tended injured warriors in the camps. Canoe operators from lake communities, forced or recruited, were part of the fleet. The silences in the sources about these experiences are themselves evidence of whose lives the surviving texts recorded. The people who suffered and labored most are the people most difficult to see.

Section 32.12

Cuauhtémoc, 13 August 1521, and What “Fall” Meant

By early August the defenders held only a small portion of Tlatelolco. Spanish accounts describe the final days as a series of attacks across ground that had been leveled, in which the coalition killed large numbers of people who were too weak to resist. Cortés reports that he sent messages to Cuauhtémoc proposing surrender and that Cuauhtémoc, in some versions, agreed to meet and then did not appear. The Mexica council, according to several accounts, refused to surrender, and fighting continued.

On 13 August 1521, the last pocket of resistance collapsed. Cuauhtémoc and his companions attempted to escape across the lake in canoes. Brigantines commanded by García Holguín intercepted them, and Cuauhtémoc was taken and brought before Cortés. Spanish accounts record words he is said to have spoken to Cortés, but the exact wording is not reliably known, and this chapter does not reproduce it. His capture ended organized resistance in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco. In the days afterward, survivors were allowed or forced to leave the ruined city, and the coalition's armies looted and killed. Cortés's own account reports the death toll and the condition of the city in terms that underline the scale of destruction, and the estimates vary widely and cannot be verified.

The immediate aftermath included killing, enslavement, looting, displacement, and physical ruin, and these were consequences of the battle itself. The coalition's Indigenous allies took plunder and captives, and Spanish accounts record Cortés's disappointment with the amount of gold recovered. Cuauhtémoc, according to Spanish sources, was later tortured in an effort to extract the location of more treasure, an episode that belongs more properly to the postwar period and to the account of colonial consolidation in Chapter 33.

What did "fall" mean? The date of 13 August marks the defeat of organized Mexica resistance in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco, not the instantaneous "conquest of Mexico." Many Indigenous communities across central and southern Mexico remained politically autonomous or resistant, and Spanish control over the wider region was incomplete. The Triple Alliance had been a network of tributary relationships, and its capital's defeat did not automatically transfer those relationships. Coalition allies expected the victory to advance their own positions, and many believed they had been victors rather than subjects. Whether those expectations would be honored was the central political question of the following years. The long, uneven process by which military victory was converted into colonial government, which Chapter 33 examines, was still ahead.

The destruction of the city was also an intellectual and cultural loss. A sacred precinct, a major market, archives, and temples were destroyed, and a population that had been among the largest in the world was scattered and diminished. The survivors, including nobles, craftspeople, and scribes, would carry their knowledge into the colonial period, and it is to their later writing, in Nahuatl and Spanish, that students owe much of what is known of the event. The chapter ends here, at the hinge between the military story and the colonial one.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Occupation to Siege

What Changed

  • The coalition moved from occupying the capital to besieging it
  • The Mexica lost two rulers within a year and installed a third
  • The Spanish force grew with the Narváez men and Caribbean reinforcements
  • The conflict spread to the whole lake system and its communities
  • The city itself was destroyed

What Stayed the Same

  • The Spaniards depended on Indigenous allies for manpower, food, labor, and intelligence
  • Indigenous communities acted on their own grievances and calculations
  • Spanish sources remained self-justifying and Nahua sources retrospective

Who experienced change most directly: The inhabitants of Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco, who lost their city and a large part of their population. Greater continuity: Many Indigenous communities outside the Basin, whose institutions persisted into the colonial period. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the fall of the capital was a break for the city and the Mexica elite, but not a uniform rupture for all of Mesoamerica.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How the Coalition Returned and Took the City

Long-term causes

The tribute grievances of subject altepetl, Tlaxcala's long resistance to the Triple Alliance, and regional rivalries around the lake.

Short-term causes

The breakdown of the occupation after Tóxcatl, the death of Moctezuma, the retreat of 1520, and the Spanish need for allies after near destruction.

Event or transformation

Tlaxcala preserved the alliance, the coalition campaigned around the Basin, Texcoco became a base, and the allied forces besieged the island city.

Immediate effects

Wider Indigenous participation, a lake fleet, the cutting of water and food, and the destruction and depopulation of the city.

Long-term consequences

The end of Mexica rule in the Basin, a contested postwar order in which allies claimed rewards, and the beginning of colonial consolidation (Chapter 33).

Historical complexity

Weapons, disease, alliances, logistics, and political division all mattered. No one of them was sufficient, and the outcome was not inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Spanish-Mexica conflict from the unstable occupation of Tenochtitlan in late 1519 to the surrender of 13 August 1521. Its central argument is that Tenochtitlan did not fall because a few Spaniards possessed superior weapons. The Mexica expelled the coalition in 1520, and the coalition returned only after rebuilding its alliances, receiving reinforcements, isolating the Basin, benefiting from an epidemic, constructing a lake fleet, and conducting a prolonged siege.

The occupation of Tenochtitlan under a captive Moctezuma was inherently unstable. A few hundred Spaniards and their allies were lodged in a very large city, pressed on its religion, and dependent on its food. Moctezuma's authority declined, rival Mexica leaders such as Cuitláhuac gained influence, and the Texcocan ruler Cacama was replaced, with lasting effects on Texcocan politics.

The conflict was complicated by a Spanish war within the conquest. Pánfilo de Narváez arrived in April 1520 to arrest Cortés, and Indigenous leaders observed the division among the Spaniards. Cortés defeated him at Cempoala in late May and gained most of his men, which greatly increased Spanish manpower. The new arrivals, though, brought expectations of plunder and, according to the broader conclusion, an association with the epidemic that followed.

While Cortés was away, Alvarado's men attacked participants in the Tóxcatl festival. Nahua and Spanish accounts differ sharply about motive, and historians cannot settle it. The massacre triggered a large-scale uprising and destroyed the diplomatic equilibrium. Cortés's return in late June did not restore control, and Moctezuma died, with the cause disputed between stoning by his own people and killing by the Spaniards. In either case, the strategy of governing through him ended.

The retreat from Tenochtitlan, traditionally called the Noche Triste, on the night of 30 June–1 July 1520 was a catastrophe for the coalition. Spaniards and especially Indigenous allies died in large numbers on the Tlacopan causeway, and the occupation was destroyed, though the survivors remained a shattered but functioning force. The survivors reached Tlaxcala after the Battle of Otumba, in which factors other than Spanish prowess help explain the outcome.

Defeat did not end the alliance. Tlaxcalan leaders, reasoning from their own interests, sheltered the survivors and refused Mexica offers. The Tepeaca campaigns and the surrounding operations secured routes, brought new communities into the coalition, and weakened Mexica support networks. This phase connects the retreat to the siege and shows that the road back to the Basin was built in 1520.

A smallpox epidemic struck central Mexico in the second half of 1520, killing many people in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco, disrupting households and government, and killing Cuitláhuac. Cuauhtémoc became tlatoani. The epidemic weakened a society at war without deciding the war: the final defeat still required campaigns, enormous Indigenous armies, control of the Basin, and a siege.

From Texcoco, the coalition reshaped the political geography around the lake. Tlaxcalans, Huexotzincas, Chalcas, Texcocan factions, and others joined or shifted allegiance for their own reasons, while the Mexica conducted counter-diplomacy. Thirteen brigantines, built with Spanish knowledge and Indigenous labor, were launched on 28 April 1521. They operated together with canoes, causeway armies, and engineering, against defenders who adapted with stakes, barriers, and ambushes.

The siege opened in late May or early June 1521 along several causeways. It was not an effortless encirclement: the defenders counterattacked and captured enemies, and at times forced allied contingents back. The coalition's progress cut food and water, demolished the city, and compressed the population toward Tlatelolco. Famine, thirst, disease, and civilian death were central to the outcome and not incidental to it.

On 13 August 1521 Cuauhtémoc was captured and organized resistance in Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco ended. That date marks a defeat of the capital, not the instantaneous conquest of Mexico, since many communities remained autonomous and Spanish control was incomplete. The force that took the city was overwhelmingly Indigenous in manpower, and the human cost fell overwhelmingly on Indigenous people on both sides. Students should also take away a method: sources written by participants served their authors' purposes, Indigenous accounts were composed under colonial conditions, and every figure must be treated as an estimate.

A channel carrying fresh water from Chapultepec to the island city, cut by the coalition in 1521.
Chinampa
An intensively cultivated artificial garden plot in the lake system that fed the city.
Brigantine
A small sailing-and-rowing vessel; thirteen were built at Texcoco for lake warfare.
Canoe warfare
Combat from and against canoes, a major Mexica and allied capability on the lake.
Siege
A military operation to isolate and reduce a place, here by cutting food, water, and reinforcement and by demolition.
Coalition
A temporary alliance of distinct groups with differing goals, such as the Spanish-Tlaxcalan-Texcocan force.
Auxiliary
An Indigenous participant in Spanish campaigns, whose goals and status differed from those of Spanish soldiers.
Smallpox
A viral disease that reached central Mexico in 1520 and killed many, including Cuitláhuac.
Succession dispute
A contest over rulership, as in Texcoco, that divided a polity.
Cuitláhuac
Moctezuma's brother, leader of the resistance, who ruled briefly and died of the epidemic.
Cuauhtémoc
The tlatoani who led the final defense and was captured on 13 August 1521.
Narváez expedition
The force sent by Velázquez in 1520 to arrest Cortés, defeated at Cempoala.
Tepeaca campaign
The autumn 1520 operations securing routes and weakening Mexica supports before the return to the Basin.
Carta de relación
A formal report to the Crown by a conquest leader, a legal and political document.
Annals of Tlatelolco
A Nahuatl account from the 1520s, preserving a Tlatelolca perspective on the conquest.
Florentine Codex
A colonial-era compilation by Nahua scholars with Sahagún; Book 12 treats the conquest.
Lienzo de Tlaxcala
A sixteenth-century pictorial manuscript presenting a Tlaxcalan view of the alliance and war.
Conquest versus colonization
Related but distinct processes: military seizure versus long-term institutional and economic control.
Capitulation
The end of organized resistance by a besieged force or city; here the events of 13 August 1521.

Mapping the Final Siege and Comparing Its Narrators

This project asks you to map the 1521 siege and compare how Spanish, Nahua, and Tlaxcalan sources describe it.

  1. Build a map. Using a reconstruction of the Basin in 1519, mark the causeways, camps, aqueduct, lake towns, and the stages of the coalition's advance.
  2. Select an episode. Choose one: the brigantine launch, the causeway fighting, or the fall of Tlatelolco.
  3. Locate Spanish accounts. Using translated editions of Cortés's Third Letter and Díaz's memoir, summarize how each describes the episode.
  4. Locate Indigenous accounts. Using selections from the Florentine Codex Book 12, the Annals of Tlatelolco, the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, or Muñoz Camargo, summarize their treatment.
  5. Compare and write. In a 3–5 page essay, identify agreements, contradictions, and silences, and explain what can be reconstructed with confidence.

Library resources to use: Translated primary-source collections on the conquest of Mexico, scholarly studies of Nahua-language sources and of the siege, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Mesoamerican history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the fall of Tenochtitlan was not a single decisive blow. The Mexica won in 1520, and the city was lost only after a year in which Indigenous decisions rebuilt a coalition larger than the one that had been expelled. Tlaxcalan rulers kept an alliance alive when abandoning it was possible, Texcocans and lake communities chose sides in a politically divided landscape, and tens of thousands of Indigenous workers and warriors made the siege possible. The Spaniards were essential, but they were a small part of the force and a dependent part of its political order.

The chapter also shows how violence, disease, and legitimacy interacted. The epidemic weakened a society at war, but did not end the war. The siege destroyed a city by cutting off food and water and by demolishing its fabric, and the people who bore that destruction were largely noncombatants. The sources that record it were written to justify, to remember, or to claim rewards, and learning to read them critically is a skill that recurs through Volume II.

Chapter 33 takes up the consequences. The victors had to decide what to do with a ruined capital, a diminished population, and allies who expected rewards. The slow, uneven conversion of military victory into colonial government, with its encomiendas, missionaries, Indigenous elites, and a new city built on the ruins of the old, is the next stage of the narrative. Students should carry forward the view that the allies who took the city in 1521 had interests of their own, and that the story of 13 August is as much a story of Mesoamerican politics as of Spanish arms.
