Chapter Opening: One Empire, Many Outcomes

Between 1810 and 1862, territories that had once answered to the same Spanish crown produced a loose confederation, a centralized constitutional republic, a buffer state, an isolated personal dictatorship, and a federation that dissolved. The end of imperial rule settled who would not govern; it did not settle who would.

This chapter compares how the Southern Cone (Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, Paraguay) and Central America formed states after Iberian rule. It takes up where the independence chapters leave off. Chapter 54 told the campaigns of San Martín; Chapter 57 described the end of Spanish authority in Central America and the founding of the federation. Here the question is institutional: what replaced the viceroyalty and the captaincy general, and why did outcomes diverge?

The chapter's governing question is why these societies developed such different political systems, and how federalism, regional authority, economic geography, military power, and competing social interests determined whether states fragmented or consolidated. It challenges the assumption that republicanism naturally produced chaos. Argentine provinces were functioning governments, Chile's order was built by coercion as well as constitution, and Paraguay's centralized state had real administrative capacity.

Evidence differs sharply by case. Constitutions, pacts, and proclamations record the aims of elites. Customs records and treasury accounts show fiscal capacity. Petitions, military muster lists, and municipal records reach ordinary people, though unevenly. Exile writings, such as those of Argentine opponents of Rosas, are vivid but partisan. Indigenous voices survive mostly through colonial and republican documents written by others.

The chapter runs from about 1810 to 1862, ending with Argentine unification under Mitre, Chile's transition to Pérez in 1861, the passing of Paraguay's presidency to Francisco Solano López, and the defeat of William Walker in 1857. The Paraguayan War, the War of the Pacific, and the export boom belong to Chapters 64, 65, and 68–69.

Historical Context

Spanish rule had organized these regions through overlapping jurisdictions: the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata (created 1776), the Captaincy General of Chile, and the Captaincy General of Guatemala. When Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808 broke royal legitimacy (Chapter 51), authority passed to cities and juntas. Municipal councils (cabildos) claimed to hold sovereignty in the king's absence, and cities then disputed whether they spoke for their hinterlands.

Geography shaped what followed. Buenos Aires controlled the Río de la Plata's mouth and its customs; interior and littoral provinces wanted a share of that revenue and free river navigation. Chile was a narrow valley between desert, Andes, and Pacific, with Santiago and the port of Valparaíso close together. Paraguay sat upriver, reachable only through rivers others controlled. Central America lay between two oceans, with highland Indigenous majorities in Guatemala and rival cities across the isthmus.

Wars of independence had militarized society and left treasuries empty. Armed leaders commanded loyalty that civilian institutions lacked. Slavery persisted in the Río de la Plata until the 1850s–60s, though gradual emancipation measures began earlier; Central America abolished it in 1824. Indigenous communities held land and political roles under colonial law that republics redefined.

The Problem of Sovereignty after Spanish Rule, 1810–1825

Section 62.1

62.1 Competing sovereignties

When royal authority collapsed, juntas and cabildos inherited it. Buenos Aires's government claimed to speak for the old viceroyalty, but Paraguay, the Eastern Province, and interior cities refused subordination. In Central America, the cities of Guatemala, San Salvador, León, and Comayagua each had their own interests. The core dispute was whether a former jurisdiction was one nation or a collection of sovereign peoples.

The jurisdictional problem had deep colonial roots. The viceroy in Buenos Aires could issue commands, but practical administration passed through governors, municipal councils, parish networks, and locally raised forces. Spanish officials had sought to balance provincial jurisdictions, not to prepare one national electorate. When the crown's legitimacy fractured, each town could argue that its own corporate community possessed authority until a legitimate sovereign appeared. Revolutionary language about the sovereignty of the people therefore raised a difficult question: did the word 'people' describe inhabitants of one projected nation, or the distinct political communities of cities and provinces? The answer affected who could levy taxes and recruit soldiers, not merely which flag flew above a capital.

Central America presented a comparable but not identical difficulty. The commercial dominance of Guatemala City provoked resistance from San Salvador, León, Granada, Comayagua, and Costa Rican towns; meanwhile, river basins and mountains made the five eventual states economically diverse. In the Southern Cone, Paraguay's leaders interpreted autonomy partly as protection against direction from Buenos Aires, while the Eastern Province's options were narrowed by Portuguese-Brazilian occupation and competing regional alliances. Chile's central valley gave Santiago a stronger logistical advantage, but opposition in Concepción and Coquimbo made centralization contentious. The constitutional vocabulary of one age thus concealed a spectrum of territorial and political relationships.

To study these processes, historians must separate an assembly's claim from the administrative power to enforce it. A constitution could proclaim territorial unity while a provincial governor maintained customs posts and troops outside the central government's command. A city might recognize a federal pact but refuse the fiscal contributions intended to sustain it. Indigenous nations, whose lands were claimed by emerging republics, were frequently neither participants in these negotiations nor passive subjects of them. Their diplomacy and resistance remind us that the boundaries appearing on later maps should not be projected uncritically onto the 1820s. State formation must be reconstructed through implementation, bargaining, and coercion alongside formal declarations.

Section 62.2

62.2 Provinces, cities, and caudillos

Legitimacy was now sought through elections, pacts, and armed followings. A caudillo rested authority on personal military and patronage networks, but caudillos often governed through real institutions such as legislatures and provincial treasuries. Federal arrangements aimed to preserve provincial autonomy; centralizing (unitarist) projects aimed at a single national government.

Personal leadership mattered because provincial offices had limited means to enforce decisions beyond their immediate districts. Militias were usually supplied through local patrons; livestock, horses, and credit could be requisitioned more readily by a commander who had relationships with ranchers than by an unfamiliar central minister. The same commander might preside over elections, convene a legislature, negotiate a treaty, and guarantee local security. This institutional hybridity explains why caudillos cannot be divided neatly into constitutional leaders and military outsiders. Their authority could be simultaneously personal, legal, and coercive, and it depended on cooperation that sometimes disappeared when resources ran short.

Federalism also had divergent meanings. In Argentina, it often defended provincial control of revenues and appointments against Buenos Aires; in Central America, it was supposed to join five former provinces while protecting state governments from Guatemala City's dominance. Some Chilean reformers tried to distribute authority across newly defined provinces in the 1820s, but critics feared that decentralized taxation and recruitment could not sustain a viable republic. Paraguay, by contrast, regarded the political autonomy of its own capital and countryside as the precondition for a strong single state. A federal label therefore tells students little unless they ask who controlled troops, customs houses, courts, and debts.

Legitimacy was not only an elite matter. Rural communities could support a provincial leader for access to markets or protection against forced recruitment; opponents could mobilize through parish networks and religious festivals. Enslaved and free Afro-descended soldiers sometimes obtained manumission or bargaining power through military service, though promises were unevenly fulfilled. Women participated in provisioning armies, managing property, carrying intelligence, and defending households during campaigns while facing restrictions on formal political representation. These relationships made provincial politics social history as well as constitutional history. Comparing them requires asking whose consent was claimed and whose obligations the new state could actually impose.

Historical Vocabulary
Caudillismo

Caudillismo describes political authority based on a leader's personal following, often built on military command, patronage, and regional ties. The term was often used by later liberal writers as a synonym for backwardness.

Historians now stress that caudillos operated within legislatures, treaties, and local institutions, and that they varied from Rosas to Carrera to Rivera. The word explains less than the networks beneath it.

Section 62.3

62.3 Geography, customs, and state capacity

Port customs were the largest and most reliable revenue in an age with little direct taxation. Buenos Aires, Valparaíso, and Montevideo could finance governments and armies; inland provinces and Central American Pacific and Caribbean routes offered less. States with a dominant, wealthy center had advantages, but a rich port could also provoke resentment from poorer partners.

The geography of revenue was especially decisive because most governments lacked a reliable cadastral system or a large salaried bureaucracy for collecting direct taxes. Customs duties on imports could be assessed at a harbor and paid by merchants whose goods passed a narrow point. Such receipts financed salaries, military stores, public debt, and diplomatic missions. Interior authorities consequently sought transit taxes or requisitions on production, measures that impeded exchange and could provoke resistance. A government with a prosperous port could subsidize allies and purchase weapons; a poorer province might need a powerful patron or a federal agreement that actually redistributed money.

Yet neither ports nor mountains determined political outcomes by themselves. Chile's Santiago-Valparaíso corridor combined access to overseas trade with a relatively concentrated administrative core, but regional movements still contested the capital's dominance. Montevideo's port created resources for rival governments during the Guerra Grande, yet foreign blockades and control of the surrounding countryside affected how far urban revenue could support war. Buenos Aires's centrality to Atlantic customs simultaneously enabled the Argentine Confederation and encouraged its littoral partners to oppose the city's monopoly. Paraguay's inland position encouraged diplomatic insistence on river navigation and on freedom from Argentine control of the lower Paraná.

In Central America, roads across volcanic highlands and through rainy coastal regions were expensive to maintain, and some Caribbean export routes connected more directly to foreign merchants than to a federal capital. Costa Rican coffee moved westward toward the Pacific before routes to the Atlantic improved; Nicaragua's San Juan-Lake Nicaragua transit corridor attracted strategic competition precisely because it provided a rare link between oceans. Geographic explanations must therefore incorporate specific technologies, commercial arrangements, and the political decisions that financed or blocked them. A mountain range may hinder administration, but an unequal customs system turns inconvenience into a political grievance.

Section 62.4

62.4 Hierarchy, mobilization, and citizenship

Republics proclaimed equality but kept legal and social hierarchies. Indigenous communities, Afro-descended soldiers and workers, rural laborers, and women were essential to armies, farms, and households yet had limited formal power. Mobilization created claims: soldiers and militias expected land, pay, or recognition, and caudillos who could meet them gained followers.

Different regions inherited different legal classifications. Slavery, manumission, and freedom-of-womb laws did not operate uniformly across the Southern Cone; Central American legislators prohibited slavery early, yet bonded labor and coercive obligations survived in other forms. Constitutional clauses could proclaim civic equality while restricting the vote by age, sex, literacy, occupation, dependence, or property. In addition, proclamations often claimed sovereignty over territories occupied by independent Indigenous polities. In Chile, Mapuche leaders negotiated across a frontier the republic could not simply administer; in Argentina, Indigenous groups made treaties, traded, and fought with provincial authorities; in Paraguay and Guatemala, state institutions altered older communal rights.

Military service was one channel through which disadvantaged people entered public life. A soldier might expect pay, food, exemption for relatives, or an improved legal status, but governments frequently lacked the funds or political will to honor promises. Forced enlistment disrupted seasonal work and household production, especially during prolonged civil wars. Afro-descended men served in the Río de la Plata's armies under conditions that combined emancipation opportunities with lethal exposure. Women carried supplies and information and confronted military authorities to secure missing relatives or wages. Regional political projects were thus carried by households and workers as well as by elite constitutional delegates.

The social consequences of legal change need direct evidence. Petitions reveal people invoking new rights; notarial records can record manumission and property transactions; parish books show marriages and demographic losses; and military rolls document recruitment, though often without the recruit's consent. They rarely capture people outside written legal institutions. Students should distinguish a law's promise, a court's interpretation, and the daily outcomes recorded in provincial communities. That distinction will recur in Artigas's agrarian regulation, Uruguay's wartime abolition, Paraguay's Indigenous policy, and Central American federal legislation.

Argentina: Provincial Sovereignty and Failed Centralization, 1820–1829

Section 62.5

62.5 The Collapse of Central Authority in 1820

By 1819 the government in Buenos Aires, headed by the Supreme Director José Rondeau, still claimed authority over the territory of the former viceroyalty, but in practice it governed little beyond the city and its hinterland. It had no secure revenue except Buenos Aires customs, its armies were committed to wars against Spanish forces and Portuguese invasion, and a centralist constitution it issued in 1819 alienated provincial leaders who had already organized their own governments. Santa Fe had adopted a provisional statute in 1819, and Entre Ríos and Corrientes had been shaped by the federal movement of José Gervasio Artigas. The government's claim to represent a single nation rested on a shrinking base.

The decisive blow came on 1 February 1820 at Cepeda, where the forces of Francisco Ramírez of Entre Ríos and Estanislao López of Santa Fe defeated Rondeau's army. The defeat followed the mutiny at Arequito in January 1820, when part of the Army of the North refused to march against the provinces, and it left the Directory without troops. The Buenos Aires cabildo took control of the city and the Directory and Congress dissolved. The victors dictated terms in the Treaty of Pilar of 23 February 1820, which ended hostilities among Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, and Entre Ríos and committed them to a federal form of association, though without creating a common government.

The year 1820 is often called a year of anarchy because Buenos Aires changed governors repeatedly before Martín Rodríguez took office in the autumn. That description reflects the experience of the capital's elite and the hostility of later liberal historians. A closer look shows institutions being rebuilt: Buenos Aires convened a representative legislature, and provinces from Córdoba to Santiago del Estero and the Cuyo region declared themselves autonomous governments with their own governors, treasuries, and militias. Sovereignty had passed from the former viceregal center to the provinces, as it had earlier passed from the crown to the cabildos.

The collapse also changed who held power. Armed leaders of the littoral and interior, supported by rural militias and local property owners, gained authority that no longer depended on Buenos Aires. Historians rely on cabildo and legislative records, the text of Pilar, correspondence among governors, and later memoirs; each reflects the interests of its authors, and rural people appear mostly through military rolls and complaints. The settlement of 1820 left unresolved how the provinces would eventually relate to one another, a question that dominated the next four decades.

The Treaty of Pilar did not in itself establish a national executive or treasury. Its federal principle depended on a meeting of representatives whose legitimacy would come from the provinces; the planned assembly did not produce a durable government. Buenos Aires's willingness to bargain reflected the military balance immediately after Cepeda, not unconditional acceptance of permanent equality. This helps explain why the apparent settlement of one month could coexist with renewed armed confrontation and negotiation in the same year. Provincial autonomy emerged from the failure of central command, but the long-term relationship among provinces remained open.

62.6 Artigas and the Federal League as an Alternative Political Project

José Gervasio Artigas, a rural officer from the Banda Oriental who had broken with the Buenos Aires government in 1814, led the League of Free Peoples (Liga de los Pueblos Libres), a loose association of the Eastern Province, Entre Ríos, Corrientes, Santa Fe, Misiones, and, briefly, Córdoba. Its political program called for provincial sovereignty, a confederal association of equals, republican government, and limits on the domination of Buenos Aires over trade and revenue. Though later nationalists in Uruguay and Argentina each claimed him, the League was a distinct political project with its own territory and institutions.

The League's social program was equally important. The Provisional Regulation of 1815 for the Eastern countryside authorized distribution of confiscated estates, especially to poor rural people, including free Afro-descended and Indigenous families, and favored those with the fewest resources. Historians debate how far the regulation was actually carried out, since war, resistance by landowners, and the League's short life limited its implementation. Even so, it shows a political vision in which land and citizenship were linked and in which the rural poor were treated as political participants.

The movement was destroyed by combined opposition. Portuguese-Brazilian forces invaded the Banda Oriental in 1816 and took Montevideo in January 1817; Buenos Aires leaders, uneasy with Artigas's program, gave little help and at times cooperated with the invaders. Artigas was defeated at Tacuarembó in January 1820, and his lieutenant Francisco Ramírez of Entre Ríos, who had broken with him, defeated his forces. In September 1820 Artigas crossed into Paraguay, where he lived under Francia's authority until his death in 1850.

The League's institutional legacy was larger than its military record. Its language of federal association entered the pacts and provincial charters of the 1820s, and its rural constituency became part of the political base of later federalist leaders. In the Eastern Province it left a political identity that Chapter 62's Uruguayan sections will trace. Evidence comes from the Regulation, correspondence, and cabildo records, but much of the narrative was shaped by later partisan writers; the voices of poor rural people appear mainly through the grievances and land claims of those who sought to benefit.

Artigas's program should be read through the conflicts between a regional war economy and peasant claims. Landed families who had fled or supported enemy forces possessed estates that the League considered potentially distributable, whereas livestock herding required grazing routes and a degree of security military campaigns repeatedly destroyed. The 1815 regulation used social distinctions that privileged poor applicants but did not erase hierarchy; officials still decided which claimants qualified and how disputed estates should be assigned. The decree is evidence of an egalitarian intention within a wartime coalition, not proof that land distribution transformed the countryside. This qualified interpretation avoids turning Artigas into either a flawless social reformer or a mere military separatist.

Section 62.7

62.7 Provincial Caudillos and the Politics of Confederation

After 1820, power in the provinces rested on leaders who combined military command, control of local revenue, and ties to landowners and rural militias. Estanislao López governed Santa Fe from 1818 until his death in 1838 and kept the province's army effective despite its small population. Francisco Ramírez briefly created a Republic of Entre Ríos in 1820 before dying in battle in 1821, during conflicts involving forces from Córdoba and Santa Fe, after alliances among provincial leaders shifted. Bustos governed Córdoba from 1820 to 1829 and was one of the key interior leaders until his death in 1830. In the west, Juan Facundo Quiroga was building authority in La Rioja and the Andean interior.

These leaders were not merely warlords. They made treaties, issued provisional regulations, convened legislatures, and collected taxes. Santa Fe had its 1819 statute and Córdoba adopted a provisional regulation in 1821; provinces appointed judges and maintained militias. The Treaty of Benegas in November 1820 between Buenos Aires and Santa Fe and the Quadrilateral Treaty of January 1822, joining Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, Entre Ríos, and Corrientes, show a diplomatic system of interprovincial relations. The treaties served immediate purposes, such as defense and the exchange of prisoners, but they also established the principle that provinces could bargain as equals.

Their power depended on resources. Littoral provinces controlled river access but had small populations and limited revenue, while interior provinces had trade in textiles, wine, and livestock hurt by competition from imported goods. Provincial governments relied on duties on goods in transit, rural levies, and contributions from landowners, and they were vulnerable to Buenos Aires, which retained customs revenue from the port. This inequality was the structural basis of repeated conflict.

Rural people supported these leaders for reasons beyond coercion: protection, access to land, and recognition of customary rights could matter more than formal citizenship. Historians draw on provincial archives, treaties, and military records, but also on hostile accounts by unitarist writers, so assessments of caudillos vary. The older view of anarchy has given way to an understanding of provincial governments as functioning, if fragile, political systems.

The interprovincial treaties also reveal the limits of formal equality. An agreement could promise mutual defense, but provincial forces were paid and equipped by their own governments, and a governor who feared unrest at home might withhold soldiers. Economic competition complicated cooperation: Córdoba's commercial links across the interior did not always align with Santa Fe's access to the Paraná. Ramírez's final campaign ended in his death in July 1821 after conflict involving forces from Córdoba and Santa Fe, demonstrating how quickly alliances could realign. By examining who signed a pact, who supplied the troops, and which revenue obligations were actually fulfilled, students can distinguish confederal aspiration from practical collective authority.

Section 62.8

62.8 Buenos Aires Reformism and Bernardino Rivadavia

Between 1821 and 1824, Governor Martín Rodríguez and his minister Bernardino Rivadavia led reforms that made Buenos Aires the most institutionally developed province. They created the University of Buenos Aires in 1821, founded a discount bank in 1822, expanded the legislature's role, and reorganized the courts and police. In 1822 an ecclesiastical reform law abolished the tithe, suppressed some religious houses, and brought clerical property and appointments under provincial control, provoking opposition from Church defenders and rural communities. Rivadavia also sought British investment; the 1824 Baring loan, issued in London, left Buenos Aires with a large debt and little of the money in hand.

The reforms depended on the province's customs revenue, on peace with the frontier, and on Buenos Aires's elite. They were meant as a model for the nation, and in 1824 the province sponsored a congress to draft a national constitution. Under Governor Gregorio de Las Heras, the congress met in Buenos Aires and in February 1826, after a law created a national executive, elected Rivadavia president. He took office that month. In March the congress declared Buenos Aires city a national capital, taking its revenue and territory from the province, which angered porteño federalists and made the provinces suspect Buenos Aires dominance.

Rivadavia pursued further measures, including a national bank and an 1826 law on long-term land leasing (enfiteusis), meant to raise revenue while keeping public land in state ownership. In practice, large landowners gained most of it. The congress then sanctioned a centralist constitution in December 1826 that would have placed provincial governors under national oversight. Most provinces refused to accept it, and several leaders, including Quiroga, rejected it forcefully. The national project was also hurt by the war with Brazil, which absorbed resources and brought a blockade.

Rivadavia resigned in June 1827, and the congress dissolved, leaving Buenos Aires again to govern itself as a province. Historians still debate his legacy. Admirers stress institution-building and secular reform; critics emphasize that reform served Buenos Aires's elite and ignored the interior. The evidence comes from legislative debates, laws, and correspondence, along with partisan accounts of both sides, and offers little direct testimony from rural workers and Indigenous people affected by land and Church policy.

The Baring Brothers loan illustrates the gap between nominal borrowing and usable funds. Commissions, discounts, and arrangements for placing securities meant that the full face value did not enter the treasury as spendable cash, while interest commitments survived political changes. Land-leasing policy likewise has to be analyzed through implementation: publicly owned land could generate rental payments without immediate sale, but those able to finance herds, secure favorable contracts, and influence officials were better positioned to benefit. The reformers' faith in European capital and education was substantial; their program nevertheless assumed that Buenos Aires could direct national modernization without settling the relationship of the provinces to the port. That unresolved assumption contributed to constitutional rejection.

Historical Vocabulary

Unitarism and Centralization

Unitarism describes a political preference for a national government exercising important powers directly over the provinces rather than through independent provincial agreements. In the Río de la Plata, many centralists argued that one treasury and administration were necessary for efficient governance and international relations.

The label did not identify every person who supported government reform, and provincial federalists also favored coercive authority within their own jurisdictions. Distinguish constitutional proposals, the interests of Buenos Aires merchants, and individual leaders instead of assuming one fixed ideological bloc.

Section 62.9

62.9 War with Brazil, Constitutional Breakdown, and the Crisis of 1828–1829

The war with Brazil began in December 1825, after the Thirty-Three Orientals' landing and the Congress's acceptance of the Eastern Province's union with the United Provinces. Argentine forces, with Uruguayan allies, won at Ituzaingó on 20 February 1827 under General Carlos de Alvear, but the Brazilian navy blockaded the river and cut trade, and the conflict could not be won decisively. For Argentina, the war's significance was financial and political: customs revenue fell, the new national bank issued paper money that could not be converted into metal, and prices rose.

The war exposed the weakness of the national government and of Rivadavia's project. Provinces objected to contributing troops and money for a conflict in which they saw little advantage, and the national executive lacked authority to compel them. After Rivadavia's resignation in 1827, Manuel Dorrego, a Buenos Aires federalist, became governor and managed the war and negotiations. Britain, which wanted the river reopened to trade, mediated; the Preliminary Peace Convention of 27 August 1828 recognized an independent Eastern state, a settlement examined in Chapter 62's Uruguayan sections, while Brazilian history belongs to Chapter 61.

The peace brought home an army and a political crisis. General Juan Lavalle, a unitarist officer returning from the Brazilian front, overthrew Dorrego on 1 December 1828 with the support of unitarist politicians and some officers. Dorrego was captured and executed on 13 December 1828 on Lavalle's order, an act that shocked federalists and made reconciliation harder. Lavalle's government could not control the countryside; Rosas, commander of rural militias, and López of Santa Fe defeated him in the battle of Puente de Márquez in April 1829, and the Pact of Cañuelas of June 1829 ended the immediate fighting.

The crisis of 1828–1829 reshaped Argentine politics. The execution of Dorrego created a martyr for federalism and discredited unitarism for years, while the war and failed constitution showed that a national government could not be imposed without provincial consent. Rosas, backed by rural militias and landowners, emerged as the arbiter, and in December 1829 the legislature elected him governor with extraordinary powers. Evidence includes proclamations, military reports, and the polemical memoirs of both factions, which differ sharply on responsibility; the next division examines the system that followed.

The execution of Dorrego became a central episode in the political memory of nineteenth-century Argentina because it seemed to cross an unwritten line separating battlefield rivalry from the elimination of a legally constituted governor. Lavalle's supporters had argued that extraordinary intervention could rescue the national project from federal domination; opponents interpreted the execution as evidence that centralists would impose government through military force. These competing narratives were later repeated in newspapers, public commemorations, and histories of the period. Students should separate the documented sequence of rebellion, capture, and execution from partisan explanations of necessity or betrayal.

Rosas and the Argentine Confederation, 1829–1852

Section 62.10

62.10 Rosas, Rural Power, and the Governorship of Buenos Aires

Juan Manuel de Rosas, born in 1793 into a Buenos Aires landowning family, built his fortune in ranching and salting plants (saladeros) and his political base in the countryside. He commanded the rural militia known as the Colorados del Monte and acquired a reputation for imposing order on the frontier and among ranch workers. His network linked estancieros, militia officers, and the families who worked on the estates, including free Afro-descended and Indigenous people. After Dorrego's execution, the legislature elected Rosas governor in December 1829 and gave him extraordinary powers; the city's federalists hailed him as the Restorer of the Laws.

His first term ended in December 1832, when the legislature refused to renew the extraordinary powers and he declined the office on those terms. He then led the 1833 expedition against Indigenous groups south of the settled frontier while allies governed Buenos Aires. Political maneuvering continued; Facundo Quiroga was assassinated in February 1835, which shocked the federalists and strengthened the case for strong rule. Governor Manuel Vicente Maza resigned and the legislature elected Rosas again, this time granting the suma del poder público, the sum of public power, ratified by a plebiscite in Buenos Aires.

The two grants were not the same. The 1829 powers were extraordinary but temporary and limited by the legislature's oversight, while the 1835 grant concentrated executive, legislative, and judicial authority in a single person, in principle without limit of time. Historians debate how far the legislature and electorate freely consented, and how far Rosas's supporters made opposition dangerous. The evidence includes legislative acts, plebiscite records, correspondence, and hostile exile accounts, so assessments depend on which sources are trusted.

Rosas's patronage had material foundations. Estate owners and contractors could furnish horses and provisions, while militia service linked rural communities to provincial power. In the capital, public ceremonies, symbols, and celebrations promoted an identification of the federal cause with social order. These mechanisms helped secure compliance, but neither ritual nor patronage should be confused with an unrestricted popular mandate. Records of public acclamation were produced in a setting where political dissent could be dangerous. His enemies, meanwhile, often minimized the appeal of his promises of order after the violence of the late 1820s.

The legal differences between his two governorships matter because the provinces remained separate jurisdictions even while Buenos Aires concentrated power within itself. Rosas was not president of a modern unified Argentina; other governors retained their offices and depended on their own provincial alliances. The foreign-relations delegation that made Buenos Aires the confederation's outward voice did not create a national legislature or shared treasury. This combination of provincial sovereignty and a dominant Buenos Aires executive distinguished the confederal experiment from constitutional unitarism.

Historical Vocabulary
Suma del poder público

The suma del poder público ("sum of public power") was the grant by the Buenos Aires legislature in 1835 of executive, legislative, and judicial authority to Rosas, ratified by plebiscite.

It differs from the extraordinary powers of 1829, which were temporary and subject to legislative oversight, and from modern dictatorship, since it was legally conferred by existing institutions. Whether consent was free is debated, and the term helps students separate Rosas's two forms of authority.

Section 62.11

62.11 The Federal Pact of 1831 and the Confederal Order

The Federal Pact of 4 January 1831 joined Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, and Entre Ríos in an alliance against the Unitarist League of the interior led by General José María Paz, with Corrientes adhering soon after. It affirmed provincial autonomy and created a Representative Commission seated at Santa Fe with defined powers over war, treaties, and military coordination, and it anticipated a future congress to frame a national constitution. The pact preceded the capture of José María Paz on 10 May 1831 and was designed to coordinate resistance to the Unitarist League.

In practice the confederation worked through Buenos Aires. Provincial governors delegated the handling of foreign relations to the Buenos Aires governor, a practice that began in the 1830s and was renewed repeatedly, giving Rosas the right to speak abroad for the whole. The Representative Commission was dissolved on 13 July 1832, long before the mid-1830s. Facundo Quiroga urged the convening of a constitutional congress, but Rosas replied that the provinces were not ready and that a premature constitution would repeat the failure of 1826.

The confederal order had real strengths: it allowed provinces to keep their own governments and militias, kept the peace among federalist leaders, and gave foreign powers a single interlocutor. Its weakness was the absence of common institutions, revenue, or a shared army beyond the ones controlled by Buenos Aires. Historians therefore describe it as a system of interprovincial diplomacy and alliance rather than a nation-state. The texts of the pact and later delegations are the main sources, supplemented by correspondence among governors.

The timing is essential: the Federal Pact was signed in January 1831 before the capture of José María Paz in May, and it was one means of organizing opposition to the Unitarist League. Its signatories created a Representative Commission at Santa Fe, empowered to address specified common concerns and to prepare the conditions for a later general congress. That body was abolished in July 1832, so the treaty's promise of a standing interstate mechanism was short-lived. The disappearance of the Commission left negotiated delegations and personal diplomacy between governors, not a continuously functioning federal council, as the ordinary basis of confederal coordination.

The treaty's clauses also qualify the idea that Buenos Aires 'controlled' every province through simple command. Other provincial governments could challenge its preferred tariff or river policy and had the theoretical right to appoint and remove representatives in common institutions. Their practical bargaining power was unequal because Buenos Aires collected the main Atlantic customs, and local leaders were often preoccupied with armed opponents at home. The pact reveals a constitutional problem that persisted until 1853: agreement about provincial autonomy did not settle the allocation of commercial revenue, the timing of constitutional convention, or the composition of a national army.

Historical Vocabulary

Federal Pact and Confederal Association

A federal pact is an agreement among political communities that retain important powers while accepting specified common obligations. In the Argentine setting the 1831 agreement created obligations of defense and cooperation, but it did not immediately establish a functioning common president, national revenue service or permanent congress. Its Representative Commission was temporary.

The term must be distinguished from a fully organized federal constitution, which allocates powers to national institutions directly as well as to member provinces. The difference between treaty relationships and an operating national state helps explain the political transition from 1831 to 1853 and 1862.

Section 62.12

62.12 Coercion, Opposition, Political Exile, and the Indigenous Frontier

Rosas's regime used coercion systematically. The Sociedad Popular Restauradora, whose armed auxiliary was known as the Mazorca, enforced loyalty through intimidation and violence, especially in the late 1830s and 1840. Officials required the federal red badge and slogans in public life, censored the press, and dismissed or imprisoned opponents. Exact numbers of victims are disputed: accounts by exiles and the regime's defenders differ, and records are incomplete, so historians avoid precise figures.

Opponents fled to Montevideo, Santiago, and elsewhere, and from exile created an opposition literature. The group known as the Generation of 1837, including Esteban Echeverría, Domingo F. Sarmiento, and Juan Bautista Alberdi, wrote sharp critiques of Rosas and offered visions of a different nation. Sarmiento's Facundo, published in Chile in 1845, shaped later memory of the regime. These sources are vivid but partisan, and the regime's own press and records must be read alongside them. Rosas also had support, including among rural workers and part of the city's Afro-descended population, whose motives historians still debate.

On the frontier, Rosas combined war with diplomacy. His 1833 expedition attacked some Indigenous groups, while a system of rations of cattle, tobacco, and other goods, known as the negocio pacífico, sustained agreements with allied Pampa and Ranquel leaders and gave Buenos Aires influence beyond its settled lands. Indigenous communities were political actors who used trade and alliances to preserve autonomy, and the arrangement lasted until Rosas's fall. Sources include treaties, military reports, and correspondence by officers and Indigenous leaders' intermediaries, which seldom record Indigenous voices directly.

The central struggle over public expression was not limited to newspapers. Officials and loyalist associations could demand that shops, uniforms, and documents display partisan symbols, turning political affiliation into a visible mark of compliance. During intense crisis, denunciations and reprisals made ordinary commercial and family relations politically hazardous. At the same time, loyalty to Rosas did not automatically arise from fear. Some groups regarded the federal government as a defense against elite reformers who disparaged rural customs, while others valued a restoration of predictable administration after years of warfare. Historians need both official records and oppositional testimony without treating either as a transparent voice of all residents.

Indigenous leaders exercised independent calculations on the frontier. A treaty with Buenos Aires could secure livestock and markets for one coalition and shift military pressure onto another; commanders sometimes failed to honor promised deliveries, producing new disputes. The geography of forts, seasonal movement, and pasture shaped diplomacy, and Indigenous communities retained the capacity to refuse, negotiate, or relocate. Understanding these arrangements challenges a map that presents the entire Pampas as territory already governed in the same manner as urban Buenos Aires.

Section 62.13

62.13 Customs, Commerce, Foreign Blockades, and Federal Inequality

Buenos Aires's control of the port and its customs revenue underpinned Rosas's power. The 1835 customs law raised duties on competing imports and gave some protection to interior producers, but the provinces gained little revenue and the river routes to the interior remained under Buenos Aires's control. Littoral provinces such as Corrientes and Entre Ríos resented the restriction of direct overseas trade, and merchants in Montevideo and the interior sought wider access.

Foreign powers contested this regime. A French blockade of the Río de la Plata ran from 1838 to 1840, ending with a treaty in October 1840, and encouraged opposition: Corrientes rebelled, General Juan Lavalle campaigned against Rosas in 1839–1841, and a northern coalition formed. Rosas defeated these challenges with militia armies and the loyalty of Santa Fe and Entre Ríos. Britain and France jointly blockaded again from 1845 to 1850 and forced passage up the Paraná, but at Vuelta de Obligado in November 1845 Argentine forces inflicted costly resistance.

Britain and France eventually signed treaties in 1849 and 1850 that ended the blockades on terms respecting Argentine sovereignty over its rivers, a result Rosas's supporters presented as a victory. The conflict left the underlying inequality intact: the littoral and interior provinces remained dependent on Buenos Aires for revenue and trade policy. Customs registers, treaties, diplomatic correspondence, and merchants' accounts provide most of the evidence.

The Vuelta de Obligado engagement was tactically a severe challenge for the defenders: foreign naval vessels forced their passage despite Argentine efforts to obstruct the channel with batteries and chains. Its importance was political and symbolic as well as military, because resistance strengthened Rosas's portrayal of his government as defender of national sovereignty. British and French policymakers meanwhile weighed the commercial returns of coercion against its expense and diplomatic consequences. Peace treaties brought an end to intervention without solving the rivalry among Argentine provinces over who had the right to regulate navigation.

Commercial policy touched constituencies very differently. Protected producers in interior districts might welcome tariffs while merchants transporting imported textiles objected to them; river ports sought direct access to external shipping, and workers experienced war through fluctuating prices and military recruitment. Customs accounts can indicate changes in imports and receipts, but an economic inference drawn from a port register should not be extended to every rural household. The connection between revenue, regional bargaining and state formation is clearest where official policies are compared with actual patterns of trade.

Section 62.14

62.14 Urquiza's Challenge and the Overthrow of Rosas in 1852

Justo José de Urquiza, a wealthy rancher elected governor of Entre Ríos in December 1841, had been one of Rosas's strongest allies. His province benefited from the trade of the Uruguay and Paraná rivers, but he chafed at Buenos Aires's control of foreign relations and customs. On 1 May 1851 he issued a pronunciamiento accepting Rosas's resignation of the foreign-relations delegation, effectively withdrawing Entre Ríos's recognition of Rosas's authority over the confederation.

Urquiza built an alliance with Brazil, with the Colorado government in Montevideo, and with Corrientes, formalized in agreements of May 1851. Brazilian interests, discussed in Chapter 61, were to end Rosas's threat to Brazilian and Uruguayan security and to open the rivers. The allied army, which included Entre Ríos and Correntino forces, Uruguayans, and Brazilian troops, crossed the Paraná in December 1851 and advanced on Buenos Aires.

At Caseros on 3 February 1852 Urquiza defeated Rosas, who resigned and left for England, where he lived until his death in 1877. The victory ended a regime that had lasted more than two decades but did not decide how the provinces would be governed. Urquiza now faced Buenos Aires's elite and the question of a constitution, which the next division examines. Historians rely on military reports, diplomatic correspondence, and partisan memoirs, and debate how far Urquiza acted from principle or provincial interest.

Caseros depended on coalition politics more than on an isolated provincial rebellion. The Colorado government in Montevideo needed relief from the siege that had divided Uruguay; Brazilian authorities wanted a government across the frontier less hostile to their regional interests; and Entre Ríos sought freer river commerce. Urquiza's partnership connected these distinct aims without making them identical. Military mobilization required funding, recruitment, horses, and coordination across borders, exposing how closely Argentine constitutional disputes had become intertwined with Río de la Plata diplomacy.

The fall of Rosas was therefore an opening rather than a completed unification. His departure removed the actor who had held foreign representation for many provincial governments, but not the fiscal or institutional imbalance between Buenos Aires and the rest. Those who had supported Urquiza against Rosas could still reject his proposals for a representative national convention, especially if equal provincial representation diluted Buenos Aires's influence. The transition from military victory to constitutional settlement would expose the same problem in new form.

Historical Vocabulary
Pronunciamiento

A pronunciamiento was a public declaration, often by military or provincial leaders, announcing withdrawal of support from a government and demanding change. Urquiza's 1851 declaration against Rosas is a notable example.

It differs from a modern coup in that it was a recognized political practice that claimed legitimacy through public appeal and often ended in negotiation rather than seizure of power.

Division IV · Sections 62.15–62.18

Argentina's Constitutional Unification, 1852–1862

Section 62.15

62.15 The San Nicolás Agreement and the Constitution of 1853

After Caseros, Urquiza convened the provincial governors at San Nicolás de los Arroyos, where on 31 May 1852 they signed an agreement that named him provisional director of the Confederation, provided for a constituent congress with equal provincial representation, and confirmed existing treaties on river trade. Buenos Aires's legislature, dominated by liberals who disliked the equal representation and Urquiza's authority, refused to ratify it, and a revolt in the city on 11 September 1852 separated Buenos Aires from the rest. The constituent congress therefore met at Santa Fe without Buenos Aires.

Juan Bautista Alberdi's Bases (1852) shaped the debate. Writing from exile, he argued for a federal republic with a strong president, European immigration, and free trade and navigation, to populate and develop the country. The congress sanctioned the Constitution on 1 May 1853. It created a federal representative republic with a president chosen indirectly for six years, a bicameral congress with two senators per province, federal intervention in the provinces under specified conditions, and a federal judiciary. It declared that there were no slaves in the nation, protected freedom of worship while supporting the Catholic Church, promoted immigration, and guaranteed navigation of the rivers.

The text combined federal principles with a strong executive and reflected the interests of litoral and interior elites. Its provisions on frontiers and Indigenous peoples treated Indigenous nations as outside the constitutional community, a view that shaped later policy and is examined in Chapter 69. The Constitution was a political compromise as well as an ideological statement, and historians study the convention's debates, Alberdi's writings, and the text itself, noting that Buenos Aires's absence limited its legitimacy.

The absence of Buenos Aires was no minor procedural flaw. The province contained the country's principal customs house, the financial institutions most closely tied to foreign commerce, and a political class capable of raising its own armed forces. The Santa Fe delegates could write a constitution, but they could not compel the seceding port city to relinquish its fiscal autonomy. Urquiza's provincial allies viewed equal representation as a safeguard against the capital's demographic and commercial influence, whereas Buenos Aires liberals saw it as unfair to the region that could finance national institutions.

The constitution's commitments to navigation, immigration and the end of slavery expressed a transformative program, yet implementation required administrative reach. The provision that opened the interior rivers promised to weaken monopolies, but foreign vessels still needed ports, pilots, and political protection. Immigration clauses could encourage settlement without immediately supplying transport or title to land. Indigenous frontiers remained contested, and constitutional citizenship did not fully encompass peoples whose polities did not accept republican jurisdiction. The text established an enduring framework while leaving essential matters to subsequent political bargaining.

Section 62.16

62.16 Buenos Aires versus the Argentine Confederation

For nearly a decade Argentina had two governments. Buenos Aires ruled itself as a state, adopted its own constitution in 1854, and kept its port revenue. An attempt by Buenos Aires's rural federalists under Hilario Lagos to take the city by siege ended in 1853 when the city bought off his officers. The Confederation, with its capital at Paraná, elected Urquiza president in 1854 and was recognized by Britain, France, and the United States, with whom it signed treaties of free river navigation in July 1853.

The Confederation was institutionally real but fiscally weak. Customs duties, its main revenue, were small without Buenos Aires's port. It tried to divert trade to Rosario and the Paraná ports through differential tariffs introduced in the late 1850s, which raised some revenue but did not shift commerce. Its government paid salaries late, depended on loans, and relied on the loyalty of provincial governors. Alberdi served as its agent in Europe, seeking recognition and credit.

Buenos Aires, by contrast, prospered from commerce, wool exports, and a growing immigrant population, and its leaders insisted that a settlement respect its position. Both sides also appealed to the interior provinces, where governors weighed the benefits of association with a poor but legitimate national government against those of a wealthy but separate city. Historians use customs records, budgets, treaties, and press polemics, which frequently disagree on how much each side benefited from the division.

The conflict had an international dimension. Foreign merchants could obtain navigation privileges from the Confederation while continuing to conduct most trade through Buenos Aires, where warehousing, credit, and merchant networks were established. Tariff differentials sought to redistribute commerce, but traders calculated costs and commercial risk rather than responding solely to constitutional principles. The failure to displace the port city's commercial advantages increased pressure on the Confederation's treasury and undermined timely payment to officials and soldiers.

Buenos Aires also developed political institutions while standing outside the Confederation. Its 1854 constitution and provincial government expressed a distinct claim to sovereignty, while rural districts and city factions differed on how far reconciliation with Urquiza should go. Students should avoid depicting this decade as a complete suspension of national life on one side and a finished independent nation on the other. The two governments competed for recognition, commerce, military allies, and the loyalty of provincial officeholders, each claiming to speak for the future Argentina.

Section 62.17

62.17 Cepeda, the Pact of San José de Flores, and Constitutional Revision

Tension grew over intervention in provincial politics and over the status of Buenos Aires. In October 1859 the Confederation's army under Urquiza defeated Buenos Aires forces commanded by Bartolomé Mitre at Cepeda on 23 October. Urquiza did not enter the city; instead, with Francisco Solano López, Paraguay's war minister and the son of its president, acting as mediator, the parties signed the Pact of San José de Flores on 11 November 1859.

The pact provided for Buenos Aires to join the nation and to examine the Constitution through a provincial convention, with a national convention to consider any proposals. Buenos Aires's convention met in 1860 and proposed amendments. These stressed provincial autonomy, gave provincial constitutions a form of review rather than approval by Congress, and added an article protecting rights not listed in the text. A national convention at Santa Fe in September 1860 accepted the changes, and Buenos Aires joined the Confederation.

Reintegration was only formal. Urquiza's term ended in 1860, and Santiago Derqui became president, taking office in Paraná. Disputes over the seating of Buenos Aires's deputies, the revenue question, and federal intervention in San Juan, where a governor was killed and his challenger executed in January 1861, brought renewed conflict. Historians rely on the pact, convention records, and correspondence, and note that Solano López's mediation gave Paraguay a role in the Plata's politics that would matter later (Chapter 64).

The agreement at San José de Flores did not eliminate mistrust. Buenos Aires secured an opportunity to propose constitutional amendments, and the revised arrangements altered the balance of legal authority while leaving conflicting political ambitions unresolved. The two sides disagreed over representation, military command, and the political commitments of governors in the interior. Even when a treaty named procedures for reintegration, implementation depended on state and provincial leaders willing to accept their outcomes.

The battle of Cepeda in 1859 and the renewed conflict in 1861 illustrate why institutional history must be connected to force. A battlefield victory could compel negotiations, but it could not by itself make customs receipts flow to a shared treasury or establish trust in future elections. Periodic armed mobilization disrupted livestock production and commerce, while the prospect of new war made factions reluctant to disarm. An effective national government required both constitutional text and a coalition capable of enforcing compromises.

Section 62.18

62.18 Pavón, Bartolomé Mitre, and the Limits of Unification

The armies met again at Pavón on 17 September 1861. Urquiza withdrew his Entre Ríos cavalry from the field, though the battle's outcome was not clearly decided, and Mitre claimed victory. Historians still debate Urquiza's motives, from fear of a prolonged war to a calculated agreement with Buenos Aires. Mitre's forces then moved through the interior, replacing provincial governments hostile to Buenos Aires, and Derqui resigned in November 1861, leaving the Confederation without an executive.

Mitre, governor of Buenos Aires, exercised provisional national authority and called for elections. In October 1862 the provinces' electors chose him president, and he took office on 12 October. The central government now had a national executive, a congress, and the revenue of Buenos Aires's customs, a far stronger base than the Confederation's. The settlement was thus a political outcome of military and fiscal power, not simply the adoption of the 1853 Constitution, which had been accepted nine years earlier with amendments.

Unification remained limited. Provincial governments retained armies and local power, the capital question was unresolved because Buenos Aires city served as the seat of both provincial and national government, and large parts of the Pampas and Patagonia remained under Indigenous control, which the state would not challenge systematically until the 1870s and 1880s. The next chapters show how the national state grew through the Paraguayan War and the export economy. Sources include battle reports, election records, and political memoirs, which reflect partisan accounts of Urquiza's and Mitre's roles.

Mitre's assumption of the presidency in October 1862 marked a significant institutional settlement, but the designation of Argentina as 'unified' requires qualification. Buenos Aires's political and fiscal advantages shaped the new center, and provincial conflicts remained unresolved. Indigenous peoples continued to govern substantial frontier territories, and a national government could proclaim sovereignty without exercising consistent judicial or military power there. Treating 1862 as the endpoint of one phase of state formation is therefore more defensible than portraying it as the moment the entire national territory came uniformly under a modern state.

The debates of 1852–1862 also reshaped political thought. Alberdi and other advocates of a strong constitutional framework emphasized order, commerce and immigration, while rival leaders contested who would administer that framework and how provinces would protect their interests. The new constitution could accommodate these arguments only through future elections, legislation and political practice. The experience of Argentina thus links constitutional federalism to a social bargain that remained incomplete, setting up the later struggles over railroads, immigration and Indigenous dispossession assigned to subsequent chapters.

Division V · Sections 62.19–62.25

Chile: Constitutional Centralization and Consolidation, 1823–1861

Section 62.19

62.19 The Fall of O'Higgins and the Search for Republican Government

Bernardo O'Higgins, Supreme Director since February 1817, lost support by the early 1820s. Landowners and provincial leaders resented taxes and his attacks on entailed estates and aristocratic privileges, the Church disliked his reforms, and the treasury was burdened by the 1822 London loan and costly naval and military campaigns. After a Santiago assembly demanded his resignation and Ramón Freire's army moved from Concepción, O'Higgins resigned on 28 January 1823 and left for Peru.

Freire governed as Supreme Director until 1826. Juan Egaña's elaborate Constitution of 1823, which tried to regulate public morals through special institutions, proved unworkable and was suspended in 1824. Finances were a constant problem: in 1824 the government granted a tobacco monopoly to a company that included Diego Portales, who thereby entered public life. Freire completed the incorporation of Chiloé, Spain's last foothold in Chile, in January 1826 and then resigned.

O'Higgins's opponents did not all agree on what should replace his leadership. Provincial notables resented fiscal and military decisions imposed from Santiago, while some officers continued to defend a strong executive as essential to security. The transfer of authority to Ramón Freire opened negotiations among regional interests rather than ushering in a settled liberal republic. Constitutional initiatives during these years included Juan Egaña's elaborate 1823 charter, often called the 'moralist constitution,' which attempted to regulate public virtue and private conduct in ways critics regarded as impractical. Its limited life demonstrated how ambitious constitutional design could fail when it outran administrative capacity and political consent.

Financial stress made the search for new institutions urgent. War expenses had damaged the treasury, and the government depended on customs and loans while struggling to pay soldiers and employees. The northern mining region, the central valley, and Concepción did not receive equal benefits from decisions made in the capital. Elections and provincial assemblies became forums for disputes about participation and the Church's role in public authority. To explain Chile's later conservative settlement, the chapter should show first that it emerged from a decade of genuine institutional experimentation, not from an uninterrupted tradition of national obedience.

Early Chilean political factions were fluid; applying later party names to every participant obscures changing alliances. Religious commitments, professional ties, regional interests and views about military discipline crossed the categories of pipiolo and pelucon. Studying petitions, legislative debates and local newspapers helps explain how opposition was articulated. Many rural workers and Indigenous communities remained outside formal deliberations, although military demands and frontier negotiations affected their lives. The failure of individual constitutions must therefore be assessed alongside economic pressures, political rivalries and the practical limits of representation.

Section 62.20

62.20 Federal Experiments and the Constitution of 1828

During the governments of Ramón Freire and Manuel Blanco Encalada, José Miguel Infante promoted federal measures in 1826; Francisco Antonio Pinto later led the 1828 constitutional process. Laws created provincial assemblies and a system of provincial administration, but provinces lacked revenue and trained officials, and the experiment collapsed within a year. Critics blamed federalism for disorder and conservatives used its failure as an argument for centralization.

The Constitution of 8 August 1828, drafted with the help of the Spanish liberal José Joaquín de Mora, established a unitary but liberal system with provincial assemblies, broad rights, a limited executive, and indirect elections. It reduced the Church's privileges less than conservatives feared but enough to provoke opposition. The weak presidency and the right of Congress to choose the vice president when no candidate won a majority produced a crisis in 1829, when disputed congressional choices gave the opposition a pretext for revolt.

The federal measures of 1826 were associated especially with José Miguel Infante and the attempt to shift power to provincial institutions. Supporters believed local assemblies could respond better to regional needs than officials appointed from Santiago. Opponents worried that the provinces lacked money and administrators to support a workable distribution of powers. The problem was sharper because the central government still had to pay military expenses and service obligations contracted during independence. A constitutional experiment could allocate responsibility for collecting taxes without creating a fair and enforceable way to share them.

The 1828 charter attempted a more durable republican settlement. Its language of representative government was comparatively liberal for the region, but suffrage, municipal authority and Church relations remained contested. The real test came from elections and the contested interpretation of succession, not merely the constitution's written clauses. Political competitors disputed whether the administration had complied with constitutional rules and whether the elected institutions represented provincial opinion. A framework that required losers to recognize an adverse result became vulnerable when military commanders had the means to reject it.

The lesson is not that liberal constitutions were inherently unworkable. They depended on political coalitions, credible procedures, a functioning treasury and agreement over the army. Chile's subsequent return to a powerful executive represented a particular resolution of these conflicts, not a neutral correction imposed by geography. Compare the language of the 1828 charter with the 1833 constitution while tracing the experiences of provincial councils and ordinary voters; the contrast becomes clearer when constitutional text is connected to political practice.

Section 62.21

62.21 The Civil War of 1829–1830 and the Battle of Lircay

In late 1829 conservative officers led by Joaquín Prieto rose in Concepción against the liberal government. The indecisive battle of Ochagavía in December 1829 led to an agreement but not to peace, and the conservatives, joined by supporters of O'Higgins and the group around Diego Portales, regrouped under Prieto and Manuel Bulnes.

The decisive battle took place at Lircay on 17 April 1830, when the conservative army defeated liberal forces led by Freire. The victors dismissed or exiled many liberal officers and officials, and Freire went into exile. Portales joined the new government as minister, first of the interior and foreign affairs and later of war and the navy, and became its leading organizer.

The civil war was rooted in the controversy surrounding the presidential and vice-presidential selections of 1829. The appointment of the vice president became a focal point for conservatives who argued that the constitutional order had been manipulated. General Joaquín Prieto's revolt was supported by an alliance of conservative landowners, politicians and military officers, while forces associated with Ramón Freire and liberal constitutionalism resisted. The alignment was neither an uncomplicated confrontation between civilians and soldiers nor a struggle between two timeless parties. Each side claimed legitimacy through a different reading of the republic's institutions.

The fighting culminated at Lircay on April 17, 1830, near Talca. Prieto's victory gave conservatives the force needed to redesign government, but it also carried costs: defeated officers faced removal, political careers were interrupted, and opponents were driven toward exile or accommodation. These measures mattered because a future constitution could operate only after the victors had altered the composition of the armed forces and administrative elite. The repression underlying conservative consolidation should stand beside the later history of fiscal regularity and political continuity.

Regional memory of the war also shaped subsequent government. Provincial leaders who had defended their autonomy could accept central authority in exchange for predictable administration and a share of public resources, while others retained grievances that reappeared in 1851 and 1859. Contemporary partisan newspapers often portrayed opponents as enemies of order or liberty, leaving historians to reconstruct intermediate positions through correspondence, military reports and local records. Chile's stability was constructed through victory and subsequent negotiations rather than produced automatically by the battle alone.

Section 62.22

62.22 Diego Portales and the Constitution of 1833

Diego Portales, a Valparaíso merchant, did not draft the Constitution but shaped its spirit. He argued for a strong centralized government that would impose order, defend property, and enforce obedience, and he used extraordinary powers, censorship, and military discipline against opponents. A convention of 1831–1833, with jurists such as Mariano Egaña and Manuel José Gandarillas, drafted the text, which was promulgated on 25 May 1833.

The Constitution created a unitary republic with a president elected indirectly for five years and eligible for one immediate reelection. The president appointed intendants and governors, controlled the administration, and could use emergency powers. Congress consisted of a Senate and Chamber of Deputies elected under restrictive suffrage requirements of property and literacy; Catholicism was the only public religion; and entailed estates were allowed to continue.

Portales's political significance lay less in drafting individual constitutional articles than in building a governing coalition committed to executive authority, administrative discipline and distrust of uncontrolled factional competition. The legal text was shaped by a constitutional convention and jurists including Mariano Egaña and Manuel José Gandarillas. It provided a presidential system with substantial powers, institutional safeguards for the established Church and restrictive qualifications for political participation. The Senate, executive appointments and extraordinary powers were designed to make government less dependent on rapidly shifting legislative alliances.

A constitutional text did not itself generate obedience. The new system drew on control of military appointments, provincial governors, public accounts and political elections. Critics saw censorship and emergency measures as evidence that the regime restricted republican liberty; supporters believed a stronger center prevented a return to the conflicts of the 1820s. Their dispute should be reconstructed from actual mechanisms: who appointed local officials, which bodies could call elections, when the executive could exercise emergency authority, and how political opposition could legally organize. Describing the system simply as 'order' risks concealing these institutional choices.

Portales was assassinated by mutinous soldiers at Quillota in June 1837 amid tensions concerning the Peru–Bolivian Confederation; his personal leadership therefore cannot explain the subsequent three decades of conservative predominance on its own. Successive presidents, ministers, legislators and provincial elites adapted the 1833 framework and occasionally altered its application. That durability is an important subject for comparison with Argentina, where constitutional drafting could not immediately settle the question of the national center. The 1833 constitution survived partly because it combined central authority with regular succession and an administrative network able to enforce decisions, even while excluding large portions of the population.

Section 62.23

62.23 Conservative Governments, Administration, and National Authority

Joaquín Prieto (1831–1841), Manuel Bulnes (1841–1851), and Manuel Montt (1851–1861) each served two terms. Prieto's government consolidated the army, courts, and treasury. Bulnes, a general who won the war against the Confederation, governed more conciliatorily and amnestied some exiles, and Montt, a civilian lawyer, strengthened administrative and educational institutions while maintaining executive dominance.

The war with the Peru–Bolivian Confederation, declared in December 1836, ended with Bulnes's victory at Yungay on 20 January 1839. It strengthened national feeling and the army's prestige. The government's revenues came from customs, the tobacco monopoly, and taxes on mining, and it kept a small but loyal military. In 1843 Chile claimed the Strait of Magellan by founding a fort. The War of the Pacific belongs to Chapter 65.

The ten-year presidencies that followed were formally separated by elections, but the governing coalition's dominance and its influence over the electoral system made alternation limited. Prieto's administration benefited from the restoration of state finances and a political settlement after civil war. Under Bulnes, the expansion of educational and cultural institutions helped establish Santiago as a national intellectual center. Montt then confronted more organized Liberal and provincial opposition despite the continuing strength of central government. The sequence matters because administrative continuity did not eliminate ideological disagreement or regional rivalry.

The 1836–1839 conflict with the Peru–Bolivian Confederation revealed how external security could reinforce domestic legitimacy. The victory at Yungay was celebrated as a national achievement, and the war contributed to military prestige, but it also demanded fiscal contributions and imposed costs on recruits and their households. The earlier independence wars had created a tradition of military leadership; conservative governments sought to place that institution under a predictable executive chain of command. Success depended on continued cooperation between officers and politicians rather than an abstract national culture of discipline.

Chile's state revenue rested on customs duties, mining taxes and later growing commerce. The connection between revenue and administration permitted investment in schools, roads and offices but did not distribute benefits equally. Public education reached towns and some rural districts unevenly, while Indigenous communities south of the Biobío remained outside routine government. Thus a national bureaucracy could grow stronger while the practical territory it governed remained bounded. The political tensions of the 1850s demonstrate why state consolidation must be distinguished from the absence of dissent.

Section 62.24

62.24 Mining, Valparaíso, Education, Society, and the Indigenous Frontier

The discovery of the Chañarcillo silver mine in 1832 and expanding copper and, later, coal mining brought wealth to the north and Valparaíso, which served as a major port for Pacific trade. Mining created a class of entrepreneurs and a mobile labor force of miners, and the wealth they generated funded the state and the commercial elite. Mining wealth gave Chile a fiscal basis that other new states lacked.

The government invested in education. Andrés Bello, a Venezuelan scholar, became the first rector of the University of Chile when it opened in 1843 and drafted the Civil Code, completed in 1855. A normal school opened in 1842, with Argentine exile Domingo Sarmiento among its supporters. Most rural people remained outside formal schooling. On haciendas, tenant laborers (inquilinos) worked in exchange for land and obligations to the owner, and a mobile laboring population (peones) lived on the margins. German settlers were encouraged to colonize the area around Valdivia from the 1840s.

The discovery of rich silver deposits at Chañarcillo in 1832 strengthened the northern mining economy and enriched a class of entrepreneurs, merchants and wage earners whose interests were not identical to those of the Santiago landowning elite. Copper production and Pacific commerce linked Chile to international markets, while Valparaíso supplied shipping services, warehouses and financial networks. These changes enlarged the state's fiscal options, but mining districts also supplied resources and grievances for opposition movements. The emergence of wealthy provincial groups did not automatically ensure their political influence in a strongly centralized system.

Educational institutions promoted an official image of a cultured and orderly republic. Andrés Bello contributed to the University of Chile, founded in 1842, and later to the civil code of 1855; literary circles, newspapers and scientific surveys helped produce a language of national progress. Such developments were real, yet schooling and legal participation remained stratified. Women played substantial roles in households, charitable work, commerce and intellectual life without acquiring equal political rights, while rural tenant laborers faced obligations rooted in estate relations. An account restricted to celebrated institutions would miss the social order that financed and constrained them.

The southern frontier exposes another limit. Mapuche communities continued to exercise political autonomy across important territories beyond the routine reach of Chilean officials; trade and diplomacy crossed the Biobío, and local commanders negotiated or fought according to changing circumstances. A map showing these lands within a modern national border should not be treated as evidence of effective state control in the 1840s. Later large-scale occupation falls outside this chapter, but its antecedents illuminate the tension between a government claiming indivisible sovereignty and the continued existence of Indigenous political nations.

Section 62.25

62.25 Liberal Opposition, the Revolts of 1851 and 1859, and the Transition of 1861

Opposition to the conservative order grew in the 1850s. The Society of Equality, founded in 1850 by Francisco Bilbao and Santiago Arcos, attracted artisans and young liberals and was suppressed. Montt's election in 1851 triggered a revolt led by Concepción's General José María de la Cruz, which Bulnes defeated at Loncomilla in December 1851.

After a period of conflict with the Church and a fusion of liberals and conservatives in 1857, a second revolt in 1859 began in the north under the silver miner Pedro León Gallo and was defeated in the spring. Both revolts show that Chile's stability rested on repression of opposition and on divisions among its challengers.

The 1851 revolt followed a contested political succession and drew support from those who believed Montt's elevation perpetuated the conservative monopoly. Fighting occurred in different parts of the country, including a major struggle in the south, and the state's eventual victory relied on organized troops and local allies. By the late 1850s, opposition also grew in northern mining districts, where regional entrepreneurs and residents objected to appointments and revenue arrangements made from Santiago. The 1859 revolt reflected these specific provincial grievances alongside liberal constitutional demands; it cannot be explained solely as a repeat of party conflict.

The 1861 succession to José Joaquín Pérez was important because a broader governing coalition sought to reduce political polarization without immediately dismantling the 1833 framework. Political clubs, newspapers and provincial networks continued to contest executive power, but government could be transferred without the same degree of confrontation as in earlier crises. This transition illustrates institutional change through negotiation, not the disappearance of social inequality or coercive authority. Chile's next decades would bring constitutional reforms, territorial wars and expanded export interests, reserved for later chapters; the question here is how a powerful state learned to manage a wider set of political participants.

Comparing the revolts of 1851 and 1859 with the founding civil war exposes the limits of any simple 'stable Chile' narrative. Regular presidential succession coexisted with repeated armed opposition, and national integration could deepen while grievances over regional fiscal control sharpened. Military reports, legislative debates, contemporary newspapers and provincial correspondence provide evidence for these competing interpretations. The sections should connect the changing claims of political actors to the administrative institutions they sought either to control or to reform.

Division VI · Sections 62.26–62.31

Uruguay: Independence, Civil War, and Reconstruction, 1825–1860

Section 62.26

62.26 The Banda Oriental, Artigas's Legacy, and Brazilian Rule

The Banda Oriental, the territory east of the Uruguay River, was a ranching frontier with its own port at Montevideo, a rural population that included Indigenous Charrúa communities, free Afro-descended and mestizo workers, and enslaved people. Under Spanish rule it was disputed between Spain and Portugal, and after 1810 it became a battleground between Buenos Aires, Artigas's federal movement, and Portuguese-Brazilian armies. Artigas's defeat left a rural political culture shaped by his program of provincial sovereignty and land distribution, even though his movement did not survive.

Portuguese-Brazilian forces took Montevideo in January 1817 and in 1821 a congress in Montevideo, assembled under General Carlos Lecor, voted to incorporate the territory into the Portuguese-Brazilian realm as the Cisplatine Province. After Brazil's independence in 1822 the province became part of the Brazilian Empire, a subject examined in Chapter 61. Local elites were divided: some Montevideo merchants and landowners accepted incorporation as a guarantee of order, while rural leaders such as Fructuoso Rivera served the new rulers before turning against them.

The Banda Oriental's strategic position gave it importance beyond its size. It commanded access along the eastern bank of the Río de la Plata and contained ranching territories connected to merchants in Montevideo and across the frontier. Artigas's followers had promoted a federal association of provincial communities, but Portuguese intervention transformed their political options. Some landowners regarded a Portuguese-Brazilian authority as protection from warfare and commercial disruption; others resisted foreign occupation or sought closer relations with Buenos Aires. The population thus did not act as a single bloc pursuing an inevitable future nation-state.

The 1821 incorporation as Cisplatina had a juridical form, but the circumstances of occupation and the limited representation of different local communities made its legitimacy contentious. Officials attempted to integrate customs administration and military garrisons into the Portuguese and later Brazilian system while confronting ranching networks that crossed provincial borders. Independence movements drew on veterans, local property owners, and military support from across the river. What later became Uruguay therefore emerged from intersecting local, Argentine, Portuguese and Brazilian projects. A chapter centered exclusively on mediation by foreign diplomats would obscure the political agency of people within the Eastern Province.

Artigas's legacy remained important but not uniform. His agrarian claims appealed to poor rural people, while later elite politicians could celebrate his defense of autonomy without embracing redistribution. Indigenous and Afro-descended groups had participated in earlier conflicts and faced changing opportunities and dangers under new rulers. Comparing these constituencies with provincial decision-makers helps explain why the legitimacy of incorporation and separation remained disputed after military campaigns formally ended.

Section 62.27

62.27 The Eastern Campaign, the 1828 Peace Settlement, and the Constitution of 1830

On 19 April 1825 a group of exiles under Juan Antonio Lavalleja, later known as the Thirty-Three Orientals, landed in the province and began an uprising. A provincial assembly met at La Florida and declared the province's separation from Brazil and union with the Argentine provinces in August 1825, and the victory of Sarandí in October broadened the rebellion. Rivera joined the insurgents. The war between Argentina and Brazil that followed is treated in §62.9 and Chapter 61. In July 1825 the same legislature had passed a law freeing children born to enslaved mothers.

The Preliminary Peace Convention of 27 August 1828, brokered by Britain, whose representative Lord Ponsonby pressed both sides, recognized an independent Eastern state, neither Argentine nor Brazilian, with a limited period of guarantee by the two powers. A constituent assembly drafted a constitution, sworn on 18 July 1830, which established a unitary republic with a president chosen by the legislature, a two-chamber congress, Catholicism as the state religion, and restricted citizenship that excluded servants, day laborers, and common soldiers and, later, illiterate people. Rivera became the first constitutional president.

The military and diplomatic settlement of 1828 did not guarantee a settled republic. The convention provided for the emergence of a separate Eastern state, but creating its governmental institutions demanded elections, constitutional deliberation, recognition, and the demobilization of troops previously loyal to rival commanders. The constitution was sanctioned in 1830 and publicly sworn on July 18; that institutional sequence should be distinguished from the earlier treaty between Argentina and Brazil. Even a formal commitment to external independence could leave questions about citizenship, provincial administration and executive powers unresolved.

The 1830 charter established a representative republic with a strong executive and a bicameral legislature, but political participation was restricted by exclusions associated with age, literacy deadlines, occupation and forms of dependence. Its provisions cannot be treated as evidence that all residents possessed effective citizenship. In particular, the constitution's original text identified conditions under which rights of citizenship were suspended, including categories of wage labor and military service. These distinctions reflected a political order that expected propertied and independent men to dominate representative institutions while relying on other groups for military service and production.

The first governments had to operate in a country where armed commanders possessed independent followings and landholdings were vulnerable to recurrent conflict. Montevideo's institutions and port commerce stood beside a vast rural area in which local alliances could shift quickly. The new republic's survival depended on political bargains that proved difficult to sustain and on the intentions of more powerful neighbors. These circumstances help explain why independent Uruguay rapidly developed organized rival factions rather than a durable, unified governing party.

Section 62.28

62.28 Rivera, Oribe, Political Factions, and Frontier Society

Fructuoso Rivera (president 1830–1834) and Manuel Oribe (1835–1838) were former comrades who became rivals. Both had followings among ranchers and rural militias bound by patronage, and their competition in 1836 produced a civil war. The names Blancos and Colorados derive from the badges worn by their forces in that conflict, and the two groupings became lasting political traditions, shaped by loyalty and kinship as much as ideology. Oribe resigned in 1838 under pressure, and Rivera returned to power.

On 11 April 1831 Rivera's forces attacked Charrúa communities at Salsipuedes, killing many and capturing others, an action aimed at ending Charrúa resistance and securing ranching lands. Several survivors were dispersed among Uruguayan households as servants, and in 1833 four Charrúa people were taken to France. Estimates of the dead vary, and the episode has been interpreted as a decisive blow against Charrúa autonomy; some scholars stress that Charrúa people and communities survived through mixing and dispersal, a subject of ongoing historical research.

Rivera and Oribe assembled different political networks, but the later party labels should not be read backward as already fixed ideologies in the early 1830s. The Colorado and Blanco identities crystallized through rivalry, military mobilization and symbolic association, with connections to ranching districts, urban commercial interests and neighboring political factions. Each leader could appeal to a language of national order while seeking support abroad. The conflict was therefore not reducible to one side defending independence and the other rejecting it; both claimed legitimate government and the right to mobilize the country's resources.

The campaign against Charrúa groups in 1831 must be analyzed as a central component of rural state-building. At Salsipuedes and through subsequent repression, state forces attacked communities whose control of land and mobility conflicted with the emerging order of ranching property and national policing. Survivors faced captivity, displacement and separation of families. The destruction of organized resistance should not be presented as the natural disappearance of an Indigenous people. Their descendants and communities continued to contest the historical erasure produced by national narratives.

Agriculture and cattle raising linked political allegiance to everyday livelihoods. Rural employers could supply horses, arms and food, while workers were exposed to conscription and raiding regardless of their personal commitments. Women provisioned households during prolonged absence of male relatives and faced the economic consequences of confiscation and debt. Records of property claims, army recruitment and petitions reveal how government competition extended into the countryside. Thus Uruguay's early party divisions were grounded not only in presidential ambition but in contested social and territorial authority.

Section 62.29

62.29 The Guerra Grande and the Siege of Montevideo, 1839–1851

Montevideo's Defense government, headed by Joaquín Suárez for much of the siege, had to maintain civil offices and military supplies in a city surrounded by its opponents. Public revenue, foreign merchant credit and port access kept the government functioning, but refugees, shortages and competing military obligations strained urban life. Outside the city, Oribe's Cerrito administration claimed national legitimacy and maintained civil and military institutions over extensive rural territory. The two governments thus represented rival assertions of sovereignty with different administrative resources, not simply an ordinary opposition movement confronting an undisputed capital.

The siege lasted until 1851, one of the longest in the Americas. Montevideo survived by sea, with the support of French and British naval forces and a force of foreign volunteers that included Giuseppe Garibaldi's Italian Legion, a French Legion, and Basque units. Oribe's government controlled most of the countryside. The war devastated ranching, disrupted trade, and forced rural families to flee or to serve armies. Anglo-French intervention in the river from 1845 added a diplomatic dimension.

The Guerra Grande began through conflicts that extended beyond Uruguay. Rivera's alliance with Argentine opponents of Rosas and Oribe's association with the Argentine Confederation made internal rivalry a regional war. After 1843 the Defense government held Montevideo while Oribe's forces controlled extensive areas of the countryside and besieged the city. These were rival administrations rather than one government and a band of irregular rebels: each sought legitimacy, collected resources and recruited allies. The long conflict therefore posed the question of which institutions could represent the republic as much as it tested military strength.

Montevideo's position as an Atlantic port allowed the Defense government to obtain supplies and mobilize foreign support, including immigrant volunteers. French, British and Italian participants were visible in the city's military and political life, but foreign assistance never erased the agency of local factions. Oribe's rural base depended on ranching networks, troops and support from Rosas. The countryside experienced destruction of herds, disrupted trade and forced movement of populations. The difference between surviving in a fortified port and governing agricultural districts should be explicit in any explanation of the war's duration.

International diplomacy altered the balance of power as Britain and France blockaded the Río de la Plata, and later Brazilian and Argentine provincial alliances changed the military situation. The defeat of Rosas in 1852 followed the end of Uruguay's siege and reflected these connected regional conflicts. Treating the settlement of 1851 merely as internal reconciliation would therefore obscure how the country's fate was linked to decisions in Buenos Aires, Entre Ríos and Rio de Janeiro.

Section 62.30

62.30 Foreign Intervention, Immigration, Slavery, and Urban Political Life

Foreign powers had interests in the river and the port. Britain and France sought open navigation and protection of their nationals and merchants, Brazil wanted security on its southern border and influence over frontier landowners, and Rosas sought to remove a haven for his opponents. Brazilian settlers owned ranches and enslaved people in northern Uruguay, which tied Brazilian policy to the war.

Recruitment introduced uneven processes of documenting freedom. Officers needed to establish who could be enlisted, whether former owners could demand compensation, and what military obligations followed a change in legal status. Because the Defense and Cerrito authorities held different districts, emancipation did not operate through a single national registry. Enlistment papers, freedom claims and decrees must be compared to distinguish formal changes in status from continued wartime coercion. Some Afro-Uruguayan families gained freedom while confronting military danger, loss of income and restricted access to land.

War created a distinctive route from slavery toward emancipation. Both rival Uruguayan governments needed soldiers, and military recruitment drove legislative action that combined formal freedom with new compulsory obligations. The Defense government's measure of December 1842 and Oribe's Cerrito government's measure of October 1846 belonged to different jurisdictions within a divided country. Their laws should be treated as separate legal acts, with attention to who could claim freedom, the demands placed on liberated recruits and the difficulty of implementing rights outside zones of actual control.

Montevideo's immigrant population complicated traditional class and racial divisions. Merchants, artisans and laborers from Europe and the wider Atlantic world worked alongside free and formerly enslaved Afro-Uruguayans, who organized mutual aid, family networks and sometimes military units. Foreign legions could offer the Defense government manpower, but their presence generated disputes about political allegiance and payment. Urban livelihoods depended on port access and wartime provisioning; when shipping was disrupted, food and rent became immediate concerns. The experiences of enslaved families and immigrant workers cannot be inferred solely from speeches by military leaders.

Land in the northern departments also drew Brazilian ranchers and merchants into Uruguayan politics. Cross-border livestock, property claims and security disputes gave the Empire of Brazil a lasting interest in Uruguayan affairs, while British and French officials pursued commercial and diplomatic objectives of their own. Foreign intervention may have helped preserve the port government's position, yet it also narrowed the autonomy of local leaders. This is one reason Uruguay's republican consolidation remained fragile after the siege ended.

Section 62.31

62.31 The Settlement of 1851 and the Continuing Search for Political Order

The war ended in October 1851 after Urquiza's alliance with Brazil and the Colorados deprived Oribe of Argentine support. A peace agreement of 8 October 1851 declared that there were neither victors nor vanquished, and the parties were to form a government of fusion. Brazil imposed a series of treaties in the same month on Uruguay's government regulating borders, extradition, and financial aid, an influence reviewed in Chapter 61.

Fusion did not hold. President Juan Francisco Giró, of Blanco origin, was overthrown in 1853 and replaced by a triumvirate of Rivera, Lavalleja, and Venancio Flores. Rivera and Lavalleja both died in 1853–1854, leaving Flores, a Colorado, as dominant. Later governments of Gabriel Antonio Pereira and Bernardo Berro faced revolts, including a violent suppression of a Colorado uprising at Quinteros in 1858.

The end of the long war did not produce a triumphant monopoly for one party. A formula sometimes remembered as 'no victors, no vanquished' sought to reduce retaliation and permit reconstruction, but commanders and regional clients retained influence. State revenues were small, public debts had grown, and the restoration of rural production required security for herds and roads. Rival constitutional claims could be reconciled on paper more readily than competing armed networks could be dissolved. Efforts to govern through 'fusion' of factions therefore met practical limits.

The postwar republic faced decisions about citizenship, the status of veterans, foreign property and international obligations. Formerly enslaved people confronted unequal access to land and employment, while immigration continued to alter Montevideo's social composition. Indigenous survivors and descendants remained excluded from many official histories. The state's policy toward the rural frontier reflected pressure from ranchers seeking predictability, not an inevitable progression toward universal citizenship. Studying legal petitions and records of land transactions clarifies whose claims the government was prepared to enforce.

Uruguay's independence survived because local political agency operated within regional diplomatic constraints. Its leaders negotiated with Brazil, Argentina and commercial powers, and its port provided a bargaining resource, but military factionalism repeatedly reopened disputes about government. The important comparative question is therefore not whether the country was a 'buffer,' but how a small sovereign republic attempted to exercise authority amid stronger neighbors and highly mobilized domestic factions.

Division VII · Sections 62.32–62.37

Paraguay: Authoritarian State Formation and Economic Sovereignty, 1811–1862

Section 62.32

62.32 Independence from Spain and Resistance to Buenos Aires

Paraguay was a province of the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata, isolated upriver, with a population largely of Guaraní-speaking rural families, a small Spanish-descended elite, and a political tradition of local militias. In 1811 a Buenos Aires force under Manuel Belgrano invaded to bring the province into the revolutionary movement and was repulsed at Paraguarí in January and Tacuarí in March. The Paraguayans' resistance was not loyalty to Spain so much as rejection of Buenos Aires's claim to rule them.

On 14–15 May 1811 officers and civilians, among them Fulgencio Yegros, Pedro Juan Caballero, and the lawyer José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia, removed Governor Bernardo de Velasco. A junta took power, and in October 1811 Paraguay reached an agreement with Buenos Aires that recognized its autonomy. A congress in 1813 proclaimed a republic and chose two consuls, Yegros and Francia, an arrangement that gave Paraguay institutions of its own.

The movement of 1811 took shape against the attempted direction of Buenos Aires and the legacy of Spanish colonial authority. A campaign sent under Manuel Belgrano failed to subdue Paraguay, demonstrating the difficulty of treating the old viceregal territories as naturally subordinate to the former capital. In Asunción, officers, merchants and rural interests were divided about their future, yet many rejected incorporation into a larger centralized state. The governments created in 1811 and 1813 should therefore be read as evolving institutions rather than one immediate announcement of a final constitutional order.

Francia's growing authority rested on arguments that Paraguay needed protection against foreign interference and against the ambitions of local grandees. Congresses and assemblies established executive arrangements and eventually concentrated power in his hands, making the route to dictatorship partly constitutional in form. The question for historians is how far those institutions represented consent and how far they were constrained by military power, surveillance and control of communication. Political independence was durable, but the range of people able to dispute government policy narrowed markedly.

Paraguay's population, language, and commercial geography also conditioned its politics. Guaraní was widely spoken in rural households and markets, while Spanish remained important in written administration. The Paraná and Paraguay rivers were transport arteries but exposed the republic to external control over downstream navigation. State-building in Asunción therefore involved the management of trade permits, river access, and frontier security alongside constitutions. The country was not outside the region; it pursued a distinctive policy in response to the region's political competition.

Section 62.33

62.33 Francia and the Construction of Personal Government, 1814–1840

In 1814 a congress named Francia dictator for five years, and in 1816 another congress made him perpetual dictator, with the title El Supremo. He ruled until his death in September 1840. His government relied on a small administrative staff, control of the militia, and surveillance of opponents. A conspiracy in 1820–1821, real or alleged, led to the arrest and execution of former colleagues, including Yegros, and Caballero died in prison.

Francia attacked the old Spanish-descended elite. A decree restricted marriages of people of Spanish birth to non-Spaniards, which reduced the elite's cohesion, and merchants and landowners faced confiscation or restriction. Political opposition was suppressed through imprisonment and exile, and Congress was seldom convened. His army and militia were small but loyal, and the state's authority reached into villages through appointed officials.

Francia's authority was concentrated through control over the armed forces, confiscation or appropriation of certain properties, and restrictions on the political influence of prominent families. Some foreigners faced significant limits on movement and commerce; traders needed official permission, and external diplomacy was narrowly managed. The state could thereby reduce dependency on neighboring governments, but it also limited the opportunities of merchants who depended on imported goods and export markets. Independence under these conditions was not equivalent to a free society. The ability to prevent provincial challenge rested on severe constraints on public opposition.

The relationship between the state and religion was similarly transformative. Francia reduced the power of ecclesiastical authorities and redirected Church property and institutions toward the needs of government. These actions cannot be summarized solely as secular modernization or as persecution, since they changed land ownership, clerical livelihoods, and the distribution of local authority. Rural communities continued religious observance and village practice even as their relationships with the hierarchy and central administration changed. Evidence from official decrees must be read against parish and local economic records wherever these survive.

Scholars have debated whether relative isolation protected small farmers and limited elite concentration or instead imposed the costs of political closure. Both propositions require qualification by time, region and social group. State farms and controlled markets could provide resources to the government, while households still participated in trade and credit arrangements. The distinction between internal economic activity and foreign commerce is crucial: a country with stringent controls on exports and imports did not thereby cease producing, exchanging or accumulating wealth.

Section 62.34

62.34 Land, Trade, the Church, and Rural Society under Francia

The state took over lands held by the Church and by exiled or condemned owners and organized them as state estates, some of them cattle ranches known as estancias de la patria. Smallholders were allowed to lease state land at modest rents, which gave many rural families access to land and food. Paraguay's agriculture, based on maize, manioc, cattle, and yerba mate, supplied local needs.

Foreign trade was severely limited. Only a few border points, at Itapúa and Pilar, were open for controlled exchange, and travelers were closely watched; the French botanist Aimé Bonpland was held in Paraguay for nearly a decade after 1821. The Church was brought under state control: monasteries were suppressed in 1824, the seminary closed, and clergy became state employees.

State landholding expanded through confiscations, purchases and the redirection of property previously held by corporations or individuals. Some families obtained use of land under leases or other arrangements that provided access while preserving the government's ownership. Such policies affected the political balance between large landowners, local farmers and officials who administered rentals and production. They did not abolish economic hierarchy or establish a uniform class of independent cultivators. The terms of occupancy, rents, livestock obligations and official discretion determined whether landholders could make durable improvements or accumulate wealth.

Restrictions on foreign trade also affected the production of yerba mate, tobacco, hides and other goods. The administration tried to prevent neighboring powers and commercial houses from controlling Paraguay's revenue, yet the state's limited outlets could depress prices received by producers. For families in rural communities, obligations to furnish labor or animals and the availability of local markets were often more immediate than constitutional rhetoric about sovereignty. Women participated in agriculture, weaving, petty trade and household management, economic roles easy to understate in official government correspondence.

Guaraní-speaking communities retained cultural and local organizational practices, but their legal position changed as the state increasingly asserted authority over communal institutions. Distinguish colonial town structures, the arrangements under Francia and the more decisive reforms of Carlos Antonio López after 1844. Historians should not assign all later dispossession to Francia or imply that a centralized government respected Indigenous property simply because it limited private land concentration. These policies require attention to the specific decrees and their consequences.

Section 62.35

62.35 Political Transition after Francia and the Reaffirmation of Independence

After Francia's death in 1840, short-lived juntas and military leaders governed. In early 1841 the Congress named two consuls, Carlos Antonio López and Mariano Roque Alonso, and López quickly became the dominant figure. The consuls reopened some trade and released political prisoners.

On 25 November 1842 a congress formally declared Paraguay independent. The declaration responded to Argentina's continued claim, under Rosas, that Paraguay was a province of the Confederation, and sought recognition from other powers. The same congress passed a law on the children of enslaved women.

The transition after Francia's death exposed the importance and limitations of a state organized around one ruler. Officials and officers had to determine who could authorize expenditures, command troops and negotiate with neighboring governments when the dictator's personal instructions ended. Several provisional combinations of authority were tried before the 1841 consulate brought Carlos Antonio López and Mariano Roque Alonso to the forefront. The process was neither complete anarchy nor an immediate democratic transition. It showed that administrative institutions existed but that succession procedures had been deliberately weak.

The 1842 reaffirmation of independence was designed to communicate Paraguay's sovereignty beyond its own territory. Buenos Aires had continued to claim influence over the region and control access to the sea; officials in Asunción wanted neighbors and foreign governments to acknowledge an independent republic. Declarations, diplomatic letters and proposed river agreements thus mattered as tools of state formation. Recognition could support trade and security without itself resolving frontier claims or ensuring free navigation.

The new leadership also began to reconsider how narrowly external commerce and technical exchange should be controlled. Maintaining autonomy remained important, but government could seek foreign machinery, technicians and armaments for its own purposes. This policy was especially visible after Carlos Antonio López assumed the presidency in 1844. The transition therefore connected the inheritance of Francia's coercive state to a different program of controlled modernization. It also required decisions about who could vote in occasional congresses, how offices would be filled, and whether government borrowing or foreign commerce could be supervised. These questions were decided within a narrow political elite rather than through a competitive electoral process. The resulting continuity of administrative capacity should not obscure how little independent space remained for popular opposition or organized criticism.

Section 62.36

62.36 Carlos Antonio López and the State after 1844

In March 1844 a congress adopted a law organizing the government and elected López president for a ten-year term. The law gave him broad powers and provided for the congress to meet rarely. López was reelected in 1854 and governed until his death in 1862, building a state that was more open to trade and technology than Francia's, but just as firmly controlled.

His government promoted schools, founded an iron foundry and arsenal at Ybycuí, built shipyards in Asunción, and began railway construction with the help of foreign engineers; the telegraph line, in contrast, was ordered in 1864 by his son Francisco Solano López after his father's death. The state held large estates and monopolies over export crops such as yerba mate and tobacco, and it dissolved Indigenous communal pueblos in 1848, turning Indigenous people into state subjects with the same obligations as other peasants.

The 1844 Law Establishing the Political Administration of the Republic placed the presidency at the center of government, with comparatively weak constitutional checks on executive discretion. Carlos Antonio López used that authority to enlarge schools, workshops, state-managed production and military capacity, often with foreign technical assistance. The ironworks at Ybycuí, developed in the 1850s, and improvements to the steamship and railway sectors became symbols of modernization. The first stage of railway operation occurred near the end of his presidency. These initiatives should be dated carefully and not confused with the telegraph developments associated with Francisco Solano López after 1862.

Administrative achievement coexisted with coercive labor arrangements, the persistence of slavery despite an 1842 measure addressing children born to enslaved mothers, and significant interference in communal rights. A decree of October 7, 1848 declared Indigenous residents of twenty-one former towns citizens, but simultaneously transferred their collective property and rights to the state. The recognition of citizenship therefore cannot be celebrated apart from the loss of corporate landholding and the unequal practical ability of affected communities to exercise the rights associated with it. This precise legal contradiction is important evidence for Chapter 62's comparative argument about the limits of republican inclusion.

New schools and works required personnel and reliable supply. The government recruited and directed workers and could compel labor in ways that reveal the distance between state capacity and civil liberty. Foreign technicians contributed expertise, but Paraguayan officials shaped priorities around defense, transport and revenue. Modernization consequently strengthened the administration without producing the political competition that contemporaries associated with liberal constitutionalism. The later war is treated elsewhere; here the emphasis belongs on the prewar institutions and their social cost.

Section 62.37

62.37 Recognition, River Navigation, Borders, and Paraguay's Regional Position

Brazil recognized Paraguayan independence in 1844, seeking an ally against Rosas, but Argentina under Rosas did not. After Caseros, Urquiza's Confederation recognized it in 1852. Paraguay's access to the sea depended on the Paraná and Paraguay rivers, controlled downstream by Argentina and Brazil, and navigation rights were a constant source of conflict. A treaty with Brazil in 1856 deferred the border question rather than solving it.

Paraguay also faced tension with the United States after the USS Water Witch was fired on by Paraguayan batteries in 1855, which led to a U.S. naval expedition and a treaty in 1859. López strengthened the fortress of Humaitá on the Paraguay River, sent his son Francisco Solano López to Europe in the 1850s to buy arms, and relied on him as war minister and diplomat, notably in the 1859 mediation at San José de Flores.

Recognition remained a matter of negotiation as long as the lower rivers passed through territories controlled by other powers. The fall of Rosas in 1852 changed the Argentine context, and subsequent agreements acknowledged Paraguay's independence and addressed aspects of navigation. Disputes nevertheless persisted over the precise boundary in the northeast and along the Paraná, while Brazilian interests in Mato Grosso made river access a security concern. Diplomacy should distinguish formal recognition from a settlement of every frontier claim. A country could be internationally recognized while still preparing for armed confrontation over transport corridors and territorial jurisdiction.

Foreign governments approached Paraguay through different priorities. Argentina sought a stable regional political order and commercial access; Brazil wanted reliable navigation into its inland provinces; European merchants and diplomats looked for trade and contractual opportunities. Paraguay's government sought machinery and recognition while resisting arrangements it regarded as threats to autonomy. The resulting diplomatic correspondence reveals negotiation, caution and periodic confrontation rather than an unbroken policy of isolation. Mid-century incidents involving foreign vessels also demonstrated the risks of unresolved jurisdiction over rivers.

When Carlos Antonio López died in September 1862, the government passed to Francisco Solano López with significant military resources, a more active diplomatic position and unresolved border disputes. It is tempting to read these conditions backward from the later catastrophe of the Paraguayan War. This chapter should instead reconstruct them as decisions made under uncertainty: officials anticipated multiple regional scenarios, and neighbors had conflicting interests. The comprehensive war narrative belongs to Chapter 64, while this section explains the state-building inheritance that made later choices possible.

The Federal Republic of Central America, 1823–1840

Section 62.38

62.38 The Provinces after Independence and the Rejection of Mexican Rule

Independence was declared in Guatemala City on 15 September 1821, largely by provincial elites seeking to avoid revolution and to preserve order. In January 1822 the leaders of Guatemala City voted to join the Mexican Empire of Agustín de Iturbide, but San Salvador and other towns resisted, and Mexican forces under Vicente Filísola occupied the region. After Iturbide's fall in March 1823, a constituent assembly in Guatemala declared independence from Spain, Mexico, and any other power on 1 July 1823 and organized the United Provinces of Central America. Chiapas chose to remain with Mexico.

The five provinces had distinct interests. Guatemala City's merchants and landowners dominated trade and the Church, and its highlands held a large Maya population. El Salvador produced indigo and had a liberal political leadership suspicious of Guatemala's primacy. Honduras was thinly settled, with mining and scattered towns. Nicaragua was divided between León and Granada, and Costa Rica, at the periphery, had its own rivalry between Cartago and San José. Chapter 57 describes the independence settlement; here the question is how these interests shaped the federation.

The political separation from Mexico in 1823 did not erase differences created during colonial rule. Guatemala City had long supplied important offices and commercial connections to the wider region, while San Salvador's economic and political leaders resented that preeminence. Honduran communities were divided between the influence of Comayagua and Tegucigalpa; Nicaragua's León and Granada had rival political networks; Costa Rican towns lay farther from the former capital and frequently saw little return from regional taxation. These circumstances produced debate about whether a federation would guarantee equality among the states or reproduce Guatemala's authority in a new constitutional form.

The transition unfolded through representative assemblies, municipal actions and military pressure rather than an uncontested vote of one unified people. Support for a Central American republic was real, especially among those who believed a larger market and common diplomacy would improve security, but opponents feared that regional officers would impose unwanted taxes and political commitments. Chiapas's different political trajectory raised a boundary question that the federal framers could not settle by assertion. The institutions created after 1823 therefore faced issues of territory and legitimacy from the beginning.

The five states also had different labor systems. Guatemala's Indigenous highlands retained communal institutions and obligations inherited from the colonial era, while Salvadoran indigo interests and Nicaraguan merchants engaged different trade routes. Costa Rican coffee cultivation was still developing, and the Caribbean ports remained subject to varied local and foreign commercial arrangements. These social and geographic realities shaped later constitutional conflicts more deeply than abstract labels such as liberal or conservative can convey on their own.

Section 62.39

62.39 The Constitution of 1824 and the Federal Republic

The federal constitution of 22 November 1824 created a republic of five states: Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica. It provided a representative Congress and a separately organized Senate with constitutionally distinct functions, an indirectly elected president, and a federal supreme court. Each state had its own constitution, chief of state, and legislature, and the federal capital was Guatemala City. The text established the Catholic religion as the only one recognized by the state.

The constitution divided sovereignty between the federation and the states, but gave the federal government little independent revenue or force. It depended on contributions from the states, which often did not pay, and it had no army that did not rely on state forces. The states controlled land, local taxes, and appointments. These design weaknesses, rather than ideology alone, explain why disputes between federal and state authority escalated into civil war.

The constitution of November 22, 1824 defined Central America as a sovereign federal republic composed of five states, each retaining constitutional institutions alongside the federal government. Its text separated the functions of the representative Congress from a Senate established under a different set of provisions; describing the two as a conventional bicameral Congress risks misleading students about how they were supposed to interact in lawmaking and review. The constitution also outlined indirect electoral processes, executive authority and the duties of the states, illustrating how elaborate federal design sought to reconcile national independence with regional autonomy.

Fiscal implementation proved much harder than drafting. The federal government required contributions and cooperation from state authorities whose own budgets were constrained. When states refused payments or opposed federal appointments, the national executive had few means to compel obedience without mobilizing military resources that might further alienate them. Rival leaders disputed whether the federal administration could intervene in state quarrels, and the constitution's provisions had to operate amid recurrent political emergencies. Institutional weakness was therefore produced by revenue arrangements, military fragmentation and political choices, not merely by the length or complexity of the charter.

The framers' commitment to liberties and representative government existed within a social order marked by unequal land access, Church authority and varied systems of communal government. State legislatures could interpret the federation's goals differently, and the power to enforce reforms did not reach every municipality equally. Comparing the constitution's promises with military correspondence, fiscal records and local petitions makes clear why formal adoption in 1824 was not the same thing as a functioning and consensual federation. This distinction is central to explaining the coming civil wars.

Section 62.40

62.40 Manuel José Arce, Francisco Morazán, and the First Federal Civil War

The first federal president, Manuel José Arce of El Salvador, was chosen by the Congress in 1825 after no candidate won a majority of the electoral vote; José Cecilio del Valle had won the most votes. Arce's relations with Guatemala's liberal chief of state, Juan Barrundia, soured, and in 1826 he arrested Barrundia and intervened in other states, which led to civil war. The conflict between federal and state authority and between conservative and liberal factions lasted until 1829.

Francisco Morazán, a Honduran liberal commander, raised forces in Honduras and El Salvador, defeated the federal and Guatemalan conservative forces, and entered Guatemala City in April 1829. He exiled conservative leaders and the archbishop and was elected federal president in 1830, serving until 1834 and being reelected for a second term. The war's costs fell on rural communities that supplied troops, food, and animals, and it left lasting animosities among the states.

Manuel José Arce emerged from a contested federal presidential selection in 1825. The political disagreement should be reconstructed through the electoral rules and congressional decision rather than presented as a routine victory of one coherent party. Arce initially enjoyed support across factional lines but soon clashed with Salvadoran liberals and with Guatemalan authorities, especially during disputes over appointments and the reach of federal intervention. The removal of Guatemalan state officials intensified fears that the federal presidency would be used against legitimate state government. Each faction claimed to defend constitutional order even while mobilizing troops to impose its interpretation.

The conflict of 1826–1829 spread beyond a single capital. Military campaigns disrupted trade routes, redirected tax receipts to war, and pulled local commanders into alliances whose benefits were uncertain. Francisco Morazán became a prominent liberal leader through operations originating in Honduras and El Salvador and entered Guatemala City with his victorious forces in April 1829. The displacement of conservative authorities and the expulsion of political and ecclesiastical opponents transformed the federation's balance of power. The victors believed they were rescuing constitutional rule; those defeated saw the federal government as the instrument of a hostile political coalition.

Morazán became federal president in 1830, but a military victory could not supply the treasury, local cooperation and administrative machinery that a federation needed. Provincial officials still guarded their independence, and armies that had fought on the same side were difficult to demobilize or integrate. The records of military commanders and political proclamations reveal competing claims of legality, while household experiences of requisition and troop movements appear more faintly. The war therefore illustrates how constitutional conflict became self-reinforcing: each intervention claimed to defend the union while deepening mistrust of its institutions.

Section 62.41

62.41 Liberal Reform, Church Conflict, and Conservative Resistance

Liberal governments in the 1830s tried to reduce the Church's wealth and privileges, expand education, and introduce new legal codes. They expelled the archbishop and some religious orders in 1829, curtailed compulsory tithes, and introduced civil marriage. In Guatemala, chief of state Mariano Gálvez introduced legal codes based on those drawn up for Louisiana by Edward Livingston, including trial by jury, which many found alien. The governments also raised direct taxes to pay for their programs.

Many rural Guatemalans, especially Indigenous and mestizo villagers, saw these measures as attacks on their faith, local authorities, and customs. A cholera epidemic in 1837 intensified distrust, as rumors spread that officials had poisoned the water, and priests helped turn grievance into resistance. The reforms were not simply progressive or reactionary: they sought to build a modern state and also imposed costs on communities that had little say. Sources include decrees, the writings of Liberal officials, and conservative accounts, with rural voices appearing mainly through petitions and later testimony.

Liberal reform was not one uniform program imposed throughout all five states. Federal and state governments pursued changes in ecclesiastical privileges, education, taxation and the organization of courts with varying degrees of energy. Mariano Gálvez's government in Guatemala became closely associated with legal experimentation, including measures influenced by Edward Livingston's penal reform ideas. Critics feared that unfamiliar courts, civil registration and the reduction of older religious privileges would weaken their communities. Reformers regarded these changes as essential to a modern republic, but the capacity to explain and implement them in remote districts was limited.

The expulsion of the archbishop and religious communities after the liberal victory of 1829 had immediate political significance beyond doctrinal disagreement. Clerics organized festivals, local charity, schooling and the circulation of information; their removal or marginalization affected forms of social authority that could not quickly be replaced by a fiscal-strapped government. Economic grievances, land conflict and military demands also shaped rural opposition. Opposition leaders were able to present liberal measures as threats to established communal life, while conservative interpretations sometimes exaggerated the uniformity of Indigenous and rural religious opinion.

The cholera epidemic of 1837 sharpened these conflicts. Disease produced fear and high mortality, and rumors that officials had contaminated water or medicines circulated in politically unstable districts. Such claims should be discussed as historical evidence of distrust, not repeated as fact. Rafael Carrera's rise occurred in this atmosphere, but the movement that supported him also drew on grievances older than the epidemic. To explain rebellion adequately, the chapter must consider government taxation, village institutions, elite rivalries and the way local knowledge of illness and authority shaped public behavior.

Section 62.42

62.42 Rafael Carrera, Los Altos, State Withdrawals, and the Dissolution of the Federation

In 1837 Rafael Carrera, a young mestizo from eastern Guatemala, led a rural rebellion against the Gálvez government with the support of villagers and local priests. After taking Guatemala City in early 1839, he became the dominant military figure. Gálvez fell in early 1838. In the western highlands, liberal elites of Quetzaltenango declared the separate State of Los Altos in 1838, opposed to Guatemala City's commercial dominance, and the federal congress recognized it. Carrera's forces suppressed it in 1840.

Meanwhile the other states left the federation: Nicaragua in April–May 1838, Honduras in October, and Costa Rica in November, after the federal congress allowed states to organize themselves as they wished. Morazán fought on until Carrera defeated him in 1840, and he went into exile; he was executed in Costa Rica in September 1842. The federation therefore ended in stages, with formal withdrawals in 1838 and effective collapse by 1840, a distinction between constitutional dissolution and the actual disintegration of authority.

Carrera's movement gained strength in the countryside east of Guatemala City before becoming a decisive force in the collapse of liberal federal authority. His relationship with rural and Indigenous supporters was negotiated and often changed with the circumstances; it combined expectations of religious protection, local autonomy, relief from burdens and access to military power. Mariano Gálvez lost his position in the political crisis of 1838, while Morazán continued to defend a broader federal project. The sequence of attacks on and entries into Guatemala City must be dated carefully rather than reduced to one victory followed automatically by dissolution.

The State of Los Altos, centered on Quetzaltenango, emerged in 1838 from western districts whose leaders resented Guatemala City's administration. Its government appealed to commercial and regional autonomy, but highland Indigenous communities did not all share the aspirations of the city's elite. Some resisted taxes and local authority under Los Altos and sought alliances elsewhere. Carrera's conquest of the state in 1840 therefore changed the balance among municipal and rural interests as well as the map of formal jurisdictions. The failure of Los Altos illustrates why the terms 'state' and 'community' can conceal conflicting populations within one claimed territory.

The federation dissolved through withdrawals, rival governments and effective loss of authority rather than one unanimously agreed constitutional termination. Nicaragua, Honduras and Costa Rica acted to secure separate sovereignty in 1838, and El Salvador later emerged as an independent republic while Morazán's efforts to preserve the union failed. Morazán was executed in Costa Rica in September 1842 after a final attempt to revive federalist rule. His fate later became a symbol for competing national memories, but contemporaries judged his campaigns through their own experiences of war, taxation and local security.

Section 62.43

62.43 Federal Finances, Trade, Slavery, Indigenous Communities, and Social Conflict

The federation's revenue came largely from customs duties and state contributions, and the states controlled most ports and taxes. Exports of indigo from El Salvador and cochineal from Guatemala brought income, but trade was disrupted by war, and Britain's presence at Belize and on the Caribbean coast gave it a commercial and political role. The federal treasury was chronically empty.

The National Constituent Assembly abolished slavery in April 1824, though the number of enslaved people was small compared with Brazil or the Caribbean. Indigenous communities held communal lands and local offices and depended on them for subsistence, and they resisted pressure from landowners and states seeking labor and taxes. Social divisions between Indigenous, ladino, and creole populations shaped political alliances, and Carrera's success showed that rural communities could be a decisive political force.

The federation's weak fiscal base made ordinary administration vulnerable. The national government lacked a stable source of customs revenue independent of states and ports, and states could resist assessments when they doubted that federal policy served their needs. Military expenses increased during civil conflict, encouraging forced contributions and borrowing that deepened public resentment. Economic activity meanwhile remained uneven: Guatemalan highland agriculture, Salvadoran indigo exports, Honduran mining, Nicaraguan regional markets and Costa Rican coffee interests each responded to different commercial opportunities. A unified republic could not be sustained merely by announcing a common tax principle.

The 1824 abolition of slavery was a significant legal change, though enslaved populations in the region were smaller than in Brazil or the Caribbean plantation colonies. Its implementation should be studied alongside other forms of dependence, including coercive rural labor and unequal access to land. Indigenous communal property and municipal authority survived in parts of the highlands but came under pressure from changing taxation, legal reform and regional commercial expansion. The experience of emancipation therefore did not exhaust the broader problem of republican equality.

The division should end by comparing what federal legislators wanted with what state officials and households could support. Local petitions, church records and tax accounts reveal obligations that do not appear in constitutional rhetoric. They also show that people excluded from high national office could shape political outcomes through refusal, alliance, flight or armed action. The federation's collapse was simultaneously a constitutional failure, a fiscal and military crisis, and a set of local political contests that produced five distinct national trajectories.

Division IX · Sections 62.44–62.48

Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras after the Federation, 1838–1860

Section 62.44

62.44 Rafael Carrera and the Construction of Conservative Guatemala

Rafael Carrera, born into a modest family in Guatemala City, rose from a rural insurgent leader to the dominant figure in Guatemala for more than two decades. His coalition combined villagers who had resisted Liberal reforms, local priests, Indigenous and ladino communities, and, increasingly, the merchants and landowners of the capital who valued order. During and after the crisis of 1838–1839, conservative governments altered Liberal codes, strengthened commercial corporate institutions and gradually restored the position of certain religious orders; the precise timing and extent of implementation differed.

Carrera also governed through concessions to rural communities. His governments partly restored the older legal protections of Indigenous communities and their local authorities, which limited the pressure of the Liberal state on village land and labor. These concessions did not mean equality: Indigenous people remained subject to labor demands and tribute-like obligations, and commercial landowners benefited from stability. Carrera's rule rested on the army, which he commanded, and on his skill at balancing rural and elite interests.

Carrera's administration relied on an ability to mediate among groups that did not always trust one another. Religious authorities valued the restoration of their public standing; merchants sought secure roads and a dependable government; rural communities expected recognition of local rights or relief from burdens; and military officers required pay and influence. These interests formed an alliance when opposition to radical liberalism was strongest, but their priorities could diverge. Carrera's personal prestige mattered because he could negotiate across those divisions while maintaining forces that discouraged rebellion.

Policies associated with the conservative restoration require chronological precision. The reinstatement of certain ecclesiastical institutions and commercial bodies was gradual and contested, and older legal protections for Indigenous villages were adapted rather than simply restored intact. The 1851 constitutive arrangements and Carrera's eventual presidency for life in October 1854 consolidated executive authority, but official titles alone say little about actual relations between the capital and the highlands. The country's declaration as a sovereign republic on March 21, 1847 did not renounce all possible Central American unity: its text explicitly left open future reorganization of the region.

Government under Carrera could provide stability for some property holders while restricting opposition and imposing obligations on others. Rural constituents were not simply manipulated; they used military allegiance and local collective action to bargain over taxation, religion and land. The term conservative should therefore describe a political coalition and institutional program, not a claim that Guatemala returned unchanged to the colonial era. An account of the period must connect decrees and campaigns to relationships between villages, landowners, religious authorities and soldiers.

Section 62.45

62.45 Guatemala's Highlands, Indigenous Politics, and Los Altos

The western highlands, centered on Quetzaltenango and Totonicapán, were home to a large K'iche' and other Maya population and to a commercial ladino elite. Highland communities had a record of political action, including the 1820 Totonicapán rising over tribute led by Atanasio Tzul and Lucas Aguilar. Their municipal offices, land holdings, and religious brotherhoods gave them organized means of bargaining with outside authority.

The elite of Quetzaltenango resented Guatemala City's commercial dominance and in 1838 formed the State of Los Altos, which the federal congress recognized. Its government and ladino leaders sought to rule a mostly Indigenous population, and many highland communities, burdened by demands for taxes and labor, did not support it. Carrera's forces suppressed Los Altos in 1840 and restored Guatemala City's authority over the region. Historians continue to debate how far Indigenous communities actively aided Carrera or simply resisted the Altos government.

The history of Los Altos demonstrates that highland political divisions were not exhausted by the rivalry between Guatemala City and Quetzaltenango. K'iche' and other Maya communities had their own authorities, landholding arrangements and memories of earlier mobilization, including the 1820 Totonicapán rising. Some municipal leaders regarded the elite promoters of Los Altos as another source of taxes and interference, while others sought opportunities through the proposed state. There was no single Indigenous position shared by every village, and historians should avoid retroactively treating support for Carrera as proof of permanent conservative ideology.

Commercial ambitions also mattered. Quetzaltenango's merchants and professionals wanted greater influence over western trade and taxation, while the highlands' agricultural and Indigenous communities faced the consequences of these policies in labor and communal resources. The administrative boundary of the new state did not determine how its residents would respond. Local petitions, municipal archives and reports from commanders are needed to reconstruct competing claims, especially because official proclamations speak mainly for urban leaders.

When Carrera's forces suppressed Los Altos in 1840, the outcome strengthened Guatemala City's territorial claims but did not end regional tensions. The coercive military settlement reshaped alliances, and individual communities continued to bargain over customary rights and government demands. The comparative lesson is that the failure of a separatist state does not establish the absence of regional identity or the uniform support of a larger national government.

Section 62.46

62.46 El Salvador: Republican Government, Liberalism, and Federalist Legacies

El Salvador, small and densely settled, remained a center of federalist sentiment and a refuge for liberal exiles after Morazán's fall. Its economy rested on indigo exports and subsistence farming, with communal and municipal lands that later became a flashpoint when coffee spread. Military leaders such as Francisco Malespín dominated politics in the early 1840s, and civilian governments struggled to establish authority.

In 1841 El Salvador declared itself a sovereign republic, though its leaders kept hoping for a revival of union. Church and state relations were again a subject of conflict, as the creation of a Salvadoran diocese in 1842 gave the state a bishop of its own. Rivalry with conservative Guatemala was a constant: Salvadoran governments sheltered Guatemalan liberals, and Guatemala supported rivals in Salvadoran politics.

El Salvador's leaders faced competing pressures from federalist sentiment, domestic factional politics and the strategic position of Guatemala. San Salvador had been a center of resistance to political domination by Guatemala City, and supporters of Morazán hoped that a larger regional federation might protect that autonomy. Others feared repeated wars and contributions to a remote central administration. Political identities crossed these divisions as commanders and civilian leaders changed alliances in response to immediate threats. Formal independence did not settle how to finance an army or protect the borders.

Church-state relations were especially contested. Liberal leaders sought to reshape ecclesiastical influence, while other politicians treated religious institutions as sources of social order and public legitimacy. The establishment of a Salvadoran diocese in the 1840s affected the relationship between civil and religious authority, yet local practice could not be transformed by a declaration from the capital alone. Land use, indigo commerce and the rights of rural communities created additional divisions that do not map neatly onto national party labels.

By the early 1850s, Salvadoran interventions in regional disputes and conflict with Carrera's Guatemala contributed to military defeats and new calculations about union. The battle of La Arada in 1851 involved Salvadoran and Honduran invading forces, not a uniform military coalition of all Central American states. A national government was emerging through these trials, but its authority remained constrained by provincial networks and by powerful neighboring regimes. The section should leave students with a sense of political contingency, not a caricature of endless military disorder.

Section 62.47

62.47 Honduras: Provincial Rivalries, Military Leadership, and Weak Institutions

Honduras had a small, scattered population and an economy based on cattle, subsistence agriculture, and silver mining around Tegucigalpa. The political class was split between Comayagua, the colonial capital, and Tegucigalpa, the mining center, and neither had the revenue to sustain a strong government. Francisco Ferrera became the first president after the state's 1841 reorganization, and later leaders, including Juan Lindo and Trinidad Cabañas, relied on alliances with neighboring states.

Honduran governments were repeatedly drawn into the quarrels of Guatemala, El Salvador, and Nicaragua because their own armies were small. A new constitution in 1848 declared the republic, but the state's capacity remained limited, and regional commanders had more local authority than the capital. Roads were poor, customs revenues small, and mining declined without capital.

Honduras's geography shaped administration in specific ways. The political centers of Comayagua and Tegucigalpa differed in their commercial relationships and claims to national leadership, while mountains and difficult roads made communication expensive. Mining had brought important economic activity in the colonial period, but the republic could not rely on a single port or treasury comparable to Buenos Aires's. Public revenue came from limited customs receipts and taxes that local elites could sometimes resist. Governments consequently struggled to maintain regular salaries, courts and military forces across scattered districts.

Francisco Ferrera and other leaders combined military command with electoral or constitutional claims, and their support depended on relationships with local officeholders and rural groups. Administrations could proclaim sovereign authority while being unable to compel distant towns to remit taxes or accept appointments. Disputes with El Salvador and Guatemala drew the country into regional campaigns, altering alliances and sometimes diverting scarce resources away from routine government. Political weakness must be explained as an institutional and economic condition rather than an inherited national characteristic.

A country with limited resources still had active local politics. Municipal authorities, parish communities, merchants and farmers negotiated obligations, resisted requisitions, and sometimes mobilized with neighboring forces. The history of Honduras is harder to reconstruct from one central archive than the history of a wealthy port state, which makes the treatment of local sources especially important. Later constitutional reforms, including those of the 1840s, should be tied to the actual powers available to the government and the practical problems of implementing legislation.

Section 62.48

62.48 Regional Rivalries and Renewed Projects of Central American Union

Despite the federation's collapse, union remained an attractive idea. In 1842 Honduras, El Salvador, and Nicaragua signed the Pact of Chinandega, creating a short-lived confederation that dissolved by 1845. Another attempt at a national representation in 1849 brought together some of the same states. These projects failed because of mutual suspicion, the lack of a common treasury, and Guatemala's refusal to join.

Guatemala's conservative governments saw such unions as a threat from Liberal neighbors. On 2 February 1851 Carrera's forces defeated a combined Salvadoran, Honduran, and Nicaraguan army at La Arada. The victory confirmed Guatemala's military primacy and checked attempts to impose union by force, but it did not end the idea, which later governments revived.

The appeal of union survived the collapse of federal government because neighboring states remained commercially and strategically interdependent. Merchants needed safe passage across boundaries, and governments faced disputes about tariffs, extradition, diplomatic representation and foreign intervention. Efforts to reconstruct confederation therefore recurred in different forms. The Chinandega agreement associated with the early 1840s brought Honduras, El Salvador and Nicaragua into a renewed union project, but political instability and disagreements delayed and weakened its institutions. The experience showed that a treaty could create a legal aspiration without providing sufficient revenue or enforcement powers.

The late 1840s produced further proposals for joint representation and defense. Their supporters could point to a common independence history and the threat of external pressure; critics worried about the loss of sovereign authority and the costs of military commitments. These debates were not simply a replay of 1824. The states now had different ruling coalitions and had experienced a decade or more of independent fiscal and military decision-making. They were negotiating from a stronger sense of separate national institutions, even while admitting the advantages of cooperation.

The defeat of Salvadoran and Honduran forces by Guatemala at La Arada in February 1851 revealed the political difficulty of union promoted through armed pressure. Guatemala's military strength, based on Carrera's coalition and command, shaped the regional balance. Yet a battlefield outcome did not erase the social and diplomatic incentives for cooperation. Later joint resistance to William Walker would again demonstrate that states unable to construct a permanent federation could nonetheless unite in defense of sovereignty.

Costa Rica, Nicaragua, and the Defense of Sovereignty, 1838–1860

Section 62.49

62.49 Costa Rica and coffee

Costa Rica's early governments under Juan Mora Fernández and Braulio Carrillo built administrative structures, and coffee exports, especially after the 1830s, created a landowning elite and funded the state. The Republic was declared in 1848. Relative social homogeneity was real but should not hide inequality or the displacement of Indigenous people.

Costa Rica's distance from Guatemala City and its developing local agricultural economy helped its leaders consolidate authority after the federation fragmented, but stability should not be attributed to a mythical society without social divisions. Competing towns and political factions contested the location of power; leaders such as Juan Mora Fernández and Braulio Carrillo pursued different combinations of legal institution-building and executive concentration. Carrillo's political program strengthened central administration while relying on authoritarian practices that complicate the familiar story of peaceful national exceptionalism. State-building remained a contested process involving armed force, taxation and the definition of political participation.

Coffee cultivation transformed relationships among landholders, merchants and transport providers. The central valley's small and medium cultivators participated alongside larger commercial families, and export networks connected production through Pacific shipping routes. Expanding coffee commerce generated revenue and credit opportunities, but benefits varied by access to land, financing and overseas markets. Transport problems required roads and coordinated commercial arrangements; the government's ability to facilitate them helped reinforce administrative authority. The establishment of a separate republic in 1848 made explicit a political autonomy that had developed through previous legal and diplomatic steps.

The social consequences were uneven. Some rural families improved their position through coffee growing, while indebtedness and market fluctuations could create dependence. Indigenous communities were often displaced or marginalized in narratives of national agricultural prosperity, despite retaining political and cultural identities in different regions. Women contributed labor and family management and sometimes participated in commerce, though formal political rights remained constrained. Churches, schools and local municipalities helped translate national government into everyday practice, but the process was neither uniform nor wholly voluntary.

The 1850s presented a demanding test of the state's capacity. President Juan Rafael Mora Porras could mobilize funds, troops and supplies against William Walker, an achievement connected to earlier institutional development. The war also spread cholera and left families coping with deaths, debts and interrupted harvests. Mora's eventual overthrow in 1859 showed that successful national defense did not eliminate political conflict. The chapter should treat Costa Rica's comparative strength as the result of particular fiscal, administrative and social conditions, not an inevitable consequence of its size or supposed homogeneity.

Section 62.50

62.50 Nicaragua: León and Granada

Liberals in León and Conservatives in Granada fought repeated civil wars, preventing consolidation and inviting foreign involvement, since each side sought allies.

Nicaragua's early republic faced a more persistent struggle between political centers. León's and Granada's elites differed over commercial opportunities, religious and educational institutions, and their relationships with military officers. Political factions identified as Democratic or Legitimist, Liberal or Conservative, sought control of the government but also drew support from local clients and rural communities. Constitutional drafts did not eliminate competition over the location of the capital and the appointment of officials. Rival authorities could maintain separate troops and fiscal networks, turning an electoral disagreement into a prolonged civil war.

Control of routes across Nicaragua amplified these disputes. The San Juan River connected Lake Nicaragua to the Caribbean, while movement across the narrow western isthmus offered an attractive path toward the Pacific. Local rulers who could command transit revenues or negotiate with foreign carriers acquired resources that neighboring rivals might not possess. European and United States interests therefore interacted with domestic conflict rather than acting on a passive country. A faction's request for foreign military assistance could alter the balance of a civil war while imposing long-term costs on sovereignty.

By the middle 1850s, the war between rival Nicaraguan groups had opened the door to the recruitment of foreign fighters. Supporters of the Democratic faction sought help, but the arrival of William Walker and his recruits transformed a local military calculation into a project connected to United States expansionist and slaveholding ambitions. Not all Nicaraguans who fought on one side initially understood or accepted these ambitions, and subsequent alliances shifted as Walker established control over institutions. The chapter must distinguish the decisions of local leaders from the independent designs of the foreign commander.

The social dimension of these conflicts includes military requisition, agricultural disruption, the loss of transport revenue and the danger posed to communities along the transit corridor. Civil war affected women and children through movement of armed groups, shortages and disease. Local governments could issue proclamations, but they often could not protect every road or settlement. This instability helps explain both Walker's opening and the eventual reaction against him, when rival Central American factions judged the defense of sovereignty more urgent than their existing domestic disputes.

Section 62.51

62.51 The transit route and the Mosquito Coast

The San Juan River and Lake Nicaragua offered a route between oceans, valuable after the California Gold Rush (1848). Britain asserted protection over the Miskito Kingdom, whose people had their own political autonomy and Anglo-Caribbean ties. Cornelius Vanderbilt's Accessory Transit Company, rival canal schemes, and the Clayton–Bulwer Treaty (1850) made the route a stage for imperial competition.

The transit corridor acquired new importance after the California Gold Rush generated demand for quicker movement between the eastern United States and the Pacific. Passengers could travel from the Caribbean up the San Juan River, across Lake Nicaragua, and then overland to Pacific embarkation points. The route involved steamships, river craft, port facilities and agreements with local officials; it was not an empty geographic shortcut. Vanderbilt-associated transportation interests and rival commercial groups sought privileges that could influence Nicaraguan revenue and foreign policy. These interests also made control of the corridor attractive to military leaders during civil war.

The Mosquito Coast had its own political history. Miskito authorities maintained relationships with British representatives and Caribbean commercial networks while asserting autonomy in territory that Nicaragua claimed. British protection and diplomacy were often treated by Nicaraguan officials as interference, whereas Miskito leaders could use foreign alliances to defend rights and opportunities. The Clayton–Bulwer Treaty of 1850 addressed British and United States ambitions for an interoceanic passage but did not settle the political status of all the communities whose lands and waterways a future route might cross. A faithful map study must show contested claims rather than only modern sovereign borders.

The connection between commercial transport and political power becomes clearest during the war against Walker. Revenues, passenger services and the movement of recruits depended on controlling boats, river landings and lake crossings. A military victory on a road could have limited consequences if a foreign force continued to receive men and supplies through the transit system. Costa Rican strategists therefore regarded control of the route as a central objective, not a secondary logistical matter. This helps explain the decisive role of the later transit campaign in Walker's defeat.

Economic opportunity did not guarantee national development. Foreign carriers could extract profits, make demands on weak governments and shift their allegiance when political circumstances changed. Local farmers and boat operators might gain employment, but warfare and epidemics threatened both commerce and families. The transit corridor illustrates an international dimension of state formation: a natural route became politically important only through technology, investment, treaties, competing sovereignty claims and sustained attempts to control it.

Section 62.52

62.52 Walker's regime

William Walker arrived in 1855 with American recruits at the invitation of Liberal leaders in León, took Granada, and by July 1856 became president in an election of doubtful legitimacy. On 22 September 1856 he issued a decree repealing earlier federal legislation, including the 1824 abolition law, which legally reopened slavery and tied his project to U.S. Southern expansion.

William Walker came to Nicaragua in 1855 amid a civil war between domestic factions; he did not initially conquer a fully unified country without local collaborators. His recruits were comparatively few, but access to modern weapons, outside commercial connections and shifting Nicaraguan alliances enabled his influence to grow. After seizing Granada, he supported political arrangements that placed friendly local leaders in office while increasing his own control. His selection as president in 1856 followed a deeply contested process that lacked broad legitimacy. The combination of constitutional forms and coercion is important: a nominal election did not establish independent national consent.

Walker regarded Nicaragua through the ideology of expansionist filibustering. The term referred to private armed expeditions whose participants sought power abroad without an ordinary commission from their home government. His ambitions were connected to debates in the United States about territorial expansion, slavery and the prospects of a stronger slaveholding sphere around the Caribbean. These motives conflicted with Central American sovereignty and with the region's earlier emancipation laws. Nicaraguan supporters of Walker's initial military intervention increasingly had reason to reassess the political arrangement they had helped create.

The decree issued at Granada on September 22, 1856 was legally consequential. It declared federal constituent and congressional laws void under the claimed independence of Nicaragua from the old federation, thereby nullifying the earlier federal abolition of slavery. It did not simply publish a new, detailed slave code in one sentence. Contemporary sources and Walker's own explanations connect the measure with the intended restoration of slavery. The distinction matters because students should analyze the mechanism of repeal and the ideology behind it, not merely memorize a statement that Walker favored slavery.

Walker also depended on supplies and recruits moving through the transit route, whose control became a military question. His factional alliances weakened as Central American governments and Nicaraguan rivals organized resistance. Even where his government claimed authority, that claim differed from administrative control of villages and provinces. Local people faced seizure of resources, dangers of combat, and uncertainty about political and legal status. The campaign against him belongs to Central American history as an assertion of regional sovereignty, not to a narrative of foreign adventure alone.

Historical Vocabulary

Filibusterism and Private Military Intervention

In the nineteenth-century Americas, filibusterism referred to private armed expeditions organized to seize influence or territory abroad, often without formal authorization from the government of the participants’ home country. Such expeditions could claim to help a local political faction while pursuing independent expansionist goals.

William Walker’s intervention in Nicaragua began amid a domestic conflict but developed into a project threatening regional sovereignty and seeking to revive slavery. The term should not be equated with ordinary immigration or with every form of foreign military assistance; its combination of private organization, coercion, and territorial ambition is crucial.

Questions for historical analysis:

  1. What makes a private armed expedition different from state diplomacy?
  2. How did Walker’s goals diverge from those of some original Nicaraguan allies?

Section 62.53

62.53 The war against Walker

The campaign later acquired powerful national memories. Costa Rican accounts commemorate Juan Santamaría, associated with the fighting at Rivas, and the state incorporated the war into patriotic ceremonies and education. Historical analysis must distinguish the contemporary military record from later commemoration; one celebrated figure does not represent the experiences of all recruits, officers, transport workers or affected households. The following chronology therefore emphasizes military operations, regional cooperation and civilian costs as well as the symbolic language of national defense.

Costa Rica's government judged the filibuster regime a threat both to regional independence and to its own security. President Juan Rafael Mora Porras called for mobilization and organized the movement of men and supplies northward. At Santa Rosa on March 20, 1856 Costa Rican troops defeated a filibuster force, showing that Walker's allied units could be challenged. The campaign proceeded into Nicaragua, where the battle of Rivas on April 11 was fought principally by Costa Rican forces. The story should not retrospectively place every later allied army at that battle merely because the overall conflict became a Central American war.

The military cost extended well beyond the battlefield. Cholera spread among soldiers and returning civilians in 1856, affecting households throughout Costa Rica and creating a demographic and social crisis. Food supplies, transportation and recruitment required extraordinary effort from the state. Communities confronted mortality, the loss of workers and growing public expenditures. Military reports may celebrate victories, but parish registers and household records disclose consequences obscured by patriotic narratives. The epidemic also helps explain why the Costa Rican army temporarily played a reduced role while other Central American forces continued the struggle.

Guatemalan, Salvadoran, Honduran and Nicaraguan forces eventually participated in the campaign against Walker, though coordination was difficult and the interests of their governments were not identical. Nicaragua itself contained numerous opponents of Walker, including former rivals who judged the filibuster regime intolerable. The later operations against the San Juan transit corridor, especially those undertaken by Costa Rican forces from late 1856, restricted the entry of supplies and reinforcements. Control of river craft and landings proved as important as set-piece battles. Walker surrendered to a United States naval officer on May 1, 1857.

Victory did not reconstruct the old federation. Each country retained its government and domestic disputes, and future attempts at union faced the same issues of finances, political control and regional mistrust. The shared campaign nevertheless created a durable memory of collective defense and demonstrated that cooperation could emerge without a permanent federal constitution. Students should distinguish the military outcomes from later national commemorations, which may emphasize the role of one state while diminishing the sacrifices of others.

Comparative Paths to Republican State Formation

Section 62.54

62.54 Federalism, centralism, and integration

Argentina's confederal system relied on Buenos Aires as an informal center; Chile's strong executive rested on a compact capital region; Paraguay's executive faced little organized opposition; Uruguay's parties competed over a small, contested territory; Central America's federation lacked revenue and force. Similar vocabulary concealed very different structures.

The first comparative lesson is that the word federalism describes a distribution of authority, not an assurance of democratic participation or mutual loyalty. Argentine provinces initially defended their own sovereignty, then accepted a national constitutional framework only after repeated warfare and negotiation over Buenos Aires's fiscal advantages. Central American states adopted a formal federation early, but their federal executive lacked predictable resources and political trust. Chile tried decentralizing experiments and moved instead toward a strongly administered unitary republic. Paraguay treated its independence from neighboring direction as an argument for concentrated national executive power, while Uruguay's relatively small territory did not prevent sustained competition among organized factions.

The practical test of these institutions lay in recruitment, revenue and enforcement. An elected legislature could authorize taxes, yet governments needed officers to collect them and sufficient legitimacy to avoid provoking resistance. Provincial militias could defend local autonomy but obstruct common security when commanders withheld support. A powerful central president could stabilize budgets while restricting freedom of association and opposition. Constitutions were therefore both blueprints and instruments in an ongoing contest over resources. Comparing only their written provisions obscures why some governments implemented their rules more effectively than others.

Political bargaining also depended on international circumstances. Brazil's intervention mattered to the end of Rosas's rule and to Uruguayan survival; British and French commercial pressure affected navigation in the Río de la Plata; British influence on the Mosquito Coast and the transit route shaped Central American conflicts. None of these encounters made the republics simply dependent puppets. Their leaders pursued their own interests and sometimes played foreign powers against one another. Effective national authority emerged from interaction between domestic coalitions and external pressures rather than from geographical isolation.

A final distinction concerns time. A republic might function without a formal national constitution for years, as Argentine provincial governments did after 1820, or possess an ambitious constitution without effective common administration, as the Central American federation did. State formation did not end on the date a document was signed. It continued through changes in taxation, judicial practice, military command and the acceptance—or rejection—of decisions made at the center.

Cause and Effect

Why Argentina's Unification Took Four Decades

Long-term causes

Colonial jurisdictions, unequal customs revenue, and rival port and interior interests.

Short-term causes

The collapse of 1820, the rejection of the 1826 constitution, and wars with Brazil.

Transformation

A confederation under Rosas, then constitutional unification after Caseros.

Immediate effects

Provincial autonomy and Buenos Aires's secession in 1852.

Long-term consequences

A national state under Mitre in 1862 with limited reach.

Complexity

Fiscal, military, regional, and international factors combined.

The Argentine case offers one causal pattern, not a universal equation. Chile’s mining and customs income aided a powerful central administration, but wars and revolts reveal the need for political negotiation. Central America’s federal executive lacked dependable finance, yet its failure also reflected armed factional politics and disagreements over religious reform. Paraguay’s strength drew on state ownership and compulsory labor as well as governmental organization.

When tracing cause and effect, ask what conditions enabled an action and what altered its outcome. A constitutional provision might enable an executive to appoint governors, but military allegiance and local cooperation determined whether such appointments functioned. Foreign blockades affected customs revenue, while epidemics changed the ability of households and states to sustain armies. Record consequences for multiple groups rather than assuming a national gain meant equal benefits for all.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Colony to Republic

What changed? Sovereignty moved from the crown to provinces and states, and independent armies, constitutions, and customs systems appeared.

What remained continuous? Landed hierarchy, Church influence, racial inequality, and dependence on commodity trade persisted.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban elites and military leaders. Greater continuity: Rural laborers and many Indigenous communities.

Why it matters: Political independence did not by itself transform society.

Constitutional languages changed faster than many property and labor relationships. Municipal officials who had governed under colonial rule sometimes continued to administer courts, collect levies, and influence military recruitment after independence. Yet new political affiliations could give local leaders leverage to bargain for different appointments or tax treatment. The significance of continuity therefore depends on the community and institution being studied.

Where change was most dramatic, it often came through force or collective claims rather than constitutional wording alone. Paraguay’s 1848 decree brought official citizenship and state appropriation of communal holdings together; Uruguay’s civil wars altered slavery laws while imposing military service; the Central American union defended independence in 1856–1857 despite an earlier failure to maintain federation. Compare the legal timetable with recorded changes in land tenure, household livelihoods and government access to revenue.

Section 62.55

62.55 Hierarchy, territory, and citizenship

Landowners and merchants gained most from new orders. Indigenous peoples lost land or autonomy in Uruguay, the Pampas frontier, and parts of Central America, but retained autonomy among the Mapuche south of the Biobío and negotiated with Carrera. Afro-descended people served in armies and gained emancipation in some cases. Women were excluded from formal politics but ran households, farms, and businesses and sometimes shaped politics through networks.

Republican citizenship was profoundly unequal. Some constitutions expanded formal rights among free men while excluding women, those judged economically dependent, and populations not accepted as members of the new political community. Uruguay's 1830 charter, for example, contained specific grounds for suspending civic rights that reveal how independence and citizenship could diverge. In Argentina and Chile, states claimed territories where Indigenous nations continued to exercise autonomous authority and negotiate with outsiders. A republican map therefore records a national aspiration, not a complete survey of who governed each village, river crossing or grazing district.

Military service and landholding connected formal political status to social relations. Afro-descended troops in Río de la Plata campaigns could gain legal freedom while facing coercive recruitment and dangerous duty. In Paraguay, state control of land and communal property affected the position of Guaraní-speaking communities; the 1848 decree that described Indigenous residents as citizens also nationalized property of twenty-one towns. Guatemalan Maya communities participated in regional politics through petitions, alliances and resistance, but no one party spoke for every village. Costa Rican agricultural growth benefited some farmers and merchants while creating other dependencies. These different experiences should be compared without assuming that all Indigenous or rural groups shared interests.

Women were central to the economies and survival of households across the region. They managed farms and businesses, carried supplies, protected property and sometimes sought the return of conscripted relatives. The legal forms of their participation varied according to marital status, property and class, while electoral systems generally excluded them. Official political histories frequently mention their roles only when they appear beside prominent male leaders; parish, notarial and court records can reveal broader networks of agency. The absence of women from constitutional convention debates should not be confused with absence from state formation.

Labor and territory remained sites of conflict after formal independence. The destruction and displacement of Charrúa communities in Uruguay, the limited reach of Chilean jurisdiction south of the Biobío, and coercive policies toward people on different frontiers illustrate the costs of national integration. An account of republican success that measures only stable ministries or growing revenues would fail to consider these consequences. Students should evaluate how claims of national equality operated within unequal property systems and contested territorial sovereignty.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Militias, and Markets

Most people lived in rural households. On the Pampas, ranch workers and their families were recruited into militias and might owe service to a patron. In Paraguay, peasant families farmed state-leased land and owed labor and military service. In Guatemala's highlands, communities organized work, festivals, and municipal offices. Women processed food, ran small businesses, and managed property. Recurrent warfare disrupted harvests, forced conscription, and spread disease, as with the cholera of 1837 and 1856. Surviving records are written mostly by officials, so ordinary voices appear through petitions and court cases.

In the Argentine littoral, a household might depend on cattle raising, sale of hides, seasonal work on a ranch or river transport. War could remove adult workers through militia service and reduce security on routes to market. A provincial governor’s promise of protection might matter more immediately than the wording of a constitution. For women who managed livestock or family credit during absences, political conflict altered property transactions and the ability to obtain food. Official army rolls reveal men’s service; letters, inventories and local petitions are needed to reconstruct households more fully.

In the Chilean central valley, hacienda tenants and wage laborers lived within relationships of land, work and patronage that a stronger central government did not abolish. Mining growth opened opportunities for some northern workers, but revenues and political appointments could still be concentrated in Santiago. Schooling and government offices expanded unevenly. South of the Biobío, Mapuche communities followed their own political decisions and maintained trade and diplomatic contacts across a frontier that national maps often simplified.

In the rural districts of Uruguay, an army’s requisition of horses could undermine a family’s economic survival. Afro-descended soldiers might receive promises of freedom and bear the dangers of prolonged service, while women dealt with the resulting absences and legal claims. Indigenous Charrúa families experienced state violence and forced displacement during consolidation of the ranching frontier. The history of the Guerra Grande therefore belongs to people whose homes and livelihoods were transformed, not solely the presidents and foreign admirals described in official dispatches.

Central American families experienced federal dissolution through changing local taxes, Church and municipal authority, and periodic passage of armed forces. Highland Maya communities contested land and village governance by petition or alliance. Costa Rican coffee growers could benefit from international trade yet later face conscription and cholera during the campaign against Walker. In Nicaragua, the transit route created livelihoods for boat operators and merchants while exposing villages to war and foreign control. These experiences show how the same institutional crisis could produce sharply different consequences in different regions.

Section 62.56

62.56 Why did some republics consolidate?

Consolidation correlated with fiscal control (Chile's mining and customs revenue), compact geography, a loyal army, elite agreement, and coercion. Fragmentation followed where revenue and force were divided or foreign actors intervened. These factors interacted; none alone explains the outcomes. The next chapters show how Paraguay's strong state met war in 1864, how Chile's consolidation preceded the War of the Pacific, and how export economies later transformed all these societies.

The divergence among republican systems is best explained through interacting conditions, not a ranking of peoples or constitutions. Chile's compact administrative core and access to mining and customs income strengthened governments after the conservative victory of 1830, while political repression and subsequent bargaining sustained the 1833 settlement. Argentina possessed substantial commercial resources, but their concentration in Buenos Aires deepened conflict with the provinces; the constitutional framework of 1853 required a later redistribution of political power before it could operate across the country. Paraguay built strong administrative and military capacity under highly concentrated authority, whereas Uruguay's sovereignty was maintained amid recurrent domestic factionalism and pressure from neighbors.

Central America supplies an especially revealing contrast. Five states shared an independence history and constitution, but their economies, fiscal resources and political coalitions diverged. Federal intervention amid civil war undermined trust, and the inability to fund common institutions made the union susceptible to military challenges. Yet the same states later cooperated against Walker, showing that a failure of permanent federation did not mean the absence of shared identity or mutual defense. Nicaragua's transit corridor and Costa Rica's coffee-based fiscal resources further demonstrate how different economic geographies shaped the ability to govern and fight.

These outcomes were not inevitable. Decisions by Rosas, Urquiza, Portales, Carrera, Morazán and many less celebrated provincial leaders altered the options available to their contemporaries. Foreign military pressure, epidemics and economic shocks changed political calculations. Indigenous communities, rural workers, enslaved people, women and local officials shaped the effectiveness of state claims through compliance, refusal, litigation and armed resistance. A satisfactory explanation must therefore connect structural opportunities with particular choices and with the unequal distribution of coercion and resources.

By the early 1860s, some governments had more consistent institutions than in the first decades of independence, but none had settled every question of territory, citizenship or civil authority. The later Paraguayan War, War of the Pacific, and export-era transformations would expose and reshape those unresolved tensions. Chapter 62 concludes not with a single victorious model but with a comparative method: distinguish constitutional design from implementation, fiscal capacity from social inclusion, and national sovereignty from the actual exercise of authority on the ground.

Historical Debate

What Explains Republican Instability and Consolidation?

The question: Did political outcomes arise mainly from institutions or from deeper fiscal, regional, and social conditions?

Interpretation C — Social bargaining and coercion: State durability depended on whose labor, land and political participation governments mobilized or suppressed. Indigenous resistance, military recruitment, property systems and urban political networks could sustain or undermine institutions.
Interpretation A — Institutions and leadership: Well-designed constitutions and elite pacts, such as Chile's 1833 settlement, created order; weak or unrealistic designs, like Central America's, failed.
Interpretation B — Structural conditions: Revenue, geography, regional economies, and military organization determined outcomes, and constitutions mostly reflected them.

Evidence: Treasury records, customs data, constitutional texts, and the course of civil wars. Most historians combine the views, giving weight to both.

Why it matters: If institutions matter most, designs could have saved the federation; if structure matters most, outcomes were largely constrained.

Student task: Choose one case and argue which factor mattered more, naming evidence that could change your view.

Comparative test: Contrast Chile’s executive stability with the persistent revolts of 1851 and 1859; contrast Paraguay’s state-directed modernization with the 1848 nationalization of Indigenous communal property; contrast Costa Rica’s stronger fiscal capacity with the heavy human costs of mobilization and cholera. Each case places a different burden on institutional, structural and social explanations.

Historical method: Do not accept a verdict based on one national historian or one constitutional preamble. Examine tax records, army muster rolls, electoral qualifications, municipal petitions and contemporaneous accounts that disagree about the same event. Students should specify what evidence could falsify their preferred interpretation.

Chapter Summary

The dissolution of the Spanish imperial system created disputes not only between new nations and Spain but also among cities, provinces and regions that had belonged to a common jurisdiction. Municipal councils, provincial legislatures and military leaders developed competing claims to sovereignty. Political actors used constitutions and treaties to articulate those claims, yet effective authority required regular taxation, courts, recruitment and administrative recognition. The resulting diversity was not evidence that particular populations lacked the capacity for republican government. It reflected the difficulty of deciding which community had the right to govern and the unequal resources available to competing centers.

In Argentina, defeat of the central government at Cepeda in 1820 opened a prolonged phase of provincial political sovereignty. Local governors organized legislatures, raised revenues and entered treaties, while Artigas’s earlier League had advanced a federal and redistributive vision in the Eastern Province. Buenos Aires reformers developed ambitious educational, financial and ecclesiastical programs, but Rivadavia’s national presidency and the rejected 1826 constitution demonstrated that a centralist plan could not operate through the provinces without consent. The war with Brazil strained finances, and the execution of Manuel Dorrego in 1828 turned constitutional disagreement into renewed armed conflict.

Rosas subsequently constructed a confederal system in which provincial governors retained formal authority while Buenos Aires managed much foreign representation and collected the principal Atlantic customs revenue. The Federal Pact of January 1831 organized alliance and established a temporary Representative Commission, but that commission disappeared in 1832. Rosas’s powers in Buenos Aires became increasingly extensive after 1835, and his rule combined patronage, political symbolism, repression and negotiation with Indigenous frontier leaders. Provincial dissatisfaction with his trade and river policies, supported by foreign and regional alliances, ultimately enabled Urquiza to overthrow him at Caseros in 1852.

Argentina’s 1853 constitution created a durable legal framework but did not immediately integrate Buenos Aires, which maintained its own government and customs house. The Confederation governed from Paraná while rival leaders debated fiscal and military arrangements. The 1859 conflict at Cepeda, the Pact of San José de Flores, constitutional revision and the Battle of Pavón in 1861 preceded Mitre’s national presidency in 1862. These events show why adopting a constitution and achieving political integration were distinct processes. Even after unification, national authority remained uneven, especially in Indigenous territories and provinces where local elites controlled important resources.

Chile moved from constitutional experimentation and regional opposition toward stronger central government following the conservative victory at Lircay in 1830. The constitution of 1833 placed substantial authority in the executive, but its continued operation depended on administrative appointments, military support, revenue and elite bargaining. Prieto, Bulnes and Montt governed through a relatively continuous institutional framework. Mining, Valparaíso’s commercial position and the development of education strengthened the state, while the revolts of 1851 and 1859 demonstrate that stability coexisted with dissent and repression. Mapuche communities retained significant autonomy south of the Biobío despite Chilean claims of national sovereignty.

Uruguay emerged from Artiguist movements, Portuguese-Brazilian occupation, the 1825 uprising and the 1828 diplomatic settlement as a new sovereign state. Its 1830 constitution provided national institutions but did not prevent conflict among rival armed political networks. Rivera and Oribe became associated with the Colorado and Blanco traditions, and the Guerra Grande from 1839 to 1851 divided the republic between rival authorities. Foreign intervention affected the siege of Montevideo, but Uruguayan leaders and communities pursued interests of their own. Wartime abolition under separate administrations and state violence against Charrúa communities reveal the uneven social meaning of republican independence.

Paraguay resisted incorporation under Buenos Aires and developed an unusually concentrated state under José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia. Restrictions on political opposition and foreign commerce coexisted with administrative organization and arrangements for state landholding. After his death, the consulate and the presidency of Carlos Antonio López pursued controlled modernization, including schools, transport and industrial works. The law of 1844 gave legal form to executive predominance. The decree of 1848 illustrates a crucial contradiction: it granted citizenship to residents of twenty-one Indigenous towns while transferring their communal property to the state. Paraguay’s strength should therefore be analyzed alongside coercion, dispossession and unequal participation.

Central America’s federal constitution of 1824 attempted to join five states while preserving their separate institutions. The Congress and Senate had distinct constitutional functions, but a complex design could not compensate for unreliable fiscal contributions, divided armed forces and competing state and federal claims. Conflict under Manuel José Arce and Francisco Morazán, disputes surrounding ecclesiastical reforms, rural grievances and epidemics contributed to repeated civil wars. Rafael Carrera’s coalition challenged liberal authority in Guatemala, while Los Altos revealed divisions within the highlands. The federation fragmented through withdrawals and military losses rather than through a single consensual dissolution.

After the federation, Guatemala’s conservative consolidation, El Salvador’s liberal and federalist traditions, and Honduras’s difficult fiscal and regional administration followed distinct paths. Carrera negotiated with religious authorities, merchants and rural communities while retaining powerful coercive means. Guatemala’s 1847 declaration of separate republican sovereignty still left open a possible future Central American union. Attempts at confederation, including Chinandega and later pacts, failed to supply dependable institutions. The battle of La Arada demonstrated the military consequences of rival regional projects without eliminating the enduring appeal of cooperation among the Central American republics.

Costa Rica developed stronger central institutions around a growing coffee economy, yet its political history also included coercive leadership, social inequality and later conflict over executive power. Nicaragua’s rivalry between León and Granada made governments vulnerable to foreign military assistance. The San Juan River and Lake Nicaragua transit corridor attracted commercial and imperial competition, while Miskito political autonomy challenged simplified maps of national territory. William Walker used Nicaraguan civil war to establish a coercive regime and in 1856 annulled federal legislation that had abolished slavery. Costa Rican forces fought the early campaign against him, and a wider Central American coalition later succeeded in defeating him in 1857.

Across the cases, national consolidation was neither automatically good for all inhabitants nor determined solely by the wording of a constitution. Ports and customs receipts financed governments but encouraged conflicts over redistribution; armed commanders could support provincial rights or suppress them; foreign intervention altered bargains without eliminating local agency. Indigenous nations, Afro-descended communities, enslaved and freed people, rural households and women shaped political outcomes through alliances, labor, petitions, commerce and resistance, despite exclusion from many formal institutions. The later wars and export transformations examined in Chapters 64–69 would develop from these partially consolidated states and still-unresolved questions of sovereignty, land, representation and coercion.

Commercial and diplomatic principle contested in the Río de la Plata and Paraguay river systems.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the end of empire opened rather than closed the question of political order. The same imperial heritage produced confederation, centralization, personal rule, and failed union.

The outcomes shaped what came next: Paraguay's capable but isolated state met war in 1864, Chile's consolidated order prepared its Pacific expansion, and Argentina's unification set the stage for export-era growth. Central America's fragmented states entered the commodity age separately.

Chapter 63 turns to Mexico's wars with the United States and France, where similar questions about sovereignty and reform recur under external pressure.

Documentary Edition Register and Editorial Source Note

The thirteen Primary Source Windows summarize documents rather than presenting invented direct quotations. Most link to transcribed constitutional, treaty or decree texts; the Paraguay 1848 and Costa Rica proclamation windows identify institutional archival or documentary collections and distinguish source identification from unexamined archival implementation. The selected documents do not constitute exhaustive verification of every secondary historical statement in the chapter.

  1. Treaty of Pilar, 23 February 1820 — Published transcription of the treaty; the article numbering belongs to the original agreement.
  2. Artigas’s Provisional Rural Regulation, 10 September 1815 — Uruguay Educa presentation of the numbered provisions of the 1815 regulation.
  3. Federal Pact, 4 January 1831 — Full transcription published by Argentina’s Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Históricas Juan Manuel de Rosas.
  4. Constitution for the Argentine Confederation, 1 May 1853 — Infoleg transcription of the original 1853 text; do not substitute a later amended constitution without noting revisions.
  5. Chilean Constitution, 8 August 1828 — Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional de Chile historical constitutional record and text access.
  6. Chilean Constitution, 25 May 1833 — Biblioteca del Congreso Nacional de Chile, original constitutional text dated 25 May 1833.
  7. Constitution of the Oriental Republic of Uruguay, 1830 — Uruguayan parliamentary archive; original text is linked from the finding aid. The constitution was publicly sworn 18 July 1830.
  8. Paraguayan Law Establishing Political Administration, 13 March 1844 — Transcription of the 1844 law as sanctioned 13 March and promulgated 16 March; corroborated by the Paraguayan Chamber of Deputies historical record.
  9. Paraguayan Decree Affecting Twenty-One Indigenous Towns, 7 October 1848 — Secretaría Nacional de Cultura del Paraguay, documentary analysis of the 1848 decree.
  10. Federal Constitution of Central America, 22 November 1824 — Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes, complete Spanish transcription of the 1824 constitution.
  11. Guatemala’s Declaration as a Republic, 21 March 1847 — Guatemala Ministry of Foreign Affairs documentary catalogue for Manifesto and Decree No. 15, dated 21 March 1847; compare a full text edition for each clause.
  12. William Walker’s Decree Revoking Federal Laws, 22 September 1856 — Instituto de Historia de Nicaragua y Centroamérica documentary edition, No. 115, reproducing El Nicaragüense of 27 September 1856.
  13. Juan Rafael Mora Porras’s Warning to Costa Ricans, 20 November 1855 — Archivo Nacional de Costa Rica catalogue of Mora’s messages and proclamations; 20 November 1855 warning is listed and a historical published transcript is also available.

Interpretive framework: study constitutional text alongside provincial treasury records, diplomatic correspondence, municipal petitions, court proceedings, Indigenous diplomatic records, military rolls, and scholarship attentive to social difference.
