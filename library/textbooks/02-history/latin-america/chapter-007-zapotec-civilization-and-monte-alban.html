Zapotec Civilization and Monte Albán

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A City Built on a Hill That Had No Water

Around 500 BCE, people living in three separate arms of a fertile highland valley in what is today the Mexican state of Oaxaca made a striking decision: rather than continuing to build their largest communities on the well-watered valley floor, they began moving substantial numbers of people onto a defensible but comparatively dry hilltop rising nearly four hundred meters above the surrounding plain. Within a few generations, that hilltop — later named Monte Albán by Spanish-speaking visitors, though its Zapotec-language name or names in antiquity are not securely known — had become one of the largest and most politically complex cities anywhere in Mesoamerica.

Chapter 6 examined the Olmec world of the Gulf Coast lowlands and closed by rejecting a simple "mother culture" model in which Gulf Coast societies alone generated Mesoamerican civilization while neighboring regions merely received it. Oaxaca supplies one of the clearest demonstrations of why that rejection matters. Long before Monte Albán existed, communities in the Valley of Oaxaca — most notably the town archaeologists call San José Mogote — had already developed public architecture, social ranking, evidence of interregional exchange and interregional conflict, and some of Mesoamerica's earliest experiments with calendrical notation and hieroglyphic writing. Oaxacan societies were in dialogue with Gulf Coast, Pacific coastal, and central Mexican traditions, borrowing certain symbols, materials, and ideas while developing others independently and on their own timeline.

The eventual founding of Monte Albán was not, therefore, an imitation of an Olmec or any other outside model transplanted wholesale into the highlands. It emerged from a specifically Oaxacan history of competing valley communities, in which political rivalry, the threat or practice of warfare, ritual life, and long-distance exchange combined to produce, within a relatively short span of time, a new kind of settlement: a large, planned, hilltop city that appears to have served, from very early in its history, as a shared political and religious center for peoples drawn from multiple parts of the valley.

This chapter follows that story from the towns of the valley floor through the dramatic founding of the hilltop city, the construction of its monumental plaza, and its growth into a genuine early state — one of the earliest in the Americas for which extensive archaeological and textual evidence survives together. It also follows Monte Albán through its most controversial evidence: the carved stone figures long called "Danzantes," or "dancers," and the later carved slabs on Building J that many archaeologists have read as records of military conquest. Both bodies of evidence remain actively debated, and this chapter treats that debate honestly rather than presenting either the figures or the stones as settled proof of a fully reconstructed political history.

Monte Albán also matters because of what it can tell historians that purely material evidence cannot. Beginning by at least the sixth century BCE and developing substantially in the centuries that followed, Zapotec communities in Oaxaca produced one of Mesoamerica's earliest sustained traditions of hieroglyphic writing and calendrical notation — a tradition documented in inscribed monuments, carved building stones, and painted or incised ceramics that allow historians to move, cautiously, from purely material inference toward evidence that at least gestures toward names, dates, and specific historical claims made by the people who carved it. Recent scholarship, including a 2026 reanalysis of early Monte Albán hieroglyphic texts, has refined the dating and interpretation of this tradition considerably, and this chapter incorporates that reanalysis directly rather than repeating older, now-revised claims.

Finally, this chapter insists on a distinction that recurs throughout Volume I: the eventual political decline of Monte Albán as a dominant regional capital, beginning in the centuries after roughly 700–800 CE, did not mean the disappearance of the Zapotec people. Zapotec communities, language, and cultural traditions continued in Oaxaca long after Monte Albán's central plaza ceased to function as a great capital, a continuity examined further in Chapter 11's treatment of the Mixtec and Zapotec worlds of the Postclassic period. Political centers rise and decline; peoples endure.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the environmental and political setting of the Valley of Oaxaca before the founding of Monte Albán, including the role of San José Mogote.
  2. Explain the competing hypotheses historians and archaeologists have proposed for why Monte Albán was founded around 500 BCE.
  3. Analyze the engineering and labor challenges involved in building a major city on a defensible but water-scarce hilltop.
  4. Evaluate the evidence archaeologists use to argue that Monte Albán functioned as an early state, and the limits of that evidence.
  5. Assess competing interpretations of the carved figures conventionally called the Danzantes without treating any single interpretation as fully settled.
  6. Distinguish evidence for Monte Albán's political and military power from stronger claims about external conquest, territorial extent, or a uniformly administered "empire."
  7. Trace the development of early Zapotec hieroglyphic writing and calendrical notation and explain its significance relative to other early Mesoamerican writing traditions, including the Gulf Coast evidence discussed in Chapter 6.
  8. Examine Zapotec religious life, mortuary practice, and elite tombs, while recognizing the limits of projecting later-documented deities backward onto the earliest periods.
  9. Explain Monte Albán's connections to other Mesoamerican regions, including the Gulf Coast and, later, Teotihuacan.
  10. Describe the transformation and political decline of Monte Albán after roughly 700–800 CE and distinguish this decline from the disappearance of the Zapotec people.
  11. Apply careful historical reasoning to actively revised scholarship, including the 2026 reanalysis of early Zapotec hieroglyphic chronology.
  12. Connect Oaxacan political and cultural development to the broader Mesoamerican world examined throughout Part II of this volume.

Historical Context

Chapter 5 traced the emergence of settled agricultural life and the first Mesoamerican villages, and Chapter 6 examined the Olmec world of the Gulf Coast lowlands, a region whose monumental art, exchange networks, and early writing tradition — represented by the authentic but still-undeciphered and contextually unusual Cascajal Block — raised difficult questions about how much of early Mesoamerican civilization originated in one place and diffused outward, and how much developed independently in multiple regions in dialogue with one another. This chapter turns to the Valley of Oaxaca, a highland region in southern Mexico whose own trajectory toward urbanism, writing, and state formation offers one of the clearest test cases for that question.

The Valley of Oaxaca sits at an elevation of roughly 1,500 meters in the rugged highlands of southern Mexico, considerably higher and drier than the tropical Gulf Coast lowlands examined in the previous chapter. The valley is not a single continuous plain but three converging arms — commonly called the Etla, Tlacolula, and Valle Grande (or Zaachila) arms — that meet near the center of the valley, precisely where Monte Albán's hilltop rises above the converging floodplains. This tripartite geography mattered enormously for the political history examined in this chapter, because for centuries before Monte Albán's founding, the three arms of the valley were occupied by separate, often competing communities rather than a single unified population.

Agricultural life in the valley depended on a mix of rainfall farming and, especially in drier areas and drier seasons, irrigation drawing on wells, canals, and seasonal streams. Maize, beans, and squash formed the agricultural foundation, supplemented by chili peppers, other cultivated and wild plants, and hunted game. The valley's relatively dry, temperate climate differed sharply from the humid tropical lowlands of the Gulf Coast, requiring different agricultural strategies, water management techniques, and settlement patterns — a reminder that Mesoamerican civilizations developed within a mosaic of distinct environments rather than a single uniform ecological setting.

By early in the first millennium BCE, communities in the Etla arm of the valley, especially the town archaeologists call San José Mogote, had already developed some of the hallmarks of complex society examined in Chapter 5's discussion of early villages and Chapter 6's discussion of Gulf Coast civilization: public architecture, evidence of social ranking, specialized craft production, exchange of valued materials such as marine shell, obsidian, mica, and magnetite over long distances, and, as this chapter discusses in detail, some of the earliest surviving evidence anywhere in Mesoamerica for both organized violence between communities and experimentation with calendrical and hieroglyphic notation. Oaxacan communities exchanged materials, motifs, and probably ideas with Gulf Coast Olmec communities and with other regions, but archaeological evidence increasingly supports understanding this exchange as a two-way, multi-regional process rather than one-directional transmission from a single "mother culture," consistent with the more cautious model introduced at the end of Chapter 6.

Section 7.1

Oaxaca Before Monte Albán

For roughly a thousand years before Monte Albán's founding, the Valley of Oaxaca was already home to settled agricultural communities whose history is not simply a prelude to the hilltop city but a significant period of Mesoamerican civilization in its own right. As Chapter 5 explained for early Mesoamerican villages generally, communities in the valley cultivated maize, beans, and squash, supplemented their diet with chili peppers and wild plant and animal resources, and gradually developed larger, more socially differentiated settlements over many generations.

The valley's tripartite geography shaped this development from the outset. The Etla arm, extending northwest from the valley's center, the Tlacolula arm, extending east, and the Valle Grande or Zaachila arm, extending south, each supported their own local communities, exchange networks, and, over time, political identities. Before roughly 500 BCE, no single community dominated all three arms simultaneously; instead, the valley's political landscape more closely resembled a set of competing chiefly centers, each drawing on the resources and population of its own arm, occasionally cooperating and occasionally coming into conflict with neighboring centers.

Chapter 6 closed its discussion of the Olmec world by rejecting a simple "mother culture" model in which Gulf Coast civilization alone generated the broader phenomena associated with early Mesoamerican complexity, proposing instead a "sister cultures" framework in which multiple regions developed in dialogue with one another. The Valley of Oaxaca supplies some of the clearest supporting evidence for that framework. Oaxacan communities exchanged goods and, very likely, ideas and religious concepts with Gulf Coast communities: excavated Oaxacan sites have yielded imported Gulf Coast-style ceramics and iconographic motifs, while Gulf Coast sites have yielded materials, including magnetite ore used for polished mirrors, that most plausibly originated in or near the Oaxaca region. This exchange demonstrates genuine interregional contact. It does not, however, demonstrate that Oaxacan political institutions, architecture, or ideology were simply copied from the Gulf Coast; the specific trajectory toward urbanism and writing that this chapter traces developed on a distinctly Oaxacan timeline and took distinctly Oaxacan forms.

Section 7.2

San José Mogote and Early Political Competition

By roughly 1150 BCE, San José Mogote, located in the Etla arm of the valley, had grown into the largest and most complex community in the entire Valley of Oaxaca. Excavations there have revealed public buildings interpreted as early temples or council structures, evidence of craft specialization including the production of ornaments from imported marine shell and worked magnetite, and burials suggesting differences in wealth and status among the town's inhabitants. San José Mogote's role as the valley's leading center for several centuries closely parallels the kind of early political and religious centrality Chapter 5 described for other early Mesoamerican villages, but on a scale and with a degree of craft specialization that place it among the more complex communities of its period anywhere in Mesoamerica.

San José Mogote's dominance did not go uncontested. Archaeological evidence from roughly 900 to 700 BCE indicates episodes of intercommunity conflict within the valley, including the deliberate burning of an important public building at San José Mogote itself. This evidence should not be overstated into a full narrative of continuous warfare, since the archaeological record for this period remains fragmentary and open to more than one interpretation regarding the frequency, scale, and specific causes of such conflicts. It does, however, indicate that the political landscape of the pre-Monte Albán valley involved genuine competition among communities in different arms of the valley, competition that plausibly included both cooperative exchange and periodic violence, rather than a single, peacefully unified regional society.

It is within this context of competition, exchange, and periodic conflict that Monument 3 was carved, an object discussed in detail in the Primary Source Window below and in Section 7.3, and it is against this same background of rival valley-floor centers that the eventual decision to found a wholly new, shared hilltop settlement should be understood.

Section 7.3

Writing, Calendars, and Historical Evidence

Chapter 6 introduced the Cascajal Block from the Gulf Coast lowlands, an object most current specialists now accept as an authentic Olmec-era inscribed artifact, even though its script remains undeciphered and its archaeological recovery context is unusual, since it was found out of clear stratigraphic association with a datable structure. This chapter must be similarly careful with Oaxacan evidence. Older textbooks and popular accounts have sometimes claimed that Oaxaca produced "the earliest writing in Mesoamerica," a claim this textbook does not repeat, because the comparative dating and interpretation of the earliest inscribed materials from multiple regions remain actively studied, and because "earliest" depends heavily on how narrowly or broadly a given mark or symbol is defined as writing rather than as non-linguistic symbolic notation.

A more defensible and more useful historical claim is this: the Valley of Oaxaca provides one of Mesoamerica's earliest securely contextualized traditions of calendrical notation, and, beginning at Monte Albán, the earliest substantial corpus of hieroglyphic texts currently documented anywhere in Mesoamerica — meaning not a single isolated inscribed object, however early or authentic, but a growing body of related inscriptions that can be arranged in a meaningful chronological and contextual sequence and studied as a developing tradition over time. This distinction matters. The Cascajal Block may well be an earlier individual artifact than any single Monte Albán text discussed in Section 7.9, and San José Mogote's Monument 3, conventionally dated to around 600 BCE, may also be earlier than the bulk of the Monte Albán corpus, but both survive as comparatively isolated examples rather than as part of a large, internally comparable textual sequence. Monte Albán, by contrast, preserves dozens of related carved texts that specialists can compare, cross-reference, and analyze for internal chronological and iconographic development — a research advantage that neither the undeciphered Cascajal Block nor the isolated Monument 3, each discussed further below, currently offers on its own.

This distinction — between plausible chronological priority on one hand and a genuinely rich, contextualized, comparable body of texts on the other — is itself an important lesson in historical method. A single early object, however striking, often cannot support the same kind of historical reconstruction that a developing corpus of related texts can support. Section 7.9 returns to this point in detail once the chapter has established the broader political history of Monte Albán within which its writing tradition developed.

Section 7.4

Why Found Monte Albán?

Around 500 BCE, a striking transformation occurred in the Valley of Oaxaca: a previously lightly settled hilltop rising steeply above the point where the valley's three arms converge began to attract a large and rapidly growing population, apparently drawn from multiple existing valley-floor communities, including San José Mogote itself, which declined sharply in size and importance at almost precisely the moment Monte Albán's population began to grow. This is not a gradual, unplanned urban drift of the kind that sometimes occurs as villages slowly expand into towns; the evidence instead suggests a relatively rapid, and quite possibly deliberate, founding event.

Archaeologists have proposed several explanations for why people would choose to relocate substantial populations onto a hilltop that offered no permanent natural water source, required extensive terracing before it could support significant agriculture or dense settlement, and stood at a considerable elevation above the valley's most fertile farmland. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and most current scholarship treats Monte Albán's founding as the product of several converging factors rather than any single cause.

One prominent explanation emphasizes defensibility. Monte Albán's hilltop location, visible for great distances across all three arms of the valley, would have offered a strong defensive position at a time when, as Section 7.2 discussed, intercommunity conflict was a real feature of valley political life. A second explanation emphasizes neutrality and cooperation: because the hilltop did not belong to the traditional territory of any single existing valley-floor community, its selection as a shared center may have represented a negotiated compromise among rival groups from different arms of the valley, each unwilling to accept the political dominance of a rival center but willing to invest in a new, shared capital that no single faction could claim as exclusively its own. A third explanation emphasizes strategic control of movement and exchange, since the hilltop's position at the valley's geographic center would have allowed its inhabitants unusually effective oversight of trade and travel routes connecting all three arms.

Each of these explanations finds some support in the archaeological evidence, and each faces limitations. The defensive explanation is supported by the site's commanding, easily monitored position, but it does not by itself explain why a shared new capital, rather than fortification of an existing center, was the chosen response to conflict. The cooperative-founding explanation is supported by evidence, discussed further in Section 7.6, that Monte Albán's earliest population appears to have been drawn from multiple valley communities rather than expanding outward from a single dominant group, but this evidence cannot by itself establish the specific political negotiations, alliances, or coercive pressures that may have accompanied the founding. The exchange-control explanation is supported by the site's central geographic position but is difficult to test independently of the other two explanations. Students should understand Monte Albán's founding as a genuinely important, and only partially resolved, historical problem rather than a settled fact with a single confirmed cause.

Section 7.5

Building the Hilltop City

Whatever combination of motives lay behind Monte Albán's founding, converting the hilltop into a functioning city required an extraordinary and sustained investment of collective labor. The summit itself needed extensive leveling and terracing before it could support the dense residential occupation, monumental architecture, and agricultural terracing that archaeologists have documented across the site. Within the first two centuries of occupation, conventionally labeled the Early I period, the population of Monte Albán appears to have grown rapidly, quite possibly reaching several thousand inhabitants, a scale of population concentration that few, if any, earlier Mesoamerican settlements had achieved this quickly.

Because the hilltop lacked a permanent natural water source, Monte Albán's residents developed a genuinely substantial engineered water-control system rather than relying primarily on carried supplies. Archaeological survey has documented extensive agricultural terracing across the surrounding slopes, built specifically to capture and manage rainfall runoff and reduce erosion; numerous constructed reservoirs, drains, and catchment features integrated into the terracing and residential architecture; and, on the piedmont below the city at Xoxocotlán, a documented Late Formative dam-and-canal irrigation system that extended cultivable land well beyond what rainfall agriculture alone could support. This engineered infrastructure worked alongside, rather than instead of, continued agricultural production on the valley floor, from which additional food could be transported up to the hilltop settlement. The combination illustrates that Monte Albán, however dramatic its hilltop setting, never functioned in isolation from the surrounding valley; the city, its engineered water systems, and its agricultural hinterland were interdependent from the outset.

The heart of the new city was its Main Plaza, an immense leveled and paved open space, oriented along a roughly north-south axis, around which the city's most important temples, elite residences, and later, ballcourts and administrative structures were arranged over the following centuries. The scale of labor required to level this plaza and to terrace the surrounding hillsides for residential occupation indicates a population capable of sustained, coordinated collective effort — evidence that some form of effective political authority or widely shared communal commitment, or some combination of the two, organized Monte Albán's construction from a very early stage in its history.

Section 7.6

From Regional Center to Early State

Archaeologists generally regard Monte Albán, by some point in its early centuries, as having developed into one of the earliest genuine states in the Americas — a political system marked not simply by a large population or impressive architecture but by a settlement hierarchy in which smaller communities throughout the valley, and eventually well beyond it, were integrated into a system administered, at least in some respects, from the hilltop capital. Evidence for this settlement hierarchy comes from regional archaeological surveys documenting multiple tiers of settlement size across the valley — a small number of very large centers, a somewhat larger number of mid-sized towns, and a great many small villages and hamlets — a pattern archaeologists generally interpret as consistent with some degree of centralized political and economic integration rather than a landscape of entirely independent, equally sized communities.

Other evidence consistent with early statehood includes marked differences in the size, construction quality, and contents of residences and burials at Monte Albán itself, suggesting a genuinely stratified society with an elite population distinct from commoners; specialized craft production, including fine ceramics and worked stone and shell ornaments, concentrated in particular areas of the site; and the sheer scale of monumental construction, which most archaeologists interpret as requiring some form of centralized labor mobilization, whether through coercive tribute obligations, negotiated communal contribution, religiously sanctioned obligation, or some combination of these.

Students should nonetheless treat claims about the precise administrative machinery of the early Monte Albán state with appropriate caution. Coordinated large-scale construction is strong evidence of organized collective effort, but it does not, by itself, prove the existence of a fully bureaucratic administrative apparatus, a single supreme ruler analogous to a later Mesoamerican king, or a codified system of taxation. Much of what historians can confidently say concerns the observable results of political organization — settlement hierarchy, monumental construction, social stratification — rather than the specific internal workings of Monte Albán's early government, which remain considerably less well documented than the comparable institutions of later Mesoamerican states examined in Parts II and III of this volume.

Section 7.7

The Danzantes: Ritual, Rulership, and Warfare

Among the most striking and most debated evidence from Monte Albán's early centuries is a large collection of carved stone slabs — more than three hundred surviving examples, primarily associated with a structure archaeologists call Building L-sub — depicting human figures in unusual postures: reclining, contorted, often with closed or half-closed eyes, and in some cases displaying features that some interpreters have read as evidence of physical alteration. Early twentieth-century archaeologists, struck by the figures' fluid, twisting body positions, gave them the now-conventional name "Danzantes," meaning "dancers" in Spanish. This chapter uses that name because it remains the standard term in scholarship and site signage, but students must understand clearly that "Danzantes" is a modern descriptive label reflecting an early and now firmly rejected interpretation, not an ancient Zapotec term or a settled statement about what the figures actually depict. The bulk of this corpus is associated with Monte Albán's earliest occupation, the Danibaan phase and early Monte Albán Ia (roughly 500–300 BCE), with some individual monuments securely dated to around 450–350 BCE, a considerably broader and earlier window than older accounts sometimes suggested.

The original "dancing" interpretation is rejected by essentially all current specialists, but the corpus should not be reduced to a single alternative reading either. Scholarship published in 2025 and 2026 argues that the more than three hundred Building L-sub orthostats are not a uniform category of imagery and instead combine several distinct themes. A substantial portion of the figures have been reinterpreted as depicting ritual specialists or rulers performing genital autosacrifice — the deliberate, ritualized bloodletting attested across many later Mesoamerican societies — in order to invoke ancestors or supernatural beings, a reading that reframes postures once assumed to show death or unconsciousness as postures of ritual trance or transformation instead. Other monuments in the same architectural context reference rulership, enthronement, and political authority through accompanying glyphic elements. A smaller set of figures continues to be read plausibly as depicting the defeat, capture, or sacrifice of rival individuals. Some 2026 analysis further connects a subset of the imagery to what researchers describe as "oracular warfare," in which ritual self-sacrifice and ancestor invocation were bound up with the sanctioning or interpretation of military action rather than being wholly separate categories of activity.

The historical lesson here is not that scholars have simply replaced one confident answer with another. It is that a large, stylistically varied corpus of carved figures very likely served more than one purpose simultaneously — commemorating rulers, invoking ancestors through ritual self-sacrifice, and, in some cases, recording the defeat of rivals — and that forcing every figure into a single explanatory category, whether "dancers," "sacrificial victims," or any other single label, oversimplifies genuinely mixed evidence. The Evidence and Interpretation feature below examines this problem in greater depth, modeling the kind of careful evaluation of ambiguous and evolving archaeological evidence that this textbook asks of students throughout Volume I.

Section 7.8

Building J, Inscribed Slabs, and Political Claims

During the period archaeologists label Monte Albán II, roughly 200 BCE to 200 CE, the city's rulers constructed an unusually shaped building, oriented differently from the rest of the Main Plaza and conventionally labeled Building J, whose exterior walls incorporate a series of carved stone slabs. Many of these slabs combine a distinctive glyph — often described as a hill or place sign, sometimes shown inverted or accompanied by additional imagery suggesting defeat or destruction — with accompanying hieroglyphic signs long read by many twentieth-century archaeologists as a list of place names representing communities conquered by Monte Albán, extending well beyond the Valley of Oaxaca. For several decades, these "conquest slabs" were presented in scholarship and in popular accounts as reasonably direct evidence of a militarily assembled Monte Albán "empire."

This interpretation has been substantially revised by more recent epigraphic and archaeological work, and this chapter presents the older conquest-list reading as an important historiographical interpretation rather than as an established fact. A detailed 2017 reassessment of the Building J texts concluded that the script remains undeciphered in crucial respects, and argued that the proposed identifications of numerous distinct conquered place names are epigraphically and structurally implausible; on this reading, the slabs cannot, by themselves, establish the territorial extent of a Monte Albán empire. Building on this reassessment, synthesis work published in 2025 goes further, arguing that many of the carved slabs now set into Building J were reused from an earlier architectural context, that their original placement is unknown, and that recent analysis increasingly favors reading them as referring to a single place — quite possibly Monte Albán itself — rather than to a long list of distant conquered communities. On this view, several of the figures associated with these slabs more plausibly depict revered rulers or ancestors than defeated foreign enemies.

Students should draw two lessons from this revised scholarship. First, the Building J material still provides genuine evidence that political authority, ancestral legitimacy, rulership, and sacrificial practice were important themes in Monte Albán II's public monumental program; that broader point has not been overturned. Its relationship to warfare or the conquest of specific external communities, by contrast, is now considerably more disputed than older accounts suggested. Second, the specific and once-confident claim that these slabs record a long list of named, distant conquered towns — and therefore chart the borders of a Monte Albán "empire" — is no longer well supported and should not be presented to students as settled fact. Historians should avoid describing Monte Albán II as having governed a clearly bounded, uniformly administered empire on the strength of this evidence alone; the more defensible claim is that Monte Albán's rulers used monumental art, including reused and recontextualized slabs whose original meaning may already have shifted by the time they were installed in Building J, to assert political authority and ancestral legitimacy, in ways whose relationship to external military conquest current scholarship is still working to disentangle.

Section 7.9

Zapotec Writing and Calendrical Knowledge

Section 7.3 introduced the general significance of Monte Albán's hieroglyphic tradition relative to other early Mesoamerican writing evidence. This section develops that tradition in greater detail. Early Zapotec hieroglyphic texts combine calendrical signs, place glyphs, and probable personal or title glyphs, carved onto stone monuments associated with public buildings, elite tombs, and, as Section 7.8 discussed, structures commemorating military and political claims. Two calendrical systems appear throughout this textual tradition and throughout later Mesoamerican calendars examined in subsequent chapters: a 260-day ritual calendar combining twenty day names with the numbers one through thirteen, and a 365-day calendar approximating the solar year. Together, these two calendars combined to produce a longer fifty-two-year cycle, a calendrical structure that recurs, with variations, across many later Mesoamerican civilizations, including the Maya and Mexica traditions examined in Parts II and III of this volume.

Scholarly understanding of the precise chronology of Monte Albán's earliest hieroglyphic texts has been substantially refined in recent years. A study published in Latin American Antiquity in June 2026 reanalyzed the corpus of Early and Middle Preclassic Zapotec hieroglyphic texts from Monte Albán, proposing a chronological range of approximately 496 to 221 BCE for this early textual tradition — a dating that places the beginning of Monte Albán's substantial hieroglyphic corpus close to the city's own founding around 500 BCE. The same study offered a refined analysis of a recurring calendrical sign known to specialists as Glyph W, arguing, on the basis of its patterning across multiple inscriptions, that it most plausibly functioned as a lunar-day count rather than as one of the more familiar solar-calendar or ritual-calendar signs. This reanalysis illustrates a broader pattern this textbook has emphasized since Chapter 2: even well-studied bodies of evidence continue to be reexamined and refined as scholars apply new analytical methods, and students should expect the precise dating and interpretation of specific glyphs to continue developing in future scholarship.

Compared with the authentic but undeciphered and contextually isolated Cascajal Block discussed in Chapter 6, the Monte Albán corpus offers historians a genuine, internally comparable sequence of related texts, allowing specialists to trace the gradual development of specific signs, their combinations, and their association with particular kinds of monuments over roughly three centuries. This does not resolve the question of which Mesoamerican region produced the single "earliest" example of writing in the strictest sense, since an authentic early artifact and a decipherable, extensive corpus answer different historical questions; it does, however, make Oaxaca one of the most historically valuable regions for studying how a Mesoamerican writing tradition actually developed and changed over time, because the evidence survives in sufficient quantity and context to support that kind of developmental analysis.

Section 7.10

Religion, Ancestors, Tombs, and Mortuary Practice

Religious life at Monte Albán, as at other early Mesoamerican centers, was closely bound up with agricultural fertility, rain, the natural landscape, and, especially, the veneration of ancestors. Temples arranged around the Main Plaza served as settings for public ritual, while elaborate underground tombs, particularly associated with the residences of Monte Albán's elite population, provide some of the richest surviving evidence for Zapotec religious practice and beliefs about death and the afterlife.

Many of these tombs were constructed with carefully built stone chambers, painted or carved interior surfaces, and offerings including fine ceramic vessels, some in the shape of deities, ancestors, or supernatural beings, along with jade, shell, and other valued materials. Some tombs show evidence of reopening and reuse over extended periods, suggesting continued ritual attention to specific ancestors long after their initial burial — a pattern consistent with broader Mesoamerican traditions of ancestor veneration in which deceased elite individuals continued to play an active role in the political and religious life of their descendants.

Students should exercise particular caution when encountering popular accounts that attach specific, fully developed deity names — often drawn from much later colonial-era Zapotec religious documentation — directly onto the earliest phases of Monte Albán's religious imagery. While broad continuities in Mesoamerican religious themes, including rain, fertility, and ancestor veneration, plausibly extend across many centuries, the precise identity, name, and attributes of specific supernatural figures represented in Monte Albán's earlier imagery cannot always be established with confidence, and historians must avoid projecting later, better-documented religious systems uncritically backward onto centuries-earlier material for which comparably detailed textual explanation does not survive.

Section 7.11

Monte Albán in the Wider Mesoamerican World

Monte Albán never developed in isolation. Throughout its history, the city maintained exchange relationships and cultural contact with other Mesoamerican regions, including continued connections to Gulf Coast communities descended from the world examined in Chapter 6, exchange networks extending toward the Pacific coast, and, especially from the Monte Albán IIIA period onward (roughly 200–500 CE), substantial evidence of interaction with the great central Mexican metropolis of Teotihuacan, examined in full in Chapter 8.

Archaeologists have identified a neighborhood at Teotihuacan, conventionally called the "Oaxaca Barrio," containing pottery, burial practices, and architectural features consistent with an Oaxacan or Zapotec-affiliated population living within the larger central Mexican city, suggesting sustained migration, merchant activity, or diplomatic residence connecting the two centers. Correspondingly, imagery at Monte Albán from this period shows stylistic influence consistent with contact with Teotihuacan. This relationship illustrates a broader pattern in Mesoamerican history that recurs throughout this volume: major centers were rarely self-contained, and even cities separated by hundreds of kilometers and different highland regions maintained active diplomatic, commercial, and cultural relationships with one another. Chapter 8 examines Teotihuacan's own development, urban organization, and regional influence in detail; this chapter's purpose is only to establish that Monte Albán's history cannot be told as a purely local Oaxacan story.

Section 7.12

Transformation and Decline of Monte Albán

Monte Albán reached its greatest population, architectural elaboration, and apparent regional influence during the Monte Albán IIIA and IIIB periods, roughly 200 to 700 CE, a span of several centuries during which the city's Main Plaza reached its final monumental form and elite tomb construction, discussed in Section 7.10, continued at a high level of sophistication. Beginning sometime around 700 to 800 CE, however, archaeological evidence indicates a substantial and sustained decline in Monte Albán's population and political centrality. Major construction at the site slows and largely ceases, elite residential occupation of the hilltop diminishes considerably, and archaeological survey evidence indicates that population in the broader valley increasingly concentrated in a number of smaller, competing regional centers rather than continuing to be drawn toward a single dominant capital.

The precise causes of this decline remain debated among specialists, and this textbook does not present any single explanation as fully settled. Proposed factors include internal political fragmentation, possibly connected to succession disputes or the weakening authority of Monte Albán's ruling elite; changing regional and interregional trade relationships, including shifts connected to the roughly contemporaneous decline of Teotihuacan discussed in Chapter 8; agricultural or environmental pressures on the hilltop's already challenging water and soil conditions; and simply the long-term political cycles common to many Mesoamerican and world civilizations, in which a dominant capital's political authority eventually fragments in favor of multiple smaller successor centers.

It is essential that students draw the correct historical conclusion from this decline. Monte Albán's diminished role as a great political capital did not mean the disappearance of the Zapotec people, their language, their religious traditions, or their continued political organization in Oaxaca. Zapotec communities continued to inhabit the valley and surrounding highlands, developing new regional centers and political configurations that Chapter 11 examines in detail as part of the broader Postclassic Oaxacan world shared with Mixtec-speaking communities. The pattern established here — the decline of a specific political center without the disappearance of the people who built it — recurs throughout this textbook's treatment of Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations, and students should resist collapsing "the end of a great city" into "the end of a people."

Historical Vocabulary
The Valley of Oaxaca

The Valley of Oaxaca refers to the highland valley in southern Mexico formed by the convergence of three geographic arms — Etla, Tlacolula, and Valle Grande (or Zaachila) — at an elevation of roughly 1,500 meters. Its relatively dry, temperate highland environment differs considerably from the humid tropical lowlands of the Gulf Coast examined in Chapter 6, requiring distinct agricultural strategies centered on a combination of rainfall farming and irrigation.

Before the founding of Monte Albán around 500 BCE, the valley's three arms were occupied by separate, often competing communities, most notably San José Mogote in the Etla arm. The valley's convergent geography made the site later occupied by Monte Albán an unusually central location relative to all three arms, interpreted by some archaeologists as potentially politically neutral ground — a fact many archaeologists consider relevant to why that particular hilltop was chosen as a shared capital.

Historical Vocabulary
"Danzantes"

"Danzantes" ("dancers" in Spanish) is the conventional modern name for a corpus of more than three hundred carved stone monuments, primarily associated with Monte Albán's Building L-sub and dating mainly to roughly 500–300 BCE, with some securely dated to around 450–350 BCE. Early twentieth-century archaeologists chose the name because the figures' fluid, twisting postures reminded them of dancers; that reading is rejected by essentially all current specialists and should never be presented to students as an accurate description of what the figures depict.

Current scholarship, refined substantially in 2025 and 2026 research, treats the corpus as thematically mixed rather than uniform: a large portion of the figures are now read as depicting ritual genital autosacrifice performed to invoke ancestors or supernatural beings, others reference rulership and enthronement, and a smaller subset appears connected to the defeat, capture, or sacrifice of rivals, with some material linked to a practice researchers describe as "oracular warfare." Because the name "Danzantes" persists in scholarship and site signage despite reflecting a rejected interpretation, students must treat it as a conventional label only, discussed further in Section 7.7 and the Evidence and Interpretation feature.

Primary Source Window

San José Mogote Monument 3

Object: A carved stone slab excavated at San José Mogote · Conventional date: c. 600 BCE (approximate, based on archaeological context and stylistic comparison) · Type: Carved stone monument, associated with early calendrical notation

Context: Monument 3 was found built into a later construction phase at San José Mogote, a placement that complicates precise dating and requires archaeologists to reason carefully about how much older the carved image itself might be than the context in which it was eventually incorporated. The stone depicts a reclining human figure in a posture some archaeologists have compared, cautiously, to later Mesoamerican depictions of sacrificial or defeated captives, accompanied by a small glyph that a number of specialists have read as an early calendrical day sign.

Rather than presenting a transcribed inscription, since the monument's imagery consists of carved figures and a glyph rather than an extended text, students should understand its significance in summary form: if the calendrical reading of the accompanying glyph is correct, Monument 3 would represent one of the earliest examples anywhere in Mesoamerica of a specific day associated, through carved notation, with a specific event or figure — an early step toward the fuller hieroglyphic and calendrical tradition that developed at Monte Albán in the following centuries, discussed in Section 7.9.

Analysis: Monument 3's significance depends heavily on accepting a particular interpretation of both its imagery and its accompanying glyph, and specialists do not agree uniformly on either point. Its posture has been read by some as consistent with the later Danzante tradition discussed in Section 7.7, suggesting continuity in how Oaxacan communities visually represented defeated rivals across several centuries. Its glyph, if correctly read as calendrical, would represent important early evidence for the beginnings of the numerical and calendrical notation system that became far more extensively documented in the Monte Albán corpus discussed in Section 7.9.

Questions for students:

  1. Why does the monument's placement within a later construction phase complicate efforts to date the carving itself precisely?
  2. What are the risks of interpreting a single, largely isolated monument as securely representative of a broader early writing tradition?
  3. How does Monument 3's possible thematic connection to the later Danzante monuments illustrate continuity in Oaxacan political imagery?
  4. Why might historians treat Monument 3 as historically significant while still declining to call it, without qualification, "the earliest writing in Mesoamerica"?
Historical Debate

Why Was Monte Albán Founded?

The question: Why did valley communities relocate substantial populations to a hilltop lacking reliable water, and what does this decision reveal about political relationships within the pre-Monte Albán valley?

Interpretation A — A defensive response to intercommunity conflict: This view emphasizes archaeological evidence for pre-founding violence, including the burned public building at San José Mogote, and points to Monte Albán's commanding, easily monitored position as evidence that defense against rival communities was a central motivation for the site's selection.
Interpretation B — A cooperative, negotiated new capital: This view emphasizes evidence that Monte Albán's earliest population appears to have been drawn from multiple existing valley communities rather than expanding outward from a single dominant town, suggesting that rival factions from different arms of the valley may have agreed to build a shared, politically neutral capital that no single faction could claim as exclusively its own.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same excavation data — population and ceramic evidence documenting the decline of valley-floor centers alongside Monte Albán's rapid growth, and evidence of earlier intercommunity conflict — but weigh the relative importance of conflict versus cooperation differently.

Why it matters: The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and most current scholarship treats defense, cooperation, and control of trade routes as overlapping, mutually reinforcing motivations rather than competing single-cause explanations. Understanding Monte Albán's founding as a genuinely open historical question, rather than a settled fact, models the kind of cautious reasoning about incomplete evidence this textbook asks of students throughout Volume I.

Student task: Using the evidence discussed in Sections 7.2 and 7.4, construct an argument for which motivation — defense, cooperation, or exchange control — you find best supported by the evidence, while explaining why the other explanations cannot be entirely ruled out.

Evidence and Interpretation

The Danzantes: What the Evidence Can and Cannot Establish

The carved figures conventionally called the Danzantes present a valuable case study in how archaeological interpretation changes as new analysis accumulates. The following summarizes, without presenting any single view as final, what the evidence more strongly supports and what remains actively debated.

Firmly rejected: The original early twentieth-century "dancing" interpretation, based on the figures' fluid postures, is rejected by essentially all current specialists.

Increasingly well supported by 2025–2026 scholarship: The corpus of more than three hundred Building L-sub monuments is not stylistically or thematically uniform. A substantial portion of the figures are now interpreted as depicting ritual specialists or rulers performing genital autosacrifice to invoke ancestors or supernatural beings, reframing postures once read as death or unconsciousness as postures of ritual trance. Other monuments in the same context reference rulership and enthronement. A smaller subset continues to be read as depicting the defeat, capture, or sacrifice of rival individuals, and some analysis links a portion of the corpus to what researchers term "oracular warfare," connecting ritual self-sacrifice to the sanctioning of military action.

Still uncertain: The exact proportion of the corpus that belongs to each proposed category; whether individual figures depict specific, historically identifiable people or generalized ritual and political roles; and whether every monument in the corpus can be confidently assigned to autosacrifice, rulership, or warfare-related categories at all, since some figures resist clear classification.

Why this matters for historical method: The Danzante corpus shows that a single dramatic reinterpretation — from "dancers" to "sacrificial captives" — can itself later prove too simple. Responsible historical and archaeological practice involves revisiting even a well-established reinterpretation as new epigraphic and iconographic analysis accumulates, and resisting the temptation to force a stylistically varied body of evidence into one uniform explanatory category — a practice this textbook models throughout its treatment of ambiguous or evolving evidence.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Rise of Monte Albán

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across the evidence. They are not quotations from any named historical individual.

From the perspective of a resident of San José Mogote during Monte Albán's founding: A person whose community had long been the valley's leading center might have experienced the rise of a new hilltop capital as a significant loss of local prestige and autonomy, even while participating in, and perhaps benefiting from, the new shared political and religious center.
From the perspective of a resident of the Tlacolula or Valle Grande arm: A community from an arm of the valley previously overshadowed by the Etla arm's dominance might have experienced Monte Albán's founding as an opportunity for greater political inclusion in a shared capital not identified exclusively with any single arm's historical dominance.
From the perspective of an elite family constructing a tomb at Monte Albán: Elite Zapotec families invested considerable resources in elaborate tombs and ongoing ritual attention to specific ancestors, reflecting a belief system in which deceased elite individuals continued to matter actively to the political and religious standing of their living descendants.
From the perspective of a person from a rival community affected by Zapotec military claims: A person from a community that Monte Albán's rulers claimed to have defeated or dominated, whether through warfare imagery in the Danzante corpus or other monumental art, would likely have experienced Zapotec political and military assertion very differently than a resident of the capital itself, though the specific nature of that experience — direct conquest, negotiated subordination, or a more symbolic claim of authority — cannot be established with confidence from the carved evidence alone, especially since current scholarship increasingly questions whether specific monuments such as the Building J slabs record a list of distant conquered towns at all.
Continuity and Change Over Time

San José Mogote to Monte Albán

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from a single dominant valley-floor town to a newly founded, shared hilltop capital drawing population from multiple parts of the valley
  • Settlement patterns shifted toward a clearer multi-tiered hierarchy of large capital, mid-sized towns, and small villages
  • Calendrical notation associated with isolated monuments such as Monument 3 developed into a substantial, internally comparable hieroglyphic corpus at Monte Albán
  • Public claims of rulership, authority, ancestry, sacrifice, and political conflict became increasingly elaborate, while the geographic extent of Monte Albán's external military power remains harder to reconstruct than older conquest-list interpretations suggested

What Stayed the Same

  • Public monuments continued to connect rulership, ritual sacrifice, ancestry, and political conflict from Monument 3 through the Danzante figures and the Building J slabs, although the specific meaning and relative importance of these themes within each monument group changed over time and should not be reduced to a single continuous "warfare and captivity" narrative
  • Ancestor veneration and elite mortuary ritual remained central to Zapotec religious life throughout the periods examined in this chapter
  • Agricultural dependence on the valley floor persisted even after political authority relocated to the hilltop capital
  • Zapotec cultural and linguistic identity persisted in Oaxaca well beyond Monte Albán's political decline, continuing into the Postclassic world examined in Chapter 11

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic political transformation Monte Albán represented and the underlying continuities in religious practice, agricultural dependence, and cultural identity helps students avoid treating the founding of Monte Albán as a total break from earlier Oaxacan history rather than its most significant early culmination.

Cause and Effect

From Regional Competition to Political Consolidation

Long-term causes

Centuries of agricultural development, craft specialization, and long-distance exchange at San José Mogote and other valley communities, producing increasingly complex and competitive local political centers.

Short-term causes

Episodes of intercommunity conflict around 900–700 BCE, combined with the strategic appeal of a centrally located, defensible hilltop at the convergence of the valley's three arms, interpreted by some archaeologists as potentially politically neutral ground.

Effect

The rapid founding and growth of Monte Albán around 500 BCE, the decline of San José Mogote, and the emergence of a new settlement hierarchy integrating much of the valley under a shared capital.

Historical significance

Monte Albán's consolidation produced one of the earliest genuine states in the Americas and a hieroglyphic and calendrical tradition that historians can study as a developing corpus, shaping later Mesoamerican political and intellectual traditions examined throughout this volume.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Monte Albán's founding; environmental setting, political rivalry, cooperative negotiation, and control of exchange routes each likely contributed in ways that current evidence cannot fully disentangle.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life on the Hilltop

Beyond its monumental plaza, elite tombs, and carved political monuments, Monte Albán was home to a large population of farmers, craftworkers, and laborers whose daily lives archaeology allows historians to glimpse only partially. Residential terraces excavated on the hillsides surrounding the Main Plaza reveal modest house platforms, storage pits, and household refuse indicating a mixed economy of farming, craft production, and, for some households, specialized work supplying goods or labor to the city's elite and religious institutions.

Water management would have shaped daily routines considerably, given the hilltop's lack of a permanent natural water source; households participated in maintaining terrace catchments, drains, and reservoirs, and drew on both stored rainwater and agricultural produce carried up from valley-floor and piedmont fields, requiring sustained cooperation and labor investment simply to maintain ordinary domestic life. Archaeological evidence of household shrines and small domestic ritual objects suggests that religious practice extended well beyond the grand temples of the Main Plaza into the daily lives of ordinary Monte Albán residents, a pattern of household-level religion that recurs across many of the Mesoamerican civilizations examined throughout this volume.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the development of Zapotec civilization in the Valley of Oaxaca from the pre-Monte Albán world of competing valley-floor communities, centered on San José Mogote, through the dramatic founding of Monte Albán around 500 BCE and the city's growth into one of the earliest genuine states in the Americas. Rather than presenting Oaxaca as a passive recipient of Gulf Coast civilization, the chapter has demonstrated, following the "sister cultures" framework introduced at the end of Chapter 6, that Oaxacan communities developed their own trajectory toward political complexity, warfare, ritual life, and calendrical and hieroglyphic notation, while remaining genuinely connected to Gulf Coast and other Mesoamerican traditions through exchange and cultural contact.

Students have examined several competing, non-exclusive explanations for why Monte Albán was founded on a defensible but water-scarce hilltop rather than continuing to develop existing valley-floor centers, including explanations emphasizing defense, cooperative political negotiation among rival valley communities, and control of interregional trade routes. The chapter has also examined the extraordinary labor investment required to convert that hilltop into a functioning city, including large-scale terracing, water management, and the construction of the immense Main Plaza.

The chapter has treated Monte Albán's evidence for early statehood carefully, distinguishing well-supported claims about settlement hierarchy, social stratification, and coordinated monumental construction from more speculative claims about the precise administrative machinery of Zapotec government. It has examined the carved Danzante figures as a case study in how archaeological interpretation evolves, tracing the shift from the discredited "dancing" reading through the mid-to-late-twentieth-century emphasis on warfare and captivity to the more thematically differentiated 2025–2026 scholarship recognizing rulership, ancestor-invoking autosacrifice, and warfare together within a single varied corpus. It has likewise examined the Building J inscribed slabs, showing how the long-standard "conquest list" interpretation has been substantially revised by 2017 and 2025 scholarship questioning both the decipherment of the proposed place names and the slabs' original context, and cautioning against overconfident claims about a fully unified Monte Albán "empire."

Students have been introduced to the development of early Zapotec hieroglyphic writing and calendrical notation, beginning with the isolated but suggestive evidence of San José Mogote Monument 3 and developing into the substantial, internally comparable textual corpus at Monte Albán, whose chronology and interpretation, including the calendrical sign Glyph W, were significantly refined by a 2026 scholarly reanalysis dating the earliest substantial corpus to roughly 496–221 BCE. The chapter has distinguished this rich, developing textual tradition from the authentic but undeciphered and contextually isolated Cascajal Block discussed in Chapter 6, while declining to declare either region definitively "first" in the strictest sense.

The chapter has also examined Zapotec religious life and mortuary practice, particularly elite tomb construction and ancestor veneration, while cautioning against projecting later, better-documented religious systems backward onto Monte Albán's earliest periods. It has placed Monte Albán within the wider Mesoamerican world, particularly its documented relationship with Teotihuacan, examined fully in Chapter 8, and it has traced the city's political decline after roughly 700–800 CE, emphasizing that this decline marked the end of a specific political center rather than the disappearance of the Zapotec people, whose history continues in Chapter 11's treatment of the Postclassic Mixtec and Zapotec worlds.

Chapter 7 has asked how a highland Mesoamerican valley of competing communities produced one of the earliest cities and states in the Americas. Chapter 8 turns to a very different kind of Mesoamerican metropolis: Teotihuacan, the great multiethnic city of the central Mexican highlands, whose scale, planning, and regional influence would eventually touch Monte Albán itself.

This project asks you to investigate how archaeologists build and revise interpretations of a specific body of evidence from Monte Albán or San José Mogote.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look closely at a civilization too often reduced, in popular accounts, to a footnote before the Mexica or Maya worlds examined later in this volume. The Valley of Oaxaca's own history — its competing valley-floor communities, its dramatic founding of a shared hilltop capital, its development of one of Mesoamerica's richest early textual traditions, and its sustained connections to the wider Mesoamerican world — demonstrates decisively that early Mesoamerican civilization did not radiate outward from a single source. It developed through the parallel, interconnected efforts of multiple regions, each drawing on shared traditions while producing genuinely distinct political and cultural institutions.

The chapter has also modeled, deliberately and repeatedly, a central habit of mind this textbook asks of students: distinguishing what evidence supports strongly from what it supports only tentatively. The Danzante monuments, the Building J inscribed slabs, and the shifting scholarly chronology of early Zapotec writing all illustrate that even well-studied archaeological evidence often resists simple, fully settled conclusions, and that historians serve their subject best by stating clearly where genuine uncertainty remains.

Finally, this chapter has established a pattern that will recur throughout Volume I: the political decline of a great capital does not mean the disappearance of the people who built it. Monte Albán's importance as a political center diminished after roughly 700–800 CE, but Zapotec communities, language, and cultural life continued in Oaxaca for many centuries afterward, reemerging in new political configurations examined in Chapter 11. Readers who carry this distinction forward will be well prepared for the study of Teotihuacan in Chapter 8, and for the broader Mesoamerican world examined throughout the remainder of Part II and Part III of this volume.
