Conquest and Colonial Formation in the Northern Andes

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Three Armies on One Plateau

In 1539, on the high plateau around present-day Bogotá, three separate Spanish-led expeditions found themselves in the same place. One had come up the Magdalena River from the Caribbean coast, one had marched north from Quito, and one had crossed the plains and mountains from Venezuela. If Spain had conquered the Northern Andes, why were three conquest companies arguing over who had done it?

The question is a good entry into a region that does not fit the story told in Chapters 35 and 36. In Peru, the capture of one ruler at Cajamarca and the occupation of one capital, Cusco, gave Spaniards leverage over a vast and centralized empire, and even there conquest proved long and incomplete. North of the Inca heartland there was no comparable prize. The highlands of what is now Ecuador had been incorporated into Tawantinsuyu only within living memory. Farther north, in the cold basins of the eastern cordillera, the Muisca lived in many communities linked by exchange, marriage, ritual, and rivalry. Beyond them lay river valleys, rainforested slopes, Caribbean plains, and mountain chains inhabited by peoples whose political organization varied from large chiefdoms to small, fiercely independent communities. There was no Northern-Andean Cajamarca.

What took the place of a single decisive event was a convergence. Between 1525 and about 1550, Spaniards entered the region from the Caribbean coast, from the Venezuelan coast, and from the former Inca territories of the south. They were not a coordinated machine. They were rival enterprises, each financed and authorized differently, each pursuing a claim that overlapped with others. Some were led by veterans of Caribbean and Peruvian campaigns, some by a university-trained lawyer, and one by an agent of a German banking house operating under a concession from Charles V. They wanted gold and emeralds, but they also wanted Indigenous labor, titles, jurisdiction, and the right to found towns, and those aims shaped where they went and how they behaved.

Indigenous communities were not scenery for these rivalries. Cañari communities in southern Ecuador allied with Spaniards against the Inca-aligned forces of Quito. Muisca rulers calculated how to deal with armed strangers while managing their own relations with one another. Peoples of the Sierra Nevada, the Sinú plains, the Cauca valley, and the southern Colombian massif fought, fled, negotiated, withheld tribute, and in some cases resisted for generations. The decisions of these communities determined what Spanish settlement could accomplish, and they explain why founding a town and controlling a territory were different things.

That distinction is the central argument of the chapter. Santa Marta, Cartagena, Quito, Popayán, Cali, Santa Fe, and Tunja were all founded within about fifteen years. Each was a legal act and a physical settlement, and each created a focus of Spanish authority. None of them meant that the surrounding peoples had been conquered. Early colonial formation depended on Indigenous labor and food supplies, on existing local authorities who mediated between communities and settlers, on contested grants of tribute, on roads and river routes, and on continuing military campaigns. Settlement came first. Effective territorial control came later and, in many places, much later.

The chapter is built on evidence that must be read with care. Most narratives of these events were written by Spaniards or their descendants, sometimes decades afterward. Juan de Castellanos wrote a vast verse history in the later sixteenth century, Pedro Simón compiled his Noticias historiales in the early seventeenth, and Juan Rodríguez Freyle, a Santa Fe-born chronicler, wrote El Carnero in the 1630s. An anonymous sixteenth-century Epítome of the conquest of the New Kingdom of Granada survives in related versions. No Muisca-authored account from the time of the conquest survives. Archaeology, colonial legal records, tribute inspections, and Indigenous-language fragments allow historians to recover much that Spanish chronicles omitted, but the voices of Muisca, Cañari, Pasto, and Tairona people reach us mostly through the writing of others.

This chapter occupies a particular place in Part VIII. Chapter 22 described the preconquest Muisca, Tairona, and other Isthmo-Colombian societies, and this chapter recalls only what students need to understand the conquest. Chapter 38 turns south to Chile and the Mapuche frontier, and Chapter 40 will examine the mature machinery of Spanish American government. Here the task is narrower and, in some ways, more revealing: to show what conquest looked like in a fragmented, mountainous, and politically plural region, and how conquest expeditions became colonial settlements.

Learning Objectives

  2. Describe the role of Santa Marta and Cartagena as Caribbean bases and distinguish coastal foundation from control of the interior.
  3. Trace the northern phase of the Inca war in Ecuador, including Rumiñahui, Sebastián de Belalcázar, and Cañari alliances.
  4. Distinguish the legal act of founding a Spanish town from effective control over its Indigenous hinterland, using Quito as a case.
  5. Analyze how the Cauca corridor and inter-Andean valleys shaped Spanish expansion and Indigenous resistance north of Quito.
  6. Assess the Quesada expedition up the Magdalena River as a problem of logistics, attrition, and Indigenous knowledge.
  7. Evaluate Muisca political organization without treating it as an empire equivalent to Tawantinsuyu.
  8. Interpret sources on Tisquesusa, Quemuenchatocha, and Sagipa critically, recognizing their origins in Spanish narratives.
  9. Compare the three expeditions of Quesada, Belalcázar, and Federmann and explain why their convergence reveals fragmentation within Spanish conquest.
  10. Explain how El Dorado changed from a report about a person and ritual into a legend of a golden kingdom.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did founding Quito, Santa Fe, Popayán, Cartagena, or other Spanish towns not mean that the surrounding Indigenous territories had been conquered?
  3. In what ways did Indigenous rivalries and alliances shape the routes, timing, and results of Spanish expeditions, and in what ways did Indigenous peoples refuse to cooperate?

  5. Why should historians avoid describing Muisca society as an empire or the zipa as king of all the Muisca?

  9. What does the northern case suggest about the idea that the conquest of the Americas was swift?

Historical Context

By the mid-1520s the Spanish Caribbean had been transformed. Chapters 27 and 29 described the Taíno collapse, the spread of coercive labor, and the movement of Spanish raiding outward from Hispaniola and Cuba to the mainland. Cortés had taken Tenochtitlan in 1521 (Chapter 32), Spanish captains were pushing through Central America (Chapter 34), and Francisco Pizarro's expedition had begun the operations in Peru described in Chapters 35 and 36. These successes shaped expectations. Men who had missed the great captures looked for the next Mexico or Peru, and they carried with them the practices of Caribbean slave raiding, the legal instrument of the capitulación (a contract with the Crown granting rights to conquer and settle), and the habit of founding towns as instruments of claim.

The Northern Andes were a poor match for those expectations. The Andes divide in this region into three principal cordilleras in Colombia, separated by the deep valleys of the Magdalena and Cauca Rivers, and they narrow and merge into two highland ranges around the inter-Andean valley of Ecuador. The Caribbean lowlands and the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, a coastal massif rising above 5,000 meters, lay to the north, with the Llanos and Amazonian slopes to the east and the Pacific lowlands to the west. Movement between zones was physically hard and often seasonally impossible. Communities in one valley could be out of contact with neighbors a few days' walk away, and political organization reflected this. Highland basins supported dense agricultural populations, while lowland and slope peoples relied on riverine fishing, root crops, maize, and exchange.

The Inca presence in the south was recent. Chapter 20 showed that Tawantinsuyu expanded into the highlands of Ecuador in the generation or two before the Spanish invasion, partly through hard campaigns against the Cañari, Palta, Puruhá, and other peoples. Huayna Capac spent much of his later reign around Tomebamba and Quito, and the Inca state invested in roads, storehouses, and garrisons there. The civil war between Huáscar and Atahualpa (Chapter 35) was in part a war over Quito's political and military resources. Ecuador was therefore a region of recent imperial incorporation, with resentments among peoples such as the Cañari that Spaniards would exploit and with Inca-aligned military commanders who would resist them.

To the north the Inca had not governed. Chapter 22 described the Muisca of the high eastern cordillera, with their dense farming populations, salt production, cotton cloth, emerald and gold working, and ritual landscapes, as well as the Tairona of the Caribbean slopes and the Zenú and other societies of the lowland river systems. Those chapters showed that these were complex societies, but not that they shared a single political model. That variety is the starting point for this chapter. Because Spanish chroniclers often described the societies they met in familiar terms such as kingdom, lord, and temple, readers must distinguish what the sources claim from what the Indigenous institutions actually were.

Finally, the Spanish side was not unified. Authority to explore and settle was granted by the Crown to individuals and companies, and those grants overlapped. The governor of Santa Marta, the conquerors of Peru who held Quito and its surroundings, and the Welser company in Venezuela all claimed rights that could extend into the same interior. The legal and political apparatus that would eventually adjudicate those claims, including the Council of the Indies and the audiencias, was only taking shape. Historians therefore read the conquest of the Northern Andes as a story of competing enterprises as much as of Spanish–Indigenous conflict.

Section 37.1

Beyond Peru: A Different Northern Andes

The conquest of Peru produced a model that is easy to carry into other regions: a small force, a captured ruler, a ransom, a capital, and then a slow work of consolidation. The Northern Andes did not follow it. The reasons were political and geographic at once. Politically, the region was divided among many societies with their own leaders, languages, and alliances. Geographically, it was cut by cordilleras, gorges, and rivers that made unified rule difficult for any state and difficult for any invader.

Three zones matter for this chapter. The first is the Ecuadorian highlands, a chain of high basins between two ranges of the Andes, home to the Cañari, Puruhá, Panzaleo, Palta, and numerous local chiefdoms around Quito and the northern highlands. This zone had been drawn into Tawantinsuyu in the decades before the Spanish arrival, and it contained roads, tambos or waystations, and imperial garrisons that the Spaniards would use. The second is the Colombian Andes, where the Western, Central, and Eastern cordilleras enclose the Cauca and Magdalena valleys and where the eastern range carries the high basins of the Muisca. The third is the Caribbean and its hinterland: the Sinú plains, the lower Magdalena, and the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, with their own populous societies. A fourth, outer zone, the Llanos and the slopes toward Venezuela, was crossed by one of the three expeditions that converged in 1539.

This variety explains why the chapter treats routes as seriously as it treats rulers. A Spanish expedition did not capture a state when it captured a leader, because there was no state that depended on a single leader. A Spanish town did not gain control of a region when it was founded, because the countryside answered to local authorities who might or might not cooperate. And because there was no single prize, there was no single Spanish enterprise. The region was approached from the Caribbean, from Peru, and from Venezuela, and the Spaniards on each route were competitors as well as colonizers.

The chapter also resists treating the whole region as a single stage. Ecuador and Colombia are modern countries that did not exist in the sixteenth century. At the time the relevant Spanish categories were provisional: Quito and its hinterland were tied to the Peruvian conquest, Popayán was carved out of the territory of Belalcázar's government, and the "New Kingdom of Granada" was a name Quesada applied to the Muisca highlands. Chapter 37 uses these historical formations rather than modern borders, and it braids the Ecuadorian and Colombian stories so that neither becomes an appendix to the other.

Geography Study

Geography Study: Three Corridors into the Northern Andes

Using a physical map of northwestern South America, students should locate and trace the following three conquest corridors, noting the barriers and opportunities each presented:

  • The Magdalena corridor (Quesada, 1536–37): Santa Marta on the Caribbean coast; the lower Magdalena River; the river's long upstream journey; the turn east into the Opón River valley; the climb into the eastern cordillera and the Muisca plateau around Bacatá and Hunza.
  • The southern corridor (Belalcázar, 1534–39): San Miguel de Piura and the Peruvian coast; the highland road through Tomebamba and Riobamba to Quito; the road north through Pasto; the Patía and Cauca valleys to Popayán and Cali; the eventual crossing of the Central Cordillera toward the upper Magdalena and the Muisca plateau.
  • The eastern corridor (Federmann, 1537–39): Coro on the Venezuelan coast; the interior plains or Llanos; the foothills of the eastern cordillera; the climb through passes to the Bogotá highlands.

Also locate Cartagena, the Sinú plains, the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, the Cauca and Magdalena Rivers, and the Pacific lowlands. Remember that the modern boundaries of Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela did not exist, and that sixteenth-century maps of these regions were drawn by people who often had never seen the places they depicted.

Section 37.2

The Caribbean Gateways: Santa Marta and Cartagena

Spanish contact with the Colombian Caribbean coast began early. Rodrigo de Bastidas explored it in 1500–1501, and during the next two decades slave raiders, pearl seekers, and traders visited its harbors. Permanent settlement came later. In 1525 Bastidas founded Santa Marta, at the foot of the Sierra Nevada, and in 1533 Pedro de Heredia founded Cartagena on a deep, sheltered bay to the west. Both were conceived as bases: for trade and plunder along the coast, for provisioning expeditions, and for exploration of the interior.

Neither was secure. Santa Marta stood beside populous and organized societies. The Tairona communities of the Sierra Nevada, described in Chapter 22, controlled terraced settlements, stone-paved roads, and agricultural zones across several altitudes, and they were connected by trade to the coast and to the highlands. They did not submit to the first settlers. Spanish records describe repeated raids, counterattacks, and punitive expeditions around Santa Marta during the 1520s and 1530s, as well as periodic famine in the settlement itself. The town survived because it could be resupplied by sea and because it served as a staging point, not because its governors controlled the surrounding mountains. Spanish campaigns against the Tairona continued for decades, and the societies of the Sierra Nevada were reshaped by war, forced labor, epidemics, and displacement rather than by one conquest.

Cartagena followed a similar pattern. Heredia raided the Sinú region, whose Zenú communities were known for large burial mounds, elaborate goldwork, and extensive wetland agriculture, and Spanish soldiers dug up tombs for precious metals in the early 1530s. The seizure of gold from burial sites generated wealth quickly and aroused resistance just as quickly. The coastal hinterland was a zone of slaving, ransom, and plunder, and the Spanish presence there involved captives taken for sale in the Caribbean and for use as porters. Over the following decades the harbor of Cartagena would grow in importance as a fortified port in the imperial system, a development explored in later chapters.

For the purposes of this chapter, the key point is that these were foundations without control. Santa Marta and Cartagena were Spanish towns on a coast whose peoples were still mostly independent, and their governors had little knowledge of the interior. Yet the coast mattered because it supplied the first information about wealth upriver. Indigenous traders told Spaniards about salt, cotton cloth, gold, and emeralds in the highlands, and the Magdalena offered a path toward them. In this sense the coastal settlements were the entry points for the conquest of the interior while remaining unconquered in their own right.

Section 37.3

Quito after Atahualpa: Rumiñahui and the Northern Inca War

While the Caribbean bases were being established, the aftermath of Cajamarca was unfolding in the south. Chapter 35 described the execution of Atahualpa in July 1533. That event removed a claimant to the Inca throne but did not end the civil conflict among Inca elites, and in the north it left a large army and a powerful commander in control of Quito and its surroundings. The commander was Rumiñahui (Rumiñawi, "stone-face" in Quechua), a general associated with Atahualpa's cause. Spanish accounts describe him as a captain who brought part of Atahualpa's forces to Quito and prepared to defend it.

Sources for these events are thin and partisan. Spanish chroniclers wrote of Rumiñahui as treacherous and cruel, accusing him of killing members of Atahualpa's household and hiding treasure, charges that served to justify his later execution and that are difficult to verify. Quito itself, the sources say, was burned and stripped before the Spaniards arrived, though how far this was a deliberate Inca-aligned policy and how far it was the work of the fighting or of Spanish raiding is unclear. What can be said is that the northern Inca forces, including soldiers from the region and groups drawn from elsewhere in Tawantinsuyu, remained a coherent military force after Cajamarca. Another Inca general, Quizquiz, had been pushed north from the Cusco region and also entered the northern conflict, and his forces were weakened by internal disputes that ended with his killing by his own commanders.

Sebastián de Belalcázar, a veteran of Caribbean and Panamanian campaigns who had joined Pizarro, left the new settlement of San Miguel de Piura in early 1534 for Quito, taking with him, according to the sources, a few hundred Spaniards and a much larger body of Indigenous allies. Chief among these were the Cañari. The Cañari homeland around Tomebamba had been conquered by the Incas after a long struggle, and Spanish accounts record Cañari hostility toward the Inca state and its northern army. Cañari leaders recognized in the Spaniards a chance to weaken those who had subjugated them, and they provided warriors, supplies, and information. Their alliance was not a gesture of submission. It was a political decision by communities with their own history of conflict with the Inca state, and, as in Tlaxcala (Chapter 31), the allies would later seek recognition for their service.

The campaign was hard. In the central highlands near the Tiocajas pass and elsewhere, Belalcázar's column faced repeated attacks by forces under Rumiñahui's command, including ambushes in difficult terrain. Spanish accounts describe fierce fighting and give large figures for the opposing forces, numbers that should be treated with caution. At the same time, a separate Spanish expedition arrived on the Ecuadorian coast from Guatemala under Pedro de Alvarado, who had taken advantage of rumors of Inca wealth. His force of several hundred men crossed the Andes at heavy cost from cold and exhaustion, then entered a negotiated arrangement with Diego de Almagro, Pizarro's partner, who paid Alvarado to leave his ships and men and return north. The episode shows that more than one Spanish enterprise was already competing for the same region.

Rumiñahui withdrew from Quito as the Spaniards advanced. Belalcázar entered the town late in 1534 and found it largely destroyed. Rumiñahui continued to operate in the surrounding country until he was captured, probably in the first months of 1535, and then tortured and executed. Spanish writers claimed that he refused to reveal the location of hidden treasure, a story that later fed treasure legends and that cannot be verified. The significance of his capture is easier to establish: the organized military force that had been aligned with Atahualpa in the north was broken. But Indigenous resistance in the north did not end. The point of the section is continuity. Cajamarca had not delivered Ecuador, and the conquest of the Ecuadorian highlands required a year of campaigning and alliances before a Spanish town could be maintained at Quito.

Section 37.4

Spanish Quito and the Limits of Foundation

Foundation was a legal ritual. A Spanish captain, usually acting under authority from the Crown or from a superior, named a town, appointed a cabildo (town council) of magistrates and councilmen, laid out a plaza and lots, and assigned town lots to the founding settlers, who became vecinos, or citizens of the town. These acts created a municipality that claimed authority over a surrounding territory. They did not create the means to enforce that claim.

Quito shows how loose the connection could be. The surviving 1534 cabildo record of Quito preserves three distinct legal acts. On 15 August 1534, before Belalcázar reached the ruined city, Diego de Almagro established a Spanish town named Santiago de Quito near Riobamba in the highlands to the south, partly to assert a claim against Alvarado's company and Belalcázar's independent action. On 28 August Almagro established the Villa de San Francisco de Quito at the same place. On 6 December 1534 Belalcázar, now on the site of the old Inca-aligned city, established San Francisco de Quito at the present location, appointing a cabildo and distributing lots. The repetition is not an oddity. It reflects the political competition among Spaniards, since a legal foundation by an authorized official strengthened a claim against rivals. In practice it meant that the legal existence of Spanish Quito owed as much to jurisdictional rivalry as to any establishment of colonial rule.

The settlers' power depended on Indigenous communities. The Quito basin and surrounding valleys contained farming populations that had lived under Inca rule and, before that, under local lords. Spanish settlers redistributed land and labor, took women as household servants and companions under conditions of coercion, and demanded food, cloth, and porters. In the early years, many settlers were bound to the settlement only by the prospect of grants, and some left for the richer opportunities in Peru or in the north. Indigenous local leaders, called caciques in Spanish usage and kuraka or other titles in Andean usage, mediated between communities and settlers. They collected tribute, assembled workers, and bargained over what could be demanded. The Spaniards needed them, and the communities needed them to limit the Spanish burden.

The coast posed another problem. Spaniards needed a port to communicate with Panama and Peru, and they began a settlement in 1535 on the coast. The coastal site was hot, forested, and seasonally flooded, and the Indigenous peoples of the Guayas region resisted. The Spanish settlement that evolved into Guayaquil was repeatedly founded, transferred, attacked, and re-established from the mid-1530s into the 1540s, reaching a durable location only around 1547, and even the settlement's name changed in the process. Guayaquil illustrates, in miniature, the way a foundation could be abandoned and repeated because the people on the ground could not hold it.

By the end of the 1530s, then, Quito existed as a small Spanish town surrounded by Indigenous communities that provided its labor and food. The settlers' ability to enforce demands was limited by their numbers and by the willingness of caciques to cooperate. Their claim, however, was growing as the settlement became the base for the northward campaigns described in the next section.

Section 37.5

North from Quito: Pasto, Popayán, and the Cauca Corridor

Belalcázar looked north almost immediately. Indigenous informants spoke of rich lands and rulers beyond the Ecuadorian highlands, and Belalcázar's commission from Pizarro was limited, which gave him reason to seek a government of his own. In 1535 and after, he sent lieutenants, among them Juan de Ampudia and Pedro de Añasco, to explore the road north, and he followed in person. The route led through the high plateaus and gorges of the Pasto and Quillacinga country, down the Patía valley, and into the valley of the Cauca River.

The Pastos and their neighbors had resisted Inca expansion before Spanish arrival and were farmers, traders, and builders of dispersed settlements in a cold highland landscape. They also resisted Spanish demands. Spanish towns founded in the region in the late 1530s, including Pasto, were small and vulnerable, and Indigenous communities around them negotiated, withheld food, and moved away when possible. The consequences were severe in some areas, since forced labor and imported diseases reduced populations, but the process was gradual and uneven.

Farther north, the Cauca valley and its flanks contained numerous communities, many of them organized as chiefdoms or small confederations with their own leaders, fortified sites, and long histories of conflict. Spanish expeditions founded Cali in 1536 and Popayán around 1537, and these became the centers of what would be called the Governorate of Popayán, which the Crown formally granted to Belalcázar in 1540. The foundations established a chain of Spanish towns along an inter-Andean corridor, connected by a road, but they did not give the Spaniards control of the surrounding highlands. Communities in the Central Cordillera and on the slopes toward the upper Magdalena fought the incursions, and the valleys remained dangerous.

One episode captures the character of this resistance. Spanish chronicles, including those of Pedro Simón and Castellanos, tell of a woman, known as La Gaitana, whose son was killed by Spaniards under Añasco in the upper Magdalena region around 1539. According to these accounts, she organized a coalition among neighboring communities and led an attack that killed Añasco. The narratives were written decades afterward, and details, including her name and the manner of Añasco's death, have the marks of later retelling. But the story is consistent with other records of coordinated Indigenous resistance in this region and with the fact that women held leadership roles in several northern Andean societies. It also reminds students that the people Spaniards called indios included political leaders who organized, planned, and waged war.

The larger point is structural. Spaniards in the Cauca corridor were building a chain of towns and communications rather than conquering a political center. Each town depended on local labor, each was vulnerable to local resistance, and each was, in effect, a node in a network that was only partly under Spanish control. This pattern would also characterize the highlands of the east, where a very different kind of society awaited a very different kind of expedition.

Section 37.6

Up the Magdalena: Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada's Expedition

In April 1536, Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada left Santa Marta with a force that sources place at roughly 800 men, though the figure is uncertain, along with horses and a flotilla of boats on the Magdalena. He was a lawyer from Granada, not a professional soldier, and he led the expedition as the lieutenant of Pedro Fernández de Lugo, the governor of Santa Marta. Part of the force marched along the river bank and part traveled on the water. Their purpose, according to the narratives, was to find the source of the Magdalena and the rich lands about which Indigenous traders had spoken.

The journey was brutal. The Magdalena valley is hot, humid, and swampy, with rapids, sandbars, and insects, and the population along it was organized in numerous communities that did not all welcome the Spaniards. Spanish accounts describe attacks from canoes, hunger, tropical disease, jaguars and caimans, and the destruction of boats. Hundreds of men died or deserted, and the expedition lost much of its equipment and many of its horses. Chroniclers, writing for audiences who valued heroic endurance, emphasized the suffering of the Spaniards, but the burden of the march also fell on the Indigenous porters and guides pressed or recruited from riverside communities and, according to the sources, on enslaved Africans and Indigenous people brought from the Caribbean.

Knowledge, not just endurance, made the journey possible. The Spaniards relied on Indigenous guides, on food obtained from riverside settlements by trade and requisition, and on information about routes. In the lower and middle Magdalena they encountered networks of exchange that carried salt, cotton cloth, and gold. Eventually, the expedition turned east from the river along the Opón valley and climbed into the eastern cordillera, where, according to accounts, they came upon salt and woven cotton mantas that were evidence of a populous highland society. The turn was a decision, informed by Indigenous trade goods and reports, and it determined the shape of the later conquest.

When the survivors, perhaps around 170 to 200 men by most accounts, reached the high plateau in early 1537, they had a small force with few horses and no secure supply line. They entered a region of dense farming settlement, unlike anything on the river. The Spaniards' own accounts describe wonder at the number of communities and at the cold, cultivated landscape. It is a mistake, however, to read the survivors' small number as evidence of Spanish weakness alone. Their weapons, horses, and willingness to use terror mattered, but so did the divisions among the Muisca, which the Spaniards learned to exploit. The expedition was fragile, and its success depended on conditions it had not created.

Everyday Life and Social History

Porters, Salt, Cloth, and the Households Along the Routes

Expedition routes were not empty roads. They passed through communities whose members were asked, often compelled, to feed, house, and guide the visitors. Porters carried baggage, weapons, and provisions across mountain passes and along river banks, and many died of exhaustion, exposure, or disease. Women ground maize, cooked, and spun and wove, and in highland communities women's cloth production supplied the mantas that were gift, tribute, and currency in local exchange. Spanish demands for food and cloth therefore fell directly on household labor, especially women's labor.

Women also appear in Spanish records as captives, servants, and companions taken by soldiers, a pattern of coercion that was part of the conquest and that left descendants of mixed ancestry in the new towns. Africans, both enslaved and free, took part in these campaigns as laborers and soldiers, an early reminder that the colonial societies emerging in the north would be Indigenous, Iberian, and African from their beginnings. For ordinary Muisca households, the arrival of the Spaniards meant requisitions of maize, potatoes, salt, and cloth, the loss of men to porterage, and the destruction of fields and storehouses during campaigns. Some communities fled to higher ground or into the forest. The silences in the surviving sources about these experiences are themselves evidence of whose lives the chroniclers chose to record.

Section 37.7

The Muisca Political World at Contact

Chapter 22 described Muisca society in detail, and this section recalls only the features needed to understand the conquest. The Muisca, Chibcha-speaking farmers of the high basins around present-day Bogotá, Tunja, and Sogamoso, occupied a densely populated agricultural landscape, but archaeological evidence generally indicates dispersed settlements and farmsteads of varying size rather than urban-style population clustering. Houses were round with thatched conical roofs, called bohíos in Spanish sources, and Spanish accounts describe the compounds of local leaders as larger and more elaborate than ordinary dwellings. They grew maize, potatoes, quinoa, beans, and other crops at different altitudes, produced cotton cloth by trade or by sending people to lower, warmer lands, and exchanged salt, emeralds, gold ornaments, and textiles across wide networks. Gold was worked by specialists, often into alloyed ornaments made by lost-wax casting, and used chiefly for ritual offerings and the display of status.

Political authority was distributed. Spanish chroniclers described a kingdom of the south ruled from Bacatá (near modern Funza and Bogotá) by a leader they called the zipa, and a northern realm centered on Hunza (later Tunja) under the zaque, with additional authorities at Sugamuxi (Sogamoso), an important religious center, and at Duitama and other places. Beneath these leaders were many regional and local chiefs, called caciques in Spanish usage, who ruled communities and received tribute, labor, and ritual deference. Succession rules, the strength of obligations to higher leaders, and the extent to which lesser chiefs could act independently are all debated.

Historical Vocabulary
Zipa and Zaque

The zipa and the zaque are the titles Spanish sources give to the leaders associated with Bacatá in the south and Hunza in the north. The words come from the Muisca language, and both appear in Spanish narratives, including those based on interviews with Muisca informants after the conquest.

Spanish writers often rendered these titles as "king" and described a two-kingdom system. That language was useful for Europeans who sought a familiar hierarchy but it probably overstated the unity and reach of both offices. The zipa should not be called "king of all the Muisca," and the northern and southern leaders were only two among several important authorities. The terms help students see that Muisca politics had real hierarchy and also real fragmentation.

Relations among these authorities mixed rivalry, marriage, and exchange. Spanish accounts speak of wars between the zipa's and the zaque's followers in the generation before the conquest, and some writers, with hindsight, treated this rivalry as the key to Spanish success. The explanation is attractive and incomplete. Rivalry offered Spaniards opportunities, but individual Muisca leaders pursued varied strategies: fleeing, negotiating, resisting, hiding wealth, or seeking Spanish favor against a local competitor. A single account of division among the Muisca does not explain what each community did or why Spanish control was slow to extend beyond the places the invaders could directly reach.

It is equally important to say what the Muisca were not. They were not an empire comparable to Tawantinsuyu. There was no empire-wide military, road, and provincial administrative system comparable to Tawantinsuyu's, and no single capital whose capture would end resistance. Muisca polities had many of the features of complex chiefdoms, with agricultural surplus, craft specialization, ritual centers, and elite status, but political authority did not run through a unified bureaucratic state. The Spanish habit of describing the Muisca in the language of kingdoms created a false resemblance to Peru, and Spaniards who had learned from the capture of Atahualpa tried to apply that lesson to leaders who did not occupy the same position.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Centralized Was Muisca Political Organization?

The question: Did the Spanish conquest overthrow a Muisca confederation with real centralized authority in the zipa and zaque, or did colonial writers impose too much coherence on a more decentralized landscape of chiefdoms?

Interpretation A — A hierarchical confederation: Some scholars, building on Spanish chronicles and on later accounts that draw on Muisca testimony, describe a two-part confederation led by the zipa and the zaque, with a hierarchy of subordinate chiefs, regular tribute, and recognized succession. On this view the Muisca possessed a political system sufficiently integrated to field coordinated forces and to bargain over succession and treasure, and the Spanish conquest disrupted an organized polity.
Interpretation B — Decentralized chiefdoms narrated as kingdoms: Other scholars, particularly archaeologists and ethnohistorians who emphasize regional variation, argue that political authority was more localized and that leaders such as the zipa and zaque held influence rather than command over wide territories. They point out that Spanish writers needed hierarchies they could recognize and that later chroniclers imposed order on oral reports. On this view, describing Muisca society as a confederation, still more as a kingdom, imports European categories.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same limited body of material: Spanish chronicles, tribute inspections and legal records from the colonial period that describe local authorities, archaeological evidence of settlement patterns, and the Muisca language as recorded by Spanish friars. Settlement archaeology tends to show dispersed communities with variable size, which supports the second reading, while the consistent Spanish reports of regional leaders and their claims support the first.

Why it matters: The question affects how the conquest is understood. If the Muisca were a centralized state, the deaths of the zipa and the zaque might have undermined Muisca resistance. If they were decentralized, removing leaders would not by itself end resistance, and Spanish control would depend on the slow subordination of many separate communities. The evidence for continuing resistance and for the persistence of local leaders after 1540 favors a cautious reading in which leaders had real but limited authority.

Section 37.8

Bacatá, Tunja, and the Conquest of Muisca Territories

Quesada's survivors entered the Muisca highlands in 1537 and moved through the plateau over the following months. Spanish accounts describe skirmishes and a series of encounters with local leaders, some of whom fled while others tried to negotiate or resist. The Spaniards had few men but possessed steel weapons, a handful of horses, and a willingness to burn and kill, and they gathered information about the political geography through captives and interpreters. Interpretation was a major difficulty, since the expedition included no one who spoke Chibcha. They relied on Indigenous intermediaries who knew coastal or river languages and gradually acquired Muisca speakers.

The southern leader associated with Bacatá, Tisquesusa, is the best-known Muisca figure of the invasion, and he is also among the least well documented. Spanish narratives say that he organized resistance, withdrew from his settlement as the invaders approached, and was wounded in an attack and died during the invasion, conventionally in late 1537 or 1538 depending on the chronology followed. Accounts differ about the circumstances, and some details, including the supposed cause of his death, carry the marks of later embellishment. What can be said is that the Spaniards seized his principal settlement, took gold and other goods, and then faced a succession problem. Sagipa, identified in Spanish sources as his successor, first resisted and then negotiated with Quesada. He was kept in Spanish custody, pressed to reveal hidden treasure, and mistreated, and he died in the course of the process around 1539. Spanish sources disagree about whether he was killed, tortured to death, or died of illness; the sources do agree that his treatment was a scandal that later critics used against Quesada.

The northern leader, Quemuenchatocha, ruled from Hunza. According to the narratives, Quesada's men approached the town in August 1537, seized the leader, and took gold, emeralds, and cotton cloth, with the leader's treatment under duress described in terms of imprisonment and demands for treasure. Spanish accounts also tell of the burning of the important temple center at Sugamuxi (Sogamoso), in a fire that the sources attribute to carelessness by soldiers. Whether accident or not, the destruction removed a major religious site and its offerings. Meanwhile other Muisca leaders, including those associated with Tundama at Duitama, resisted in the following months. Spanish sources report a battle against forces under Tundama and give contradictory accounts of his fate.

Three features of these events should be noted. First, they do not amount to "the fall of the Muisca." Spanish forces defeated, captured, or negotiated with important leaders, but local chiefs and communities responded individually, and many remained outside Spanish control for years. Second, the central Spanish activity was extraction: the seizure of gold, emeralds, and cloth from leaders and temples. The total taken was large by the standards of the New Kingdom, though sources give widely varying totals and the amounts are often described in inconsistent units. Third, the leaders' deaths and captures reached us through Spanish narratives that had reasons to dramatize or to exculpate. Quesada's conduct was criticized later, and some accounts blame subordinates or leaders, while others portray the treatment of captured rulers as a necessary response to treachery. Students should read each version as testimony with a purpose.

Historical Vocabulary
El Dorado

El Dorado, Spanish for "the gilded one," entered Spanish reports in the 1530s as a description of a person and a ritual, not a city. Informants told Spaniards in Quito and Popayán of a ruler in the highlands who was covered in gold dust and who made offerings in a lake. Later Spanish writers, including Juan Rodríguez Freyle in the seventeenth century, associated the story with the lake of Guatavita, where Muisca communities performed ceremonies in which valuables were offered in the water. The famous surviving Muisca raft, a gold ornament in the form of a raft with a central figure, was discovered near Pasca in 1969. It had been preceded by another raft found at Siecha in 1856, which was later lost. Scholars have associated both with traditions of aquatic offerings and with the El Dorado story, although interpretation remains debated. Archaeology confirms Guatavita as an important offering landscape, but it does not independently verify every detail of the lavish investiture ceremony described by later Spanish chroniclers.

Gold was real, Muisca goldwork was sophisticated, and Guatavita had ritual significance. The later fantasy of a golden city or kingdom was a European development. As expeditions searched the highlands without finding the imagined source, the story was moved: to the Llanos, to the Orinoco and Guiana, and to the mythical lake city of Manoa. The term therefore illustrates how Spanish expectations shaped exploration and distorted reporting, and it should not be described as a Muisca legend of a golden city.

Section 37.9

Three Conquests Meet: Quesada, Belalcázar, and Federmann

The Muisca plateau did not stay Quesada's alone for long. News of the highlands had traveled, along trade routes and through Spanish settlements, and two other expeditions were already on their way. The meeting of the three in 1539 is the most revealing episode in the chapter, because it shows that Spanish expansion was not directed by a central plan.

Sebastián de Belalcázar had left Quito and Popayán, following reports of a wealthy interior and a gilded ruler, and led a force of a couple of hundred men, with Indigenous allies and porters, northeast across the Central Cordillera and into the upper Magdalena valley before crossing into the Muisca country. His route followed communications that his lieutenants had been exploring for several years. His authority came from Pizarro's delegation and, later, from his own governorship of Popayán, and he regarded the highlands as an extension of the territory he had pacified.

Nikolaus Federmann came from the other direction. A German from Ulm, he was an agent of the Welser banking house of Augsburg, which held from Charles V a concession over the province of Venezuela, granted in 1528 as part of Charles V's financial relationship and indebtedness to the Welser house. Spaniards often called the concession "Welser Venezuela," and German historians sometimes call it Klein-Venedig. The Welser company aimed to explore the interior and to find a route to wealth, and Federmann led expeditions from Coro on the Venezuelan coast. Leaving Coro in 1537, he spent roughly two years crossing the Llanos and the eastern Andes before reaching the Muisca highlands from the east in 1539. His story is a reminder that early Spanish imperial expansion involved European merchant-financiers acting under the authority of the monarchy, but the Welser connection should not distract from the Northern-Andean setting in which the encounter occurred.

The three leaders met in the Bogotá highlands in the first half of 1539, each with a company of tired men and each with a claim. Quesada's men had campaigned across much of the plateau and founded a town. Belalcázar had the strongest legal position in the south and the support of a powerful Pizarrist patronage network. Federmann had a license to explore and the backing of a financial house. There was real risk of fighting among the Spaniards, with Indigenous communities in the middle. Instead, the leaders negotiated. They agreed to travel together to Spain and place their claims before the Crown, a decision that reveals how much royal justice, however slow, mattered to men who might otherwise settle matters with arms. Some of Federmann's men were persuaded to remain in the highlands, strengthening the new settlement, and Quesada left his brother Hernán Pérez de Quesada in charge. The three descended the Magdalena and sailed from Cartagena in the summer of 1539.

The outcome was a patchwork. Belalcázar received the governorship of Popayán in 1540, confirming his southern jurisdiction. Welser government in Venezuela collapsed de facto in the mid-1540s, with litigation over the company's privileges continuing into the 1550s, and Federmann died in 1542 without having secured the highlands. Quesada eventually received honors but not the governorship he sought, and his campaigns on the plateau were incorporated into a new jurisdiction that came to be called the New Kingdom of Granada. The settlement of the highlands, then, was determined in part by Spanish imperial arbitration, not by a single victor. For students of the Indigenous history of the region, the lesson is that the Spaniards who arrived were not a unified force. The Muisca and their neighbors faced several enterprises, each seeking tribute, labor, and gold, and each treating the others as rivals.

Section 37.10

Santa Fe and the Making of the New Kingdom of Granada

Quesada had founded Santa Fe on the savanna near the Muisca settlement of Bacatá. Tradition dates the foundation to 6 August 1538, when, according to later accounts, Quesada's men built a small number of huts and a chapel. The date is a convention, and historians point out that the foundation was a process: a camp, a claim, and then, in 1539 (27 April is the date usually given for the formal municipal foundation), municipal acts that established a cabildo and gave the town a more formal legal footing. Students should therefore treat 1538 as the traditional date and 1539 as marking the legal consolidation. The Spanish settlement was called Santa Fe; the Indigenous place-name Bacatá, rendered Bogotá, later became attached to the city and ultimately became its official name.

Quesada also named the territory. He called it the New Kingdom of Granada, after his native Granada, and Spaniards used the label for the highlands and then for a wider claim. It is important to understand the name as an assertion. In the late 1530s it described a possibility, not a coherent colonial state. The boundaries were undefined, the Spanish population was a few hundred, and the area was contested by Belalcázar's government in the south, the Venezuelan province in the east, and the coastal governors, to say nothing of the Indigenous communities that remained unconquered. Not until the following decades would a coherent colonial region, New Granada in its later sense, take shape.

Santa Fe and its companion towns were instruments for turning conquest into settlement. Tunja was founded in 1539 near the old seat of the zaque, and Vélez and other towns followed. Each had a cabildo, a church, and a group of settlers who held rights and obligations. The Spaniards divided the Muisca communities among themselves in encomiendas, Crown grants of rights to receive tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services from a specified community, formally paired with obligations of protection and Christian instruction, though not grants of land or of ownership of people. Chapter 33 introduced the institution in New Spain, and Chapter 40 will discuss it as part of the imperial system. In the Muisca case, encomiendas often followed existing chiefdoms, so that a Spanish settler's income depended on the cooperation of a cacique who had previously collected tribute for his own purposes. The results were often continuity of local authority under a new overlord.

Religious institutions took shape alongside. Priests accompanying the expeditions held masses and baptisms, and by the late 1540s Dominican and other friars began systematic work among Muisca communities, learning the language, building chapels, and destroying or relocating ritual objects. The campaigns against Muisca religious sites, including the burning at Sogamoso, made the religious dimension of conquest visible early. At the same time, the friars' linguistic work produced documents, grammars, and catechisms that later historians would use to reconstruct Muisca institutions.

Section 37.11

Indigenous Resistance after the Foundations

Conquest did not end when towns appeared on maps. In the 1540s and after, Indigenous communities across the Northern Andes continued to resist, to withdraw, and to bargain, and Spanish officials spent years campaigning against them. Resistance took many forms. Some communities attacked Spanish settlements and travelers. Others refused tribute, moved to inaccessible country, hid valuables, or used the new legal system to contest demands. Local leaders sometimes cooperated with Spaniards and sometimes organized opposition, depending on circumstances.

In the Muisca highlands, resistance was often indirect. Muisca caciques negotiated with encomenderos over the quantity and timing of tribute, and communities used flight and concealment to protect themselves from the heaviest demands. Spanish sources complain of hidden gold, of leaders who feigned ignorance, and of villages abandoned on the approach of Spanish parties. Indigenous people also learned to use Spanish institutions, and as the century advanced, Muisca communities brought petitions to colonial officials. These strategies did not prevent severe damage. Forced labor, relocation, and imported diseases caused serious population loss over the following decades, including during major epidemics in the later 1550s, and the demographic shock reshaped Muisca communities even where political continuity survived.

Armed resistance was stronger elsewhere. The Panche and other peoples on the western slopes of the eastern cordillera resisted both Spanish invaders and, according to Spanish sources, their Muisca neighbors. In the emerald country around Muzo, communities fought Spaniards for years and held out into the 1550s and beyond, an example of a resource zone that Spaniards coveted and could not easily take. To the south and west, Indigenous peoples of the upper Magdalena and the Cauca region, whose later resistance is associated with names such as Pijao and Yalcón, fought Spanish settlement with sustained campaigns, including coalition building of the kind recalled in the story of La Gaitana. Pasto and Quillacinga communities in the south pressed their own claims. Around Santa Marta, Tairona communities continued fighting and negotiating, and over decades the societies of the Sierra Nevada were transformed by war, labor demands, and disease while some groups survived in the mountains.

Spaniards also expanded the violence. Expeditions in search of gold, of El Dorado, and of new encomienda grants plundered communities along their routes, seized captives, and forced porters. In 1541, for example, Hernán Pérez de Quesada led an expedition from the highlands toward the eastern plains in search of the legendary gilded ruler. It ended in losses and failure. Such expeditions show that the Spanish presence was a source of new violence in the region as well as a settlement process, and that the search for wealth created cycles of exploration and plunder that fell on Indigenous peoples. The persistence of resistance explains why a Spanish map of the 1540s showing towns from Quito to the Caribbean coast would have misrepresented the reality: those dots were connected by roads and rivers that were only partly secure.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Coastal Bases to Unfinished Settlement

Long-term causes

Caribbean slaving and raiding, the practice of founding towns as claims, Iberian competition for jurisdiction, and the expectation, based on Mexico and Peru, of finding rich states in the interior.

Short-term causes

Information from Indigenous traders about gold, salt, and emeralds; the establishment of Santa Marta, Cartagena, and Quito as bases; and the desire of leaders for independent governments.

Event or transformation

Converging expeditions from the Caribbean, Quito, and Venezuela reached the Muisca plateau and founded Spanish towns amid continuing warfare and negotiation.

Immediate effects

Seizure of gold and emeralds, encomiendas, church foundation, competing Spanish claims, and Indigenous responses ranging from alliance to flight to armed resistance.

Long-term consequences

A chain of Spanish towns dependent on Indigenous labor and intermediaries; royal intervention through the audiencia; and long frontier wars that continued in several areas into the seventeenth century.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the outcome. Gold drew the expeditions, but jurisdiction, labor, Indigenous politics, geography, and disease shaped what the settlements became.
Section 37.12

From Conquest Companies to an Early Colonial Region

By the late 1540s the Northern Andes had begun to acquire a more durable colonial shape. Spanish towns stood from the Caribbean coast to Quito, and between them Spanish settlers held encomiendas, built churches, and relied on a growing network of roads and river passages. Governors answered to the Crown, at least in principle, and the instability of the conquest companies was giving way to a competition among settlers, officials, and clergy over how the colony should be run. Disputes over encomienda, over the treatment of Indigenous people, and over the authority of conquerors against royal officials produced pressure for royal intervention.

The Crown's response was to extend the audiencia, a royal court with governing powers that had already been established elsewhere. Royal measures creating and staffing an audiencia for Santa Fe began in the late 1540s, and the court was formally installed there in April 1550; one for Quito followed in 1563. Their purpose was to limit the power of conquerors and to enforce royal legislation, including rules on tribute and labor that the Crown issued after the 1542 New Laws. The audiencias are introduced here only as the closing development of the conquest period. Their structure, their relations with viceroys and cabildos, and their role in colonial governance belong to Chapter 40, which treats the system as a whole.

Even as institutions arrived, authority remained dependent on Indigenous communities and intermediaries. Caciques continued to collect tribute and organize labor, Indigenous leaders and their communities used litigation to resist excess, and the number of Spaniards in the region remained small. The demographic decline of Indigenous populations, driven by epidemic disease, forced labor, and disruption of agriculture, undermined the economic base of encomiendas and pushed Spanish settlers toward other forms of labor and land tenure. Enslaved Africans, introduced from the 1530s onward, would become important in the mines and in the lowlands, a subject developed in Chapter 44.

Compared with Peru, then, the Northern Andean conquest looks slower, more dispersed, and more competitive. In Peru, the capture of the Inca ruler and the occupation of Cusco gave Spaniards a central point of leverage, though it was followed by the long wars of Chapter 36. In the north, no equivalent point existed, and the conquest advanced town by town and community by community. Compared with Mexico, the Northern Andean case lacks the single, massive Indigenous alliance of Chapter 31, though local alliances mattered. What it shares with both is the pattern of Indigenous politics shaping Spanish possibilities and of conquest being only the beginning of a long process of colonization.

The next chapter turns south to a very different frontier. In Chile, Pedro de Valdivia and his successors would meet a society, the Mapuche, whose resistance would block Spanish control for centuries and set limits on conquest that, like the Northern Andean case, challenge the image of rapid victory.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Indigenous Authority under Spanish Foundations

What Changed

  • Spanish municipalities, churches, and encomiendas were laid over existing communities and chiefdoms
  • Gold, emeralds, and religious objects were seized, and ritual sites were destroyed or relocated
  • Population fell sharply because of epidemics, forced labor, and relocation
  • Land, labor, and tribute were redirected toward Spanish settlers and the Crown

What Stayed the Same

  • Local caciques continued to mediate between communities and outsiders
  • Farming households continued to grow the crops and weave the cloth that supported the new towns
  • Indigenous communities continued to resist, negotiate, and move as they had before 1536
  • The geography of mountains, rivers, and valleys continued to limit control

Who experienced change most directly: Muisca leaders and households near Spanish towns, and the peoples whose temples and burial sites were plundered, experienced the sharpest disruption. Greater continuity: Communities in remote highlands and lowlands outside Spanish reach, and local authorities who kept their positions as intermediaries. Why the pattern matters: It shows that colonial formation in the north was an overlay and negotiation, not a replacement, and that Indigenous institutions shaped the colony from its beginnings.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Spanish conquest of the Northern Andes was not one campaign but a convergence of competing expeditions, moving through a politically fragmented and geographically divided region where no ruler, capital, or battlefield could deliver control. Where Chapters 35 and 36 showed the capture of a centralized empire, Chapter 37 has shown what conquest looked like when that kind of leverage did not exist.

The region's geography and politics explain the difference. The Ecuadorian highlands had only recently been incorporated into Tawantinsuyu, the Colombian Andes were divided by cordilleras and river valleys, and the Muisca and other societies of the north and the Caribbean slopes were organized in chiefdoms and local communities rather than a state with a single center. Spanish expectations drawn from Mexico and Peru fit poorly, and the routes into the interior were as important as the rulers at their ends.

The Caribbean towns of Santa Marta (1525) and Cartagena (1533) were the first gateways. They were founded beside populous Tairona and Zenú societies that resisted for decades, and they were precarious settlements sustained by sea. Their importance lay in providing a base for slaving, plunder, and the gathering of Indigenous information about wealth upriver. Coastal foundation did not mean control of the Sierra Nevada, the lower Magdalena, or the highlands beyond.

In Ecuador, the conquest continued directly from the Inca wars. Rumiñahui held Quito after Atahualpa's execution, and Sebastián de Belalcázar advanced with Cañari allies whose own history of conflict with the Inca state shaped their decision. The campaign took a year or more, and the successive foundations of Spanish Quito in 1534 reflected rivalry among Spaniards as much as control of the land. Settlers depended on Indigenous caciques and communities for food and labor, and the legal act of foundation outpaced the ability to enforce it.

North of Quito, Spaniards built a chain of towns, including Pasto, Cali, and Popayán, through inter-Andean valleys where Indigenous communities negotiated, fled, and fought. Women such as the figure remembered as La Gaitana appear in the chronicles as organizers of resistance, though the sources are late and must be read with caution. The pattern was a network of settlements, not the conquest of a political center.

Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada's expedition up the Magdalena from Santa Marta in 1536–37 was an ordeal of attrition, borne also by Indigenous and African porters and guides, and made possible by Indigenous knowledge of routes and exchange. The survivors entered the Muisca highlands, where political authority was distributed among the zipa at Bacatá, the zaque at Hunza, the religious center at Sugamuxi, and many local chiefs. The Muisca were not an empire, and the deaths, captures, and treatment of leaders such as Tisquesusa, Quemuenchatocha, and Sagipa did not amount to the fall of the Muisca. Historians debate how centralized their political organization was, and the sources are Spanish narratives with their own purposes.

The convergence of Quesada, Belalcázar, and Federmann in 1539 revealed that Spanish conquest itself was fragmented. Each arrived from a different imperial corridor with a different authority, Federmann under the Welser concession in Venezuela, and their claims were resolved not by force but by referral to the Crown. The outcome divided jurisdiction among Popayán, the New Kingdom of Granada, and the Venezuelan province, and showed that imperial arbitration was part of the conquest.

Santa Fe, founded traditionally in 1538 with legal consolidation in 1539, and Tunja, founded in 1539, converted a conquest campaign into settlement. Encomiendas, cabildos, and churches were laid over existing Muisca communities and often relied on existing caciques. The name New Kingdom of Granada was an assertion of claim rather than a description of a coherent colonial state. El Dorado, originally a report of a gilded person and ritual associated with Guatavita, became a roving legend that distorted Spanish exploration.

Indigenous resistance continued after the foundations, from the indirect strategies of Muisca caciques and households to the sustained armed resistance of the Panche, the peoples of the Muzo emerald country, the Pijao and their neighbors, Pasto and Quillacinga communities, and the Tairona. By the late 1540s royal institutions, notably the Audiencia of Santa Fe, began to replace conqueror improvisation, though authority remained dependent on Indigenous intermediaries. The conclusion is that settlement preceded effective control, and that colonial New Granada emerged through a long and uneven process in which Indigenous politics continued to determine what Spanish settlement could accomplish.

Key Terms

Northern Andes
The Andean highlands and adjacent lowlands from southern Ecuador through Colombia, divided by cordilleras and river valleys and politically fragmented in the early sixteenth century.
New Kingdom of Granada
The name Quesada gave to the Muisca highlands, later applied to a wider Spanish claim; an assertion of jurisdiction rather than an established colonial state in the 1530s.
Capitulación
A contract between the Crown and a leader granting rights to explore, conquer, and settle in return for obligations to the monarchy.
Adelantado
A title given to a leader with authority to conquer and govern a frontier territory, often with rights to appoint officials.
Cabildo
The municipal council of a Spanish town, whose creation was a central act of foundation.
Vecino
A recognized citizen and householder of a Spanish town, with rights and obligations, often an encomienda holder.
Encomienda
A Crown grant of rights to receive Indigenous tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services, formally paired with obligations of protection and Christian instruction; it was not itself a grant of land or ownership of Indigenous people.
Cacique
A Spanish term, originally Caribbean, for an Indigenous local leader, applied to a wide variety of Andean and Muisca authorities.
Zipa
The title Spanish sources give to the leader associated with Bacatá; not a king of all the Muisca.
Zaque
The title given to the leader associated with Hunza, the principal northern Muisca center.
Chiefdom
A political community led by a hereditary or prestigious leader, with surplus and ritual authority, but without the bureaucracy of a state.
Bohío
A Spanish term, from the Caribbean, for the thatched round houses of Muisca communities.
El Dorado
Originally a report of a gilded ruler and ritual; later a legend of a golden kingdom or city that moved as expeditions failed to find it.
Guatavita
A lake and community on the Muisca plateau associated with ritual offerings and with Spanish reports of the El Dorado ceremony.
Tumbaga
An alloy of gold and copper widely used in northern Andean and Isthmo-Colombian metalwork.
Welser concession
A grant to the German banking house of the Welsers over the province of Venezuela, in force from 1528, under which Federmann explored.
Foundation (fundación)
The legal and ritual act of establishing a Spanish town, which created a claim to territory without ensuring control.
Hinterland
The territory around a town on which it depended for food, labor, and tribute, and over which it claimed authority.
Ally (auxiliary)
An Indigenous participant in Spanish campaigns, such as the Cañari, with political aims of his or her own.
Porters
Indigenous and enslaved laborers who carried loads for expeditions, often at great cost.
Audiencia
A royal court with governing powers; the Audiencia of Santa Fe was organized through royal measures in the late 1540s and formally installed in April 1550.
Epítome
An anonymous mid-sixteenth-century summary narrative of the conquest of the New Kingdom of Granada.
Inter-Andean valley
A valley between mountain ranges, such as the Cauca, which formed a corridor for movement and settlement.
Rumiñahui
Inca-aligned general who resisted Belalcázar around Quito until captured about 1535.
Cañari
A southern Ecuadorian people who allied with Spaniards against the Inca-aligned forces of Quito.
Conquest versus colonization
Related but distinct processes: military seizure versus institutional, economic, and religious control, which in the Northern Andes lagged behind the founding of towns.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a story that conquest narratives encourage. The capture of a ruler and the fall of a capital make for clear history, but they describe only some of the encounters between Iberian and Indigenous societies. In the Northern Andes, conquest was a slow accumulation of expeditions, foundations, negotiations, and wars, in which no single event settled anything. Seeing that pattern helps students understand why the conquest of the Americas is better described as a set of regional processes than as a single episode.

The chapter also shows how the structures of Indigenous politics shaped colonial outcomes. Because authority among the Muisca and their neighbors was distributed, Spaniards had to work through caciques, communities, and households, and the colonial region that emerged was built on that foundation. Because the Spaniards themselves were divided among rival enterprises, the Crown had to intervene, and the audiencia organized in the late 1540s was a response to the conflicts that the conquest had produced. These patterns of intermediaries, jurisdictional rivalry, and incomplete control would recur across colonial Latin America.

The chapter also supplies a vocabulary for thinking about colonial power. A Spanish legal claim, such as the assertion of a New Kingdom of Granada, was a statement of jurisdiction. A town foundation was a physical and legal node, with a cabildo, a plaza, and a few dozen households. A settlement network was a chain of such nodes joined by roads and rivers. Effective territorial rule meant the ability to collect tribute, command labor, and move safely through the country between the nodes, and in the sixteenth-century Northern Andes that ability was always partial and always negotiated with Indigenous communities.

Chapter 38 follows the same logic in the far south. In Chile, Pedro de Valdivia would found towns and meet the Mapuche, whose resistance would set limits on Spanish power that lasted for centuries. Students should carry forward the view that the Northern Andes were not conquered in a single campaign but were being transformed into a colonial region through a long and uneven process, and that the dots on a Spanish map from Quito to Santa Fe did not represent uniform control of the spaces between them.
