Chile and the Mapuche Frontier

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Curalaba and the Cities That Were Lost

In 1553 Pedro de Valdivia had imagined a chain of Spanish towns extending through southern Chile. Forty-five years later another governor lay dead at Curalaba, and over the next several years most of those southern cities disappeared from Spanish control. If conquest meant founding towns, distributing encomiendas, building forts, and claiming land for the king, Spain had conquered southern Chile repeatedly. Why, then, did it keep losing it?

The question belongs at the beginning because the answer runs against the grain of the conquest narratives in earlier chapters. In Mexico and Peru, the destruction of a great state, with the help of Indigenous allies and epidemic disease, opened the way to colonial rule. In Chile, Spaniards did establish a durable colonial society, but only in the central valleys around Santiago. South of the Biobío River, in forests and river valleys inhabited by the people now called Mapuche, Spanish towns were founded, sacked, rebuilt, besieged, and finally abandoned or isolated. The conflict that resulted lasted generations and produced one of the most enduring frontiers in Spanish America.

To understand 1598, the reader has to go back to the first Spanish expedition under Diego de Almagro, to Pedro de Valdivia's enterprise of the 1540s, to the Indigenous leaders who resisted or negotiated with him, and to the political world that Spaniards attempted to subdue. That world was not a centralized empire. Its communities were organized around kin groups and local leaders, linked by marriage, ritual, and exchange, and capable of assembling much larger alliances when war required it. There was no Chilean Cajamarca, and no single leader whose capture could end resistance. Spaniards who killed or captured one commander found that others took his place, and that those successors had learned from the conflict.

The chapter therefore rejects a simple sequence of Valdivia, Lautaro, Caupolicán, and an ending. It follows instead an arc that runs from invasion through attempted settlement, Indigenous adaptation, repeated war, an apparently expanding colonial occupation, the collapse of 1598, the creation of a fortified frontier, and, finally, a negotiated coexistence that included raids, slavery, trade, missions, captivity, and diplomacy. The end point, the Parliament of Quilín in 1641, was neither a peace nor a treaty between nation-states, and a short coda on the uprising of 1655–56 shows why.

A note on terminology governs the whole chapter. This chapter uses Mapuche as the familiar modern collective term, while noting that sixteenth-century communities did not ordinarily describe themselves through one standardized ethnic label and that early colonial sources used several different names, including local designations, indios de Chile, and araucanos. Where the distinction matters, the term Reche-Mapuche is used for the developing colonial-era political and cultural formations. The chapter also distinguishes three kinds of Indigenous experience: communities incorporated into Spanish central Chile, Indigenous auxiliaries who fought alongside Spaniards, and the independent Reche-Mapuche communities south of the frontier, who did not act as a single permanent bloc.

The evidence needs careful handling. Pedro de Valdivia wrote letters to Charles V that are among the most important contemporary documents, but they were designed to win support and reward. Alonso de Ercilla, who served in the campaigns of 1557–59, wrote the epic poem La Araucana, first published beginning in 1569, which shaped later memory of Lautaro, Caupolicán, and Mapuche valor but cannot be read as a transcript. Soldier-chroniclers such as Góngora Marmolejo and Mariño de Lobera, and the Jesuit Diego de Rosales writing in the seventeenth century, supply other traditions with their own purposes. Archaeology, colonial legal records, parliament minutes, and captivity narratives such as that of Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán allow historians to recover more than the military narratives suggest. No sixteenth-century Mapuche written account survives.

In the textbook's architecture, this chapter completes the argument of Part VIII, which has treated conquest as a series of wars, alliances, rebellions, and negotiated settlements. Chapter 24 described the southern Indigenous world on the eve of conquest, and this chapter recalls it only as needed. Chapter 39 turns east to Brazil and the Río de la Plata. Here the task is to show how a conquest that repeatedly succeeded on paper failed on the ground, and how that failure produced a frontier society.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Spaniards established durable colonial rule in central Chile but not south of the Biobío.
  2. Trace the expeditions of Almagro and Valdivia and distinguish conquest as private enterprise from effective territorial control.
  3. Analyze how encomienda labor, gold washing, Indigenous allies, and demographic disruption shaped central Chile.
  4. Distinguish the terms Reche, Mapuche, and Araucano, and explain why historians avoid projecting a modern collective identity or a centralized state backward.
  5. Evaluate the rise of Lautaro, the defeat of Valdivia at Tucapel, and the limits of Mapuche coalition-building after 1553.
  6. Assess how horses, metal weapons, fortification, and new war leadership changed both Mapuche and Spanish warfare.
  7. Explain Curalaba and the 1598–1604 uprising as the structural turning point from expansive conquest to a fortified frontier.
  8. Interpret the Valdivia letters, La Araucana, and other narrative sources critically, separating event from later memory.
  9. Describe the frontier system, including the Real Situado, the 1608 slavery authorization, and the Defensive War.
  10. Analyze captivity, trade, intermarriage, interpreters, indios amigos, and parlamentos as parts of a frontier society.
  11. Evaluate the Parliament of Quilín of 1641 without treating it as a modern treaty with a unified Mapuche nation.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part VIII's argument that conquest was often prolonged, incomplete, and negotiated.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Spaniards establish durable colonial rule in central Chile but fail to conquer the Mapuche territories south of the Biobío?
  2. How did decades of warfare produce a frontier based on violence, exchange, negotiation, and mutual adaptation?
  3. What does it mean to call the conflict a "war" when fighting alternated with alliances, trade, captivity, diplomacy, missions, and negotiated peace?
  4. How did Mapuche political organization change under the pressure of war, and why is neither "tribal anarchy" nor a centralized "Mapuche state" an adequate description?
  5. Why is the chain of Spanish forts and towns south of the Biobío better understood as a set of claims than as secure territorial rule?
  6. When does a famous story tell us about an event, and when does it tell us more securely about how later generations remembered the event?
  7. Why should Curalaba be understood as a catalyst for a wider uprising rather than as the single cause of the collapse?
  8. How did the Crown's 1608 authorization of enslavement and the Defensive War reflect competing views about how to end the conflict?
  9. What did the Parliament of Quilín accomplish, and what did it not accomplish?

Historical Context

Chile is a long, narrow country squeezed between the Pacific and the Andes, and its sixteenth-century geography shaped both conquest and resistance. In the north lies the Atacama, among the driest deserts in the world, crossed by oasis valleys. South of the desert, a band of semi-arid valleys around Copiapó and Coquimbo gives way to the Mediterranean-climate central valley around Santiago, where rivers descending from the Andes supported irrigated agriculture. Farther south, rainfall increases, forests thicken, and a series of rivers, including the Maule, Itata, and Biobío, cut across the land. South of the Biobío stand the coastal range of Nahuelbuta, wet temperate forests, volcanic lakes, and the archipelago of Chiloé.

Chapter 24 described the Indigenous societies of this region. In the semi-arid north and in the central valley, communities such as the Diaguita and the Picunche farmed irrigated land, and the Inca state had extended its influence into the central valleys, leaving roads and administrative traces, though its southern reach was limited and its hold uneven. South of the Maule, and especially south of the Biobío, lived the communities that Spaniards would later call Araucanians. They cultivated maize, potatoes, quinoa, beans, and other crops, kept domesticated camelids, fished, gathered, and lived in dispersed settlements of houses called rukas. Archaeology and ethnohistory show substantial populations and political complexity, especially around Nahuelbuta, though not a state.

On the Spanish side, Chile was the outermost extension of the Peruvian conquest described in Chapters 35 and 36. It lay far from the centers of imperial wealth, and its reputation among Spaniards in Peru was poor. The desert crossing was brutal, there was no obvious Inca treasure, and the great prizes of the 1530s were in the central Andes. Men who came to Chile were often those who had missed rewards in Peru, and they carried the expectations that Chapters 33 through 37 have described: that conquest would yield encomiendas, gold, and honors, and that founding a town would secure a claim.

The chapter also depends on a basic distinction. Conquest, in the narrow military sense, means defeating Indigenous forces and occupying territory. Colonization means establishing institutions, economies, and settlements that persist. In central Chile the two overlapped, because Spaniards built a colonial society on irrigated land with an existing farming population. South of the Biobío they did not, because the Spaniards could neither defeat Mapuche forces decisively nor sustain the settlements they founded. The same Spanish legal and military practices that produced a colony in one region produced a long war in the other.

Section 38.1

Almagro and the First Spanish Expedition into Chile

The Spanish history of Chile begins before Pedro de Valdivia. In 1534 Charles V divided the southern Andes between the two partners who had conquered Peru: Francisco Pizarro received a governorship called New Castile, and Diego de Almagro received New Toledo, a territory lying farther south. Almagro's grant gave him a legal incentive to find something worth governing. In July 1535 he left Cusco with an expedition that Spanish sources describe as a few hundred Spaniards, accompanied by a much larger body of Indigenous auxiliaries and porters and by enslaved Africans. Among the Indigenous members were Paullu Inca, a member of the Inca ruling family allied with the Spaniards, and Villac Umu, a high priest. Their presence is a reminder that this was a Peruvian enterprise, using Inca roads and Inca authority, as well as a Spanish one.

The crossing of the Andes was disastrous. Spanish accounts describe a march through high, cold, waterless country during which men and animals died of exposure and hunger. The sources count Spanish deaths more carefully than Indigenous ones, but the porters who carried loads at altitude must have borne the heaviest losses. The route the expedition took has been debated, and both routes through the northern desert margins and across high passes have been proposed. What is clear is that the army reached the Copiapó valley and then moved south into the valleys of central Chile, where the Aconcagua valley offered food and the possibility of settlement.

Almagro sent Gómez de Alvarado south to reconnoiter the land beyond the central valley in 1536. Alvarado's party reached the region around the Itata and Ñuble rivers and, according to the tradition associated with the name Reinogüelén, fought a battle against a large Indigenous force of the people Spaniards called promaucaes. The sources describe an indecisive fight, after which the Spaniards turned back. The episode is important less for its details, which come from later narratives, than for what it showed: south of the Maule, Spaniards met communities willing and able to fight, and they found neither the gold nor the political structure they had hoped to capture.

Almagro withdrew in 1537. The reasons were practical and political. The expedition had found little treasure, the logistics of a desert crossing and a distant base were severe, and news from Peru was alarming. Manco Inca's great rebellion, described in Chapter 36, had besieged Cusco, and Almagro's quarrel with Pizarro over who controlled Cusco itself had made the distant north of Chile seem a poor use of time. He returned through the Atacama, took Cusco in 1537, and was defeated and executed after the civil conflict of 1538. The expedition left almost no Spanish institutions behind, but it left a reputation. Chile, in the minds of many Spaniards in Peru, was a cold, poor, and dangerous land.

Students should avoid the phrase "discovery of Chile." The valleys Almagro entered had been inhabited for millennia, parts of them had been drawn into the orbit of the Inca state, and Indigenous communities already knew the routes. What the expedition accomplished was to open a Spanish route and to demonstrate that central Chile contained farming populations and river valleys capable of supporting settlement. It also began the pattern, repeated for the next century, in which Spanish plans about Chile were shaped by events and interests in Peru.

Section 38.2

Valdivia's Venture: Conquest as a Private Enterprise

Pedro de Valdivia was a soldier from Extremadura who had served in Europe before coming to Peru and who rose to prominence as a military officer under Francisco Pizarro, fighting on Pizarro's side at Las Salinas in 1538. In 1539 Pizarro authorized him to undertake the conquest of Chile. As in Chapter 35's treatment of Pizarro, the important point is not heroic biography but the structure of the enterprise. Conquest was a private business under license from a royal official. The leader raised money from partners and investors, recruited men with promises of shares, and expected to recoup his costs through grants of Indigenous labor, mines, and offices. Failure meant debt and ruin. Success meant titles and income.

Valdivia's expedition left Cusco in January 1540, starting small and gathering recruits along the way. Spanish sources suggest a company that grew to something over a hundred Spaniards, with Indigenous auxiliaries and servants, many of them yanaconas, Andean people in personal service to Spaniards, who numbered perhaps in the hundreds or more, together with enslaved Africans. They brought horses, pigs, seed, tools, and the elements of a colonial household. At least one woman accompanied the expedition as a full participant: Inés de Suárez, a Spanish woman from Extremadura whose role in the early years of Santiago was recorded with admiration by later writers. Women were fewer than men in these companies but they were not absent, and Suárez's career, which included a grant of land and later a marriage into the colonial elite, shows how Spanish women could take part in conquest and benefit from it.

The enterprise was shaky from the beginning. A rival, Pedro Sancho de Hoz, held a royal license for the same territory and joined the expedition with a claim to share authority, leading to a dispute that Valdivia resolved by compelling him to accept a partnership on terms that left Valdivia in command. The episode suggests why Valdivia's later legal moves, including having his own cabildo elect him governor, mattered to him: his authority rested on a shaky foundation and he repeatedly worked to strengthen it with formal acts.

The route followed the one Almagro had pioneered, up through the northern desert to the Copiapó valley and then south, benefiting from Inca roads and Indigenous provisioning where available. The caravan reached the Mapocho valley in late 1540. By then the expedition's survival depended on the willingness of valley communities to supply food, and on the leaders' skill in negotiation and display of force. Indigenous leaders had to decide how to respond to a small but well-armed party that had moved in with animals, steel, and the demand to be fed. Their choices, which varied from valley to valley, determined what the Spaniards could do next.

Section 38.3

Santiago, Michimalonco, and a Colony on the Edge

On 12 February 1541, Valdivia founded Santiago del Nuevo Extremo in the Mapocho valley, at the foot of the hill called Huelén, later Santa Lucía. The site made sense. The valley held fertile, irrigated land, probably worked by Picunche farmers using canals that predated the Spaniards, and it lay at a convenient distance from the sea and from the Andean passes toward Cuyo. The new town received a plaza, house lots, and a cabildo, and a few months later its cabildo named Valdivia governor, a legal act that supplied him with a source of authority independent of the rival license of Sancho de Hoz and his dependence on Pizarro. As Chapter 37 emphasized, a foundation was a legal claim, not a conquest.

The Spanish settlement was tiny and fragile, and Indigenous leaders understood that. A principal figure was Michimalonco, an Indigenous leader associated with the Aconcagua valley north of the Mapocho. Spanish accounts describe an initial period in which he and other leaders negotiated with Valdivia, supplied food, and tested the strangers. The accounts are not neutral, but they show leaders who had their own relationship with the Inca state, with other valley communities, and with each other, and who considered whether the newcomers could be turned to advantage or expelled.

On 11 September 1541, while Valdivia was away from the settlement with a part of his force, an Indigenous coalition attacked Santiago. Spanish narratives describe a determined assault that burned the town and nearly destroyed it. Almost all the settlers' possessions were lost, and the chroniclers record that the Spaniards salvaged little more than the clothes they wore, a small amount of seed, and a few animals. Inés de Suárez features prominently in later accounts of the defense. A well-known tradition holds that she ordered the execution of captive leaders during the attack, but the story comes from later writers and cannot be verified. What can be said is that the attack was serious, that Spanish survival depended on a small garrison and on the return of Valdivia's force, and that the settlement was rebuilt from ruins.

The next two years were a period of hunger and improvisation. The colony survived on Indigenous food production, on the planting of the seeds that had been saved, and on the arrival of reinforcements from Peru. Alonso de Monroy traveled north to seek help and returned with men and supplies in 1543. The lesson of 1541 is the one Chapter 37 drew from Quito and Santa Fe: the foundation of a Spanish town was a claim, and the claim could be erased by the people on whose labor and land it depended. Santiago endured not because the Spaniards controlled the Mapocho valley but because, over the following years, they and the valley's leaders worked out arrangements that permitted survival, arrangements that were coercive and unequal but that made the colony viable.

Section 38.4

Central Chile: Encomienda, Gold, and Indigenous Labor

How did the colony survive and grow? The answer lies in the combination of food, gold, and labor. Central Chile had an agricultural base, and Spaniards depended on farming communities for maize, beans, potatoes, and other foods in the first years. Valdivia also introduced European crops and livestock, including wheat, grapes, cattle, sheep, and pigs, whose importance would grow over the following decades. At the same time, the search for gold shaped Spanish priorities. Spanish settlers found gold in river sands and washed it in streams in places such as Marga Marga, near the coast, and later in the south. Gold washing was hard work, and the Spaniards demanded it from Indigenous workers.

The legal instrument that made this possible was the encomienda. As Chapter 33 explained, an encomienda was a Crown grant of rights to receive Indigenous tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services, formally paired with obligations of protection and Christian instruction. It was not itself a grant of land or ownership of Indigenous people, though in practice holders often treated the arrangement as permission to compel work. Valdivia distributed encomiendas to Spaniards in the central valley, assigning to each holder the right to the services of a specified community under its local leader. For settlers, the grants were the reward of conquest. For Indigenous communities, they meant demands for work in fields, households, and gold-washing sites, as well as relocation of people to work.

Central Chile's incorporation was therefore neither a simple Spanish victory nor a peaceful accommodation. It combined violence, negotiation, and demographic disruption. Indigenous leaders became intermediaries, assembling workers and supplying goods, and in return they sought protection of their communities and preservation of their status. Some communities resisted, fled, or were punished. Over time, forced labor, disease, relocation, and the disruption of agriculture brought sharp population decline among the valley peoples. Estimates of the preconquest population are uncertain, but all agree that the decline within a generation was large. Andean yanaconas from Peru, enslaved Africans, and later mestizos joined the colonial labor force, and the social landscape began to change.

Africans and women appear in this world in ways that the narrative of conquest often obscures. Enslaved Africans had traveled with the expeditions as laborers and soldiers, and a few gained freedom through their service. Juan Valiente, an African who had been enslaved and who served in the Chilean campaigns, is recorded as having received an encomienda before dying in the wars of the south. Spanish women such as Inés de Suárez held land and encomiendas, while Indigenous women supplied household labor, cooking, weaving, and, under coercion, sexual and domestic service to Spanish men. Their children, of mixed Spanish and Indigenous descent, formed the beginnings of a mestizo population.

This chapter does not treat the later colonial economy in detail, which belongs to chapters on the colonial economy and society. The relevant point here is that central Chile was incorporated into Spanish colonial structures in a way that the south was not, and that this incorporation supplied the base of labor, food, and settlers from which Valdivia and his successors attempted to move south. The gold, the encomiendas, and the labor that central Chile provided made the southern expansion possible, and the failure of that expansion is better understood in light of what the center could and could not supply.

Section 38.5

Building a Corridor South: La Serena, the Maule, and Concepción

Valdivia's plan was to build a chain of Spanish bases linking Chile to Peru and extending toward the south. To the north, La Serena was founded in 1544 by Juan Bohón in the Coquimbo valley, partly to secure a link between Santiago and Peru by land and sea. Indigenous communities, notably Diaguita people, attacked the town in 1549, destroying it. It was refounded in the same year under Francisco de Aguirre, a pattern familiar from Chapter 37: a foundation that survived by being repeated. The Pacific coast also began to matter. A small maritime link, connecting the new port of Valparaíso with Peru, brought supplies, people, and information.

Valdivia's own career depended on events in Peru. In 1547 he traveled north to support the royal representative Pedro de la Gasca against the rebel Gonzalo Pizarro, an intervention that won him confirmation of his governorship in 1548 and recruited reinforcements. His reliance on Peruvian politics illustrates how closely the Chilean enterprise remained tied to wider imperial currents, and how his legal legitimacy had to be renewed in Lima as well as defended in Santiago. When he returned to Chile in 1549, he brought men and resources for a southward advance.

South of the Maule, the character of the land and of the people changed. The Spaniards encountered more rain, denser forest, and more populous Indigenous communities that were not under any local equivalent of Spanish-style hierarchy. In early 1550 Valdivia led a campaign toward the coast near the Andalién River and the bay of Penco, where, according to Spanish accounts, he fought a large Indigenous force. His army included, along with Spaniards, thousands of Indigenous auxiliaries, many of them from the central valley, whose participation reminds us that the Spanish advance was never merely Spanish. In October 1550 Valdivia founded the city of Concepción on the bay, giving the Spaniards a base near the Biobío.

The Biobío itself needs to be introduced as geography before it becomes a political frontier. It is one of the large rivers of Chile, wide and deep enough to be a significant obstacle, flowing from the Andes to the Pacific near Concepción. In 1550 it was not a boundary known to either side. It was a river, with communities living on both banks and trading, marrying, and fighting across it. It became the frontier later, through a long process of conflict, and the next sections follow that process.

Geography Study

Geography Study: From the Atacama to Chiloé

Using a physical map of Chile and adjacent Argentina, students should trace the geography of conquest and resistance from north to south:

  • The desert approach: the Atacama, with its oasis valleys, and the route to the Copiapó valley that Almagro and Valdivia used.
  • The semi-arid north: Copiapó, the Huasco and Coquimbo valleys, and La Serena.
  • Central Chile: the Aconcagua valley, the Mapocho valley and Santiago, the Maipo and the central valley as far as the Maule, with irrigation, existing farming populations, and Andean passes.
  • The central rivers: the Maule and the Itata, the northern edge of the territory of the promaucaes and the southern limit of Inca influence.
  • The Biobío and the coast: the Biobío River and its mouth, Concepción and Penco, and the coastal range of Nahuelbuta.
  • The southern forts and towns: Arauco, Tucapel, Purén, Angol (Los Confines), La Imperial, Villarrica, Valdivia, and Osorno.
  • The far south: the lakes and volcanoes of the southern Andes and the archipelago of Chiloé with the town of Castro.

Note the Andean passes and the rivers that run from the cordillera to the sea, the dense forests and heavy rainfall south of the Itata, and the fact that Spanish towns south of the Biobío were separated from Santiago by long distances and by communities that did not recognize Spanish authority. Remember that the modern borders of Chile and Argentina did not exist, and that Indigenous communities lived and traveled on both sides of the Andes.

Section 38.6

Who Were the Peoples the Spaniards Called “Araucanians”?

Before following the war south of the Biobío, students need to understand the people Spaniards fought, and the vocabulary used to describe them. Chapter 24 introduced the Indigenous peoples of southern South America, and the section recalls only what the conquest narrative requires. The word Mapuche, from mapu, "land," and che, "people," is the familiar modern collective name for the Mapuche-speaking communities of south-central Chile. It is the correct term for the present and the term this chapter uses. But it was not the normal generic label in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century written sources.

Historical Vocabulary
Reche, Mapuche, and Araucano

Araucano was the Spanish term for the peoples of the region around Arauco and the Nahuelbuta range, taken from a place name and spread by Ercilla's poem. Spanish writers also used local names and the broad expression indios de Chile. Reche, often glossed as "true people," is a term many scholars use for the Indigenous populations of the early colonial period, before the broader name Mapuche became the standard collective label.

These terms matter because they remind students that a single, standardized ethnic identity was not a fixed starting condition. Sixteenth-century communities shared language, ritual, and kinship ties, but they did not ordinarily describe themselves through one label. This chapter uses Mapuche as the familiar collective term and Reche-Mapuche where the developing colonial-era political and cultural formations are at issue. The word Araucano appears in quotation or as a Spanish usage, not as a neutral description.

The social organization of these communities is often misunderstood. The basic unit was the lof, a local community of related households under a leader, the lonko ("head"). Several lof were linked, ritually and politically, in a larger grouping often associated with a ritual altar called a rehue, and groupings of rehue could form larger alliances, sometimes called ayllarehue, "nine rehue," in the sources. Beyond these, in the seventeenth century Spanish and Mapuche sources describe still larger territorial divisions sometimes called butalmapu. Specialists called machi, often women, provided healing and ritual leadership. A leader called a toqui could be chosen for war, and his authority was tied to the war.

Historical Vocabulary
Lonko, Toqui, Rehue, and Ayllarehue

The lonko was the head of a local kin-based community. The toqui (from a word associated with the stone axe) was a war leader, chosen when large-scale fighting required coordination across communities. A rehue was a ritual center, marked by an altar, that anchored a cluster of communities, and the ayllarehue was a larger federation of such clusters assembled for ritual and defense.

These terms must not be read as timeless administrative offices. Their meanings changed over time, Spanish transcriptions were inconsistent, and some of the larger structures took shape under the pressures of war. A toqui was not a permanent hereditary king, and the lonko was not a subordinate official of a unified state. The vocabulary helps students see that Mapuche politics was decentralized but capable of larger coordination, and that Spanish writers mapped it imperfectly onto European categories.

Two interpretive errors should be avoided. The first is the old image of "tribal anarchy," in which Mapuche society was so fragmented that it was incapable of sustained collective action. Archaeological and ethnohistorical work, particularly around Nahuelbuta, shows substantial population, agricultural production, and political complexity, and the history of the war shows that communities could assemble large alliances. The second error is the opposite one: to imagine a centralized Mapuche nation or state with a fixed hierarchy. There was no capital, no standing bureaucracy, and no permanent supreme ruler. Authority was dispersed, and the large alliances that Spaniards confronted were assembled for particular purposes and often dissolved afterward.

This dispersal created a strategic problem for Spanish conquest. In Mexico and Peru, as earlier chapters showed, the seizure of a ruler or a capital gave Spaniards leverage over a large system. In Chile there was nothing comparable. Spaniards could defeat a group, burn its houses, and take captives, but the communities around it could continue to resist, and new alliances could form. At the same time, this decentralization was not a timeless property of Mapuche society that automatically defeated empires. It interacted with Spanish tactics, with the political skill of Indigenous leaders, and with the transformations that war itself produced, as the following sections show.

Section 38.7

Forts and Cities South of the Biobío

Between about 1550 and 1553, Valdivia pursued an aggressive strategy of southern expansion. After founding Concepción, he and his captains established a series of forts and towns south of the Biobío. Fort Arauco and Fort Tucapel were built on or near the coastal range to guard routes and to control gold-bearing areas, and Purén was founded as another fort. La Imperial was founded in 1551 near the Cautín River, Valdivia in 1552 on a river to the south, Villarrica in 1552 near the lake, and Los Confines, later Angol, in 1553. Their names and layout reflected Spanish concepts: a plaza, a church, a cabildo, and house lots for settlers who would become vecinos of the new places.

The strategy was driven by gold and labor. Spaniards found gold in the south, notably in the region near Concepción and at sites farther south, and the desire to exploit it drew settlers. Valdivia granted encomiendas in the new districts, awarding Spaniards the rights to tribute and services from Indigenous communities that were, in many cases, not under Spanish control at all. These grants were aspirations. A settler who held an encomienda over a community in the forests south of the Biobío could claim tribute only if that community was willing or compelled to provide it. Meanwhile, Indigenous workers were pressed into gold washing in the mines, and communities that resisted were attacked.

The key analytical point is that a chain of forts and towns represented Spanish claims, not secure territorial rule. Each settlement was a small island of Spaniards, often a few dozen households, surrounded by communities that had not accepted Spanish authority. The towns could be supplied only with difficulty, relying on Indigenous food and labor, which were provided under pressure and with resentment. Communication between the towns depended on roads and rivers that could be cut. A Spanish map would have shown a chain of cities from Concepción to the lakes, but the spaces between them were controlled, in practice, by Mapuche communities.

Valdivia himself shared the vision of an expanding frontier. His letters to Charles V described Chile as a fertile and healthy land and sought reinforcements and royal favor, and he spoke of pushing south toward the Strait of Magellan. He sent ships along the southern coast, and his lieutenants looked to extend the Spanish presence. This ambition, characteristic of a conqueror who needed continued success to justify his authority, led him to stretch his small force across a very large area. When resistance came, the stretch proved dangerous.

Indigenous communities had reasons to resist that the sources partly reveal. Spanish demands for labor in the mines, for food, and for service disrupted agriculture and households, and the settlement of forts and towns on their land threatened their autonomy. Spanish sources acknowledge these tensions, though they often frame them as rebellion. In the early 1550s, resistance was building in the coastal range and the forests, as communities discussed how to respond and as leaders considered the knowledge that some Indigenous people had gained of Spanish methods. The rising that came at the end of 1553 was the product of two or three years of Spanish expansion, not a sudden event.

Section 38.8

Lautaro, Tucapel, and the Death of Valdivia

The most famous episode of the war is the defeat of Valdivia at Tucapel on 25 December 1553. The figure at the center of later accounts is Lautaro (Leftraru, a name often glossed as "swift hawk" or "swift caracara"). According to the sources, he was a young Indigenous man who had been taken into Spanish service, perhaps as a page or groom in Valdivia's household, where he learned about horses, weapons, and Spanish military habits. He is said to have escaped and returned to his people, bringing that knowledge with him. This is the broad outline. Much of his later biography, as it appears in Ercilla's poem and in later chronicles, contains literary embellishment, and students should treat the details of his life as less secure than the outline.

The uprising began, according to Spanish narratives, with an Indigenous attack on Fort Tucapel in December 1553. When news reached Valdivia, he left Concepción with a small force of Spaniards, perhaps fifty according to the sources, and a number of Indigenous auxiliaries, and marched toward Tucapel. On the way he found the fort destroyed. Near the site, a large Mapuche force confronted him. Spanish accounts describe a prolonged fight in which successive waves of fresh warriors replaced those who had been fighting, wearing down the Spanish horsemen and eventually overwhelming them. The sources credit Lautaro with the tactic of rotating squads in battle, though whether he alone devised it is uncertain. Among those who died in the fighting, according to the records, was Juan Valiente, the African encomendero who had served in the Chilean campaigns.

The rising did not come from nowhere. Spanish sources acknowledge, in their own terms, that the demands of the new southern settlements fell heavily on nearby communities, especially the labor required in the gold-washing districts and the food and service demanded by garrisons and encomenderos. The forts of Arauco, Tucapel, and Purén stood in territory that Mapuche communities used and regarded as their own, and the Spaniards' practice of seizing captives and burning houses in response to resistance widened the circle of people with grievances. Mapuche leaders, according to the chronicles, consulted across communities before moving against the forts, which suggests deliberate planning rather than spontaneous revolt. The Spanish accounts credit the speed of the defeat to surprise and numbers, but the preparation behind it was political as well as military.

Valdivia was defeated and captured. What followed is known only through narratives that differ. He was killed, but how and by whom are contested. The Spanish defeat at Tucapel was nonetheless one of the major turning points of the conquest, because it showed that Spaniards could be beaten by an Indigenous force in open battle, that their cavalry and weapons did not guarantee victory, and that the governor whose authority held the whole enterprise together could be removed. The campaign also reveals that the Spanish advance had depended on a leader whose death opened a political crisis among the Spaniards themselves, as rival captains and town councils disputed who should govern while the war continued.

Source Caution

How Did Valdivia Die? Competing Traditions and the Problem of Dramatic Stories

The problem: No Spanish survivor of Tucapel witnessed Valdivia's death and wrote it down immediately. The accounts we have come from chroniclers who relied on reports from Indigenous captives, participants, and later retellings, and they differ on how he died and who was responsible.

The traditions: Some chroniclers say that he was killed in a blow or beating soon after capture. Others describe a more elaborate scene in which he was tortured or forced into a ritual death, and later traditions include a vivid story that molten gold was poured down his throat in mockery of his greed. The poem La Araucana and other works contributed to the shaping of memory. The molten-gold story has been widely repeated in popular accounts, but it is not supported by a contemporary eyewitness record, and it fits a pattern of moralizing tales about Spanish greed.

What historians can say: It is secure that Valdivia was defeated and killed at or near Tucapel in late December 1553. The manner of his death cannot be established reliably, and the dramatic versions tell us more about the values and anxieties of later writers than about the event. This chapter therefore does not adopt any one version as fact.

Section 38.9

From Tucapel to Santiago: Lautaro's War, 1554–1557

After Tucapel, Mapuche forces pressed their advantage. Francisco de Villagra, who assumed leadership of the Spanish forces, marched south in early 1554, and at Marihueñu he was defeated by a Mapuche army. Spanish accounts describe heavy losses and a disordered retreat. The battle left Concepción exposed, and in 1554 the town was evacuated and burned. Spaniards withdrew northward, and the Spanish presence south of the Biobío shrank to the isolated southern towns of La Imperial, Valdivia, Villarrica, and a few forts that held out with difficulty.

The following years display the tactical adaptation of Mapuche forces. The sources describe Mapuche commanders using forts and palisades built in strong positions, pits and obstacles to impede horses, ambushes in forests and river valleys, and the use of long pikes against cavalry. Fighters rotated in organized squads, and communication networks allowed assemblies to gather quickly. Mapuche warriors also captured and used Spanish equipment, including horses and metal weapons, a theme that Section 38.11 develops. Spanish chroniclers, writing for audiences that expected European military superiority, often explain these tactics as the work of one brilliant commander. The evidence shows broader learning across Mapuche communities, with Lautaro as one prominent leader among several.

In 1556, according to the sources, Lautaro led a force north across the Biobío, aiming to carry the war into the central valleys. The aim was ambitious. Success would have threatened Santiago itself, the center of the colony and the site of its encomienda economy. But the campaign also revealed limits. Many communities in the central valley were already incorporated into Spanish encomienda, and some Indigenous leaders and their followers sided with the Spaniards for reasons of local rivalry, survival, or advantage. Lautaro could not rely on a uniform response from the peoples north of the Biobío. Famine and disease, which Spanish chroniclers report as accompanying the war, further weakened communities on all sides.

Lautaro was killed in April 1557, according to Spanish sources, in a surprise attack on his encampment near the Mataquito River by a Spanish force that included Indigenous allies. His death ended his campaign, though not the war. It also illustrates the point that the conflict was never simply Spaniards against Mapuche. Indigenous auxiliaries, indios amigos in the Spanish term, fought on the Spanish side, and the people who took part in the attack on Lautaro's camp included men whose own communities had been drawn into the colonial system. Their loyalties were not betrayals of a Mapuche nation that did not yet exist in the form later nationalism would imagine. They were choices made in a political landscape that was local, plural, and under pressure.

Taken together, the years 1553 to 1557 show both Mapuche success and its limits. The Mapuche destroyed or forced the abandonment of Spanish settlements, inflicted major defeats, and demonstrated the capacity to coordinate large forces. They did not, however, eliminate the Spanish presence, and they could not extend their campaign into the central valley where Spanish encomienda and Indigenous auxiliaries gave the colony resilience. The pattern of advance and recovery that marked later decades was already visible.

Section 38.10

Caupolicán, García Hurtado de Mendoza, and the War after Lautaro

The Spanish response came with new resources. In 1557 García Hurtado de Mendoza, the young son of the viceroy of Peru, arrived in Chile as governor with reinforcements, artillery, and ships. His appointment shows that the Crown and its Peruvian officials had concluded that the loss of the south was serious. His army included veterans of the Peruvian wars and many Indigenous auxiliaries, and among the soldiers was a young nobleman and writer, Alonso de Ercilla, whose experiences supplied the material for La Araucana.

Hurtado de Mendoza's campaign had immediate military success. He fought Mapuche forces at Lagunillas and at Millarapue in 1557, where Spanish artillery and organization, together with Indigenous allies, helped win a costly victory, and he crossed the Biobío, refounded Concepción, and built new forts, including one at Cañete. In 1558 he founded Osorno in the south, expanding the Spanish presence beyond the previous limits. For the Spaniards, the campaign demonstrated that a well-supplied force could recover ground.

The Mapuche leader most associated with this period is Caupolicán. Spanish accounts describe him as a leader chosen to direct the war after Valdivia's defeat and as a prominent commander in the fighting against Hurtado de Mendoza. He was captured, according to the sources, in 1558, and executed at Cañete. Many details of his capture and death are tied to literary tradition. The famous account that he was impaled and faced death with calm dignity comes largely from Ercilla and later chroniclers whose purposes included praising the courage of the enemy and, in some cases, criticizing Spanish cruelty. It cannot be read as a reliable transcript. What can be said is that a major Mapuche commander was captured and executed, and that Spanish authorities hoped the execution would break resistance.

It did not. This is the central lesson of the period after Lautaro. The capture or death of a leader, which in other conquests could shift the political balance, did not end Mapuche resistance. New leaders emerged, and the political structures of the Mapuche, being dispersed, were not dependent on any one commander. Within a few years, Mapuche forces were again attacking Spanish forts and towns, and the Spanish occupation, though expanded, remained under threat. Hurtado de Mendoza's own governorship ended in 1561, and the cycle of war and recovery continued under his successors.

Pedro de Valdivia's Letters to Charles V and Alonso de Ercilla's La Araucana

Creators: Pedro de Valdivia (letters); Alonso de Ercilla (poem) · Dates: Valdivia wrote in the 1540s and early 1550s; Ercilla's poem was published in parts beginning in 1569, with later parts in 1578 and 1589 · Type: Letters to the Crown from a conquest leader; an epic poem by a soldier-poet who took part in the campaigns of 1557–59

Context: Valdivia's letters are among the most important contemporary documents on the conquest of Chile. They were written to persuade Charles V and his officials that the enterprise deserved support and reward, and they describe the land, the Indigenous peoples, the difficulties faced, and the services rendered. Ercilla wrote a very different kind of text. La Araucana is an epic poem in the Renaissance tradition, composed in part during and after the campaigns, narrating the war with attention to Indigenous leaders and battles. It was enormously influential, shaping the memory of figures such as Lautaro and Caupolicán in Chilean culture and, later, in Mapuche historical memory.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: In his letters, Valdivia presents Chile as a healthy, fertile land capable of supporting Spanish settlement, stresses his own hardships and expenses, describes the construction of towns, and asks for men, arms, and royal confirmation of his authority. Ercilla's poem presents the war as a heroic struggle in which Mapuche leaders display courage and skill, Spanish commanders sometimes act cruelly, and the Spanish soldier-narrator reflects on the cost of conquest.

Analysis: Valdivia's letters are contemporary and informative about Spanish aims, about the settlement of towns, and about how a conqueror framed his project, but they are advocacy and should never be treated as neutral battlefield reports. They tell us little about how Indigenous communities understood events. Ercilla's poem is a masterpiece of the literary imagination and a major source for how the war was remembered. Its speeches and scenes follow poetic conventions and cannot be extracted as eyewitness transcripts, and the poet's admiration for Mapuche courage coexisted with the use of that admiration to dignify Spanish victory. Neither source is a transparent Mapuche voice. Historians combine them with other chronicles, such as those of Góngora Marmolejo and Mariño de Lobera, with legal documents, and with archaeology.

Section 38.11

A War That Changed Both Sides

Before taking stock of the Spanish position in the later sixteenth century, it is useful to pause and ask what the war itself did. The fighting that began in the 1550s did not merely test two fixed societies against each other. It changed both of them, and the changes shaped what followed.

The most visible change was the adoption of horses and metal by Mapuche communities. Horses were new to the Americas, and in the 1550s only Spaniards possessed them. Mapuche fighters captured them in battle and began to breed and ride them, and over the following decades the horse became an important part of Mapuche warfare, mobility, and wealth. By the late sixteenth century Mapuche cavalry had become a significant military force, a development that undermined the Spaniards' original advantage. Iron weapons and tools, obtained by capture and later by trade, supplemented stone and wood. Livestock, including sheep, cattle, and pigs, also entered Mapuche economies, changing diets, settlement patterns, and exchange. These adoptions were not passive borrowing. They were choices by communities adapting objects to their own needs and combining them with existing skills.

Tactics changed as well. Mapuche leaders developed fortified positions, ambush techniques, the use of terrain, and organized rotation of fighting squads, and they learned to counter cavalry with pikes, pits, and broken ground. Spaniards, for their part, adjusted. They relied on Indigenous scouts and auxiliaries who knew the terrain, built forts to protect supply lines, and adopted mobile tactics suited to the forests. The Spanish army of the later sixteenth century was not the army of 1541. Fort warfare, the burning of crops and houses, and the seizure of captives became standard Spanish practice, and they shaped the wider society in which Mapuche communities lived.

Political organization changed under pressure. The conflict encouraged wider alliances than had been common among local communities, and it elevated war leaders whose authority came from success in battle. Scholarship stresses the development of broader Reche-Mapuche alliances and new forms of military leadership under colonial pressure. The toqui, for example, was a leader for war, and the experience of long conflict gave such offices new importance. This point is important for understanding Mapuche history. Mapuche resistance was not simply the automatic result of a decentralized society that had always been perfectly suited to defeat empires. It was also the product of active institution-building and coalition-making by Mapuche leaders responding to a prolonged threat. The argument is that decentralization prevented a single decapitation strike while Mapuche communities also built new institutions and coalitions appropriate to prolonged warfare.

Finally, the war changed the Spanish colony. It tied Chile to a permanent military problem that consumed resources, shaped the Spanish population, and created a constituency of soldiers and captains whose careers depended on the war. It also hardened Spanish attitudes toward the Mapuche, whom writers increasingly described in terms of both admiration and hostility. Those attitudes fed into the legal justifications for enslavement and into the policies examined in later sections.

Section 38.12

Spanish Chile South of the Biobío: An Occupation That Looked Stronger Than It Was

From about 1560 to the 1590s, Spanish Chile gave the impression of a successful conquest. After Hurtado de Mendoza, Spanish governors rebuilt forts and towns, founded new settlements, and extended encomiendas. Concepción was reestablished, and the southern cities of La Imperial, Valdivia, Villarrica, and Osorno grew, with churches, cabildos, convents, and in La Imperial's case a bishopric. In 1567 Castro was founded on the island of Chiloé, extending the Spanish presence into the archipelago and drawing Indigenous communities there into encomienda. Religious orders arrived, including Franciscans and Mercedarians, and Jesuits reached Chile in 1593. Gold washing continued in the southern districts, and the encomienda grants of earlier decades were maintained or renewed.

To a visitor reading the records, the colony would have seemed to be consolidating. There was a royal court for a time, the Audiencia of Concepción, which operated for part of the later sixteenth century before being suppressed, a reminder of the Crown's concern with the war. Governors such as Rodrigo de Quiroga and Alonso de Sotomayor strengthened defenses and paid attention to supply and recruitment. Pacific connections brought the colony into the wider world: the English captain Francis Drake raided the coast of Chile in 1578, an event that heightened Spanish concern for the security of the Pacific.

The impression was misleading. The southern towns were military islands, supplied with difficulty and dependent on local labor and communication routes that could be cut. Spanish settlers formed a small minority, and their prosperity depended on Indigenous workers whose cooperation was coerced and resented. Indigenous communities had never accepted Spanish authority in the countryside around the towns, and periodic uprisings, in the 1560s and 1570s and in the following decades, reminded Spaniards of the fragility of the system. These uprisings recurred without producing a decisive outcome, and the governors who suppressed them returned to a situation in which the same causes persisted: demands for labor, for tribute, and for land.

The central analytical point of this section is the gap between appearances and realities. A map showing Spanish towns from Concepción to Chiloé, with their cabildos and encomienda lists, suggests control. The reality was a set of settlements whose authority extended only as far as the Spaniards could protect them and the surrounding communities tolerated them. In the 1590s, as Spanish demands intensified, the underlying pressure grew. The following sections show how it broke.

Everyday Life and Social History

Rukas, Fields, Forts, and the Households of War

For Mapuche households, daily life centered on the ruka, a thatched house, and the work of farming, herding, gathering, and weaving. Families grew potatoes, maize, quinoa, and beans, kept domesticated camelids and, after contact, sheep and cattle, and moved among settlements according to seasons and social ties. Women did much of the agricultural, cooking, and weaving work, and weaving carried economic and social value. Machi, often women, provided healing and ritual leadership. War disrupted this life: crops were burned, houses destroyed, and families displaced, and the departure of men for war increased the burdens on women and elders. Archaeological work on sixteenth-century households shows how communities adapted their settlement patterns and material culture in response.

For Spaniards in the southern towns, life was precarious. Soldiers lived in forts and barracks, often poorly supplied, and settlers depended on Indigenous workers for food and labor. Spanish women, fewer in number, managed households in a setting of constant threat. For Indigenous people incorporated into the colony, including workers in gold washing and farm labor, life meant tribute, forced service, and the risk of death from overwork and disease. Captives, whether Spanish or Indigenous, entered households in positions of servitude or, in some cases, adoption. These experiences are recorded unevenly. Spanish writers focused on battles and governors, and Mapuche voices survive mainly through later texts and archaeology.

Section 38.13

Curalaba, 1598: The Collapse of the Southern Conquest

In December 1598 Governor Martín García Óñez de Loyola, traveling with a small party, was killed at Curalaba. Óñez de Loyola, a Basque-born soldier who had taken part in the war for years, had been governor since 1592. He was married to Beatriz Clara Coya, a woman of the Inca royal line, a marriage that connected the conquest of Chile to the legacy of the Inca state described in Chapters 35 and 36. According to Spanish accounts, he was camped with perhaps fifty or sixty Spaniards and some Indigenous auxiliaries in the Curalaba area when a Mapuche force attacked at dawn, and the party was destroyed. Spanish sources place the event around 23 December.

The attack was not an isolated stroke of fortune. It was the catalyst for a wider uprising that had been developing for years. Spanish pressure had grown, especially in the southern districts, where demands for labor in mines and farms, for tribute, and for service had intensified, and where captive-taking and violence against communities had accumulated grievances. Mapuche leaders, among them Pelantaro and Anganamón as the Spanish sources name them, organized a broad coalition across several districts. Modern research emphasizes large-scale Reche-Mapuche alliance formation in these years, a coalition larger and better coordinated than the alliances of the 1550s. The death of the governor was a major blow, but the rising that followed was the work of leaders and communities who had prepared for it and who took advantage of the moment.

Students should avoid saying that Curalaba by itself destroyed Spanish rule. The battle destroyed a party and killed a governor, and in doing so it removed the head of Spanish authority and shattered Spanish confidence. But the destruction of the southern towns was the product of an uprising that spread across the region in the months and years that followed. The sources describe attacks on forts and towns, sieges, and the movement of Mapuche forces that included cavalry. The coalition won not only because of one battle but because it was able to sustain pressure across a wide area for several years.

The consequences for the Spaniards were severe. The death of the governor left a political vacuum, since the Spanish system depended on a commander who could coordinate defense, and the settlers in the south were suddenly isolated. Villagers and soldiers faced the choice of fortifying and enduring a siege or abandoning their homes. In the Spanish telling, the disaster of Curalaba is the hinge of the entire history of Chile, and in many ways that judgment is correct. It marks the moment at which the century-long Spanish effort to extend colonial settlement south of the Biobío was overturned.

Section 38.14

The “Destruction of the Seven Cities,” 1598–1604

Spanish writers later summarized the aftermath of Curalaba as the destruction of seven cities. The phrase is conventional, and the exact list varies by author but usually includes Santa Cruz de Coya, Angol, La Imperial, Valdivia, Villarrica, and Osorno. It is important to treat the expression critically. The cities were not destroyed simultaneously on one night. They were attacked, besieged, evacuated, or abandoned over several years, in different circumstances, and the process of Spanish contraction continued into the early seventeenth century.

Angol was abandoned in 1599, and in November 1599 the town of Valdivia was attacked, sacked, and destroyed, with many of its inhabitants killed or captured. La Imperial, a city with a cathedral and a large Spanish population, was besieged and suffered terribly from hunger before being abandoned in the early years of the century. Villarrica and Osorno were besieged and held out for several years, until they too were abandoned, Osorno by about 1604. Survivors fled north, to Concepción and Chillán and beyond, or took refuge in the island of Chiloé, which remained under Spanish control. The pattern, in each case, was a long siege or a sudden assault, followed by evacuation or destruction.

Captives were a prominent feature of the aftermath. Spanish sources record that Mapuche forces took large numbers of Spanish women and children, as well as Indigenous servants, and that many of them remained in Mapuche communities for years. Some were ransomed, some were integrated into Mapuche households, and some bore children whose descent was mixed. Mapuche communities also gained horses, livestock, iron, gold, and other goods from the abandoned towns, extending the processes of change described in Section 38.11. The experience of captivity, which affected both sides, became a lasting feature of the frontier.

The scale of the Spanish retreat should not be understated. Spanish colonization south of the Biobío contracted drastically. Chiloé and, later, a reestablished Valdivia became exceptional enclaves, and the area between the Biobío and Chiloé fell outside Spanish control. This was the moment when a century of attempted conquest turned into a durable frontier. At the same time, the frontier was not instantly fixed. The Biobío became the institutional frontier through a process in the early seventeenth century, shaped by the defensive measures of governors and by the negotiations that followed, not by a single decision in 1598.

The loss of the south also reveals the Pacific dimension of the conflict. In 1600 a Dutch expedition under Baltasar de Cordes seized the Spanish town of Castro on Chiloé, with the cooperation, according to Spanish accounts, of some local Indigenous people, before the Dutch were driven out. The episode, along with Drake's earlier raid, showed that Spain's rivals could take advantage of the weakness of Spanish control in the far south, and it fed Spanish fears that Mapuche and European enemies might combine. Those fears would recur in the 1640s.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: From Southern Expansion to the Biobío Frontier

Long-term causes

Spanish expansion south of the Biobío in search of gold and encomienda labor, the founding of towns and forts as claims, and Spanish dependence on Peruvian support and on Indigenous food and labor.

Short-term causes

Intensifying demands for labor and tribute in the 1590s, captive-taking and violence, the organization of a broad Reche-Mapuche alliance under leaders such as Pelantaro and Anganamón, and the death of Governor Óñez de Loyola at Curalaba.

Event or transformation

The uprising that began in 1598 destroyed, besieged, or forced the abandonment of nearly all Spanish towns south of the Biobío between 1599 and about 1604.

Immediate effects

Refugee flight north, the capture of Spanish and Indigenous people, Mapuche acquisition of horses, livestock, and iron, and a Spanish crisis of confidence and finance.

Long-term consequences

A fortified frontier along the Biobío, a professional army funded from Peru, intensified slave raiding, the Defensive War experiment, and a frontier society of trade, captivity, and negotiation.

Historical complexity

No single battle explains the outcome. Curalaba was a catalyst. The collapse reflected long-accumulated Spanish overreach, Mapuche political adaptation, and the limits of a conquest that depended on small garrisons and coerced labor.
Section 38.15

From Conquest Army to Frontier System: Biobío, Soldiers, Slavery, and Defensive War

The Spanish response to the disaster was to reorganize. In 1601 Alonso de Ribera, a veteran soldier with experience in the European wars, became governor and began to build a permanent defensive system on and around the Biobío. He established a line of forts along the river and its approaches, among them forts at Arauco, Nacimiento, Yumbel, and other points, and he organized a professional army of paid soldiers, who by the early seventeenth century numbered in the low thousands. Spanish Chile had previously relied on settlers who fought as part of their obligations and rewards as encomenderos. The new army was a salaried force that lived in garrisons and served for pay.

The cost of that army was far beyond Chile's resources, and the Crown financed it from outside. Funds known as the Real Situado, an annual subsidy drawn from the royal treasury in Peru and based largely on the silver economy of the Andes described in Chapter 42, were sent to Chile to pay the troops. The arrangement tied the Chilean frontier to the wider imperial system: silver mined at Potosí by Indigenous workers helped pay for soldiers fighting Mapuche communities on the edge of the empire. The garrison also became a major source of demand for supplies, and it shaped Chilean society, drawing settlers, merchants, and suppliers into an economy of war.

In 1608 Philip III authorized the enslavement of Indigenous people captured in the Chilean war, with age categories specified in the decree. The measure responded to the army's frustration and to the belief that the threat of slavery would deter resistance, and it gave Spanish soldiers and settlers a financial incentive to capture people. It also differed fundamentally from encomienda. Whereas an encomienda was a Crown grant of rights to receive Indigenous tribute and, under early colonial arrangements, labor or services, formally paired with obligations of protection and Christian instruction, the 1608 decision allowed the legal enslavement of war captives. The practice of capturing people in raids called malocas became an important activity, and captives were sold or kept as servants in Chile and elsewhere.

Not all Spaniards agreed with the policy of offensive war and enslavement. The Jesuit Luis de Valdivia, who had come to Chile in 1593 and had observed the war closely, argued that offensive war and slave-taking perpetuated the conflict, and he proposed an alternative. In 1612 the Crown authorized what became known as the Guerra Defensiva, the Defensive War. Spanish forces were to stop offensive incursions south of the Biobío, and contact with communities beyond the frontier was to be entrusted to missionaries, who would seek peaceful relations and conversion. The policy suspended slave-taking and aimed to replace the cycle of raids with persuasion.

The Defensive War was a serious attempt but not a durable one. Many soldiers and settlers, whose income depended on raiding and captives, opposed it. Some Mapuche communities were willing to accept missionaries, while others rejected Spanish presence in any form. The killing of Jesuit missionaries in the Elicura region in late 1612 by Mapuche men, which Spanish sources describe as a major blow to the project, strengthened the opponents of the policy. The experiment continued in modified form for years, but the Crown eventually reversed course, and by the mid-1620s offensive war had been restored, with the associated practices of raiding and enslavement. It would be wrong to describe the Guerra Defensiva as a successful permanent peace. It was, instead, an experiment that showed that the cycle of violence could not be ended unilaterally.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was There One Continuous “Arauco War”?

The question: Chilean historical tradition has long used the phrase "Arauco War" for the conflict between Spaniards and Mapuche. Does the phrase describe a single continuous war, or does it compress many different wars, uprisings, raids, truces, missions, and negotiations across generations?

Interpretation A — A continuous conflict: Some historians emphasize the persistence of the underlying conflict: Spanish efforts to expand, Mapuche determination to preserve autonomy, and repeated cycles of violence from the 1550s through the seventeenth century. On this view, the phrase captures a real continuity of causes and effects, including the enslavement of captives, the militarization of Chile, and the long colonial violence experienced by Mapuche communities, and Mapuche historians in particular have stressed that periods of apparent calm did not erase that violence.
Interpretation B — Distinct wars within a frontier society: Other historians, emphasizing the work on the frontier world, point out that the conflict was punctuated by long intervals of relative peace, by trade, intermarriage, diplomacy, and missions, and by alliances in which Mapuche communities fought on different sides. They argue that the single label "Arauco War" obscures this variety and that the frontier is better understood as a complex society in which warfare was only one dimension.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same sources: chronicles, legal documents, records of parliaments, captivity narratives, and archaeology. Military narratives emphasize war, while records of trade, intermarriage, and diplomacy emphasize exchange. The balance between them depends partly on which sources are privileged and on whether the historian emphasizes the experience of violence or the everyday life of the frontier.

Why it matters: The question affects how Mapuche history is told. If the conflict is a single long war, the story is one of continuous resistance. If it is a series of distinct episodes within a frontier society, the story is one of adaptation and exchange. The strongest accounts combine both: warfare and exchange coexisted, and neither erased the other.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, and identify one kind of evidence from this chapter that supports each. Then explain whether the two interpretations are truly opposed.

Section 38.16

Frontier Society: Trade, Captivity, Diplomacy, and Quilín

The Biobío frontier that emerged in the early seventeenth century was not a sealed wall. It was a zone of intense contact, in which people, goods, and information crossed in both directions. Trade was constant. Mapuche communities supplied textiles, livestock, and other goods, and Spanish traders brought iron tools, knives, cloth, wine, and other manufactured items, in an exchange often conducted by itinerant traders in a practice called conchavo. Alcohol became a significant commodity, with social effects on both sides. Livestock, especially horses, moved across the frontier, and so did weapons, in spite of Spanish efforts to prevent it.

People crossed the frontier as well. Captives, taken in raids by both sides, were among the most important. A frontier household might contain people whose identities and affiliations were products of war, captivity, adoption, ransom, servitude, marriage, and escape. Francisco Núñez de Pineda y Bascuñán, a Spanish officer's son captured in 1629, lived for months among Mapuche and later wrote an account of his captivity, Cautiverio feliz, in 1673. The work describes Mapuche households, leaders, and customs, and it shows how a captive could be treated as a guest, a servant, or an object of negotiation. It is a Spanish text, shaped by the author's interests and literary aims, but it is also a rare window into frontier life. Interpreters, called lenguaraces, moved between worlds, as did missionaries, refugees, runaways, and mestizo families. Indigenous allies, indios amigos, living near Spanish forts, fought for Spaniards and maintained their own communities.

Frontier Study
Maloca, Captive, Indio Amigo, and Parlamento

A maloca (or maloqueo) was a raid, usually to seize captives and livestock; Spanish forces and Mapuche groups both conducted them. A captive (cautivo or cautiva) was a person taken in such a raid or in battle, who might be enslaved, ransomed, exchanged, adopted, or married into the captor's community. An indio amigo was an Indigenous person or community allied with the Spaniards, often living near forts and fighting on their side. A parlamento was a formal negotiated assembly between Spanish authorities and Mapuche leaders.

These four terms show how warfare and exchange became inseparable on the frontier. Raids produced captives, captives became part of households and negotiations, allies provided information and warriors, and parliaments tried to regulate the relationship. The terms help students see the frontier as a society and as a political relationship, and not simply as a line on a map.

Parliaments were the political form of this relationship. From the early seventeenth century, Spanish governors and Mapuche leaders held assemblies, often near the frontier, in which they negotiated terms. These meetings followed protocols of speeches, gifts, feasting, and ceremonial exchange, conducted through interpreters and often with missionaries present. Both sides used them to pursue their interests. For Spaniards, a parliament could secure a pause in fighting, the return of captives, access for missionaries, and recognition of the king's authority. For Mapuche leaders, it could secure peace for their communities, trade, the release of captives, and acknowledgment of their territorial autonomy. The meetings were not one-time events but part of a tradition of negotiation that continued for generations.

The culminating case was the Parliament of Quilín in January 1641, convened by Governor Francisco López de Zúñiga, Marqués de Baides, with numerous Mapuche leaders and in the presence of Jesuits and other Spanish officials. It was a major accommodation. According to Spanish documentation, the terms included a recognition of the Biobío as the effective limit of Spanish settlement, permission for missionaries, provisions about the return of captives, and an acknowledgment by Mapuche leaders of the king's lordship. Students should not read this as a modern treaty between Spain and a unified Mapuche nation. Spanish authorities negotiated with numerous Mapuche political leaders and lineages whose practical independence south of the Biobío the monarchy had been unable to extinguish. Quilín formalized a major accommodation and became part of a continuing parliament tradition, but different Mapuche groups retained their own interests and participation, alliances remained plural and unstable, and warfare could resume.

The wider world remained present. In 1643 a Dutch expedition arrived at Valdivia, seeking to establish a base and to find Indigenous allies against Spain, and some Indigenous communities in the south received them. The Spaniards reacted by reoccupying and fortifying Valdivia in 1645, with resources from Peru, turning the old site into a major Pacific fortress. The episode shows that the frontier was not isolated from imperial rivalry and that Spanish sovereignty in the south was defended partly through the commitments of Lima. It also illustrates the persistence of Spanish fear that Mapuche and European enemies might combine.

Quilín did not end the war. In 1655 a large Mapuche uprising broke out, in which, according to Spanish sources, leaders such as Clentaru played a prominent part, and it again forced the evacuation of Spanish settlements and the redeployment of troops before the Spaniards restored the frontier line in 1656. The coda is important because it prevents a romantic reading of the frontier as a stable arrangement of mutual respect. The frontier remained a zone of violence, negotiation, and exchange, in which each of these was always possible. Further episodes of the story, including later eighteenth-century parliaments and the very different conditions of the nineteenth century, belong to later chapters.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Biobío Frontier in the Early Seventeenth Century

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom.

A Mapuche household south of the frontier: Families farmed, herded, and traded, but lived with the risk of raids, the loss of men and livestock, and the displacement of settlements. Women carried much of the work of production and often negotiated the integration of captives.
A wartime toqui or lonko: Leaders balanced the need for unified action against Spanish expansion with the autonomy of local communities, and they used parliaments to pursue peace, trade, and the return of captives on terms they could defend.
An Indigenous ally of the Spaniards (indio amigo): Allies fought for Spaniards for reasons of local rivalry, survival, or advantage, and lived with pressure from both sides. Their loyalties reflected local politics, not a betrayal of an unformed Mapuche nation.
An Indigenous worker in central Chile: Workers in fields, households, and mines lived under encomienda and other forms of coerced labor, with population loss from disease and overwork, and with limited ability to negotiate.
A Spanish frontier soldier: Soldiers lived in garrisons, paid irregularly, and depended on raids and captives for supplementary income. The Defensive War threatened their livelihood, and many opposed it.
A captive woman or child: Captured in raids by one side or the other, captives entered households as servants, adoptees, or wives, and their treatment varied. Most of their voices are missing from the sources.
A missionary or interpreter: Jesuits and lenguaraces worked as intermediaries, sometimes advocating peace, sometimes serving Spanish interests, and often placed at risk by both sides.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Conquest Frontier to Frontier Society

What Changed

  • Spanish goals shifted from conquest and settlement of the south to containment, defense, and negotiation
  • Mapuche political organization developed wider alliances and new forms of war leadership
  • Horses, livestock, iron, and trade goods altered Mapuche economies and daily life
  • A professional army, the Real Situado, and legalized enslavement of war captives reshaped the frontier

What Stayed the Same

  • Mapuche communities south of the Biobío preserved their autonomy and territory
  • Local leadership, kinship, and ritual remained the basis of Mapuche political life
  • Spanish claims to sovereignty continued to exceed Spanish control
  • Central Chile remained a colonial society based on encomienda and Indigenous labor

Who experienced change most directly: Captives, communities near the frontier, and people in the abandoned southern towns experienced the sharpest disruptions, and soldiers and workers in the fort economy found their lives reorganized. Greater continuity: Mapuche communities far from the frontier retained established patterns, and the central valley's colonial order continued. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the failure of conquest did not produce separation. It produced a frontier society in which violence, exchange, and negotiation coexisted.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Spanish conquest in Chile produced two different outcomes. Central Chile was incorporated into a colonial society centered on Santiago, while the territory south of the Biobío resisted conquest and became the site of one of the most durable frontiers in Spanish America. The difference was not the result of a single battle or a single leader. It emerged from the interaction of Spanish methods, Indigenous political structures, geography, and the transformations that decades of war produced.

The first Spanish expedition, under Diego de Almagro in 1535–37, was a Peruvian enterprise that used Inca roads and Inca allies, crossed the Andes at heavy cost, and withdrew when it found little treasure and when Peruvian politics demanded attention. Pedro de Valdivia's expedition of the 1540s was a private business under license, built on investors, yanacona and African labor, and the promise of encomienda. The foundation of Santiago in 1541 and its near destruction by an Indigenous coalition under leaders such as Michimalonco illustrated the lesson of Chapter 37: a foundation was a legal claim, not control.

Central Chile survived through Indigenous food production, gold washing, and the encomienda, under which Spanish holders received rights to tribute and services from Indigenous communities. Incorporation was violent and negotiated, with local leaders acting as intermediaries and with severe demographic disruption. Enslaved Africans, yanaconas, Spanish women such as Inés de Suárez, and Indigenous women and men all took part in building the colony. The central valley supplied the labor, food, and settlers that made southern expansion possible.

Between 1550 and 1553 Valdivia pushed a chain of forts and towns south of the Biobío, including Concepción, Arauco, Tucapel, La Imperial, Valdivia, and Villarrica. These represented Spanish claims, backed by encomiendas over communities that were not under Spanish control. The Spaniards confronted Reche-Mapuche communities organized in kin-based lof and linked by rehue and larger alliances, led by lonko and, in war, by toqui. These communities were decentralized but politically capable, neither a state nor an anarchy.

The defeat and death of Valdivia at Tucapel in December 1553, associated with the war leader Lautaro, showed that Spaniards could be defeated in open battle. Lautaro's campaigns between 1554 and 1557 destroyed Concepción and reached toward Santiago but also revealed the limits of coalition building, since many central-valley communities were already incorporated into the colony or allied with Spaniards. The Spanish counteroffensive under García Hurtado de Mendoza recovered ground, and the capture of Caupolicán in 1558 did not end resistance. The sources for these events, including Valdivia's letters and Ercilla's La Araucana, must be read critically, and dramatic stories, such as those about Valdivia's death, tell us as much about later memory as about events.

The war changed both sides. Mapuche communities adopted horses, livestock, and iron, developed fortification and ambush tactics, formed wider alliances, and created new forms of war leadership, while Spaniards adopted Indigenous scouts and auxiliaries and new forms of fort warfare. Between about 1560 and the 1590s, the Spanish occupation looked stronger than it was, with towns, bishoprics, missions, and settlements as far south as Chiloé, but these were military islands dependent on coerced labor and vulnerable lines of communication.

The death of Governor Martín García Óñez de Loyola at Curalaba in December 1598 was the catalyst for a wider uprising, organized by a large Reche-Mapuche coalition, that destroyed, besieged, or forced the abandonment of nearly all Spanish towns south of the Biobío by about 1604. The conventional phrase "destruction of the seven cities" compresses a process of several years. Captives were taken on a large scale, and Spanish colonization contracted drastically, leaving Chiloé and, later, a refortified Valdivia as enclaves.

The Spanish response created the frontier system. Alonso de Ribera organized a fortified Biobío line and a professional army funded by the Real Situado from Peru, tying the frontier to the silver economy of the Andes. In 1608 the Crown authorized the enslavement of Indigenous war captives, intensifying raiding and captive-taking, and the Jesuit Luis de Valdivia's Defensive War from 1612 attempted to replace offense with missions before collapsing by the mid-1620s. The Defensive War was neither a permanent peace nor a success, and the 1608 policy was not ordinary encomienda.

The frontier that resulted was a society as well as a line. Trade, livestock, textiles, alcohol, captives, interpreters, missionaries, indios amigos, refugees, and intermarriage crossed the Biobío, and parliaments served as negotiated political assemblies. The Parliament of Quilín in 1641 was a major accommodation between Spanish authorities and participating Mapuche leaders, not a modern treaty with a unified nation, and the uprising of 1655–56 showed that it did not end the war. The historiographical question of whether there was one continuous "Arauco War" is best answered by recognizing that warfare and exchange coexisted.

The chapter's lasting significance is that it carries the series' conquest argument to its conclusion. Sometimes Iberian conquest took decades to complete. In southern Chile it failed to produce territorial control at all, forcing the empire to coexist with an Indigenous political world it could fight, influence, exploit, negotiate with, and trade with, but could not simply absorb. Spanish sovereignty remained a claim greater than Spanish control.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it carries the series' central conquest argument to its logical conclusion. Earlier chapters showed that conquest was prolonged, negotiated, and dependent on Indigenous politics. In southern Chile the argument reaches its limit: after more than a century, Spain still claimed sovereignty but could not extend territorial control. A map of the 1640s showing the Spanish king's dominions would have included territory where Spanish soldiers could not safely travel, and the chapter teaches students to ask what a claim of sovereignty actually meant. It also gives them a vocabulary for answering. A legal claim was a statement of right. A town foundation was a physical and legal node with a cabildo, a plaza, and a few households. A chain of forts and towns was a network of such nodes joined by roads that could be cut. Effective territorial rule meant being able to collect tribute, command labor, and travel safely between the nodes, and south of the Biobío Spaniards never achieved it.

It also shows that resistance was historical, not timeless. The Mapuche communities that stopped Spanish conquest did so partly through a decentralized structure that denied Spaniards a single target, and partly through new alliances, new leaders, and new military practices that they created in response to the conflict. That conflict changed them, as it changed the Spaniards. The Spanish colony became a militarized society financed by Andean silver, and the frontier that resulted became a place where violence, trade, captivity, and diplomacy coexisted.

The chapter's lessons connect to the larger book. The frontier of Chile was not the only region where empire met Indigenous peoples it could not absorb, and later chapters will meet similar zones in the north of New Spain, the Amazon, and the Río de la Plata. The parliaments and negotiated accommodations of the seventeenth century also foreshadow later relations between states and Indigenous peoples, a subject that Volume III and Volume V revisit. Chapter 39 turns east to Portuguese Brazil and the Río de la Plata, where another set of frontiers, shaped by missions, bandeirantes, and Indigenous societies, completes the picture of South American conquest and its limits.
