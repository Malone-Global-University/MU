Cortés, the Maya Coast, and Indigenous Diplomacy

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Conversation Passed Through Three Languages

When the Spanish expedition of 1519 met envoys of Moctezuma II on the Gulf coast, the words exchanged traveled through a chain: Nahuatl into a Maya language, then Maya into Spanish. Every link in that chain was a human being with her or his own history, interests, and choices, and the conquest of Mexico cannot be understood without them.

In the spring of 1519, roughly five hundred Spaniards, sixteen horses, a small number of cannon, and an uncertain number of Indigenous and African auxiliaries and servants stood on the coast of what is today the Mexican state of Veracruz. They had sailed from Cuba without a secure legal mandate, with limited knowledge of the land, and with no shared language with the people they were about to meet. Within two years, the Mexica capital of Tenochtitlan would be in ruins. That outcome was not foreseeable in 1519, and this chapter does not narrate it as though it were. It asks instead how a coastal expedition came to be entangled in the politics of Mesoamerica.

The answer begins with information. Two earlier expeditions, led by Francisco Hernández de Córdoba in 1517 and Juan de Grijalva in 1518, had mapped the Yucatán and Gulf coasts, fought and traded with Maya communities, and heard reports of a wealthy, powerful polity in the interior. These voyages turned Cuba into a staging ground and gave the third expedition, led by Hernán Cortés, a sense of what might be found. They also showed that the mainland was not like the Caribbean islands. It was densely populated, politically organized into many competing communities, and capable of inflicting heavy losses on Europeans who underestimated it.

The answer continues with translation. At Cozumel, Cortés recovered Jerónimo de Aguilar, a Spaniard who had lived among Maya speakers for about eight years. At Potonchán in Tabasco, after a battle, Maya-Chontal leaders gave the Spaniards a group of women, among them a young woman the Spaniards baptized as Marina and whom Nahuatl speakers called Malintzin. She spoke Nahuatl as well as Maya languages, and she became the decisive intermediary in every negotiation with the Nahua world. This chapter treats her as a historical actor and diplomatic intermediary, not as an ornament to Cortés's story or as a later nationalist symbol.

The answer concludes with the Indigenous political landscape itself. On the coast, the Spaniards met emissaries of the Mexica ruler Moctezuma II, and then the Totonac communities of Cempoala, who paid tribute to the Triple Alliance and had their own reasons to listen to outsiders. Mexica power was substantial but uneven. Tribute bound many communities to Tenochtitlan, and it also generated resentment, calculation, and opportunity. Totonac leaders, Mexica officials, Maya lords, merchants, and interpreters each pursued interests that had nothing to do with Spain. The Spaniards' survival depended on their skill at entering those interests, and on the choices Indigenous people made about them.

The chapter also concerns evidence, and students should read it with caution. Most detailed accounts of these events were written by Spaniards, often to win favor from the Crown: Cortés's own letters to Charles V, Bernal Díaz del Castillo's long memoir composed decades afterward, and the chronicle of Francisco López de Gómara, who never visited the Americas. Nahua perspectives survive in sources such as the Florentine Codex, compiled in the 1550s–1570s by Indigenous scholars working with the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún, and in Indigenous pictorial histories such as the Lienzo de Tlaxcala. Each source has a purpose, a date, and a viewpoint, and no single one reveals what happened on the beach at San Juan de Ulúa.

This chapter is the entry point to Part VII. It stops before the alliance with Tlaxcala and the march inland, which Chapter 31 examines, and it leaves the fall of Tenochtitlan to Chapter 32, the building of New Spain to Chapter 33, and the prolonged wars in Guatemala, Yucatán, and Central America to Chapter 34. Its task is narrower and, in some ways, more important: to show that the conquest of Mexico began as a problem of communication, diplomacy, provisioning, and politics before it became a problem of military force.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the Spanish expeditions of 1517, 1518, and 1519 and explain how each contributed knowledge, expectations, and resources to the next.
  2. Explain how privately financed conquest expeditions operated within imperfect royal authority, using the Velázquez–Cortés relationship as an example.
  3. Describe the political and cultural setting of the Maya coast and Tabasco, distinguishing Maya communities from one another and from the Nahua world.
  4. Analyze the Battle of Centla as an episode in which warfare and diplomacy were intertwined.
  5. Assess Malintzin's historical role using the limited and problematic evidence available, distinguishing evidence from later legend.
  6. Explain how interpreters functioned as political agents and why imperfect translation shaped events.
  7. Describe the first Nahua-Mexica diplomatic exchanges at San Juan de Ulúa from both Spanish and Indigenous perspectives.
  8. Identify the structure of the Mexica imperial system as seen from the coast, including altepetl, tribute, and autonomous rivals.
  9. Evaluate why Totonac leaders at Cempoala engaged with the Spaniards, avoiding the model of Indigenous "liberation" or simple welcome.
  10. Interpret the founding of Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz as a legal, political, and logistical act.
  11. Distinguish the kinds of evidence, Spanish and Indigenous, available for these events and their limitations.
  12. Connect this chapter to the larger Part VII argument that the conquest was a multinational conflict rather than a simple Spanish victory.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did the Spaniards learn from the expeditions of 1517 and 1518, and how did that knowledge shape the expedition of 1519?
  2. How did the legal ambiguity of Cortés's authority shape his decisions, and why did legitimacy matter in a conquest?
  3. Why did the ability to translate change what Cortés could do on the mainland?
  4. How do historians reconstruct Malintzin's life and role from sources written largely by men who benefited from her services?
  5. In what ways were warfare and negotiation inseparable in the encounters at Centla and on the Gulf coast?
  6. What was the nature of Mexica power over coastal communities, and why did it create both compliance and resentment?
  7. Why did Totonac leaders choose to engage the Spaniards, and what did they hope to gain?
  8. What did the Spaniards depend on Indigenous people to provide, and what does that dependence reveal about the nature of conquest?
  9. How should historians treat accounts of these events written by participants with reasons to exaggerate their own achievements?

Historical Context

Volume I examined the societies Cortés would meet. The Postclassic Maya, examined in Chapter 12, lived in a mosaic of independent polities across Yucatán and the southern lowlands, linked by trade and kinship but not by a single ruler. The Mexica and their Triple Alliance partners, examined in Chapters 13 through 15, had built a tribute empire in central Mexico, and they coexisted with states such as Tlaxcala and the Purépecha kingdom that remained outside their control. The Gulf coast lowlands were home to Totonac, Huastec, and other communities, along with Chontal Maya traders who moved goods along the coast and through river systems.

On the Spanish side, Chapter 27 traced the Caribbean conquest of the 1490s and early 1500s, and Chapter 29 examined the demographic catastrophe that accompanied it. By 1519, Spanish settlements existed in Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and Cuba, and the Caribbean Indigenous population had collapsed. Colonists, governors, and adventurers were searching for new sources of labor, gold, and status. Expeditions were typically organized as private ventures: a leader received a license from a governor or the Crown, raised capital from investors, recruited followers who expected shares of any profit, and operated with enormous autonomy at a distance from royal oversight.

The Caribbean experience had shaped Spanish expectations in ways that would prove misleading. Island societies, organized as chiefdoms of modest scale, had been overwhelmed by disease, forced labor, and violence. The Spaniards had little experience with large, densely settled states with sophisticated diplomacy, standing armies, and long-distance commerce. They also carried assumptions about religion and kingship, and about the legitimacy of war against peoples who refused Christian conversion, that informed how they read what they saw. Historians must therefore read Spanish accounts as documents shaped by these assumptions, not as neutral observations of Indigenous societies.

The Indigenous sources are equally complex. The Florentine Codex and other Nahua-language texts were composed after the conquest, by authors who had survived the destruction of their world and who wrote under Spanish supervision. Their accounts include moments of acute observation and also later interpretation. One example is the widely repeated idea that the Mexica believed Cortés to be the returning god Quetzalcoatl. Modern historians generally regard this idea as a post-conquest development, not a reliable description of what Moctezuma or his advisers thought in 1519, and this chapter does not rely on it.

Section 30.1

Spanish Expeditions before Cortés: Caribbean Bases and the Maya Coast

The expedition of 1519 was the third Spanish voyage from Cuba to the western Caribbean mainland in three years. In 1517, Francisco Hernández de Córdoba led an expedition seeking new lands and profit; its preparations also included a proposed slave-raiding component. It sailed west and reached the Yucatán coast. The Spaniards observed substantial stone buildings and large settlements, and they met Maya communities who were neither surprised nor intimidated. At Cabo Catoche and then at Champotón, the Spaniards were attacked by organized forces. Champotón cost them a large share of their men, and Córdoba himself died of his wounds after returning to Cuba. The survivors nonetheless carried back small quantities of worked gold and stories of a wealthy land.

In 1518, Governor Diego Velázquez sent a larger expedition under his relative Juan de Grijalva. It followed the coast, stopped at Cozumel, fought again at Champotón, and then entered the Tabasco region, where Chontal Maya communities traded with the newcomers, exchanging food and objects for European goods. Continuing along the Gulf coast, the expedition reached the vicinity of the island later called San Juan de Ulúa. There, according to the Spanish accounts, officials connected with the Triple Alliance came to observe them and exchange gifts. Grijalva did not attempt settlement, and he returned to Cuba with gold and a report of a powerful ruler inland. Velázquez was dissatisfied that Grijalva had not established a base, and this disappointment shaped the decision to send a third expedition.

Three consequences of these voyages matter for what follows. First, the Spaniards acquired a rough map of the coastline and knowledge of river mouths, harbors, and landmarks. Second, they acquired an impression, exaggerated but not baseless, of wealthy interior states. Third, they learned that the mainland could not be treated like the islands. Its communities were large, organized, and willing to fight. These lessons did not make the third expedition wise, but they made it better informed than its predecessors. They also meant that Maya and Gulf coast communities had already formed impressions of the newcomers, including their ships, their weapons, and their appetite for gold, before Cortés arrived.

Section 30.2

Cuba, Velázquez, and the Cortés Expedition of 1519

Hernán Cortés had come to the Caribbean around 1504, held an encomienda and a municipal office in Cuba, and emerged as a capable organizer within Velázquez's circle. In late 1518 Velázquez commissioned him to lead a third expedition to explore and trade along the coast and to seek the Grijalva survivors' reports of wealth. The terms of the commission have been debated. Velázquez's surviving instructions authorized reconnaissance, trade, information gathering, and the search for Spanish captives or survivors; they did not authorize Cortés to undertake an independent inland conquest or colonization. As preparations grew, Velázquez began to doubt Cortés's loyalty and moved to cancel the commission. Cortés sailed in February 1519 before the order could be enforced.

This episode should not be reduced to a contest of personalities. Conquest expeditions were structured as contractual partnerships in which a leader recruited men who supplied their own weapons and sometimes their own ships, and who expected shares of any gold, land, and Indigenous labor. The Crown issued licenses and expected a fifth of treasure, but it exercised little direct control. Authority was therefore always partly improvised, and leaders sought to secure it through later royal recognition. Cortés's later maneuver at Veracruz, examined in Section 30.10, is best understood within this system.

The fleet that sailed comprised eleven ships carrying, by common estimates, about five hundred to five hundred fifty Spaniards, sixteen horses, and a modest artillery train of small guns. It also carried Indigenous and African servants and enslaved people from Cuba, whose numbers are poorly documented but whose labor supported the expedition. The figures are known from Spanish narratives and should be treated as approximate. The expedition's military power was real but small, and its members understood that any extended campaign would require food, information, and allies they did not yet have.

Section 30.3

Cozumel and Jerónimo de Aguilar: Translation Begins

Cozumel, an island off the east coast of Yucatán, was a ritual and commercial center for Maya communities, and it had a sanctuary that drew pilgrims from the mainland. The Spanish narratives describe Cortés's stay there in early 1519, during which the expedition faced tension with local communities, destroyed religious images, and was frustrated in early efforts to communicate. Its most consequential event was the arrival of Jerónimo de Aguilar.

Aguilar had been a survivor of the wreck of a ship in 1511. After the wreck, he and other survivors had been captured by Maya communities on the Yucatán peninsula, and, according to Spanish chronicles, most died or were sacrificed. Aguilar, according to Spanish accounts, lived as a servant and then in a more integrated status for about eight years and learned a Yucatecan Maya language. A second survivor, Gonzalo Guerrero, is reported to have married into a Maya community, to have had children, and to have refused Cortés's invitation to rejoin the Spaniards. Guerrero's case is a reminder that cultural movement occurred in both directions and that Spanish accounts of it were shaped by their interest in presenting the rejection as a moral failure.

Aguilar's recovery gave Cortés something that Córdoba and Grijalva had lacked: a Spaniard who could speak with Maya communities directly. Earlier expeditions had relied on Maya captives trained as interpreters, including Julián and Melchor; by 1519 Julián was reportedly dead, while Melchor later left the expedition in Tabasco, though sources disagree on details. Aguilar made possible a new kind of diplomacy. He could explain Spanish intentions, demands, and gifts, and he could listen. It would remain limited by the fact that he spoke a Maya language but no Nahuatl, which would matter as soon as the expedition moved toward the territory of the Triple Alliance.

Section 30.4

Potonchán and the Battle of Centla

After rounding the peninsula, the expedition entered the Tabasco River region around March 1519. Potonchán and the surrounding communities belonged to the Chontal Maya world, a network of river and coastal polities engaged in long-distance trade that connected Yucatán, the Gulf coast, and the Mexican interior. Earlier contacts with Grijalva had been relatively peaceful and commercial. This time, Cortés demanded supplies and submission, and Chontal leaders, who had reason to be wary, assembled forces. The resulting fighting at Centla, conventionally dated to about 25 March 1519, is described in Spanish sources as a triumph in which a small force defeated thousands.

These accounts deserve caution. Spanish narratives give very large numbers for the Indigenous forces and emphasize the terror inspired by horses and artillery, and they sometimes attribute victory to divine intervention. Indigenous warriors, however, were experienced, used coordinated archery and close combat, and inflicted real casualties. Horses mattered, especially in open ground, but they were not magical, and Maya fighters adapted. Spanish advantages lay in steel weapons, crossbows, armor, cavalry charges on open terrain, and coordination. Their disadvantages, in unfamiliar swampy country, were serious. The outcome reflected a combination of tactical circumstances, and historians do not regard it as evidence of inevitable Spanish superiority.

What followed was as important as the fighting. Chontal leaders, having tested the newcomers' military capacity, sought terms. They delivered gifts of cloth, food, small gold objects, and a group of women, and they swore peace. Spanish narratives describe the women as given to the Spaniards and baptized soon afterward. The transfer occurred within a diplomatic settlement after the fighting. Transfers of women could carry meanings of alliance, submission, service, or kin-making in Mesoamerican politics, but the precise intentions of the Chontal leaders in this case cannot be recovered. The women themselves were enslaved and were not free participants in the transfer. The episode shows that war and negotiation ran together from the beginning of the expedition. It also introduces the woman who would make the next phase possible.

Section 30.5

Malintzin: Enslavement, Language, and Historical Agency

The woman the Spaniards baptized Marina, and to whom they later added the honorific "Doña," was called Malintzin in Nahuatl, a name that Spanish speakers rendered as Malinche. Her Indigenous birth name is not securely known; Malintzin is generally understood as the Nahuatl honorific form of the Christian name Marina. Her early life is known almost entirely from Bernal Díaz, whose account was written decades afterward and whom other sources partly contradict. According to Díaz, she was born into a family of local rank in the Coatzacoalcos region on the southern Gulf coast, a Nahuatl-speaking area, and was given or sold away as a child after her father's death, eventually passing into the Chontal Maya world of Tabasco. Other sources offer differing details, and the precise facts of her origin cannot be established.

What the evidence does support is this: Malintzin spoke Nahuatl, the language of the Triple Alliance and of central Mexico, and she spoke a Maya language used in Tabasco. She had been enslaved, and in March 1519 she was transferred to the Spaniards with other women. Because Aguilar spoke a Yucatecan Maya language and Malintzin also spoke one intelligible to him, a chain of translation became possible: Nahuatl into Maya through Malintzin, Maya into Spanish through Aguilar. The chain was slow and vulnerable to error. Within a short time, according to Spanish narratives, she learned Spanish, which reduced the dependence on Aguilar and increased her own importance.

Malintzin's position was precarious and unequal. She was a young, enslaved woman among armed men, and she was assigned to Alonso Hernández Portocarrero and later became associated with Cortés, with whom she later had a son, Martín. The evidence for the nature of these relationships is thin and filtered through Spanish sources, and this chapter does not characterize them beyond what it supports. Nevertheless, she was present in virtually every major negotiation for the next two years, and Indigenous sources frequently depict her prominently beside Cortés, in the Lienzo de Tlaxcala and in the Florentine Codex. Bernal Díaz reports that Indigenous people sometimes called Cortés "Malinche" because Marina was habitually beside him during diplomatic exchanges, which suggests how closely observers associated the diplomacy of the expedition with her voice.

Historians therefore face a methodological problem. Spanish writers had reasons to minimize her role, to praise her loyalty, or to present her conversion as a moral victory. Later Mexican tradition transformed her into a symbol: sometimes of betrayal, in the figure of the traitor sometimes called the malinchista, and more recently of a forced intermediary and mother of a mixed people. None of these later images is contemporary evidence. A historically careful approach treats her as an enslaved woman who used rare linguistic skills in a situation of extreme constraint, and who shaped events in ways that can be documented in part but whose motives cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

Section 30.6

Translation as Political Power

Interpreters in the conquest did far more than transfer words. They rendered speeches in ceremonial registers, explained titles and relationships among rulers, conveyed gifts and their meanings, and negotiated concrete matters such as food, lodging, and safe passage. They also gathered intelligence. By listening to conversations among Indigenous people, they learned about rivalries, tribute obligations, and the location of powerful states. They assessed threats and helped decide whom to trust. Because Europeans depended on them, interpreters held extraordinary influence over what each side understood about the other.

Translation was also inherently imperfect. Nahuatl diplomatic speech used metaphor, honorific forms, and conventions that had no Spanish equivalents, and Spanish statements about the Christian God, the Spanish king, and requests for submission had little grounding in Mesoamerican categories. The chain of translation added further distortion. Interpreters were also not neutral. Each had interests, including personal survival, and each could emphasize, soften, or omit. Malintzin's position placed her at a point where she could shape what both sides heard, although the extent to which she did so deliberately is unknowable. Later Spanish accounts sometimes credit her with warnings and decisions that may be embellishments, and Indigenous accounts sometimes credit her with agency that Spanish sources omit.

Recognizing the importance of translation changes how the conquest should be understood. The expedition's early success did not depend mainly on weapons. It depended on communication: on Cortés's ability to present himself to Indigenous leaders as a potential ally, patron, or threat, and on his ability to understand their disputes. For this reason the intellectual center of Chapter 30 is not battle but conversation. Conquest, at this stage, was a political process in which information and language were forms of power, and in which an enslaved interpreter, a recovered captive, and a network of Indigenous leaders shaped the direction of events.

Historical Vocabulary
Altepetl

The altepetl (Nahuatl, literally "water-mountain") was the basic unit of Nahua political life: a community with a territory, a ruling house, internal subdivisions, local gods, and a capital. Altepetl were the building blocks of larger arrangements. The Triple Alliance, for example, was an alliance of altepetl, and its tribute empire was a web of relationships between a few dominant altepetl and many subordinate ones.

The concept matters because it corrects the image of a single "Aztec empire" confronting Spaniards. Cortés met and negotiated with altepetl and their rulers, each with its own interests and history. Although "altepetl" is a Nahua term, comparable local political communities existed across Mesoamerica, including among Totonac and Maya peoples, though with their own names and institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Tlatoani

A tlatoani (Nahuatl, "speaker"; plural tlatoque) was the ruler of an altepetl. The Mexica ruler Moctezuma II was tlatoani of Tenochtitlan; there were also tlatoque in Texcoco, Tlacopan, Tlaxcala's constituent communities, and countless smaller altepetl. Spanish writers often rendered the term as "king," "emperor," or, for lesser rulers, "cacique," a Caribbean word they applied widely.

These translations were approximations. A tlatoani ruled with a council and within expectations about kinship, religion, and military success, and the authority of a tlatoani differed from that of an absolute monarch. Attention to the original term helps students see that Spanish descriptions of Indigenous rulers were already acts of interpretation.

Section 30.7

San Juan de Ulúa and the First Nahua-Mexica Diplomacy

In April 1519, the fleet anchored near the island of San Juan de Ulúa, off the present Veracruz coast, at a place Grijalva had already visited. Spanish accounts place the landing at Holy Week, and they describe canoes approaching the ships, officials of the Triple Alliance coming ashore, and an exchange of gifts and greetings. Díaz names the envoys and records the use of Malintzin and Aguilar. Nahua sources preserve different recollections, including the sight of the ships, the reporting of the arrivals to Tenochtitlan, and the preparation of gifts.

The exchanges were mutual inquiries. Mexica officials wanted to know who the strangers were, what they wanted, and whether they intended to stay. The Spaniards wanted to learn about the ruler, the location of his capital, and the wealth of his domain. Gifts carried messages. The Mexica presented valuable objects, including textiles, featherwork, and gold, which served as acts of hospitality, displays of resources, and perhaps attempts to satisfy the strangers into departing. Spanish accounts report that Cortés responded with European goods and a display of weapons, and with a declaration that he represented a great monarch and wished to meet Moctezuma in person. The gifts had the opposite effect from any intention to encourage departure: they convinced the Spaniards that a rich state lay within reach.

This chapter stops short of Moctezuma's full story, which belongs to Chapter 31. It is enough here to note that the evidence for his deliberations is limited and heavily shaped by after-the-fact interpretation. The Mexica ruler repeatedly sent messages asking the Spaniards not to advance. The reasons for that position, and the debates within the Mexica court, can only be partially reconstructed. What matters at the coast is that the first Nahua-Mexica diplomacy established a pattern: envoys, gifts, requests, refusals, and information exchanged through an imperfect chain of translators, each side attempting to assess the other.

Section 30.8

The Mexica Imperial System Encountered from the Coast

From the Gulf coast, the political world the Spaniards were entering was a complex one. The Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan dominated central Mexico and extended influence to the coasts through conquest and tribute. Imperial relationships varied. Many tribute provinces delivered regular goods such as cloth, food, feathers, and precious materials through tribute institutions, overseen by officials called calpixque, while subject states and strategic provinces could have different political, military, or defensive obligations. Local rulers commonly remained in place, and the degree of direct Triple Alliance intervention differed substantially by region.

The system was powerful but uneven. It depended on the threat of force and on the benefits of participation, including access to markets and protection. Some provinces were tightly integrated, and others were held loosely and with periodic revolt. Beyond the tribute provinces lay independent states, among them Tlaxcala, which was encircled by Triple Alliance territory and had long resisted it. Tribute bound communities to Tenochtitlan and also generated resentment, particularly where demands were heavy or where rulers of subject altepetl believed they could gain by changing allegiance. Historians debate how stable these relationships were, and sources such as the Codex Mendoza, a colonial-era manuscript recording tribute provinces, preserve a Mexica-oriented view.

The Spaniards did not understand this system when they arrived, and they learned it piece by piece, through translators and through conversations with aggrieved subjects. Their growing understanding of Mexica tribute and its tensions gave them an opening. Cortés's approach in 1519 depended on a recognition that the coast was not simply a doorway to a single enemy but a zone where subject communities might be persuaded that the newcomers offered an alternative. Chapter 31 will show what that recognition produced in Tlaxcala and beyond.

Section 30.9

Cempoala and the Totonacs: Diplomacy before Alliance

After the initial camp and municipal founding described in Section 30.10, the Spaniards moved north along the coast and reached the Totonac region. Cempoala, a large and prosperous altepetl in the coastal plain, was the principal community, and Quiahuiztlan, a fortified settlement on a hill near the coast, was another. The Totonacs spoke their own language, Totonac, distinct from Nahuatl, and they had been brought under Triple Alliance tribute in the decades before 1519. Spanish accounts describe Cempoala as a substantial town of well-built structures, and they give large population figures that historians treat cautiously.

The meeting with Cempoala's ruler, whom Spanish sources call the "fat cacique," was a negotiation conducted through interpreters. Totonac leaders complained of Mexica tribute demands and of abuses by officials. The Spaniards offered a patron and a possible counterweight. The account Díaz and others give portrays the Totonacs as welcoming the Spaniards as liberators from oppression, but this image is too simple. The Totonac leaders were pursuing their own interests: relief from tribute, protection from reprisals, and perhaps benefit from trade and from the newcomers' military capacity. They were also testing the Spaniards, with the knowledge that a Mexica response could be severe.

A revealing incident occurred when Mexica tribute collectors arrived in the region while the Spaniards were present. According to Spanish accounts, Cortés urged the Totonacs to seize the collectors and to refuse further payment, and then secretly released two of them so that they could carry a conciliatory message to Moctezuma. The story, which rests on Spanish testimony, suggests that Cortés was playing both sides, seeking to bind the Totonacs to him by committing them to defiance while preserving his options with Tenochtitlan. The Totonacs, for their part, were not passive, and their decision to stop payment was a serious political act with risks they bore. Their alliance with the Spaniards, once formed, meant war against the Triple Alliance, a prospect that the Totonacs entered with considerable calculation and, as events would show, considerable danger.

Section 30.10

Veracruz and the Problem of Spanish Authority

Cortés's commission from Velázquez was limited and contested, and he understood that any wealth or land he obtained without valid authority could be challenged in the courts of the Indies and Spain. To resolve this problem he used a device familiar in Spanish legal tradition: the founding of a municipality. While still camped at Chalchihuecan, opposite San Juan de Ulúa, in April–May 1519, Cortés's faction constituted Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz and its cabildo, or town council, chosen from among his followers. After subsequent contact with the Totonac region, the physical settlement was transferred north to a site near Quiahuiztlan. This section therefore describes events that began before the Cempoala meetings of Section 30.9 and continued alongside them.

The cabildo then received Cortés's resignation of the commission from Velázquez and, in the name of the Spanish crown, elected him captain general and chief justice of the new jurisdiction. Whether this maneuver had legal force was debatable, and Velázquez's partisans disputed it. Yet it gave Cortés a claim to direct royal authority, freed him from the governor of Cuba, and bound his followers to an institution in which they held offices. The cabildo also sent envoys to Spain with a large portion of the treasure gathered so far, along with letters presenting the expedition as a loyal undertaking on behalf of Charles I. Cortés also faced opposition within the expedition. Some followers supported Velázquez, and Spanish sources describe a plot that was suppressed with executions and punishments, and the decision to disable the ships, which reduced the possibility of desertion.

The founding of Veracruz mattered in three ways. Legally, it gave the expedition a formal basis that it could plausibly defend. Logistically, it created a coastal base where ships could be secured, wounded men could recover, and supplies could be gathered. Politically, it signaled to Indigenous leaders that the Spaniards intended to remain. It should not be overstated as a stroke of individual genius. It was a known legal technique, adopted by many conquest leaders, and its later relocation near Quiahuiztlan depended on Totonac support, while its eventual acceptance depended on a far-off Crown willing to judge results more than procedure.

Section 30.11

Indigenous Intelligence, Provisioning, and the Making of an Expedition

The Spaniards could not have advanced into the interior on their own logistical power. A force of several hundred men and horses required daily food, water, fodder, shelter, and labor, and in an unfamiliar land they depended on local communities for all of it. Totonac and coastal communities provided maize, tortillas, turkeys, and other foods, and they supplied workers who carried the expedition's cannon and baggage. Such carriers, called tamemes in Spanish usage from the Nahuatl term, had long performed this work in Mesoamerica, where there were no draft animals, and their labor was a precondition of any overland movement.

Indigenous people also supplied information. Guides knew routes, water sources, and the dispositions of neighboring communities. Messengers carried word to and from communities and Tenochtitlan. Through Malintzin and other interpreters, the Spaniards learned about the Triple Alliance's strength, its tribute structure, the rivalry with Tlaxcala, and the geography of the Basin of Mexico. Some of this information was offered freely, some was offered strategically to draw the Spaniards into an existing conflict, and some was probably misleading. The Spaniards therefore depended on Indigenous sources that had their own purposes.

Totonac leaders also supplied warriors. In preparing for the inland march, according to Spanish accounts, Cempoala and neighboring communities contributed fighters and carriers, committing themselves to the expedition's fortunes. Their participation transformed the nature of the enterprise. A Spanish raid had become a partnership in which Indigenous communities supplied the material and human basis for an advance. This dependence is a central theme of Part VII, and it is visible here at the coast before the larger alliances that Chapter 31 examines.

Section 30.12

From Coastal Encounters to an Indigenous Political War

By the late summer of 1519, the expedition was a different enterprise from the one that had left Cuba. It possessed interpreters, a coastal base, and Totonac partners. It had gathered political intelligence about the Triple Alliance and about communities resentful of its demands, and it had committed itself, and its Totonac allies, to a path that made retreat difficult. Its leader had restructured its legal basis and sent his case to the Crown. None of this guaranteed success. The Mexica state was still intact, its armies had not been tested against the Spaniards, and its ruler had not yet decided how to respond.

The deeper change was that the Spaniards were being drawn into conflicts that predated them. Totonac grievances against Mexica tribute, the long enmity between Tlaxcala and the Triple Alliance, and the ambitions of subject rulers looking for leverage were all pre-existing features of Mesoamerican politics. The Spaniards were entering these conflicts as a new participant, and, increasingly, they were being used by Indigenous actors as much as they were using them. The scale of this political world, in which any advance would bring new negotiations, new rivals, and new risks, was only beginning to be visible to them.

This is the point at which the chapter stops. The march inland, the encounter with Tlaxcala, the massacre at Cholula, and the meeting with Moctezuma II belong to Chapter 31. What this chapter has established is the foundation for those events: that the conquest of Mexico began not with a clash of armies but with language, information, provisioning, legal improvisation, and the choices of Indigenous people, and that the outcome depended on all of these rather than on European weapons alone.

Primary Source Window

Accounts of Doña Marina in Spanish and Nahua Sources

Creators: Bernal Díaz del Castillo, a Spanish soldier who accompanied Cortés; and Nahua scholars and painters of the Florentine Codex and the Lienzo de Tlaxcala · Dates: Díaz wrote in the mid-sixteenth century, decades after the events, and his work was not printed until 1632; the Florentine Codex was compiled in the 1550s–1570s; the Lienzo de Tlaxcala was produced in the sixteenth century · Type: A soldier's memoir, an Indigenous-authored encyclopedic history, and a pictorial manuscript

Context: Díaz wrote partly to correct what he saw as López de Gómara's exaggeration of Cortés's role, and he describes Doña Marina's origins, her transfer to the Spaniards, and her services as interpreter. The Nahua sources, produced under Spanish colonial conditions, record Indigenous recollections of the encounter and depict Malintzin prominently in scenes of negotiation.

Paraphrase of the sources' central claims: Díaz presents Marina as a woman of noble birth who was given away as a child, who spoke several languages, and who served the Spaniards faithfully and was honored with the title "Doña." The Nahua sources, in text and image, show her standing beside Cortés as the speaker in diplomatic meetings, while Díaz separately reports that Indigenous people sometimes called Cortés "Malinche" because Marina was habitually beside him in diplomacy.

Analysis: These sources agree that Malintzin was central to communication. They differ in emphasis. Díaz stresses her loyalty and her conversion, framing her role in terms of Spanish success and Christian identity, and his claim of noble birth may partly reflect a desire to explain her abilities. The Nahua sources portray her as visible and influential in diplomacy and do not present her as a mere assistant. None of the sources gives her own voice, and no account from Malintzin herself survives. Historians therefore cannot reconstruct her motives, her feelings, or the choices she made under coercion, only the way others represented her role.

Questions for students:

  1. What do these sources say or show about Malintzin's role in the expedition?
  2. Who created each source, when, and for what possible purposes?
  3. What can historians learn about Indigenous perceptions of diplomacy from the Nahua depictions?
  4. What can historians not establish about Malintzin's own perspective from these sources alone?
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Should Historians Understand Malintzin?

The question: Was Malintzin primarily a translator serving Cortés, an enslaved woman with little real choice, a diplomat who exercised independent political judgment, or a figure whose meaning is largely a product of later myth?

Interpretation A — A constrained intermediary: Some historians emphasize her enslavement, her youth, and the coercive conditions under which she served. On this view, it is misleading to attribute to her the political purposes of a free diplomat, because she had no ability to refuse and her survival depended on cooperation. Her importance lay in her skills, but her agency was narrowly bounded.
Interpretation B — A political actor within constraint: Other historians emphasize that interpreters necessarily exercised judgment. Malintzin could shape what was said, emphasize or soften, and decide what to convey, and Indigenous sources treated her as a significant figure. On this view, her actions had consequences that cannot be reduced to obedience, even if her freedom was severely restricted.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited corpus of Spanish and Nahua sources, none of which records her thoughts. They differ in how much weight they give to silence, to Indigenous depictions, and to the logic of interpretation as a role.

Why it matters: How historians understand Malintzin shapes the story of the conquest itself. If she is a passive instrument, Spanish success looks more dependent on Cortés. If she is an active intermediary, the conquest becomes a story shaped by Indigenous and Indigenous-descended participants. The debate also intersects with modern politics, since she has been claimed and condemned as a symbol in Mexican and Chicano national memory. Historians therefore try to separate evidence from these later uses, and many now hold that the two interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation using the evidence in this chapter, then identify what kind of new evidence, if any, could change the balance between them.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Translation and Diplomacy on the Coast

Note: these viewpoints are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom.

Chontal Maya leaders of Tabasco: Having tested the newcomers in battle, they sought terms that would end the fighting and preserve their communities, and they offered gifts and people in a form of diplomacy that followed regional custom. The women given did not choose this role.
Mexica envoys and officials: They needed to learn who the strangers were, to display the resources of the Triple Alliance, and to encourage them to go away. Their gifts were meant as hospitality and display, and they did not expect the gifts to attract further advances.
Totonac leaders of Cempoala: They saw an opportunity to escape tribute and to acquire a powerful partner, and they also saw the risk of reprisal. Their choices were calculated political decisions, not simple welcome.
Spanish expedition members: They were seeking wealth, land, and status, and they interpreted what they saw through assumptions about religion and kingship. Many were also worried about their legal position and about their dependence on people they did not understand.
Malintzin: An enslaved young woman who held rare linguistic knowledge in a situation of danger, she was constrained by the power of the men around her, although how she understood her position is not recorded.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: From Caribbean Conquest to Mesoamerican Diplomacy

What Changed

  • Spaniards confronted dense populations and organized states rather than small island chiefdoms
  • Interpretation and diplomacy became essential in a way they had not been in most of the Caribbean
  • Indigenous allies supplied food, labor, and warriors, altering the expedition's capacity
  • Questions of legal authority became more pressing as the scale of possible gain increased

What Stayed the Same

  • Expeditions remained private ventures motivated by profit and status
  • Violence, enslavement, and religious destruction continued to accompany Spanish advance
  • Spanish accounts continued to present events in ways that served their authors' interests

Who experienced change most directly: Coastal Indigenous communities faced sudden new pressures and choices, while the Spanish leadership faced new needs for legitimacy and information. Some features of Spanish practice persisted across the Caribbean and the mainland. Why the pattern matters: The conquest of Mexico was not a repetition of the Caribbean, and assuming that it was obscures the role of Indigenous politics.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Diplomacy and Translation Mattered More Than Technology Alone

Long-term causes

A politically fragmented Mesoamerica with tribute relationships, regional rivalries, and trade networks; and a Spanish conquest tradition built on negotiation, improvisation, and private enterprise.

Short-term causes

The information from the 1517 and 1518 voyages, the recovery of Aguilar, the acquisition of Malintzin, and Mexica tribute demands that angered coastal communities.

Event or transformation

A small expedition gained interpreters, partners, a base, and political intelligence, and became entangled in Indigenous conflicts.

Immediate effects

The legal reconstitution of the expedition's authority at Villa Rica, followed by Totonac commitment and preparations for an inland march supported by Indigenous porters and warriors.

Long-term consequences

A conquest shaped by Indigenous alliances, which would influence the structure of colonial rule and the place of Indigenous elites in New Spain.

Historical complexity

Weapons, disease, and luck mattered, but none alone explains the outcome. Diplomacy and communication created the conditions in which they could operate.
Everyday Life and Social History

Food, Labor, and Ordinary People in the Making of an Expedition

Behind the diplomacy stood the labor of ordinary people. Women in coastal communities ground maize, made tortillas, and prepared food for hundreds of visitors, and their work, rarely recorded, was a condition of the expedition's survival. Porters carried loads across difficult terrain, and farmers supplied the food that Cempoala and other communities delivered. The women given in Tabasco were themselves part of this world of labor: Spanish accounts describe them as grinding maize and cooking for the expedition, and they were also subject to sexual exploitation.

Coastal communities also had to manage the disruption of hosting a foreign force, including shortages, the demands of feeding animals, and the risk of violence. The sources record these burdens unevenly, because Spanish authors were interested in strategy, and Indigenous sources record them in retrospect. Historians must read between the lines to see how the expedition's presence affected ordinary households, and the silences themselves are evidence of whose experiences were recorded and whose were not.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the opening phase of the conquest of Mexico as a problem of communication, diplomacy, and politics, rather than as an inevitable Spanish victory. It has argued that Cortés did not enter a unified Indigenous world waiting to be conquered but a fragmented and densely populated landscape in which Maya communities, Totonacs, Mexica officials, merchants, and interpreters pursued their own interests.

The expeditions of Córdoba and Grijalva in 1517 and 1518 provided geographic knowledge, reports of interior wealth, and a recognition that the mainland was populous and militarily capable. They also shaped Indigenous impressions of the newcomers. The expedition of 1519 was therefore better informed than its predecessors, although it remained small, legally insecure, and dependent on resources it did not control.

Cortés's authority derived from a contested commission from Diego Velázquez, and the expedition illustrates how conquest enterprises operated as private ventures within a loosely supervised imperial system. Legitimacy was something that had to be constructed, and the founding of Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz was one means of doing so. It gave the expedition a formal basis, a coastal base, and a claim to royal authority.

Translation was central. Aguilar's recovery at Cozumel and the acquisition of Malintzin at Potonchán created a chain of interpretation that made diplomacy possible. Malintzin, an enslaved woman who spoke Nahuatl and Maya, became the decisive intermediary. Historians cannot reconstruct her motives, but the sources show that she was present at crucial negotiations and that Indigenous observers treated her as a significant figure. She should be understood neither as a mere instrument nor as a free diplomat, but as an actor operating under severe constraint.

The Battle of Centla showed that warfare and negotiation ran together. Spanish accounts exaggerate the scale of the victory and the role of horses, while Chontal Maya leaders, after testing the Spaniards, sought terms in a form of diplomacy consistent with regional custom. The gift of women, in particular, reflects both diplomatic practice and the coercion that affected those women's lives.

At San Juan de Ulúa, the first Nahua-Mexica exchanges established a pattern of envoys, gifts, requests, and mutual assessment. The Mexica imperial system, seen from the coast, was powerful but uneven: a network of altepetl bound by tribute, with independent rivals beyond its reach. Tribute created both compliance and resentment, and this ambivalence created an opening that the Spaniards learned to exploit.

Cempoala and the Totonacs were pursuing their own interests. Their leaders sought relief from tribute and a counterweight to Mexica power, and they accepted significant risk. Their decision to defy tribute collectors committed them to war with the Triple Alliance. The image of Totonac "liberation" oversimplifies a calculated political choice.

Indigenous provisioning, guides, porters, and intelligence made an inland march possible. The Spaniards could not have advanced under their own logistical power, and their dependence reveals the nature of the conquest as a partnership in which Indigenous communities supplied the material and human basis for action. This dependence would expand in the events of Chapter 31.

By the end of the chapter, the expedition had become something more than a coastal raid. It possessed interpreters, partners, a base, and political knowledge, and it was being drawn into conflicts that predated it. Chapter 31 follows it into the interior, where the alliance with Tlaxcala, the events at Cholula, and the meeting with Moctezuma II would determine the next stage.

Students should take from the chapter a method as well as a narrative. Sources written by participants serve the interests of their authors, Indigenous sources were composed under colonial conditions, and neither offers a neutral window on 1519. Reading them together, with attention to purpose and perspective, is the way historians reconstruct a past that none of the actors could have seen whole.

Comparing Spanish and Nahua Accounts of the Coastal Encounter

This project asks you to compare how Spanish and Indigenous sources describe the same events of 1519 and to evaluate what each can and cannot reveal.

  1. Select an episode. Choose one: the landing at San Juan de Ulúa, the meeting at Cempoala, or the founding of Veracruz.
  2. Locate Spanish accounts. Using translated editions of Cortés's letters, Díaz's memoir, and López de Gómara's chronicle, summarize how each describes your episode.
  3. Locate Indigenous accounts. Using translated selections of the Florentine Codex, the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, or other Indigenous sources, summarize how they treat the same episode.
  4. Compare. Identify agreements, contradictions, and silences, and consider the date, author, and purpose of each source.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what can be reconstructed with confidence, what remains uncertain, and what additional evidence would help.

Library resources to use: Translated primary-source collections on the conquest of Mexico, scholarly studies of Nahua-language sources, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Mesoamerican history.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the conquest of Mexico began in conversation. Before armies met in the highlands, a small expedition on the Gulf coast relied on a recovered captive, an enslaved interpreter, Indigenous leaders calculating their own advantage, and the labor of porters and cooks. The outcome that later generations saw as inevitable depended on choices made by many people, few of whom could see where their decisions would lead.

The themes introduced here recur throughout Volume II. Indigenous alliances and rivalries, the role of intermediaries, the improvisation of legal authority, and the dependence of conquerors on those they sought to rule will reappear in the conquests of the Andes, Central America, and Chile, and in the colonial order that followed. The figure of Malintzin reminds students that women were present at the center of these events, constrained and consequential at once.

Chapter 31 follows the expedition inland, where the alliance with Tlaxcala, the events at Cholula, and the meeting with Moctezuma II will test the political understanding the Spaniards had begun to acquire. Students should carry forward the view that Indigenous people were actors with their own goals, and that the story of the conquest is as much a story of Mesoamerican politics as of Spanish ambition.
