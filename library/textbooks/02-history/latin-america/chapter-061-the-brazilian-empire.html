The Brazilian Empire

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Monarchy in a Republican Hemisphere

When the nineteenth century began, no one in the Americas would have predicted that the largest state of the former Iberian world would be an empire ruled by an emperor of the House of Braganza. Yet while Spanish America divided into competing republics, Brazil entered the 1870s as a single constitutional monarchy stretching from the Amazon to the Río de la Plata, and it did so with a labor system built on the ownership of human beings.

On 25 March 1824 a constitution granted by Emperor Pedro I created a state with a hereditary monarch, a two-chamber parliament, indirect elections, an established Catholic Church, and a fourth constitutional power, the Moderating Power, that placed the emperor above the other branches. Seven years later the same emperor abdicated in the face of street protest and military defection, leaving a five-year-old heir and a regency that had to govern a monarchy without a monarch. Between 1835 and 1845 the empire faced rebellions in Pará, Bahia, Maranhão, and Rio Grande do Sul that ranged from an enslaved Muslim uprising in a port city to an Amazonian insurrection of Indigenous, Afro-descended, and mixed-race riverine people, to a ten-year republican war led by cattle ranchers. The state survived all of them.

This chapter asks how that survival was achieved and what it cost. It does not treat Brazilian unity as a natural outcome of a shared language or a shared Portuguese inheritance. Provincial elites in 1822 disagreed about whether they wanted a government in Rio de Janeiro at all; Pernambuco had rebelled in 1817 and again in 1824; Rio Grande do Sul fought the empire for nearly a decade; and Pará was reconquered after one of the bloodiest civil wars of the century. The empire that emerged by the 1850s was assembled through constitutional experiment, administrative centralization, political bargaining with regional elites, military force, and a shared interest among slaveholders in preserving the institution of slavery.

The chapter also asks who was included in that political order and who was not. Slavery was never named in the 1824 Constitution, but it structured the economy of the empire from the sugar engenhos of the Northeast to the coffee estates of the Paraíba Valley and the households of Rio de Janeiro, where around two-fifths of the city's residents were enslaved in mid-century. The Atlantic slave trade was formally prohibited in 1831 and reopened in practice for nearly twenty years before it was effectively suppressed in 1850. Enslaved and free Afro-Brazilians, Indigenous peoples, women, soldiers, and poor rural workers appear here not as background to the history of emperors and ministers but as people whose actions, resistance, and labor shaped the state.

Evidence deserves attention from the outset. Imperial legislation, parliamentary debates, provincial presidents' reports, ministerial reports, and the proceedings of the Council of State document how the state saw itself. Newspapers, pamphlets, travel narratives by foreign visitors such as Debret, Rugendas, and Ewbank, and the private papers of politicians reveal debate and opinion, usually among the literate minority. Judicial records of the Malê revolt, freedom suits brought by enslaved people, notarial manumission records, census returns, and baptismal registers allow historians to reach people who left few texts of their own. Each category records the empire from a particular vantage, and the chapter tries to say whose vantage that is.

The chapter's boundaries follow the division of labor in the textbook. The declaration of independence and the war that secured it belong to Chapter 56, and the opening sections here only summarize what the Empire inherited. The Paraguayan War is narrated at length in Chapter 64, and Section 61.41 asks only what that war meant for Brazilian political and military institutions. The abolitionist campaigns of the 1880s and the fall of the monarchy in 1889 belong to Chapter 66. Chapters 59 and 60 describe the Mexican and Andean paths that Section 61.45 uses for comparison. The task here is to explain how a durable but coercive political order was built and maintained between 1822 and about 1871, and why that order was neither inevitable nor uncontested.

Historical Context

By 1822 Brazil had been governed from Rio de Janeiro as a kingdom within a pluriregional Portuguese monarchy for fourteen years. The Portuguese court had moved to Rio in 1807 and 1808 when French armies invaded Portugal (see Chapter 51), and the elevation of Brazil to the status of a kingdom in 1815 had changed the legal relationship of colony and metropolis. The return of John VI to Lisbon in 1821 and the demand of the Portuguese Cortes that Brazil return to a subordinate role provoked the break. Chapter 56 narrates how Prince Pedro, the regent left behind, was acclaimed emperor in October 1822 and how Portuguese garrisons in Bahia, Maranhão, Pará, and the Cisplatine province were removed or defeated through 1823 and 1824. The new state therefore began with a war debt, a navy led by foreign officers such as Lord Cochrane, and an urgent need for foreign recognition.

The empire's population was small, dispersed, and divided by status. Estimates for the early 1820s run around four million inhabitants, of whom a third or more were enslaved, and the census of 1872, the first national count, found about 9.9 million people, of whom roughly 1.5 million were enslaved. Enumerators in 1872 classified the population as roughly 38 percent white, 38 percent pardo (of mixed ancestry), 20 percent Black, and 4 percent caboclo, a category that included many Indigenous people, though these categories reflect enumerators' perceptions as much as ancestry. Only about one in six inhabitants could read in 1872. Settlement clustered near the coast, with large interior zones such as the Amazon basin, the São Francisco sertão, and the Mato Grosso frontier controlled lightly, if at all, by the state.

Regional economies pulled in different directions. The sugar and cotton provinces of the Northeast, Pernambuco and Bahia, had the oldest plantation traditions and the largest enslaved populations in absolute terms, but faced declining prices and the loss of markets. Minas Gerais had passed its gold peak and turned to farming and supplying Rio de Janeiro. Rio Grande do Sul raised cattle and mules and exported dried beef, charque, to plantation regions, while its politics were bound up with Uruguay and the Río de la Plata. Pará and Amazonas sat on the Amazon River system, far from Rio and closer to Lisbon in sailing time during the colonial period. The southeastern provinces around Rio were just beginning the coffee expansion that would reorient the economy of the empire.

Brazil's neighbors had taken a different constitutional path. Mexico, Gran Colombia, the Río de la Plata, and Central America adopted republican constitutions and soon experienced civil wars, fragmentation, and military rule (Chapters 58 through 62). Brazil's monarchy gave contemporaries a different set of options, and Brazilian elites, many of whom had studied law at Coimbra, watched Haiti and the Spanish American wars with attention. The scholarship that frames this chapter includes the arguments of José Murilo de Carvalho on an administrative elite, Ilmar Rohloff de Mattos on the Saquarema Conservatives as builders of the imperial state, Miriam Dolhnikoff on a negotiated pact with provincial elites, and Roderick Barman on the monarchy's legitimacy, alongside social historians such as João José Reis, Mary Karasch, and Sidney Chalhoub, who recover the enslaved and the poor. Section 61.45 and the Historical Debate return to these views.

Division I

Pedro I and the Crisis of the First Reign, 1822–1831

Why the monarchy that secured independence immediately confronted constitutional conflict, provincial resistance, financial weakness, and a crisis of legitimacy.

Section 61.1

Independence and the Problem of Imperial Authority

The monarchy created in 1822 was both a rupture and a continuity. It rejected the authority of the Portuguese Cortes, but it preserved the Braganza dynasty, the Portuguese language and legal inheritance, the Catholic Church, the planter economy, and slavery. Pedro's acclamation as emperor came from town councils, or câmaras, in Rio de Janeiro, Minas Gerais, and São Paulo, whose members were largely landowners, merchants, magistrates, and officials who preferred a monarchy under a Braganza prince to a republican experiment that might unleash the social upheaval seen in Haiti and in the Spanish American wars. Chapter 56 describes the process; what matters here is the coalition that resulted, which tied the throne to the southeastern elite.

That coalition did not extend equally to every province. Bahia, Maranhão, and Pará were held by Portuguese forces until 1823, and in Pernambuco, which had risen in republican revolt in 1817, many leaders distrusted a court in Rio that had suppressed that revolution. The new state had to turn a set of former Portuguese captaincies, each with ties of trade and administration of its own, into a single political community. Provincial presidents appointed from Rio governed alongside local landowners, and arguments over who would appoint officials, collect taxes, and command troops began almost immediately.

The new emperor's position was also fragile because many officers, merchants, and officials in the army and the bureaucracy had been born in Portugal. The Portuguese-born population of cities such as Rio, Salvador, and Recife dominated retail commerce and held government positions, and the lines between Portuguese and Brazilian-born residents became a political fault line. Brazilians who had supported independence resented foreign influence; Portuguese-born residents feared expulsion. Pedro I, born in Lisbon and heir to the Portuguese throne, kept ties to Portugal and to Portuguese advisers that his critics regarded as a threat to the new sovereignty.

Finally, the empire needed recognition and money. Portugal recognized Brazilian independence in the Treaty of Rio of August 1825, mediated by Britain, in return for compensation of two million pounds sterling and a title for John VI. Britain and other powers recognized the empire in the following years in exchange for commercial concessions, including the maintenance of low tariffs and a commitment to end the slave trade. These arrangements bound the new state to British financial and diplomatic interests, and they gave Pedro I a foreign debt before he had a fiscal system.

Section 61.2

The Constituent Assembly of 1823 and Its Dissolution

The Constituent and Legislative General Assembly opened in Rio de Janeiro on 3 May 1823. Its members, elected indirectly by a limited electorate, included lawyers, magistrates, clergy, and landowners, among them the brothers Antônio Carlos, Martim Francisco, and José Bonifácio de Andrada e Silva. Almost all agreed that the monarchy should have a constitution; they disagreed about how much power the assembly should have in relation to the emperor, and about what role Portuguese-born residents should play in the new state. The deputies saw themselves as representatives of the nation with authority to define the monarch's powers, while Pedro regarded his authority as prior to the assembly's.

José Bonifácio, the architect of the independence ministry, had left the government in July 1823 after conflicts with court rivals and with the Portuguese-born merchants and officials who disliked his policies. He and his brothers moved into opposition in the assembly and in the press. Their program was gradualist and reformist: a strong monarchy with limits, a program to end the slave trade over time and to integrate Indigenous people and freed people into the nation, and restrictions on the power of Portuguese-born conservatives. This combined an authoritarian conception of order with a reform agenda, and it unsettled slaveholders and court factions alike.

The draft constitution produced by a committee led by Antônio Carlos de Andrada limited the emperor's powers. It proposed a suspensive veto, subordinated the armed forces to the legislature, and set property qualifications for voting and office expressed in measures of manioc flour, which led critics to call it the Constituição da Mandioca. Those qualifications were a form of exclusion typical of the period, and they show that the assembly's constitutionalism was elitist as well as liberal. For Pedro, the proposal threatened his control of the army and his claim to be the source of constitutional order.

The crisis came in November 1823 after an altercation between officers and a Brazilian-born pharmacist, which the assembly's critics treated as a Portuguese provocation. Troops surrounded the assembly in what has been called the Noite da Agonia, and on 12 November Pedro dissolved it, arrested several deputies, and exiled the Andrada brothers. He promised a constitution "twice as liberal" as the assembly's draft, and he delegated a ten-man Council of State to write it. The dissolution inaugurated a pattern: constitutional authority in Brazil would rest on an imperial grant, not on a sovereign assembly, and the emperor's willingness to use force would shape his reputation among provincial liberals.

Section 61.3

The Constitution of 1824 and the Moderating Power

The Constitution of the Empire of Brazil, granted by the emperor on 25 March 1824 and sworn in the capital and then in the provinces, drew on the Council of State's draft, which in turn borrowed from the assembly's text, from the French Charter of 1814, and from the writings of Benjamin Constant on constitutional monarchy. It established a unitary state divided into provinces, with presidents appointed by the emperor. It recognized four constitutional powers: legislative, moderating, executive, and judicial. The Legislative Power was exercised by the General Assembly, composed of a Chamber of Deputies elected for four years and a Senate of lifetime members, who had to be at least forty years old and meet a high income requirement. The Executive Power belonged to the emperor, exercised through ministers he appointed. Judges had security of tenure but could be suspended by the emperor.

The Moderating Power was the constitution's most original feature. Article 98 described it as the key to the whole political organization, delegated exclusively to the emperor as the supreme head of the nation and its first representative, so that he could watch over the balance and independence of the other powers. In practice it allowed the emperor to choose senators from lists submitted after provincial elections, to convene, prorogue, or dissolve the Chamber of Deputies, to appoint and dismiss ministers freely, to sanction or veto legislation, to suspend magistrates, and to grant pardons. The emperor was declared inviolable and not subject to responsibility, while ministers could be held responsible. The intention, drawn from Constant, was to place a neutral arbiter above partisan conflict; critics soon argued that the arbiter was also the most powerful political actor.

Citizenship and elections reflected a society of unequal status. All free men born in Brazil, including Brazilian-born freed people (libertos), were citizens, while African-born freed people were treated as foreigners. Elections were indirect, in two stages: parish assemblies of "active citizens" with an annual income above a modest threshold chose electors who met a higher income standard, and those electors chose deputies, who needed a still higher income. Freedmen could vote in the first stage but not become electors. Because incomes were measured in a currency of fluctuating value, and because the income test excluded few free men in the early years, the electorate was broader than that of many European states at the time, though still a fraction of the free population and almost none of the enslaved.

The text recognized Roman Catholicism as the religion of the empire, allowed other religions private worship in houses with no external form of a temple, and left the emperor with the powers of patronage over the Church. It guaranteed property rights and individual liberties in a long declaration of rights, and it abolished the most severe colonial punishments, but it contained no word on slavery. This silence was a political choice. By declaring property inviolable and avoiding the issue, the constitution protected slaveholders without naming the institution, and it later allowed slaveholders to treat any abolition proposal as an attack on property. The text remained in force until 1889, amended only once, by the Additional Act of 1834.

Historical Vocabulary
Poder Moderador (the Moderating Power)

The Poder Moderador was the fourth power created by the Constitution of 1824, held exclusively by the emperor. It combined the powers of choosing senators, dissolving the Chamber of Deputies, naming and dismissing ministers, suspending judges, and granting pardons. The concept came from the French theorist Benjamin Constant, who proposed a neutral royal power distinct from the executive that ministers exercised.

The idea matters because it made the emperor a participant in politics, not a ceremonial figure. In Brazil the executive power was exercised by ministers, but the emperor named them freely, which meant that cabinets lasted only as long as they had imperial confidence. Liberals later argued that Pedro II exercised a "personal power" that distorted parliamentary government, while defenders said that the power allowed rotation between parties. Students should not equate it with the British constitutional monarchy, in which the monarch reigns and an elected majority governs.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: The Constitution of the Empire of Brazil, 1824

Creators / issuing body: Pedro I, advised by the imperial Council of State · Date: 25 March 1824 · Source type: Granted constitution · Documentary reference: Official 1824 Constitution, Brazilian Presidency and Chamber of Deputies.

Context: After dissolving the Constituent Assembly in November 1823, Pedro I charged a Council of State with drafting a constitution and then submitted the text for approval by the municipal councils, which swore to it in the following months. It was therefore granted by the emperor and not framed by an elected assembly, though many of its provisions followed the assembly's draft.

Paraphrase of key provisions: the text divides political power into four branches and calls the Moderating Power the key to the entire political organization, delegated to the emperor as supreme head of the nation; it declares the emperor's person inviolable; it establishes indirect, income-based elections; it makes Catholicism the religion of the state while permitting private worship by others; and it guarantees property in terms that protect slaveholders without mentioning slavery.

Analysis: The constitution shows what the elite and the emperor wanted from the state: a stable monarchy, constitutional limits on ministers, and legal protection for property. It also shows what is absent. Enslaved people, women, Indigenous nations not integrated into the legal order, and African-born freed people are outside the political community, and the silence on slavery is a significant omission. Historians use it alongside parliamentary debates and provincial reports to see how the text operated in practice, since the formal powers of the emperor and the actual balance of forces changed greatly between 1824 and 1889.

Section 61.4

The Confederation of the Equator and Provincial Resistance, 1824

The granting of the constitution coincided with a crisis in Pernambuco. Recife had been the center of the 1817 republican revolt, and its political culture combined Enlightenment liberalism, Masonic networks, a lively press, and resentment of Rio de Janeiro's tax demands. When Pedro I dissolved the assembly and then replaced Manuel de Carvalho Pais de Andrade, the provincial president elected by the local junta, with a nominee of his own, many Pernambucans saw both acts as proof of centralizing absolutism. In June and July 1824, Carvalho, backed by local landowners, merchants, and radical journalists, refused to accept the imperial nominee and proclaimed the Confederation of the Equator on 2 July, calling on neighboring provinces to join.

Ceará, Paraíba, and parts of Rio Grande do Norte joined in varying degrees, and the movement favored a federal and republican form of government, with a constitution modeled on a Colombian rather than a Brazilian text. The leading intellectual of the rebellion was Frei Caneca, a Carmelite friar and teacher who published the newspaper Typhis Pernambucano and wrote one of the most influential critiques of the 1824 Constitution. He argued that a constitution should come from the nation, not from a monarch, and that the Moderating Power concentrated authority. The rebels were not united: planters distrusted the lower-class participants, and the movement's leaders disagreed about whether to abolish the slave trade or to use enslaved men as soldiers.

The empire responded with a combined naval and land campaign. Lord Cochrane blockaded Recife, and troops under Francisco de Lima e Silva marched overland from Bahia. Recife fell in September 1824, and leaders who fled or were captured faced military commissions that prosecuted them rapidly. Frei Caneca was condemned and executed in Recife in January 1825, after executioners refused to hang a priest and a firing squad carried out the sentence. Other leaders were executed or exiled, and Carvalho escaped to Britain and the United States. In Ceará, the rebel leader Tristão Gonçalves was killed in the field.

The consequences were lasting. The repression demonstrated that the new empire would use force against republicanism, and it made Frei Caneca a martyr for later liberals and federalists. It also left a political legacy in the Northeast: a tradition of federalist grievance that would reappear in the Praieira revolution of 1848 and in the provincial politics of the Regency. The rebellion further showed that a centralized monarchy could mobilize naval power, loyal provinces, and the support of slaveholders against a challenge based in one province, a pattern that would repeat in later decades.

Section 61.5

The Cisplatine War and the Loss of Uruguay, 1825–1828

The Banda Oriental, the territory east of the Uruguay River and north of the Río de la Plata, had been contested between Spanish and Portuguese empires since the colonial period (Chapters 28 and 39). Portuguese forces occupied Montevideo in 1817 and 1820, and in 1821 the territory was incorporated into the Portuguese-Brazilian monarchy as the Cisplatine Province. Its elites were divided between those who accepted Brazilian rule, those who looked to Buenos Aires, and those who favored local autonomy, and Brazilian officers and landowners from Rio Grande do Sul held important property in the region. The cattle economy across the frontier tied the territory closely to Rio Grande do Sul.

In April 1825 a group of exiles led by Juan Antonio Lavalleja, the "Thirty-Three Orientals," crossed from Buenos Aires into the territory, gathered support, and in August declared union with the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata. Buenos Aires accepted the union, and Pedro I declared war in December 1825. The Brazilian navy blockaded the Río de la Plata and disrupted Buenos Aires's commerce, while on land Brazilian forces suffered defeats, the most serious at Ituzaingó in February 1827. The war was fought largely by cavalry and irregulars over the open pampas, with foreign mercenaries and naval officers on both sides, and with Brazilian recruitment methods, forced conscription and the sending of prisoners to the army, generating resentment at home.

The war's cost strained the empire's finances. Pedro I had inherited a treasury drained by the independence war and by the compensation to Portugal. To pay for the army and navy the government issued paper money, borrowed in London, and eventually liquidated the first Banco do Brasil in 1829 after it had financed the treasury beyond its reserves. Inflation and the cost of living rose, and merchants and urban workers blamed the emperor's policies. The fiscal and military burden fell unevenly on provinces and, above all, on the families of conscripts and on the free poor.

Britain, concerned about the disruption of commerce and the prospect of prolonged blockade, mediated through Lord Ponsonby. The Preliminary Convention of Peace of 27 August 1828 declared the Cisplatine territory independent of both Brazil and Buenos Aires, creating a buffer state that Britain favored and that became the Eastern Republic of Uruguay, whose first constitution dates from 1830. For Brazil the loss of the province was a defeat that damaged the emperor's prestige, and for Rio Grande do Sul it left an unresolved frontier and an economy tied to a political space the empire no longer controlled. The war thus contributed to the crisis of 1831 and later to the Farroupilha.

Section 61.6

Political Crisis, Social Conflict, and Pedro I's Abdication in 1831

By the late 1820s the First Reign faced opposition from several directions. The General Assembly, finally elected and convened in 1826, became a forum for liberal deputies who attacked the ministers, the war, the finances, and the court. Newspapers in Rio, São Paulo, Recife, and elsewhere multiplied, and political journalism combined constitutional argument with attacks on Portuguese-born merchants and officials. The assassination in November 1830 of Líbero Badaró, an Italian-born liberal journalist in São Paulo, was widely blamed on the government and intensified opposition. The language of the press was increasingly direct, and the emperor's critics included moderate liberals, radical republicans, and officers of the army.

The question of succession in Portugal added a dynastic dimension. After the death of John VI in 1826, Pedro was the legitimate heir to the Portuguese crown, which he abdicated in favor of his daughter Maria da Glória, while his brother Miguel seized power in Lisbon in 1828. Pedro's involvement in this dynastic conflict, and his reliance on Portuguese-born advisers, made Brazilians suspect that he valued Portugal over Brazil. Portuguese residents in Rio celebrated his return from tours of the provinces, and Brazilian-born residents responded with demonstrations. In March 1831 the conflict spilled into the streets in the Noite das Garrafadas, a series of clashes in which supporters of the emperor and the opposition fought with bottles, stones, and other objects in the center of Rio.

The final crisis took place on 5 to 7 April 1831. After dismissing a moderate cabinet and appointing one of absolutist leanings, Pedro faced a large gathering in the Campo de Santana, which army units joined. Officers and soldiers, many of them Brazilian-born and some from enslaved or free Black backgrounds, sided with the crowd and refused to follow the emperor's orders. On 7 April Pedro signed an abdication in favor of his five-year-old son, Pedro de Alcântara, and boarded a British warship. He returned to Europe, fought to restore his daughter Maria II to the Portuguese throne, and died in Portugal in 1834.

The abdication was a political revolution within the monarchy, not a republic. It was carried out by a coalition of moderate liberals, journalists, military officers, and urban crowds, and its aims were to keep the constitutional monarchy while removing an emperor regarded as authoritarian and foreign-leaning. The constitution provided for a regency when the emperor was a minor, and the General Assembly, which was in recess, assembled to choose a regent. The Regency therefore began as an act of parliamentary authority, and the next nine years would test whether Brazil could govern itself through its institutions without a monarch in command.

Division II

Regency Government and Constitutional Experimentation, 1831–1840

The Regency was a serious experiment in governing a monarchy without an adult monarch, not merely a chaotic interlude awaiting Pedro II.

Section 61.7

The Regency and the Constitutional Succession Crisis

The Constitution provided that when the emperor was a minor the empire would be governed by a regency, and the General Assembly's first act after 7 April 1831 was to confirm a Provisional Trina Regency of three members: Nicolau Pereira de Campos Vergueiro, José Joaquim Carneiro de Campos, Marquis of Caravelas, and the army officer Francisco de Lima e Silva. On 17 June the Assembly elected a Permanent Trina Regency of Lima e Silva, José da Costa Carvalho, and João Bráulio Muniz. The regents governed in the name of a child emperor, with ministers who were responsible to the Chamber of Deputies, and Parliament consequently became the center of authority in a way it had never been under Pedro I.

Politics divided into three broad tendencies. Moderates, led by figures such as Feijó, Vasconcelos, and Evaristo da Veiga, the editor of the Aurora Fluminense, favored a constitutional monarchy with greater power for Parliament and the provinces, and controlled most of the Regency governments. Exaltados, or radicals, wanted federal government, in some cases a republic, and broader participation, and they drew support from urban artisans, junior officers, and journalists. Restorationists, sometimes called caramurus, wanted Pedro I to return, and found their base among Portuguese-born merchants and conservative officials. These labels were fluid and fought over, but they structured political life until the later 1830s.

The Regency faced urgent threats to public order. Soldiers' mutinies broke out in Rio in July 1831, and the government responded by disbanding units and relying on the National Guard it was creating. Riots against Portuguese-born residents, restorationist conspiracies, and provincial revolts such as the Cabanada in Pernambuco and Alagoas (1832–1835), a rural uprising of free poor, Indigenous, and enslaved people in support of the absent emperor, tested the government. The death of Pedro I in September 1834 ended the restorationist cause and removed one of the Regency's principal dangers, though it left the question of how to govern without a monarch unanswered.

The Regency also faced the problem of the emperor's education and guardianship. José Bonifácio, recalled from exile, was named guardian of the young Pedro II in 1831, and he was removed in 1833 after political disputes. The fate of the child emperor became a political question, since whoever controlled his upbringing could shape the future monarchy. The Regency years thus tested whether the monarchy could survive as an institution when its holder was a child, and whether Brazilians would continue to regard it as the source of legitimacy.

Section 61.8

The National Guard, Criminal Procedure, and Local Political Authority

The Regency's first reforms addressed security. The National Guard, created by law on 18 August 1831, replaced the colonial militias and the ordenanças with a citizen force organized by municipality under the Ministry of Justice. Citizens who met an income requirement were enrolled, and in the early years the members elected their own officers, which gave local landowners control over armed men without the intervention of the regular army, which the Regency distrusted after the mutinies of 1831. The Guard reduced the army to a small force in the 1830s and gave the Regency a militia loyal to the constitutional order.

The Code of Criminal Procedure of 29 November 1832 decentralized justice further. It expanded the authority of elected juízes de paz, or justices of the peace, an office provided for in the 1824 Constitution and organized by legislation in 1827. The code enlarged their role in local policing and the preliminary handling of criminal proceedings while structuring jury trials, habeas corpus, and other judicial procedures. These reforms extended participation in justice to local property owners, and they made elected local officials the primary agents of order in the countryside. For liberals, they were an implementation of constitutional rights; for conservatives, they were a delegation of public authority to local elites who used it for personal and factional ends.

The combined effect was to strengthen local power brokers. Wealthy landowners dominated National Guard offices, and the title of colonel, coronel, which they held, became the label of local political bosses in the later nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the origin of the word coronelismo. Guard officers and justices of the peace mobilized voters, protected clients, and persecuted rivals. The enslaved were excluded from the Guard, and free Black and mixed-race men could serve but rarely as officers. The system also depended on rural and urban poor men for service, with unpaid duty, drilling, and policing burdens that fell on those who were enrolled.

Both reforms were products of the particular fears of 1831: of the army, of a restoration, and of social disorder. They also created the institutions through which the Regency, and later the empire, could govern in a country with few salaried officials. The debates they provoked, about whether local elites could be trusted with public authority, ran through the rest of the Regency and fed directly into the conservative reaction of the late 1830s.

Historical Vocabulary
Guarda Nacional (National Guard)

The Guarda Nacional was a citizen militia created by law on 18 August 1831 to replace the colonial ordenanças and militia and to support public order. Enrollment was linked to income, and in its first years members elected their own officers. After a reform in 1850 the government appointed officers, and the Guard came under the Ministry of Justice, with commissions often given to local landowners in exchange for political support.

The Guard matters because it fused military organization with local political power. Colonels and captains of the Guard commanded dependents, mobilized votes, and exercised policing functions, and the title of coronel became a general term for a local boss. The Guard also called on men from poor families for duty, while its officer commissions became marks of status and instruments of patronage. It is not equivalent to a modern national guard, which is often a reserve force of the state.

Section 61.9

The Additional Act of 1834 and Provincial Autonomy

The reforms of 1831 and 1832 left the structure of the constitutional state untouched, and by 1832 liberals proposed a constitutional reform that would increase provincial autonomy. A proposal for a federal monarchy, with provincial assemblies and a sole regent, was debated in the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate. Conservatives resisted and radicals pressed for more, and the result was a compromise, the Additional Act, adopted as law on 12 August 1834, the only formal amendment to the 1824 Constitution during the empire.

The Act replaced the consultative provincial councils with elected provincial legislative assemblies, with authority over provincial budgets and taxes, municipal government, public employment, education, police, and public works. It also abolished the Council of State, which had advised the emperor under the Moderating Power, and replaced the three-member Regency with a single regent chosen by direct vote of the electors for a four-year term. It created the Neutral Municipality of Rio de Janeiro, separating the capital from the province of the same name, whose capital became Niterói. The assemblies could not tax imports and exports, which remained central revenue, and they could not alter the authority of the presidents appointed by the central government.

The Act did not establish a federal republic. Provinces gained legislative powers but not their own constitutions, courts, or armies, and the central government retained the army, the navy, the customs, the Senate, and the appointment of provincial presidents. In this sense, it created a decentralized unitary state with elements of federalism. Historians such as Miriam Dolhnikoff argue that the Act gave provincial elites a stable stake in the empire, while others stress that the same elites used their new authority to expand patronage and local control, and that the central government soon curbed it.

The Act's effects were mixed. Provincial assemblies became arenas for debate on taxes, roads, schools, and public order, and they gave regional politicians experience in legislation. Many assemblies also passed laws that central politicians considered abuses, creating new offices, regulating local police, and spending resources unevenly. The Act's ambiguities, especially over which provincial laws touched on judicial and police matters that belonged to the central government, were the principal subject of the conservative reinterpretation of 1840.

Section 61.10

Feijó, Araújo Lima, and Rival Regency Coalitions

In 1835 the electors chose Diogo Antônio Feijó as sole regent in the first direct vote for that office, and he defeated Antônio Francisco de Paula Holanda Cavalcanti by a narrow margin. Feijó was a priest from São Paulo, a former Minister of Justice with a reputation for energy and independence, and a critic of clerical celibacy. He took office in October 1835 as the Cabanagem raged in Pará and the Farroupilha began in Rio Grande do Sul, and the new regent had neither a dependable parliamentary majority nor an effective army.

Feijó's government was caught between provincial rebellions and conservative opposition in the Chamber. He sent military forces to Pará and the south, but his ministers lost support, and his conflict with Bernardo Pereira de Vasconcelos and others who had formerly been moderates grew. Feijó's attempts to enforce central authority, while his own reforms had devolved it, left him exposed, and in September 1837 he resigned, handing power to Pedro de Araújo Lima, the Minister of the Empire, who was elected regent in April 1838.

The Araújo Lima regency formed around a coalition of former moderates and conservatives who argued that the reforms had gone too far. Ministers including Vasconcelos, Miguel Calmon, and Honório Hermeto Carneiro Leão pressed for the recentralization of justice and police and for a stronger executive. They found support among planters and merchants in the southeast, who saw the revolts in Bahia, Pará, and the south as threats to property and slavery, and among provincial elites who had been displaced by rival factions in their own provinces. The regent himself was a cautious legalist and relied on these ministers.

The two regencies illustrate the volatility of Regency politics. Feijó's government was identified with decentralization, while Araújo Lima's was identified with the Regresso, the reaction. Yet both regents sought to maintain the monarchy and the unity of the empire, and both relied on a narrow elite with shared interests in order and in slavery. Their rivalry shows that debates over decentralization were also debates over who would control patronage, in a political system where office, tax collection, and appointments were the foundations of power.

Section 61.11

The Conservative Regresso and the Interpretation of 1840

By the late 1830s many politicians who had supported the reforms of 1831 to 1834 concluded that decentralization had weakened the state. The revolts of 1835 to 1840, the abuse of local offices, and fear of social disorder led them to propose a "return," the Regresso, to central authority. Bernardo Pereira de Vasconcelos, a Minas Gerais lawyer who had been among the most prominent liberals, became its principal spokesman. The movement coalesced in the late 1830s into a Conservative party, called the Party of Order, and its opponents, the Liberals, began to organize in response. These party names were new in this period and did not match modern ideological categories, since both groups were slaveholding, monarchist, and elitist.

The institutional result came in two stages. The Interpretative Law of 12 May 1840 reinterpreted the Additional Act to restrict provincial assemblies from legislating on the appointment and dismissal of judicial officers and other officials considered to belong to the central government, and it effectively returned control of the judiciary and police to the center. A second stage followed after Pedro II's accession: the Council of State was restored in November 1841, and the Law of 3 December 1841 reformed the Code of Criminal Procedure, replacing elected justices of the peace in many functions with police chiefs and delegates appointed by the government.

These changes did not abolish the Additional Act, and provincial assemblies continued to legislate on taxes, roads, and education. What changed was the balance of authority over justice and policing. Appointed officials, usually trained lawyers, controlled the local police and judiciary, and they answered to provincial presidents and ministers in Rio de Janeiro. This created a bureaucratic channel for central influence that did not depend on the army, and it increased the importance of patronage in the appointment of local officials.

Historians interpret the Regresso differently. Ilmar Rohloff de Mattos describes it as the project of the Saquarema Conservatives, who made the state the instrument of the ruling class of slaveholders and merchants. Miriam Dolhnikoff stresses that the empire retained provincial assemblies and negotiated with provincial elites, and that the settlement was a compromise and not a simple victory of centralization. Both views agree that the settlement of 1840 and 1841 established the institutional framework that would govern the empire for decades. The Regency, in this view, was not a failure but a laboratory in which Brazil's elites tested alternatives and chose a centralized, monarchical order.

Section 61.12

The Malê Revolt in Bahia, 1835

Salvador, the capital of Bahia, was one of the largest cities in the empire, with a population in which enslaved and free Africans and Afro-Brazilians formed a majority. Bahia received captives chiefly from the Bight of Benin, and by the 1830s a large share of its enslaved and freed population was Nagô (Yoruba-speaking) and Hausa, including many Muslims. The word malê comes from the Yoruba word for Muslim. Some had been taken captive in wars in the Yoruba region and the Sokoto caliphate in the early nineteenth century. Salvador had also seen at least half a dozen slave uprisings between 1807 and 1835, most led by Hausa, so the city's authorities were alert to the possibility of revolt.

The Malê community was literate in Arabic, which was unusual in a city where most free people could not read, and it practiced Islam in secret or semi-private settings, with teachers, prayer meetings, amulets bearing Quranic verses, and white clothing worn on ceremonial occasions. Enslaved and freed Africans worked in the city as porters, artisans, and street sellers, and many of the rebels were ganhadores, enslaved workers who hired themselves out and paid their owners a fixed sum. This semi-autonomous labor gave them mobility and networks. Leaders met in the homes of freed Africans, and the organizers planned a rising for the early hours of 25 January 1835, a religious holiday.

The plan was betrayed by informers, among them a freed woman, and magistrates were alerted shortly before the rising, so the rebels began earlier than intended on the night of 24 January. Several hundred men, perhaps six hundred, took part in street fighting in Salvador, attacking guard posts and trying to free imprisoned leaders and enslaved people. They fought bravely but were outgunned by soldiers and militia, and the rising was suppressed within hours. Government sources report dozens of rebels killed and a number of soldiers and civilians dead. Scholarship, especially João José Reis's study, shows that the participants included both enslaved and freed Africans but very few Brazilian-born people, and that the movement aimed less at overthrowing the state than at ending the enslavement of Muslim Africans and, probably, establishing a Muslim presence in Bahia.

The consequences were severe. Courts tried hundreds of people, sentencing a handful to death, many to flogging in numbers that could exceed a thousand lashes, others to imprisonment with hard labor, and many freed Africans to deportation to Africa. The Bahian authorities and the central government treated the revolt as a threat to the entire slave system. In June 1835 the national legislature passed a law imposing the death penalty on enslaved people who killed or seriously injured owners or overseers, with summary trial procedures. The revolt also strengthened suspicion of African-born residents and encouraged the emigration of many freed Africans, some of whom returned to West Africa and established communities in Lagos and elsewhere.

Section 61.13

The Cabanagem in Grão-Pará, 1835–1840

The province of Grão-Pará, which then included what is now Amazonas, was an Amazonian society of rivers, forest, and a few towns, with Belém at its center. Its population was predominantly Indigenous and mixed, composed of Indigenous people called tapuios (acculturated or detribalized Indigenous people), mestiços, free Black people, and enslaved Africans, along with a small elite of landowners, merchants, and officials, many of them Portuguese-born. The colonial Directorate system and the labor drafts that had followed had forced Indigenous people into service, and recruitment of men for military and canoe labor continued after independence. Many of the poor lived in riverside huts, cabanas, which is the origin of the name cabano given to the rebels, though that name was also used by the elite.

The immediate political context was a power struggle after independence. Pará, a Portuguese stronghold in 1822 and 1823, had been conquered by a naval expedition, and the repression, including the deaths of a large number of prisoners in the hold of a ship, the brig Palhaço, in 1823, left deep resentment. Provincial politics divided Brazilian-born landowners and Portuguese-born merchants, and in 1834 and 1835 the Regency's appointed presidents were disliked by local elites. In January 1835 a group of landowners and officers, with the support of armed poor and Indigenous people from the interior, attacked Belém, killing the president, Bernardo Lobo de Sousa, and the military commander.

The rebellion soon outran its elite leaders. Félix Clemente Malcher, a landowner, was made president and then killed by other rebels. Francisco Pedro Vinagre followed, and in August 1835 Eduardo Angelim, a young man from Ceará, became the leader of the cabano forces in Belém. The rebels drew on Indigenous and Afro-descended fighters, targeted Portuguese merchants and officials, and in some interior areas attacked the property of landowners. They did not agree on goals. Some declared loyalty to Pedro II while rejecting the president; some proclaimed independence for Pará in 1835 and 1836; others sought redress of local grievances, protection from conscription and forced labor, and control of local government.

The imperial response was a naval and military reconquest. A new commander, Francisco José de Sousa Soares de Andréa, retook Belém in May 1836 with forces sent from Rio, and in the following years pursued the rebels through the interior, with raids, executions, forced labor drafts, and the destruction of communities. The war continued until 1840, when an amnesty was declared. Estimates of the dead vary considerably; historians commonly cite figures on the order of thirty to forty thousand, a devastating share of the province’s population, though the evidence does not permit a precise death toll. The Cabanagem is considered one of the largest popular revolts of nineteenth-century Latin America, and the repression it provoked left Pará under heavy military control and shaped Amazonian labor relations into the rubber era.

Historical Vocabulary
Cabano and Cabanagem

A cabano was a participant in the Cabanagem, the rebellion in Grão-Pará between 1835 and 1840. The word comes from cabana (hut), the dwellings of the poor in Amazonian riverbanks, and it was used by elites and officials as a label for the poor rebels, later adopted with pride by historians and by participants' descendants. The name was also applied to other movements, such as the Cabanada in Pernambuco and Alagoas, so students should specify which rebellion is meant.

The term matters because it points to the social composition of the revolt. Cabanos included Indigenous and mestizo riverine workers, free Black people, and some enslaved people, alongside dissident landowners and officers, and their grievances included conscription, forced labor, and exclusion from power. "Cabano" is not a synonym for "separatist": historians debate how many rebels sought independence and how many fought for local autonomy under the emperor.

Section 61.14

The Farroupilha Revolution and the Southern Republics, 1835–1845

Rio Grande do Sul was a frontier province whose economy rested on cattle ranching, mule breeding, and the production of charque, or salted dried beef, much of it by enslaved labor, for the plantation regions of the north. Its landowners, the estancieiros, were closely tied to Uruguay and Argentina by family, property, and trade, and many were military men with armed followings, the nearest Brazilian equivalent to Spanish American caudillos. They resented central taxation, the imperial appointment of presidents, and above all the competition from Platine charque that was imported at low tariffs, which hurt them. The loss of the Cisplatine Province had also weakened the province's frontier position.

The revolt began in Porto Alegre in September 1835, when a group of estancieiros and officers led by Bento Gonçalves da Silva, a prominent rancher and Mason, deposed the provincial president. They called themselves farroupilhas (ragamuffins), a label first given by opponents. Initially they claimed to be loyal to the emperor and objected to a particular president, but after victories in the field, notably at Seival, the republic of Rio Grande do Sul, the Riograndense Republic, was proclaimed in September 1836, with its capital at Piratini and later Alegrete. A short-lived Juliana Republic was proclaimed in Santa Catarina in 1839, in which Giuseppe Garibaldi, who had arrived in Brazil as an exile and had served as a privateer, and his companion Ana Maria de Jesus Ribeiro, known as Anita, took part.

The war lasted ten years and was fought mainly with cavalry across the pampa, with raids, sieges, and pitched battles, and it ground into a stalemate. The republicans created a government with a constitution, a press, and a legislature, but never won control of the main port of Rio Grande or the capital, Porto Alegre, for long. They enlisted enslaved men, who were promised freedom for service, in the infantry and lancer units known as the Lanceiros Negros, and Black soldiers formed a large part of the republican army. The empire in turn recruited soldiers and mobilized the National Guard. From 1842 Luís Alves de Lima e Silva, later Duke of Caxias, commanded imperial forces and combined military pressure with negotiation.

The war ended in 1845 through a negotiated settlement, the Peace of Ponche Verde, signed on 1 March. The government granted amnesty, accepted republican officers into the imperial army with their ranks, and assumed some republican debts; it also raised tariffs on imported charque. What happened to the Black soldiers is contested. Many were reportedly given their freedom, but historians dispute this, and the episode at Porongos in November 1844, when imperial forces attacked a camp of Black lancers, has generated controversy, including over the authenticity of a letter attributed to Caxias suggesting an understanding with an imperial officer. Whether the republicans sacrificed their Black soldiers to secure peace is debated, and the evidence is uncertain. The Farroupilha shows a rebellion of slaveholders that depended on Black soldiers and ended in a bargain that protected slaveholding.

Section 61.15

The Sabinada in Bahia, 1837–1838

The Sabinada, named for the physician and journalist Francisco Sabino Álvares da Rocha Vieira, took place in Salvador in November 1837, two years after the Malê revolt and while Bahia was still under heavy security. Bahian politics in the 1830s were shaped by quarrels over the president's powers, the Regency's recruitment practices, the privileged position of Portuguese-born merchants, and the economic stagnation of the sugar region. A group of military officers, professionals, and journalists, joined by some soldiers and urban artisans, saw in the Farroupilha and in the Regency's disarray a chance to demand change.

On 7 November 1837 the rebels seized forts and barracks in Salvador and proclaimed the State of Bahia a republic. Their declaration set a limit: the republic would last until Pedro II came of age, which signaled loyalty to the dynasty while rejecting the Regency, and it was an act of temporary independence and not a permanent separation. The rebels' leadership included white and mixed-race professionals and officers, and their support came from garrison troops and from poor residents of the city. They controlled part of Salvador for about four months, but they failed to win over the sugar planters of the Recôncavo, who feared that the rebellion might unleash a slave uprising and who supplied the government with money and men.

The question of slavery was central. Rebel leaders appealed to Black and mixed-race people, and they reportedly offered freedom to enslaved men who would fight, but they did not endorse general emancipation, and many planters used the threat of social revolution to rally the countryside. The government blockaded the city by sea, besieged it by land, and in March 1838 forces from the Recôncavo and units sent from other provinces stormed Salvador, with heavy fighting and fire. More than a thousand people were killed, and several thousand were arrested.

The repression was harsh. Sabino and other leaders were sentenced to exile in distant provinces, some officers were executed or imprisoned, and rank-and-file participants, especially Black and mixed-race soldiers, were punished severely. The Sabinada was nevertheless a distinctive movement: it was mainly urban, led by professionals and soldiers, and temporary by design. It shows how city-based liberal radicalism could threaten the government, and how that radicalism foundered when planters of the interior decided that order mattered more than reform.

Section 61.16

The Balaiada in Maranhão, 1838–1841

Maranhão had a cotton economy in the lowlands near São Luís, worked by enslaved Africans, and a cattle economy in the interior, worked by free vaqueiros and dependents. By the late 1830s cotton prices were falling, the province's elites were divided between the Bem-te-vis, a liberal faction, and the Cabanos, a conservative one, and a provincial law of 1836 had given prefects broad power over local districts. The factional struggle between elites, in which both sides sought control of appointments and the police, created an opening for rural people who resented recruitment, forced labor, and the exactions of local officials.

The revolt began in December 1838 when Raimundo Gomes, a cowboy known as Cara Preta, led a group that freed prisoners from the jail of Vila da Manga and attacked a local prefect, in protest at a recruitment drive and in response to an offense against his family. The movement spread through the interior as other leaders joined, among them Manuel Francisco dos Anjos Ferreira, a maker of baskets, balaios, who gave the movement its name, and Cosme Bento das Chagas, a formerly enslaved Black man who led a quilombo of thousands of fugitives and called himself Tutor and Emperor of the Bem-te-vi Freedoms. The rebels took the important town of Caxias in 1839, and for months the interior was largely outside government control.

The movement was a coalition of different interests. Dissident Bem-te-vi politicians sought to use the revolt against their Cabano rivals, vaqueiros and small farmers resisted recruitment and local tyranny, and fugitives and enslaved people saw an opportunity for freedom. These groups did not share goals, and rebel leaders distrusted one another. Some Bem-te-vi leaders withdrew when the revolt became socially radical, and the participation of enslaved people alarmed planters across the province.

The government responded by naming Luís Alves de Lima e Silva as president and military commander of Maranhão in late 1839. Combining offers of amnesty to white rebels with military operations against Black fighters, he took Caxias back in 1841 and pursued the rebels until the last resistance collapsed. Cosme Bento was captured and executed in 1842, and many of his followers were returned to slavery. The war made Lima e Silva's reputation, and he received the title of Count of Caxias, derived from the town he took, which was later raised to a dukedom. The Balaiada demonstrates how factional disputes among elites could open space for popular uprising, and how elites closed ranks when slavery was threatened.

Section 61.17

Comparing the Regency Revolts: Why Did the Empire Survive?

The five major movements differed widely. The Malê rising was an urban conspiracy of African Muslims aimed at ending their enslavement and perhaps establishing a Muslim community, with no program for the whole empire. The Cabanagem was a rural and riverine revolt of Indigenous, Afro-descended, and mestizo people, joined by dissident landowners, aimed at ending exclusion and abuses, with some separatist elements. The Farroupilha was a revolt of landowners who fought a long war for autonomy and tax relief, using enslaved soldiers. The Sabinada was an urban movement of officers and professionals with a deliberately temporary republican program. The Balaiada was a revolt of factional elites and rural poor, in which a fugitive community played a major role. Treating them as one phenomenon, "regional separatism," conceals these differences.

Several shared features help explain why they failed to break the empire. None created a common front: rebels in one province did not coordinate with those in another, and their ideologies differed. Most of the elite, even where they opposed the Regency, valued the monarchy as a guarantee of order, and many invoked the child emperor as a symbol. Slaveholders, who dominated the political class, feared that prolonged conflict would encourage slave rebellion, and when revolts mobilized enslaved or poor rebels, as in Bahia, Pará, and Maranhão, planters rallied to the central government. The settlements also differed: the Farroupilha ended in a negotiated peace with amnesty and tariff protection, while the Cabanagem and Malê revolts ended in mass repression.

The imperial state used a combination of force and bargaining. It had a navy that could blockade ports and move troops, officers such as Caxias who combined military skill with negotiation, and the National Guard and provincial elites who could be mobilized against social revolt. It also offered concessions: amnesty, the preservation of local power, and promises of protection to landowners. These tools did not make the empire inevitably victorious; there were moments, such as the fall of Belém in 1835 and the stalemate in Rio Grande do Sul, when the outcome was open. Historians argue that the empire survived through an uneven combination of coercion, elite solidarity, and political adaptation.

The revolts also shaped the institutions of the next decade. The fear they created strengthened the Regresso, supported the restoration of the Council of State and the centralization of police, and persuaded many politicians that the monarchy was the best protection against both social revolution and provincial fragmentation. The experience of the 1830s and 1840s thus supplied part of the foundation of the imperial order, an order built in part on the memory of near-collapse.

How a teenage emperor, a recentralized administration, and a pragmatic political class reestablished the authority of the monarchy.

Section 61.18

The Majority Movement of 1840 and Pedro II's Accession

By 1840 many politicians believed that the Regency could not end the rebellions and that the empire needed a reigning monarch. The constitution set the age of majority at eighteen, which would not come until December 1843, but a movement in Parliament proposed that Pedro de Alcântara be declared of age early. Its leaders were mostly Liberals, among them Antônio Carlos de Andrada, Holanda Cavalcanti, and the Andrada family, who had been displaced by the Regresso and who calculated that a young emperor would turn to them. The Majority Club, founded in 1840, gathered support in the Chamber and the Senate.

The Conservative regent Araújo Lima and his allies resisted, but the movement gained momentum from the young emperor's apparent willingness to accept the throne. On 23 July 1840, the General Assembly declared Pedro II of age, at fourteen years and seven months. The decision was unconstitutional in the strict sense, because it set aside the age requirement, but it was accepted by almost all political forces, and it shows how widely the monarchy was seen as indispensable. A Liberal ministry was formed, known as the cabinet of the Majority, which lasted only until March 1841, when the emperor replaced it with Conservatives.

Pedro II had been raised in the palace under tutors, with a rigorous education in languages, sciences, history, and law, and he had little experience of the world outside the court. In the early 1840s his advisers, in particular his chamberlain Aureliano de Sousa e Oliveira Coutinho, Viscount of Sepetiba, and the leaders of the Conservative group around the court, known as the Facção Áulica, influenced his decisions. He was crowned on 18 July 1841 in a ceremony intended to present the monarchy as an institution of unity.

The accession shifted the political landscape. The emperor now exercised the Moderating Power himself, and his choices of ministers, senators, and councillors became the most important variable in imperial politics. Because he was young and relied on advisers, the early years of his reign saw continuity with the Regresso, but over time he developed his own approach, emphasizing his role as an arbiter, and cultivating an image of moderation, learning, and dedication to public service.

Section 61.19

The Conservative Restoration and the Liberal Rebellions of 1842

The new emperor and the Conservative cabinet moved quickly to complete the Regresso. The Council of State, abolished in 1834, was restored on 23 November 1841 with twelve ordinary members appointed for life, and it became the principal advisory body to the emperor on legislation, foreign affairs, and the use of the Moderating Power. The Law of 3 December 1841 reformed the Code of Criminal Procedure, creating a system of police chiefs, delegates, and subdelegates appointed by the government, and restricting the powers of elected justices of the peace. These measures gave ministers and presidents tools to influence local administration.

The Liberals, who had supported the majority movement, reacted with alarm. When the Chamber elected in 1840, which had a Liberal majority, was dissolved by the emperor in May 1842, before it had met, on grounds that its election had been tainted by fraud, many Liberals regarded the dissolution as an abuse of the Moderating Power. In May and June 1842 Liberal leaders in São Paulo, notably Rafael Tobias de Aguiar, and in Minas Gerais, among them Teófilo Ottoni and José Feliciano Pinto Coelho, rose in arms against the government. They accused the Conservative cabinet of unconstitutionally controlling the police and the National Guard, and they claimed to defend the constitution and the emperor against a faction.

The rebellions were brief. Government forces under Luís Alves de Lima e Silva, recently promoted for his service in Maranhão, defeated the Paulista rebels at Sorocaba in June and the Mineiro rebels at Santa Luzia in August 1842. Leaders were captured, imprisoned, or exiled, though most were amnestied in 1844. The military outcome was never in doubt: the rebels lacked the support of the planters of the Paraíba Valley and of most of the National Guard, and their revolt had few social roots beyond the provincial Liberal elite.

The revolts of 1842 illustrate the new political style of the monarchy. Liberal rebels used the vocabulary of constitutionalism, the government responded with force and then amnesty, and the participants returned to politics. Unlike the Regency rebellions, the 1842 movements were disputes within the ruling class over the rules of the game, not challenges to the social order. The Liberals' reintegration after 1844 demonstrates that the empire was willing to reward those who accepted its institutions, which became a pattern in later politics.

Section 61.20

Cabinets, Parliament, and the Presidency of the Council of Ministers, 1847

The imperial system of government evolved in the 1840s through practice. The constitution made ministers responsible to the General Assembly, but the emperor appointed and dismissed them freely, and cabinets could not govern without his confidence. In the early 1840s cabinets changed often and were not clearly tied to parliamentary majorities. After the Liberal-leaning cabinet of 1844 to 1848, in which Liberals held office, politicians and the emperor sought a more orderly system for coordinating ministers.

A decree of 20 July 1847 created the office of President of the Council of Ministers, with the first holder, Manuel Alves Branco, a Liberal, appointed in May of that year. The president was chosen by the emperor and served as the head of the cabinet, coordinating ministers and presenting policy to Parliament. The decree did not establish a parliamentary system in which the majority chose the government; it created an office, and the emperor retained the power to appoint and dismiss, to dissolve the Chamber, and to call new elections. The office provided a degree of collective responsibility, but it remained a creation of the Moderating Power.

The system that emerged is often compared with the British model, and sometimes called "parliamentarism in reverse," in which the emperor first chose a cabinet and then elections, managed by the government, produced a Chamber majority. Governments in the empire could almost always obtain majorities, because ministers controlled the police and electoral apparatus, and each new cabinet was followed by new elections that produced a favorable Chamber. This reversed the logic of British practice, where election results determine the cabinet. Brazilians recognized the difference, and Liberal critics argued that the Moderating Power turned the emperor into the real governor of the empire.

Defenders, then and later, stressed the system's flexibility. The emperor could alternate parties in office, bringing Liberals to power when Conservatives lost public confidence, and could dissolve a stalled Chamber, which gave the political class a peaceful means of rotating in office. The cabinet system also gave a role to the Council of State and to the Senate, whose lifetime members often held decisive influence. Brazilian parliamentary practice was therefore a hybrid, a product of the constitution of 1824, the experiments of the Regency, and the emperor's own temperament.

Section 61.21

The Praieira Revolution in Pernambuco, 1848–1850

Pernambuco in the 1840s was the leading province of the Northeast, with Recife its principal commercial center. The province's politics had long been dominated by a few landowning families, notably the Cavalcanti, and the Liberals who rose to power in 1844 broke their dominance. The Liberals took the name Partido da Praia, from the Rua da Praia in Recife, where their newspaper, the Diário Novo, was printed, and their leaders included Antônio Borges da Fonseca, Nunes Machado, and Pedro Ivo Veloso da Silveira. Their program combined demands for political reform with social grievances, particularly resentment of Portuguese-born retailers.

In 1848 the central government, now Conservative, replaced the Praieiro provincial president and the party lost control of local administration, police, and the National Guard. Praieiro leaders responded by taking up arms in November 1848, assembling rebels in Olinda and the countryside. They issued a manifesto, the Manifesto ao Mundo, calling for universal suffrage, freedom of the press, the nationalization of retail commerce, meaning its reservation to Brazilian citizens, an independent judiciary, and the abolition of the Moderating Power. The rebels' ideas were influenced by the European revolutions of 1848, and their language was more openly social and democratic than that of earlier provincial revolts.

The revolt had a social dimension, as artisans, small farmers, and Black and mixed-race workers joined, and a nativist dimension, as crowds attacked Portuguese-born merchants. But it was poorly coordinated. Government forces defended Recife in early 1849, killing Nunes Machado during an assault on the city, and the rebels were pushed back into the interior, where Pedro Ivo conducted guerrilla operations until 1850. Antônio Borges da Fonseca and other leaders were captured and imprisoned. The central government used the army and National Guard to suppress the revolt and later granted an amnesty.

The Praieira was the last major provincial revolt of the empire. It confirmed the failure of armed provincial opposition and reinforced the view among politicians that the way to change was through the political system, not the battlefield. It also left a legacy of demands, for direct elections, press freedom, and a limit on the emperor's role, that would reappear in later Liberal programs and in republican propaganda.

Section 61.22

Political Conciliation and the Consolidation of the Second Reign

By the early 1850s the empire had survived two decades of rebellion, and the Conservative and Liberal parties had developed into stable networks. The political class, bound together by education, kinship, and common interest in order, sought ways to avoid the conflicts of the 1840s. The suppression of the slave trade in 1850 and the economic prosperity of the coffee boom reduced the pressure for social reform and increased government revenue, and the end of the Praieira left no armed opposition.

In September 1853 the emperor appointed Honório Hermeto Carneiro Leão, Marquis of Paraná, a leading Conservative, as president of a cabinet intended to include moderates of both parties. The Conciliation, as this policy was called, brought into office Liberals who accepted the monarchy and Conservatives who accepted a degree of reform. The cabinet carried out measures to increase public works, banking, and railroad construction, and a new electoral law in 1855, the Law of the Circles, established single-member districts, though it was later modified. Paraná died in 1856, and the policy faded within a few years, but it provided a model of consensus politics.

The Conciliation did not widen political participation. The parties were factions of the same elite, and the electorate remained limited by income requirements and by the indirect system. Women, the enslaved, most freed people, and the poor had no vote, and illiterates, the majority of the population, were effectively excluded from public life. The settlement also relied on patronage, with offices distributed to both sides, which strengthened ties between politicians and the state and gave the bureaucracy a stake in the system.

By the mid-1850s the Second Reign had consolidated an institutional order that rested on the emperor's moderating role, the Council of State, an appointed bureaucracy, a hierarchy of police and judges, a cabinet system dependent on imperial confidence, and a political class of slaveholders and merchants that had learned to rotate in office. This order would be challenged after 1870, but it provided the framework in which the economic and social transformations described in Divisions VI and VII took place.

Section 61.23

Pedro II, the Moderating Power, and the Constitutional Monarchy in Practice

Pedro II exercised the Moderating Power throughout his reign with a distinctive style. He read reports, attended to legislation, and met with ministers and councillors, and he cultivated an image of impartiality and learning. He chose senators from triple lists submitted after provincial elections, which meant that the emperor decided which of the three candidates would gain a lifetime seat in the Senate; he selected councillors of state; and he decided when to dismiss ministries and dissolve the Chamber. Over the reign the Chamber was dissolved about a dozen times, and more than thirty cabinets served between 1840 and 1889.

The emperor's power was real but constrained. He needed ministers who could manage Parliament, and his choices had to reflect the balance of the parties, regional interests, and the views of influential senators and councillors. He rarely imposed a policy against the wishes of the political class on major matters, such as the preservation of slavery, but he used his position to influence the timing and direction of reform, such as the suppression of the slave trade and the later emancipation laws. Critics, particularly Liberals and later republicans, accused him of "personal government" and manipulating parties, while defenders emphasized his restraint and independence from faction.

The emperor's role was also symbolic. The monarchy offered a focus of loyalty that spanned regions, and Pedro II presented himself as the embodiment of the nation, patron of science and the arts, and defender of the constitution. His public persona, a modest and studious ruler, differed from the militarized rulers of neighboring states. The court, with its titles of nobility, ceremonies, and patronage, linked provincial elites to Rio de Janeiro, and the emperor conferred more than a thousand noble titles, many on planters and merchants, that cost the treasury nothing and bound the recipients to the dynasty.

The monarchy, however, rested on the personal qualities of one man. The question of succession, with only a daughter, Isabel, as heir, and the emperor's age and health, worried politicians who had no emotional attachment to a future queen. The institution's apparent strength in the middle decades masked a dependence on the individual, an important point for understanding the empire's later vulnerabilities, discussed in Section 61.44.

Section 61.24

Political Parties, Elections, Patronage, and Elite Representation

Elections in the empire were frequent and contested but narrowly based. The system of indirect elections required voters in parish assemblies to choose electors, who then chose deputies. A reform in 1846 set income requirements that allowed a substantial share of free adult men to vote in the first stage, though a small minority of them were electors, and the later Saraiva Law of 1881 would adopt direct elections and exclude illiterates. In practice a small fraction of the population took part, and the enslaved, women, and most foreigners could not.

Elections were also managed. Police officials, National Guard officers, and local bosses mobilized voters, sometimes by persuasion and favor, sometimes by intimidation, violence, fraud, or the use of the capangas, armed retainers, to control polling places. Governments used the appointment of judges, delegates, and Guard commanders to influence results, and electoral violence was common enough that elections became occasions of local conflict. The result was that cabinets could ordinarily obtain the majorities they needed, which contributed to the pattern of managed rotation between Conservative and Liberal governments.

Patronage tied the system together. Politicians controlled the distribution of public offices, from provincial presidents to local tax collectors and schoolteachers, and they gave jobs, commissions in the National Guard, and titles to supporters. Because the private sector offered few careers for educated men, public employment was a vital source of income and status, and young lawyers and graduates looked to politicians for appointments. This made the parties networks of clientele more than programmatic organizations, though they also had distinct tendencies: Conservatives emphasized order, centralization, and the Moderating Power, while Liberals emphasized local autonomy, electoral reform, and limits on the emperor.

The system represented the interests of landowners, merchants, magistrates, and professionals, who dominated Parliament and the Senate. The representation of Afro-Brazilians, Indigenous people, and the poor was almost nonexistent, and women had no formal role. Even so, the political arena allowed for vigorous debate, a lively press, and the voicing of regional concerns, and the political class regarded the empire as a constitutional regime. The tension between the formal liberalism of its institutions and the exclusiveness of its social base was a defining feature of imperial politics.

Section 61.25

Public Finance, Taxation, Customs, and Imperial Administration

The empire's fiscal system depended heavily on customs duties, which supplied well over half of central revenue for much of the century, supplemented by export taxes, stamp duties, and the profits of public monopolies. The commercial treaties of the 1820s, which limited import tariffs to fifteen percent for Britain and other powers, restricted revenue until they expired in 1844, when the Alves Branco tariff raised duties to a range of thirty to sixty percent. The tariff increased revenue and incidentally protected some domestic manufacturing, though its purpose was fiscal and not industrial.

Provinces had their own revenues after 1834, mainly from taxes on local trade and consumption, and their budgets were small compared with the central government's. Provincial assemblies could not tax imports and exports, which meant that the richest sources of revenue stayed in Rio de Janeiro. Provinces such as Pernambuco and Bahia complained that the center took revenue from their trade and spent it in the capital, and the allocation of resources between the center and the provinces remained a point of contention.

The state borrowed heavily. The loans contracted in London in the 1820s created a foreign debt, and the Brazilian treasury used paper money, internal bonds, and later further foreign loans, with the banking house of N. M. Rothschild acting as financial agent from 1855. Deficits appeared in many years, particularly in the 1830s and during the Paraguayan War, but the high value of coffee exports and the steady growth of customs revenue allowed the government to service its debts and maintain its credit abroad. The relatively good reputation of Brazilian finance in London contrasted with the repeated defaults of several Spanish American states.

The administration itself was small. A few thousand officials ran the central ministries, and provincial presidents, judges, customs officers, and tax collectors carried out local tasks, often relying on local elites. The state's capacity to collect taxes, register property, and enforce laws was uneven, and many rural areas saw little of it. The empire's administrative strength lay in a core of trained jurists and officials, many educated at the law schools of São Paulo and Olinda, founded in 1827, who circulated among provinces and positions and shared a common culture.

Section 61.26

Law, Justice, Policing, and Local Government

The legal framework combined colonial and new elements. The Philippine Ordinances of 1603 remained the basis of civil law until the Civil Code of 1916, supplemented by imperial statutes, and the Criminal Code of 1830 and the Commercial Code of 1850 modernized penal and commercial law. The Supreme Court, created in 1828, heard appeals, and judges at the district level were jurists appointed by the government, which gave the executive influence over the judiciary. The criminal code maintained distinct punishments for the enslaved, including flogging, and slaveholders retained wide authority to punish within the household.

Policing and justice after 1841 were run by a hierarchy of appointed officials: provincial presidents, chiefs of police, delegates, and subdelegates, who combined investigative, judicial, and administrative roles. In rural districts these officials were frequently landowners or their allies, who used the office to maintain order and to protect their interests. The National Guard supplemented them, and the army and navy were small, mainly deployed for rebellion and frontier tasks. In the cities, police patrolled the streets with an eye on the enslaved and the poor, enforcing curfews, controlling street trade, and arresting those who violated rules of conduct.

Municipal government was weak. Municipal councils, the câmaras, elected by local voters, had responsibility for roads, markets, and public health but few resources, and after 1828 they were subordinate to provincial presidents. Parish structures, run by the Church, handled baptisms, marriages, and burials, which meant that the state relied on ecclesiastical records for civil registration until the 1870s. Local custom and the personal authority of the landowner often counted for more than the law, which explains the importance of patron-client relations.

The empire's reach was therefore deep in cities and along coasts and rivers, where officials, courts, and police were present, and thin in the interior and in frontier zones. Historians use the term "state capacity" with caution for this period: the empire could suppress revolts and manage elections but struggled to enforce land laws, register the population, or protect the rights of the weak. The gap between formal legal equality for free citizens and the practice of hierarchy was one of the central features of imperial society.

Section 61.27

Church, Education, Public Institutions, and Imperial Legitimacy

The Catholic Church was the established church, and the emperor exercised the padroado, the right of patronage, through which he nominated bishops, approved papal documents through the beneplácito (placet), and paid clergy as state employees. This arrangement gave the state authority over the Church, and it meant that bishops and priests were in many respects functionaries. Many priests served in politics: Feijó was a priest, as were numerous deputies and senators. Religious orders declined in the early nineteenth century, with the government restricting novices, and many convents lost population.

Education was divided between the central government and the provinces. After the Additional Act of 1834, primary and secondary schooling became a provincial responsibility, while the central government controlled higher education in the capital. A law of 1827 had called for primary schools in every town, but implementation was poor, and by 1872 about 85 percent of the population was illiterate. The Colégio Pedro II, founded in Rio in 1837, set a model for elite secondary education, and the law faculties of São Paulo and Olinda (later Recife), the medical schools of Rio and Salvador, and the military and engineering schools produced the educated men who staffed the state.

Other public institutions helped build imperial legitimacy. The Brazilian Historical and Geographic Institute, founded in 1838 under imperial patronage, collected documents, sponsored research, and promoted a national history centered on the monarchy. The Imperial Academy of Fine Arts, the National Museum, the Imperial Observatory, and the Public Library gave the capital a cultural infrastructure, and the emperor visited and funded them. Telegraph lines, introduced in the early 1850s, and later railroads helped connect the capital to the provinces.

The Church and the state were allied, but tension was present. The padroado limited the Church's autonomy, and in the 1860s and 1870s a movement of ultramontane bishops, who favored papal authority, clashed with the government and with Freemasons, culminating in the Religious Question of 1872 to 1875. That conflict lies beyond this chapter, but it indicates that the relationship between throne and altar was less stable than it appeared. Meanwhile, most Brazilians' religious lives took place in popular Catholic practice, lay brotherhoods, and African and Indigenous traditions that sat outside official institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Padroado (Royal Patronage)

The padroado was the right of the Portuguese Crown, inherited by the Brazilian emperor, to nominate bishops and other church officials, to collect and distribute church revenues, and to approve or reject papal documents before they could take effect in the empire, through the beneplácito. It derived from papal grants in the fifteenth century to the Portuguese monarchs, who had financed missionary expansion.

The padroado matters because it made the Church an arm of the state in the empire, with clergy paid by the government and bishops appointed by the emperor. It explains why clergy took part in politics and why conflicts over papal authority became conflicts with the state, as in the Religious Question of the 1870s. It is not equivalent to a modern concordat, which is an agreement between equals; the padroado was a unilateral claim of royal authority within a framework of papal grant.

Division VI

Slavery, Coffee, Land, and Economic Transformation

Brazil's economy and slavery cannot be reduced to coffee exports and slave labor; they require regional, legal, and commercial analysis.

Section 61.28

Regional Economies: Sugar, Cotton, Cattle, Mining, and Domestic Markets

The empire's economy was a set of regional systems with different products, labor forces, and markets. Sugar remained the oldest export, concentrated in the Northeast, in Pernambuco, Bahia, Alagoas, and Sergipe, and in the Campos region of Rio de Janeiro province. Planters operated engenhos, mills integrated with cane fields, and relied on enslaved labor. Sugar prices fluctuated, Cuban competition grew, and by mid-century many northeastern planters faced debt and aging equipment, though the sector continued to produce wealth and to dominate local politics. Steam-powered central mills, the usinas, would transform the industry later in the century.

Cotton had a boom in the late eighteenth century, centered in Maranhão and the northeastern interior, and a second rise during the 1860s, when the American Civil War cut supplies to British mills. Cattle ranching spread across the sertão of the Northeast, the São Francisco valley, Minas Gerais, and Rio Grande do Sul, where charque was produced for slave regions. Mule breeding and the trade of mules from the south to the mining and coffee zones, through the fairs at Sorocaba, tied the south to the center. Cacao grew in southern Bahia, tobacco in the Recôncavo, and yerba mate in the south.

Mining had declined since the late eighteenth century, but not disappeared. Minas Gerais shifted toward agriculture and livestock, supplying Rio de Janeiro with foodstuffs, and British companies such as the St. John d'el Rey Mining Company opened deep gold mines at Morro Velho in the 1830s using enslaved and hired labor. Diamonds were discovered again in Bahia's Chapada Diamantina in the 1840s. The internal market was significant: Rio de Janeiro, Salvador, and Recife consumed large quantities of food and goods, and mule trains and coastal shipping connected regions.

Transport limited development. With few navigable rivers linking producing zones to ports, a poor road network, and mountainous coastal ranges, goods moved slowly by mule or ox cart, which increased costs and favored areas near ports. This pattern gave regional elites distinct interests, and it helps explain why the Brazilian economy could be described as an archipelago of regional economies connected through the capital and the sea. Railroads, discussed in Section 61.33, began to change it.

Section 61.29

The Expansion of Coffee in the Paraíba Valley and São Paulo

Coffee changed the empire. Cultivated in the Rio de Janeiro hinterland from the late eighteenth century, it spread in the 1820s and 1830s across the Paraíba Valley, in the municipalities of Vassouras, Valença, Bananal, and others, on hillsides cleared of forest. By the 1830s coffee was the leading export, accounting for roughly forty percent of export value, and by the 1850s Brazil produced about half of the world's supply, a share it kept and grew in the following decades. By the 1880s coffee made up well over half of export earnings.

The Valley's coffee growers, the fazendeiros, were a new elite. Many were Portuguese-born or sons of colonial merchants and officials who bought land, and others came from the older landowning families. They used enslaved labor, organized in work gangs under overseers, who planted, weeded, harvested, dried, and processed coffee, and they exploited the forest soils with little concern for conservation, leading to erosion and exhaustion within decades. Their wealth appeared in large houses, titles of nobility as "barons of coffee," and political influence in Rio and the province. They relied on commission agents, the comissários, in the capital, who sold the crop, supplied credit, and held mortgages on land and slaves.

From the 1840s coffee moved to western São Paulo, around Campinas and then further inland, where the red soils, known as terra roxa, were more fertile and the terrain flatter. Paulista planters adopted new machinery, expanded with rail connections to the port of Santos, and began to experiment with immigrant labor in the 1840s, such as Senator Vergueiro's partnership system at Ibicaba, which ended in disputes. They remained slaveholders until the 1880s, but they were also more open to alternatives, which would matter for abolition (Chapter 66).

Coffee had political consequences. The growers' interest in order, secure property, cheap credit, and a steady supply of workers shaped the Conservative party and the Regresso, and the Valley's planters supported the central government against rebels in the 1830s and 1840s. Rio de Janeiro's port and the capital's commercial infrastructure grew with the crop, and revenue from the export tax financed the state. The resulting alliance between coffee and the imperial state was one of the foundations of imperial stability.

Section 61.30

Enslaved Labor and the Plantation Economy

Slavery was the empire's labor system and a basis of its social structure. Brazil received roughly 4.8 million enslaved Africans over the course of the Atlantic trade, more than any other region of the Americas, and in the early nineteenth century enslaved people formed a substantial fraction of the population: a third or more in the early 1820s, and about 15 percent in 1872, with the proportion higher in plantation provinces and in cities. In Rio de Janeiro, around two-fifths of the city's residents were enslaved in mid-century, giving it one of the largest urban enslaved populations in the Americas.

On plantations and coffee estates enslaved people worked in gangs, from dawn to after dark, under the supervision of drivers and overseers, with punishment by flogging, stocks, and other methods enforced as a matter of routine. Work regimes differed by crop: sugar harvests required intense labor in mills, coffee demanded year-round tasks and a harvest season, and cattle ranching used both enslaved and free workers. Enslaved people also worked as skilled artisans, carpenters, masons, boatmen, miners, and domestic servants. Women worked in the fields, in households, as wet nurses, and as vendors, and enslaved children began work early.

Urban slavery had its own forms. Owners put enslaved workers out for hire as porters, vendors, and craftsmen under the system of ganho, in which they earned money and paid a fixed daily or weekly sum to their owners, keeping the surplus, and some used it to purchase their freedom. In Salvador and Rio, ganhadores and ganhadeiras worked in streets and markets, often in groups organized by African origin. Domestic slavery was widespread among urban households of modest means, where owning one or two enslaved people was a mark of respectability.

Slavery connected to national growth. Coffee exports financed imports, public revenue, and the expansion of Rio's commercial and financial sector, while the ownership of enslaved people was a major form of wealth, collateral for loans, and a basis of social standing. The institution also shaped culture, language, religion, and cuisine, and its legacy continued after abolition. Historians stress that the expansion of coffee after 1830 required massive imports of Africans and then of enslaved people sold from other regions, which tied the fortunes of planters in the southeast to an expanding system of human trafficking.

Section 61.31

The Atlantic Slave Trade, the 1831 Law, and the 1850 Suppression

Britain pressed Brazil to end the Atlantic slave trade as part of its recognition of independence. A convention of 1826, ratified in 1827, made the trade illegal three years after ratification, which brought the legal end to March 1830, and the Regency government passed the law of 7 November 1831, which declared that Africans landed in Brazil would be free and punished importers. This law was widely ignored. Landowners and officials, including some who held office, took part in or protected the trade, and Africans who arrived after 1831 were treated as slaves and sold, in what became known as the illegal slave trade. The phrase para inglês ver, "for the English to see," is often associated with the law, though the phrase's origin is debated.

The trade boomed. Imports surged in the 1830s and 1840s as coffee expanded, with tens of thousands of Africans landed each year, and the total landed after 1831 was on the order of seven hundred thousand or more. Traffickers operated from Rio de Janeiro, Bahia, and smaller ports, using fast ships, bribed officials, and landing places along the coast, and capital came from Brazilian merchants, Portuguese-born traders, and other investors. Britain responded with the Aberdeen Act of 1845, which claimed the right of British naval courts to try ships suspected of slaving, and its navy seized vessels in Brazilian waters, which provoked indignation and incidents.

The turning point came in 1850. In June a British ship entered the port of Paranaguá, and Brazilians saw the action as a violation of sovereignty. Under pressure from London, from the cost of conflict, and from fears of yellow fever, which arrived in 1849 and was blamed on the trade, the government of the Marquis of Monte Alegre moved. The Law of 4 September 1850, known as the Eusébio de Queirós Law after the Minister of Justice, classified slave trading as piracy, set penalties, and assigned enforcement to the navy and special tribunals. Enforcement was serious this time. Landings dropped sharply after 1850, and the last significant landings occurred around 1856.

The suppression reshaped the slave economy. With external supply cut, prices of enslaved people rose, and an internal slave trade developed, which moved enslaved people from declining northeastern provinces and from cities to the coffee zones of the southeast. Thousands of families were separated by sale. Africans seized from slave ships were declared africanos livres, or free Africans, but were assigned to labor for terms of fourteen years or more for the government or private employers, which left many in conditions close to slavery. The law ended the trade but did not touch the institution itself, so slaveholders adapted to scarcity through sales, natural increase, and coerced labor.

Historical Vocabulary
Africanos Livres (Liberated Africans)

Africanos livres, or "free Africans," were captives found aboard slave ships seized by Brazilian or British authorities after the legal prohibition of the Atlantic slave trade. Under the law of 1831 they were to be freed and re-exported to Africa, but in practice the government assigned them to work for fourteen years, first to the state and later to private employers, under the supervision of the authorities.

The term matters because it shows how legal freedom could coexist with coerced labor. Many africanos livres were employed in public works, arsenals, and private households, and many were kept beyond their terms or enslaved illegally. The category has to be distinguished from freed people (libertos), who had been enslaved and gained freedom by manumission, and from emancipados, a term used in other countries. Historians of Afro-Brazilian life use records of liberated Africans to study African communities and the limits of antislavery enforcement.

Section 61.32

The Land Law of 1850, Property, Immigration, and Colonization

Land was the other fundamental question. Under the colony, land was granted by the Crown as sesmarias, a system suspended in 1822 and not replaced. Between 1822 and 1850, access to land took place mainly through posse, occupation of unclaimed land, and large landowners expanded their holdings by occupying public land, often with enslaved labor, and later legalizing their claims. The absence of a legal framework created disputes, and as coffee expanded and the slave trade came under threat, politicians recognized that land would be a source of value and a tool for organizing labor.

Law No. 601 of 18 September 1850, the Land Law, established purchase as the general rule for acquiring unappropriated public lands, while permitting exceptional free grants along certain international frontiers and the legitimation of qualifying earlier occupations. It recognized cultivated preexisting posses subject to statutory conditions and measurement requirements. The law authorized the establishment of a public land office and directed specified revenues toward surveys and the recruitment of free colonists, predominantly envisaged from Europe. Regulations of 1854 required landholders to register their claims with parish priests, producing a register of landholdings, known as the Vicar's Land Register. The law also reserved land for Indigenous people, though in vague terms.

The effects were uneven. Large landowners, with the knowledge and means to register their claims and, in some cases, to inflate them, gained legal title to land they occupied, and the law reinforced the concentration of property. Poor free farmers, who often lacked knowledge of the rules, literacy, or money for fees, risked losing land. Freed people, fugitive communities, and Indigenous groups, whose possession was by use and custom, had few means to defend their claims, and parish registers in many places recorded the claims of the powerful. The government's survey and sale efforts were limited, and colonization projects remained small until the 1880s.

The law also connected to labor. By restricting access to land, the legislators hoped to ensure that future free laborers, including immigrants and freed people, would have to work for wages, and the revenue from land sales would finance the arrival of immigrants. Immigration began on a small scale, with German colonies in Rio Grande do Sul from 1824 and Santa Catarina, Swiss settlers at Nova Friburgo in 1819, and the Vergueiro partnership system for coffee in São Paulo, but it did not replace slave labor in the period of this chapter. Together with the suppression of the trade, the Land Law shows the empire's elites designing a transition in which property and labor control would remain in their hands.

Section 61.33

Railroads, Banks, Ports, Commercial Capital, and Mauá

The end of the slave trade freed capital that had been invested in importing Africans and drove investment into banking, transport, and manufacturing. The most prominent entrepreneur of the era was Irineu Evangelista de Sousa, born in Rio Grande do Sul in 1813, who became a merchant and then an industrialist, and was titled Baron (1854) and Viscount (1874) of Mauá. He founded the Ponta da Areia shipyard and foundry in Niterói in the 1840s, established the Banco do Brasil in 1851 (later merged into a new Banco do Brasil in 1853), operated steamship lines in the Amazon through the Amazon Navigation Company from 1853, and built the Mauá railway, Brazil's first, inaugurated on 30 April 1854 with a line of about fifteen kilometers from Rio to the foot of the mountains. He installed gas lighting in Rio and promoted telegraph and shipping ventures.

Railroads and steam transport expanded in the following decades. The Dom Pedro II Railway, begun in 1855, linked Rio to the Paraíba Valley, the Recife and São Francisco Railway opened in 1858, and the São Paulo Railway, built by British capital with Mauá's participation, connected Santos and Jundiaí from 1867, opening the route from the coffee plateau to the port. Brazil had fewer than a thousand kilometers of track in 1870 and about nine thousand by 1889. Foreign capital, mostly British, was central, and the government offered guaranteed interest to attract investors. Port improvements, steam navigation, and the telegraph connected the major cities and reduced transport costs.

Banks and commercial institutions grew. The Commercial Code of 1850 set rules for companies, and a series of banks and insurance companies opened in Rio, Salvador, and Recife. The financial crisis of 1857 and later episodes, and the restrictive "law of impediments" of 1860 that limited joint-stock companies, show government caution toward speculative finance. Mauá's career ended in a series of financial reversals, including difficulties after the Paraguayan War and a bankruptcy in 1875 that he later partly overcame, which he blamed on the hostility of the government and the competition of British banks.

These developments mark a degree of economic dynamism, but they also show limits. Manufacturing remained small, with textile and iron enterprises dependent on protective tariffs and credit, and the economy remained oriented to commodity exports and imports of manufactured goods. Mauá's career illustrates both opportunities and obstacles: in a society where wealth was invested in land and slaves, a modernizing capitalist faced hostile institutions, scarce labor, and limited domestic markets. The empire's economic growth was real but concentrated, and it left the structure of slave-based export agriculture intact.

Division VII

Society, Race, Indigenous Peoples, Women, and Culture

Imperial society was hierarchical and complex: enslaved and free people of African descent, Indigenous nations, and women shaped its institutions in ways that official politics concealed.

Section 61.34

Enslaved People's Resistance, Manumission, and Legal Strategies

Enslaved people resisted in many ways. Everyday resistance included work slowdowns, sabotage, feigned illness, theft, and flight, while larger acts included armed rebellion, from the Malê revolt of 1835 to local uprisings on plantations in Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais. Fugitives formed communities called quilombos or mocambos, from small hideouts near cities to larger settlements in the interior, and some, like the community of Cosme Bento in Maranhão, joined broader rebellions. Resistance was constrained by the power of owners, patrols, and the National Guard, and punishment of rebels was severe, as the response to Malê showed.

Manumission was also part of the system. Owners freed enslaved people by letter of freedom, sometimes as reward for service, often conditional on continued work until the owner's death or on payment, and sometimes on their wills. Self-purchase, financed by earnings from ganho, small trading, or savings, was a significant route, especially in cities, where freed people constituted a large share of the population. Women were more likely than men to gain manumission, because they worked in households and had closer ties to owners, and children were sometimes freed at baptism by purchase or by the will of godparents. Freedom was often precarious, since conditional freedom could be revoked and freed people could be reenslaved illegally.

Legal strategies were a distinctive feature. Enslaved people brought freedom suits, ações de liberdade, claiming that they had been illegally enslaved, for example because they were Africans landed after 1831, or because they had an agreement for freedom, or because they could pay their price. They often relied on lawyers, including abolitionist lawyers later in the century, and on networks of free relatives and neighbors. Judges ruled in many cases for the enslaved, though the courts upheld ownership more often than not, and the existence of these suits shows that enslaved people understood and used the law, and that the law offered limited openings.

Family and community mattered. Enslaved people formed families, held religious ceremonies, joined brotherhoods such as those of Our Lady of the Rosary, and maintained African traditions, which blended with Catholic practice. Candomblé in Bahia, Afro-Brazilian religious practice in Rio, and the music and dance of the enslaved were part of these communities. Legislation in 1869 forbade the sale of enslaved husbands from wives and of children under fifteen from mothers, an acknowledgment that slave families existed and a response to abolitionist criticism. Historians such as Sidney Chalhoub and Hebe Mattos have shown that enslaved people's actions shaped the law and the political debate on slavery.

Section 61.35

Free Black and Mixed-Race Brazilians in Imperial Society

A large share of the free population was of African or partly African descent, and in 1872 pardos and Black people were a majority of the free population in many provinces. Free people of color worked as artisans, small farmers, vendors, sailors, soldiers, musicians, and in some cases landowners and slaveholders. They included freed people from slavery and their descendants, as well as people born free of mixed ancestry. Their position differed from that of the enslaved, but it was precarious: they were liable to be taken for fugitives, subject to police harassment, and often forced into dependent labor relationships.

The law recognized free people of African descent as citizens, a fact that distinguished Brazil from slave societies where racial legal bars to citizenship were explicit. The constitution did not impose racial restrictions on voting or office for the free-born and Brazilian-born freed, and some men of color held military commissions, served in the National Guard, and became priests, lawyers, journalists, and legislators. Machado de Assis, born in 1839 to a free mixed-race family and later the greatest novelist of the empire, and André Rebouças, an engineer, are famous examples, but they were exceptions who negotiated a society that valued whiteness. African-born freed people were excluded from citizenship and faced legal restrictions, and in Bahia after 1835 many were deported.

Associations and institutions provided support. Lay brotherhoods of Black Catholics, mutual aid societies, and work groups helped members with burial, credit, and manumission, and newspapers such as Francisco de Paula Brito's publications in Rio, including the 1833 O Homem de Cor, a paper that addressed the concerns of free people of color, raised public issues. Freedom and racial status were distinct dimensions, and a free person of color could face discrimination, while a freed person could become a slaveholder. Distinctions of color, birth, and wealth created gradations within the population, and individuals could sometimes move among categories through marriage, patronage, or occupation.

Political influence was limited. Few men of color met the income requirements to be electors, and the political class was overwhelmingly white, though skin color was fluid and wealth could affect classification. The empire's ideology of a harmonious mixed society, later developed into the idea of racial democracy, coexisted with discrimination, and historians emphasize that free people of color navigated a system in which patronage and dependence on powerful protectors determined opportunities. Their actions, from military service to the press, formed an important part of imperial political culture.

Section 61.36

Indigenous Nations, Missions, Land Conflicts, and Expanding Frontiers

Brazil's Indigenous population was large and diverse but impossible to count reliably in this period: peoples of Tupi-Guarani, Jê, Arawak, Carib, and many other language families lived in forests, savannas, and river basins. The coast and nearby regions had been transformed by colonization, while the Amazon, the interior of Mato Grosso and Goiás, the forests of Espírito Santo and southern Bahia, and the southern highlands held communities that maintained political independence. Indigenous people who lived in settlements near colonial towns, often called caboclos or tapuios in the north, were in contact with colonial society through labor and trade.

Imperial policy combined assimilation and force. The constitution did not address Indigenous peoples, and the government relied on legislation of the colonial period and on regulations such as the Regulation of Missions of 1845, which established a system of aldeamentos, mission villages under the direction of Capuchin missionaries and government directors, to "civilize" Indigenous groups and settle them. Aldeamentos were intended to offer land and protection but often functioned as labor reserves, and the government used them to open frontier regions for settlers. The Land Law recognized Indigenous reserved land in principle, but implementation was weak, and local officials often supported settlers.

Frontier expansion brought conflict. In Espírito Santo, Minas Gerais, and Bahia, war was declared against the Botocudo (Aimoré) in the early nineteenth century, and violence continued in the following decades. In the south, Kaingang and Xokleng resisted the expansion of cattle ranchers and German colonists, and in Mato Grosso and Goiás missions and military posts tried to control the Indigenous peoples. In the Amazon, communities faced forced labor drafts, epidemics, and the disruption of the Cabanagem. Indigenous groups used a range of strategies: warfare, retreat, trade, alliance with settlers or with the government, and claims on authorities.

Indigenous political actors were not passive. Leaders negotiated with officials, sought the protection of missionaries against settlers, joined rebellions as in the Cabanagem and the Balaiada, and used the language of loyalty to the emperor to defend lands. The empire's claims to the territory of Indigenous peoples were far larger than its actual control, and historians stress that Indigenous autonomy remained real across much of the interior well beyond 1870. The pressure of settlement, disease, and policy would intensify later in the century with the rubber boom and the advance of coffee and cattle frontiers.

Section 61.37

Women, Households, Education, and Social Authority

Women's lives in the empire varied sharply by class, race, status, and region. Under the Philippine Ordinances, which governed civil law, married women were subject to their husbands' authority in most legal matters, and a husband administered the couple's property, though a married woman retained rights to her dowry and inheritance. Children had to obtain the father's consent to marry, and the law gave fathers broad authority. Only the Catholic marriage ceremony had civil effect until 1890. Women could not vote or hold public office.

Yet women were economically active and sometimes socially powerful. A substantial minority of households, in some towns approaching a third, were headed by women, often widows, who managed property, farms, and businesses, and urban women ran shops, boardinghouses, and workshops. Elite women administered estates, and in some regions they inherited and owned land and slaves. Poor free women worked as seamstresses, washerwomen, vendors, and farm laborers. Many households combined free and enslaved women's labor, and elite families depended on enslaved women for domestic labor, childcare, and sexual exploitation, which produced a population of mixed ancestry.

Enslaved women faced particular burdens. They worked in fields and houses, nursed owners' children, cooked and cleaned, and bore children who inherited their status. Enslaved and freed women also took leading roles in urban commerce as ganhadeiras, selling food, cloth, and goods in the streets of Salvador and Rio, and some became prominent in religious communities, such as the founders of Candomblé houses. Individual figures such as Anita Garibaldi in the Farroupilha, and the Bahian free African women who appear in Malê records, show women participating in rebellions, though their stories are often recorded by men.

Education for girls was limited. The law of 1827 provided for girls' schools with a narrower curriculum than boys', and few girls attended school. Private teachers and convents educated daughters of the elite, and some women became writers and educators: Nísia Floresta Brasileira Augusta, born in 1810, published an early feminist tract in 1832 and opened a girls' school in Rio in 1838. Women did not enter higher education until a law of 1879 allowed it. In public life women had no formal role, but they intervened as petitioners, as charitable organizers, as patrons of political men, and as authors, and the changing family structures of an urbanizing society began to open spaces that later movements would expand.

Section 61.38

Cities, Journalism, Literature, Science, and Imperial National Identity

Rio de Janeiro, the capital, grew from a colonial port into a metropolis of several hundred thousand people, with a population in 1849 of about two hundred thousand or more, a large share of them enslaved. It had a bustling port, markets, theaters, schools, and the court. Salvador, Recife, Belém, São Luís, Porto Alegre, and São Paulo were smaller but active centers, with commercial districts, churches, and distinctive street life. Cities were places of hierarchy and mixing, where enslaved and free, rich and poor, lived in close proximity, and urban culture blended European, African, and Indigenous elements in music, food, religion, and festival.

Print culture expanded. Newspapers such as the Jornal do Commercio, founded in Rio in 1827, and numerous provincial papers published political commentary, advertisements, and literature, including advertisements for runaway slaves and for sales of the enslaved that document the system. Pamphlets and caricature journals, the latter beginning in the 1830s and 1840s, brought satire into politics. Literacy was limited, but newspapers were read aloud in public places and circulated widely among artisans and shopkeepers.

The imperial government patronized an effort to construct a national culture. The Brazilian Historical and Geographic Institute defined a national history centered on the monarchy and territorial unity, and the court sponsored literature and art. Romanticism, introduced in the 1830s with works such as Gonçalves de Magalhães's Suspiros Poéticos e Saudades (1836), turned to the nation's landscape and its Indigenous past, and its Indianist strand produced Gonçalves Dias's poetry and José de Alencar's novels, O Guarani (1857) and Iracema (1865). The idealization of the Indian as a national symbol coexisted with the dispossession of actual Indigenous communities, and Afro-Brazilians, who formed a large part of the population, were largely absent from official images.

Science and travel writing also fed identity. The Imperial Observatory, the National Museum, and scientific commissions studied natural resources, and foreign artists and naturalists such as Jean-Baptiste Debret, Johann Moritz Rugendas, and the naturalists who followed Pedro II's invitations recorded landscapes and people, including scenes of slavery. The imperial image of a tropical monarchy, civilized and orderly, sought to differentiate Brazil from its republican neighbors, yet it was contested by liberals, republicans, abolitionists, and Black writers and activists who presented other visions of the nation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Markets, and Barracks: Ordinary Life under the Empire

The weight of the empire fell on households in different ways. On coffee estates, enslaved families lived in quarters near the fields and worked in gangs, with small garden plots and limited time of their own, while the masters' households in the big house depended on enslaved domestic workers. In Rio de Janeiro and Salvador, enslaved vendors carried goods on their heads through the streets, calling out their wares, and ganhadores clustered at street corners waiting for work, organized in groups that handled loads and chairs, and paid their owners a fixed sum. Free poor families farmed small plots, raised livestock, and worked seasonally for larger landowners, bound to them by obligations of loyalty and protection.

The state appeared in everyday life through taxation, recruitment, and justice. Taxes on consumption, on slaughtered cattle, and at tollgates and markets fell on poor consumers and small traders, while exemptions and favors protected the well-connected. Military recruitment was a feared burden: officials seized men for the army and navy, often from the poor and those without patrons, while the National Guard demanded unpaid drills. Families sought exemptions through marriage, status as the sole support of a mother, or patronage. Resistance to recruitment, including flight into the forest, was a factor in the Cabanagem and the Balaiada.

Religious and social life revolved around the parish, the brotherhood, and festivals. Baptisms, weddings, and funerals marked family milestones, and brotherhoods provided burial and mutual aid. Markets sold food, textiles, and tools, and women dominated street selling. Education was available mainly to boys of means: most people learned crafts and farming by apprenticeship or from parents. Disease was constant, with smallpox, yellow fever after 1849, and cholera in 1855 afflicting cities, and medical care was limited, so families relied on midwives, healers, and religious remedies. The silences in official records about these lives reflect the sources' emphasis on those who governed, and historians recover them from parish registers, wills, court cases, and travelers' accounts.

Section 61.39

Territorial Integration, the Amazon, and the Imperial Frontier

Brazil's territorial claims were vast, inherited from the colonial period and the principle of uti possidetis, under which possession, not older treaties, defined boundaries. The empire claimed the Amazon basin, the interior of Mato Grosso and Goiás, and extensive southern territories, but it controlled little beyond the coast, the main rivers, and a few interior towns. The Amazon provinces were linked to Rio by sea voyages that could take weeks, and for much of the century their trade ran through Belém to Europe and the United States.

The empire tried to extend its reach through administrative, military, and commercial measures. In 1850 the province of Amazonas was created, separated from Pará, with its capital at Barra, later Manaus, to strengthen control of the upper river. The government established military colonies and forts at strategic points, sponsored steamship service through the Amazon Navigation Company of Mauá, and negotiated boundary treaties with neighbors, including Peru in 1851 and Bolivia in 1867. In December 1866 it opened the Amazon to the merchant ships of all nations, a decision influenced by foreign pressure and the interest of upriver neighbors in river access.

Indigenous peoples and river communities were the effective inhabitants of much of the Amazon. Settlements of caboclos lived by fishing, farming, and gathering forest products, and traders collected cacao, sarsaparilla, turtle oil, and later rubber. The Cabanagem had devastated Pará, and the postwar government imposed labor drafts, for example the Corps of Workers organized in 1838, to rebuild the economy. Indigenous groups such as the Mura, Munduruku, and Mawé fought, traded, and negotiated with the state, and many remained independent of imperial authority.

The distinction between claim and control is central to understanding imperial territorial unity. Brazil kept its extensive territory partly because neighboring states were weak and also had poorly controlled frontiers, and because the empire used diplomacy, a navy, and the language of legal title to defend its claims. But in the interior, imperial authority depended on a few officers, missionaries, and settlers, and on negotiation with Indigenous groups, and the story of unity must be told as one of paper claims that gradually became real as steamboats, telegraphs, and later railroads extended the state's reach.

Section 61.40

Brazil, Uruguay, Argentina, and the Politics of the Río de la Plata

After the loss of the Cisplatine Province, Brazil remained deeply involved in the Río de la Plata. Uruguay, independent from 1828, became a battleground between factions, the Blancos and the Colorados, whose Guerra Grande, from 1839 to 1851, drew in Argentine and Brazilian interests. Juan Manuel de Rosas, the governor of Buenos Aires, supported the Blanco leader Manuel Oribe and sought to restore Argentine authority over the region, closed river navigation to foreign ships, and blockaded Montevideo with Oribe's forces. Brazil considered the independence of Uruguay and free navigation of the rivers Paraná, Paraguay, and Uruguay to be vital, since the western province of Mato Grosso could be reached only by river through Platine waters.

Brazil's response in the late 1840s and early 1850s combined diplomacy and arms. Under Foreign Minister Paulino Soares de Sousa, Viscount of Uruguai, Brazil financed the besieged government in Montevideo, supported the Argentine provincial leader Justo José de Urquiza of Entre Ríos against Rosas, and in 1851 concluded an alliance with Urquiza and the Colorados. Allied forces defeated Oribe, and at the Battle of Caseros on 3 February 1852 Urquiza, with Brazilian troops and naval support, overthrew Rosas. The campaign enhanced Brazilian prestige and lifted blockades on river traffic.

Brazil also secured treaties with Uruguay in October 1851 that defined borders, regulated the return of fugitive slaves, gave Brazil rights of intervention, and recognized its loans, terms that Uruguayans of both parties later resented. Brazilian ranchers held lands in northern Uruguay and used enslaved labor there, which tied Brazilian interests to Uruguay's domestic politics. The regional system remained unstable as Argentina consolidated under Buenos Aires and the Confederation, and as Paraguay under Carlos Antonio López and then his son Francisco Solano López built an army and sought access to the Platine system.

Brazilian policy in the region reflected domestic concerns as well as strategy. Rio Grande do Sul's economy and political elite were connected to Uruguay, and the Farroupilha experience warned the central government of the consequences of a hostile neighbor. Brazil's rulers also saw the empire as a stabilizing monarchy among unstable republics, which they described as barbarism and anarchy, a self-image that justified intervention. Neighboring states saw Brazil as an expansionist empire and distrusted its claims, a perception that would shape the alliances and conflicts of the 1860s.

Section 61.41

Brazil and the Paraguayan War: Imperial Stakes, 1864–1870

The Paraguayan War is narrated in Chapter 64; this section asks what it meant for the empire. Its origins lay in the Platine system: in 1864 Brazil intervened in Uruguay on behalf of the Colorados, who were fighting the Blanco government, after a diplomatic mission under José Antônio Saraiva failed to secure Brazilian demands. Paraguay's president Francisco Solano López, who had been seeking a role in Platine affairs and supported the Blancos, seized the Brazilian steamer Marquês de Olinda in November 1864 and invaded Mato Grosso in December, then Argentine Corrientes in 1865. By the Treaty of the Triple Alliance of 1 May 1865, Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay's Colorado government agreed to fight together.

Brazil had strong reasons to fight. The empire's rulers regarded the invasion of Mato Grosso as an attack on national territory and honor, and they believed Paraguay's army threatened river navigation and Brazil's claims in the Platine region. The Conservative and Liberal political class supported the war, and the emperor, who took the invasion personally, resisted early calls for a compromise. The war also served the interests of Rio Grande do Sul landowners and merchants, who feared a Paraguayan advance, and of the army and navy, which saw an opportunity for prestige and funding.

Mobilization strained the state. Brazil had no large standing army, and the government created the Voluntários da Pátria in January 1865 to enlist volunteers, promising bounties, land, and honors. Voluntarism faded, and the government turned to conscription, the National Guard, and substitutes. Slaveholders sent enslaved men as substitutes, in exchange for compensation and freedom for the men, and the government purchased and freed enslaved people to serve in the army; decrees in late 1866 encouraged this. Estimates of Brazilian troops mobilized run to well over a hundred thousand, and Brazilian dead are usually estimated in the tens of thousands, but figures vary and are subject to dispute.

The war lasted until 1 March 1870, when Solano López was killed at Cerro Corá. For the empire it brought victory and a heavy price. It strengthened the monarchy's prestige abroad and among supporters, but it exposed the strain of mobilizing a slave society for total war, since many soldiers were poor, Black, or enslaved, and it raised questions about slavery and citizenship. The war also contributed to the political disputes and the growth of republican and abolitionist sentiment that mark the final period of the empire.

Section 61.42

The Military, Imperial Finances, and the Political Consequences of War

The Paraguayan War transformed the military. The army expanded dramatically from a relatively small prewar standing force; estimates of the cumulative number of Brazilians mobilized over the entire conflict run into the hundreds of thousands, although no single cumulative total establishes the number serving simultaneously. The armed forces acquired experience in large-scale campaigns, logistics, and engineering. The navy modernized with steam-powered ironclads, purchased abroad and built at home, and won a major victory at Riachuelo in June 1865. Officers trained at the military school and in the field returned with new professional pride, and the National Guard, which had been the main militia, lost influence relative to the regular army.

The cost was high. The war's expense is often estimated as several times the empire's annual revenue, and it was financed by paper money, internal and foreign loans, and new taxes. Government debt rose sharply, inflation affected prices, and the treasury entered the 1870s burdened by deficits. Banks and merchants who had supplied the army and navy profited, while others suffered, and the financial crisis of 1864 and later episodes affected houses such as Mauá's. The costs fell on taxpayers and on the poor who served.

The war also changed relations among soldiers, civilians, and politicians. Veterans, many of them men of color, returned with pensions, grievances, and a sense of entitlement, and officers complained about their treatment by civilian politicians and the wartime conduct of the cabinet. In 1868 the emperor dismissed the Liberal cabinet of Zacarias de Góis, in part because of a dispute with the army commander, the Marquis of Caxias, and appointed Conservatives, an act that Liberals denounced as an abuse of the Moderating Power and that helped to radicalize the opposition. The experience of the war strengthened the army as an institution with its own interests, a fact whose consequences became clear in the 1880s.

The war thus left a double legacy: confidence in the state's capacity to mobilize and win, and a set of strains in its finances, politics, and social order. In the aftermath, reformers pressed for changes in the status of slavery, in the electoral system, and in the relationship between the military and civilian government. Section 61.43

The Free-Womb Law of 1871 and the Beginning of a New Political Era

After the war the question of slavery moved to the center of politics. The United States abolished slavery in 1865, Spain moved toward gradual abolition in Cuba and Puerto Rico, and Brazil became the only major slaveholding society in the Atlantic world besides Cuba. The emperor, who favored gradual emancipation, received petitions from abroad and asked the Council of State to consider reforms. The Conservative cabinet of the Viscount of Rio Branco, José Maria da Silva Paranhos, proposed a law in 1871 that would free children born to enslaved mothers, and after a bitter debate in which many planters opposed any interference with property, it passed. The Princess Isabel signed it on 28 September 1871 as regent while her father was traveling in Europe.

The Law 2040 provided that children born to enslaved women after its enactment would be free, but they would remain under the authority of the mother's owner until the age of eight. At that point the owner could choose between receiving compensation from the state, in the form of a government bond worth six hundred thousand réis, or using the child's labor until the age of twenty-one. Almost all owners chose to keep the children's labor; a later official registration covering 1871–1885 records only 113 children transferred to the state for compensation, out of more than 400,000 registered freeborn children of enslaved mothers. This is a retrospective measure of implementation, not a figure for 1871 itself. The law also created an emancipation fund for purchasing freedom, recognized the enslaved person's right to a personal savings fund (pecúlio) and to purchase freedom at an assessed price, and required a registration of all enslaved people, completed in 1872 and 1873.

The law's significance lay in what it opened and what it left in place. It treated slavery as an institution that could be ended by gradual measures and with state compensation, while preserving the owners' rights over those already enslaved, and it kept free-born children under owners' control for twenty-one years. For this reason historians describe it as a conservative measure that sought to control the pace of emancipation. It nevertheless acknowledged the legitimacy of self-purchase and of the enslaved person's financial independence, and it signaled to enslaved people and abolitionists that the state could intervene.

Politically the law divided the Conservative party, with the planters of the Paraíba Valley opposing it as a betrayal, and it opened a debate that could no longer be closed. The registration and the emancipation fund gave the state new information and tools, and enslaved people used the pecúlio provisions in freedom suits. The law began a period in which slavery was openly debated in Parliament, press, and courts, which Chapter 66 follows through the abolitionist campaigns of the 1880s to the Golden Law of 1888.

Section 61.44

The Empire's Emerging Contradictions after 1870

By the early 1870s the institutions that had stabilized the empire faced new challenges. The Republican Manifesto, published in Rio de Janeiro on 3 December 1870 and followed by the founding of the Republican Party, argued that the monarchy was incompatible with the continental republican environment and called for federalism, representative government, and an end to the Moderating Power. Many early republicans were Liberals frustrated by the dismissal of the Liberal cabinet in 1868 and the managed elections. Republicanism was strongest among urban professionals and among the coffee planters of western São Paulo, who resented the centralized system and the Council of State.

The slavery issue produced tensions within the planter class: coffee planters of the Paraíba Valley, with aging fields and heavy investment in slaves, resisted reforms, while Paulista planters, with younger plantations and railway access, were more open to immigrant labor. Antislavery sentiment grew in cities among journalists, lawyers, students, and some free people of color, who formed societies and used the courts. Enslaved people themselves, through flight and litigation, forced the question onto the agenda. These developments, narrated in Chapter 66, show a society in which the economic and ideological foundations of slavery were eroding.

Relations with the Church and army became strained. The Religious Question of 1872 to 1875, when two bishops challenged the state's authority over Freemasons in religious brotherhoods and were prosecuted, revealed the limits of the padroado and alienated Catholics from the throne. The army, enlarged and professionalized by the Paraguayan War, resented its subordination to civilian politicians and its low status, and younger officers, influenced by positivist ideas, grew critical of the monarchy. These conflicts of the late 1870s and 1880s lie beyond this chapter's boundary, but their roots are in the war's aftermath.

The political system's rigidity added to the strain. Electoral reforms, such as the direct elections of 1881, narrowed the electorate by excluding illiterates, and the managed rotation of cabinets left many provincial and urban interests feeling shut out. The monarchy's dependence on an aging emperor with no male heir worried supporters. None of these contradictions made the fall of the monarchy inevitable, and in the 1870s the empire seemed prosperous and secure, but they limited its ability to adapt, a theme that Chapter 66 develops.

  • Coffee displaced sugar as the leading export and Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo displaced the Northeast as the economic core

What Remained Continuous

  • Slavery remained the basis of labor and wealth
  • Portuguese legal traditions, the Philippine Ordinances, and the padroado persisted
  • Elite landowners, merchants, and magistrates dominated politics
  • Local patronage and personal authority governed rural society
  • Indigenous people and free poor people remained largely outside political representation

Who experienced change most directly: Enslaved people moved across provinces by the internal trade, Indigenous peoples faced pressure on land, and provincial elites gained assemblies and then lost control of justice and police. Who experienced greater continuity: Enslaved people on plantations, rural workers, and women in poor households saw less change in daily conditions than political leaders did. Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows how a state could adopt constitutional forms and national institutions while leaving the social structure largely intact, a pattern with parallels in other postcolonial societies.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How Rebellion, Centralization, Slavery, Coffee, and War Shaped the Imperial State

Long-term causes

Portuguese administrative traditions, the Braganza court in Rio, an economy based on slavery, regional rivalries, and the fear of social revolution after Haiti.

Short-term causes

The abdication of 1831, the Regency's experiments, the rebellions of 1835 to 1845, and the Regresso of 1840 and 1841.

Event or transformation

The consolidation of a centralized constitutional monarchy under Pedro II, with a bureaucratic administration, a cabinet system, and a political class united around order and slavery.

Immediate effects

Suppression of revolts, restoration of the Council of State and police hierarchy, political rotation under the emperor, and the economic boom after 1850.

Long-term consequences

A stable political framework that lasted to 1889, coupled with unresolved questions of slavery, representation, and the army's role, shaping debates in Chapters 64 and 66 and the transition to the republic.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the outcome. Coffee revenue, elite fear of social upheaval, military force, institutional design, and the symbolic role of the monarchy interacted, and each might have produced different results under other conditions.

Why Did the Brazilian Empire Remain United?

Comparison with Spanish America sharpens the question. Mexico (Chapter 59) lost Texas in 1836 and faced the secession of Yucatán and federalist rebellions, while the Federation of Central America dissolved between 1838 and 1840 and Gran Colombia dissolved in 1830 (Chapter 60). In the Río de la Plata, provinces fought civil wars that left Argentina divided into rival camps until the 1850s and 1860s. Brazil, by contrast, lost only the Cisplatine Province, a peripheral and recently annexed territory, and kept the rest of its colonial extent. The comparison must be careful: Brazil also experienced civil war and near-collapse in the 1830s, and the Spanish American republics did not all fragment.

The factors that historians emphasize can be grouped. The monarchy provided a symbol of legitimacy and a dynastic principle that placed the throne above faction, and the child emperor was a rallying point for rebels and loyalists alike. The constitution of 1824 gave a stable frame, and its Moderating Power allowed controlled alternation of parties. A bureaucratic state, staffed by magistrates trained in Coimbra and later São Paulo and Olinda, built a shared culture among elites, a point developed by José Murilo de Carvalho. The Additional Act and the continuing existence of provincial assemblies gave regional elites a stake, as Miriam Dolhnikoff has argued. The National Guard and the navy gave the center the means to respond to revolt, and the political class trusted the system to protect its interests.

Slavery was a binding force. Slaveholders in all provinces shared an interest in preserving the institution, feared that rebellion would unleash slave resistance, and valued a central government that could suppress it. The Malê revolt, the Cabanagem, the Balaiada, and the Sabinada all pushed planters toward the center. Economic factors reinforced the bond: coffee revenue funded the state and tied the southeast to the throne, while the northeastern provinces depended on central credit and markets. Geography also mattered, since the coast-hugging settlement pattern made naval power effective, and the distances among regional centers hampered coordination among rebels. And an emerging Brazilian national identity, promoted by the court and the Historical Institute, offered a narrative of unity.

The conclusion is that unity was historically achieved, maintained, and costly, not inevitable. It rested on coercion against rebels and the enslaved, bargaining with elites, institutional improvisation, and the fortunate coincidence of a powerful export economy. It excluded most of the population from political voice, and it deferred the resolution of slavery. The same factors that preserved the empire created the tensions that would unsettle it after 1870, and understanding that dual legacy is the key to the next stages of the story, the war narrated in Chapter 64 and the end of slavery and monarchy in Chapter 66.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: What Held Brazil Together?

The question: Was Brazil's territorial unity primarily the result of monarchy, institutions, elite negotiation, or coercion?

Interpretation A — A unified elite and a centralized state: José Murilo de Carvalho and others stress that Brazil's elite, mostly law graduates and magistrates trained in a common system, shared interests and a political culture, and that the centralized bureaucracy and the monarchy gave it a framework. On this view the Regresso and the Council of State built a state that stood above regional factions.
Interpretation B — A federal-like pact with provincial elites: Miriam Dolhnikoff argues that the empire preserved unity because it gave provincial elites real power through assemblies, budgets, and local government, and negotiated with them in Parliament. The Additional Act, in this view, created a lasting accommodation, and centralization was less complete than older accounts suggest.
Interpretation C — Slavery, coercion, and class fear: Scholars such as Ilmar Rohloff de Mattos, and social historians of slavery, emphasize that planters, merchants, and officials built a state to defend slavery and property, and that violence against rebels and the enslaved mattered. They stress the role of the National Guard, the army, and the navy, and the fear of social revolution as a source of elite cohesion.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on parliamentary debates, provincial assembly records, the careers and training of officials, legislation, correspondence of political leaders, military records of campaigns, trial records of rebels, and economic data on revenue and trade. They use some of the same sources and differ in emphasis, and many historians combine them.

Why it matters: If unity depended on centralized institutions, other states with weaker bureaucracies might have fragmented for that reason; if it depended on negotiated federalism, the empire's stability reflected regional power; if it depended on coercion and slavery, then stability came at the expense of the excluded majority. The question also shapes how historians compare Brazil with Mexico and the Andean republics.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how Brazil became and remained a constitutional monarchy while much of Spanish America fragmented, and it has argued that this outcome was achieved through a sequence of crises, institutional experiments, and political bargains, at considerable human cost. The Empire proclaimed in 1822 inherited a Braganza dynasty, a Portuguese administrative and legal tradition, a slave-based economy, and the problem of making a single state out of provinces that had been loosely connected under the colony.

The First Reign showed the fragility of that inheritance. Pedro I dissolved the Constituent Assembly in 1823, granted the Constitution of 1824 with its Moderating Power, crushed the Confederation of the Equator, and lost the Cisplatine War and with it the Banda Oriental. The war damaged the treasury, the emperor's reputation, and his coalition. Conflicts with Brazilian-born politicians and journalists, resentment of Portuguese-born elites, and the dynastic question of Portugal produced the crisis of April 1831, when the emperor abdicated rather than face an army and crowd that had turned against him.

The Regency was a serious experiment in governing a monarchy without a monarch. It created the National Guard, reformed criminal procedure, and in the Additional Act of 1834 gave provinces legislative assemblies while abolishing the Council of State. The regents Feijó and Araújo Lima governed amid factional struggle and rebellion, and by the late 1830s a Conservative reaction, the Regresso, led to the Interpretative Law of 1840 and the restoration of central control over justice and police in 1841.

The rebellions of 1835 to 1845 differed widely in their participants and aims. The Malê Revolt was an uprising of African Muslims in Salvador; the Cabanagem was a massive revolt of Indigenous, Afro-descended, and mixed-race people in Pará that provoked a devastating reconquest; the Farroupilha was a ten-year war of cattle ranchers that used enslaved soldiers and ended in a negotiated peace; the Sabinada was an urban movement of professionals and soldiers with a temporary republican program; and the Balaiada was a revolt in which factional elites, rural poor, and fugitive communities converged. None created a common front, and slaveholders rallied to the center when slavery appeared threatened.

Pedro II's accession in 1840 restored a reigning monarch. The Conservative restoration, the suppression of the Liberal revolts of 1842, the creation of the President of the Council of Ministers in 1847, the defeat of the Praieira in 1848 to 1850, and the Conciliation of 1853 established a political system in which the emperor used the Moderating Power to rotate Conservative and Liberal cabinets, elections were managed by the government, and patronage held parties together. The system represented a narrow elite, and the Council of State, the Senate, and an appointed bureaucracy gave it durability.

The economy was reorganized around coffee and the continued use of enslaved labor. The Paraíba Valley and then western São Paulo supplied an increasing share of world coffee, while sugar, cotton, cattle, and mining sustained other regions. The Atlantic slave trade, nominally prohibited in 1831, continued illegally until the Eusébio de Queirós Law of 1850 suppressed it, after which an internal trade moved enslaved people toward the southeast. The Land Law of 1850 reorganized access to land and favored those who could register claims. Railroads, banks, steam navigation, and entrepreneurs such as Mauá expanded infrastructure, with foreign capital, but the economy remained based on commodity exports and slavery.

Imperial society was hierarchical and complex. Enslaved people resisted through flight, revolt, family and religious networks, self-purchase, and freedom suits; free Black and mixed-race Brazilians were citizens who faced racial prejudice and built brotherhoods, a press, and careers in a society that valued whiteness; Indigenous nations defended autonomy against settlement and missionary policy; and women, though excluded from politics, managed households, property, and urban commerce, with a few becoming authors and educators. Cities, newspapers, historical institutes, and Romantic literature constructed an imperial national identity that celebrated an idealized Indian and left out much of the population.

The empire's diplomacy and territorial claims also depended on negotiation and force. The Amazon was claimed in vast terms while the state's effective reach depended on rivers, forts, and steamers, and in the Río de la Plata Brazil intervened to protect navigation and its frontier. The Paraguayan War of 1864 to 1870 brought victory and prestige but also debt, a larger and more professional army, the mobilization of enslaved men, and political strain, setting the stage for the questions of the 1870s.

The Free-Womb Law of 1871 opened a new period by declaring free the children of enslaved mothers, while keeping them under owners' control for years, and it marked the beginning of a state-managed, gradual approach to emancipation. By then republicanism, antislavery sentiment, Church conflict, and military discontent were beginning to challenge the political order. The empire's stability was historically achieved: it combined a symbolic monarchy, constitutional institutions, provincial bargains, military force, slavery, and an export boom, and the same arrangements that sustained it produced the contradictions that Chapters 64 and 66 follow to the abolition of slavery and the end of the monarchy.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Poder Moderador
The fourth constitutional power, held by the emperor under the Constitution of 1824, which allowed him to name senators and ministers, dissolve the Chamber, and suspend judges.
Constitution of 1824
The charter granted by Pedro I on 25 March 1824, in force until 1889, establishing a unitary hereditary monarchy with four powers and indirect, income-based elections.
Regency
The period 1831–1840 in which regents governed in the name of the minor Pedro II, first by three-member regencies and from 1835 by a single elected regent.
Additional Act
The amendment of 12 August 1834 creating provincial legislative assemblies, abolishing the Council of State, and establishing a single regent.
Interpretative Law
The law of 12 May 1840 that narrowed the Additional Act's grant of provincial authority over judicial and police appointments, a central measure of the Regresso.
Regresso
The Conservative reaction of the late 1830s and early 1840s against decentralization, culminating in the restored Council of State and police reform.
Guarda Nacional
The citizen militia created on 18 August 1831 and organized by municipality; its officers became local political bosses, or coronéis.
Coronelismo
The system of local political control by landowning bosses, whose title derived from National Guard commissions, deriving from imperial practices.
Council of State
The advisory body of lifetime councilors created in 1823, abolished in 1834, and restored in 1841.
Juiz de paz
An elected justice of the peace created by the Code of Criminal Procedure of 1832, whose functions were reduced after 1841.
Confederation of the Equator
The republican and federalist revolt of 1824 centered in Pernambuco, suppressed by imperial forces; its intellectual leader, Frei Caneca, was executed in 1825.
Cisplatine War
The war of 1825 to 1828 between Brazil and the United Provinces over the Banda Oriental, ending with the independence of Uruguay.
Malê Revolt
The January 1835 uprising of enslaved and freed African Muslims in Salvador, Bahia, suppressed within hours but followed by severe repression.
Cabanagem
The rebellion in Grão-Pará from 1835 to 1840 of Indigenous, Afro-descended, mixed-race, and dissident elite participants, with very heavy mortality.
Farroupilha Revolution
The ten-year revolt of Rio Grande do Sul landowners from 1835 to 1845, which proclaimed the Riograndense Republic and ended with the Peace of Ponche Verde.
Sabinada
The republican revolt in Salvador from 1837 to 1838, led by Francisco Sabino, intended to last until Pedro II came of age.
Balaiada
The revolt in Maranhão from 1838 to 1841 involving rural poor, factional elites, and a fugitive community led by Cosme Bento.
Majority movement
The 1840 campaign to declare Pedro II of age at fourteen, ending the Regency.
Conciliation
The policy of the 1850s, associated with the Marquis of Paraná, of uniting moderates of both parties in cabinet.
Africanos livres
Africans seized from illegal slave ships after prohibition and assigned to compulsory labor terms of fourteen years or more.
Lei Eusébio de Queirós
Law 581 of 4 September 1850, which treated slave trading as piracy and effectively ended the Atlantic trade to Brazil.
Lei de Terras
Law 601 of 18 September 1850, which generally required purchase of unappropriated public lands but also provided exceptions and ways to recognize qualifying prior occupations, and supported colonization.
Ganho
A system in which enslaved urban workers hired themselves out and paid owners a fixed sum, keeping the surplus.
Quilombo
A community of fugitives from slavery, from small hideouts to larger settlements, and sometimes allied with rebellions.
Aldeamento
A mission village for Indigenous people under imperial policy, intended to settle and assimilate them and often used to open frontier land.
Padroado
The royal right to appoint bishops and approve papal documents, exercised by the emperor.
Parlamentarismo às avessas
"Inverted parliamentarism," a description of the imperial system in which the emperor chose a cabinet and elections produced a Chamber majority for it.
Lei do Ventre Livre
Law 2040 of 28 September 1871, declaring free children born to enslaved mothers, but keeping them with owners until age eight or twenty-one.
Uti possidetis
The principle that boundaries follow actual possession, used in Brazil's frontier diplomacy.
Voluntários da Pátria
The volunteer units created in January 1865 for the Paraguayan War.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Brazilian Empire was neither an accident of geography nor a simple survival of colonial structures. It was constructed through the institutions of 1824, the experiments of the Regency, the violent suppression of rebellion, the political bargains of the Second Reign, and a coffee economy that supplied revenue and anchored an alliance between the throne and the southeastern elite. The monarchy's survival in a region of republics reveals that political form mattered less than the capacity of ruling groups to align their interests and to maintain a framework acceptable to enough of them.

The chapter also shows the cost of that stability. Slavery sat at the center of the empire's economy and shaped its politics, from the silence of the 1824 Constitution to the illegal slave trade, the internal trade, and the cautious emancipation of 1871. Indigenous peoples faced dispossession and forced settlement, free people of color navigated a hierarchy that celebrated whiteness, and women worked in a legal order that denied them political rights. The rebellions of the 1830s and 1840s revealed the grievances of the excluded, and the repression that answered them was part of the foundation of imperial order.

For the larger history of Latin America, Brazil offers a contrast and a connection. Like Mexico and the Andean republics, it faced the problems of sovereignty, representation, regionalism, military power, and fiscal weakness described in Chapter 58, and it answered them with a monarchy that was constitutional in form and personal in practice. Its experience of slavery connects it to Haiti, Cuba, and the Atlantic world, while its Platine and Amazonian policies tie it to its neighbors. The Paraguayan War, discussed in Chapter 64, would test the empire's army and treasury and affect its neighbors profoundly.

Chapter 62 turns to the Southern Cone and Central America, where state formation followed different paths through civil war, caudillos, and federalism, and Chapter 66 returns to Brazil to follow the struggle over abolition and the fall of the monarchy. Students should carry forward the view that the empire's stability was historically made and increasingly contested, and that its internal tensions, slavery above all, were never external to its institutions.
