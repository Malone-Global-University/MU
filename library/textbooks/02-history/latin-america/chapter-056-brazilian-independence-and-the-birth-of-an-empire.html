Brazilian Independence and the Birth of an Empire

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: An Empire Born of a Relocated Monarchy

Brazil became independent without ending monarchy, slavery, or the dynasty that had ruled it. This chapter explains how a colony that hosted the Portuguese court from 1808 to 1821 became a constitutional empire between 1822 and 1825, and why it kept most of its territory while Spanish America fragmented.

The process was neither peaceful nor a single proclamation. It involved a monarchy transplanted across the Atlantic, a liberal revolution in Porto, a parliament in Lisbon, provincial wars, a dissolved assembly, and a rebellion in the northeast. Evidence includes decrees, constitutional proceedings, military records, newspapers, and diplomatic treaties preserved in Brazil's Arquivo Nacional and Senado Federal; later commemorative narratives and paintings must be read as memory rather than testimony. The chapter begins with Napoleon's invasion of Portugal and ends with Portugal's recognition in 1825. Pedro I's later reign, imperial institutions, and abolition are reserved for Chapters 61 and 66.

The chapter also asks how independence looked from below. Enslaved and free Afro-Brazilians, Indigenous communities, and women appear in records of recruitment, honors, and petitions, though rarely in their own words. It uses these traces carefully, and it distinguishes the collective political process of 1821–1824 from the personal story of Pedro told by later monuments.

Comparison with Spanish America, treated in Chapters 52–55, frames the central puzzle: why a colony that experienced real wars and political conflict emerged as one large state under a monarch. The answer lies in a combination of a surviving dynasty, elite fears of social upheaval, institutions built after 1808, and force, not in any one cause.

The chapter's evidence is varied. Decrees, treaties, and constitutional texts preserved in Brazil's Arquivo Nacional and Senado Federal show how institutions changed. Newspapers and pamphlets reveal political argument among literate men. Military records, honors, and petitions reveal the participation of soldiers, women, and people of color, while court inquiries after 1817 and 1824 present rebels through the eyes of prosecutors. Later monuments, paintings, and commemorations must be read as memory, not testimony.

Within Volume III, the chapter follows the Spanish American campaigns of Chapters 52–55 and precedes Central America and the Caribbean in Chapter 57. Its task is limited: to explain the creation of Brazil's constitutional empire. The later reign of Pedro I, the Regency, Pedro II, and abolition belong to Chapters 61 and 66.

Historical Context

Colonial Brazil was governed from Lisbon through captaincies and governors-general, and its economy rested on sugar, mining, and the Atlantic slave trade (Chapters 41 and 44). Reforms under Pombal (Chapter 48) and the Minas conspiracy (Chapter 49) had already tested loyalty. Napoleon's invasion of Iberia (Chapter 51) created the crisis; unlike Spain's king, João's family escaped capture, which preserved a dynastic center.

Colonial Brazil's economy depended on sugar, mining, and enslaved African labor, and its regions looked outward to the Atlantic rather than to one another. Salvador, Recife, Rio, and Belém were ports with their own merchant and planter elites, and provincial identities were strong. That regionalism matters because the empire's unity had to be built after 1822.

Spanish America was in a different position by 1822. Its wars were largely over in Mexico and northern South America, and its leaders were building republics (Chapters 52–55). Brazil watched these events, and its elites weighed them when choosing between monarchy and the risks of revolution.

Division I — The Portuguese Court, 1807–1815

Section 56.1

Napoleon, Portugal, and the Transfer of the Royal Court

Portugal's dilemma in 1807 was that it could not resist Napoleon alone and could not abandon its alliance with Britain without losing its ports and colonies. When Napoleon and Spain agreed at Fontainebleau in October 1807 to partition Portugal, and a French army under General Junot crossed Spain toward Lisbon, the prince regent João, governing for his incapacitated mother Maria I, chose to move the court to Brazil. The idea was not new: Portuguese statesmen had considered transferring the seat of the monarchy to America since the seventeenth century. The decision was a calculated attempt to preserve the dynasty and the empire's wealth, made under British pressure and with British naval protection.

Between 27 and 29 November 1807, as Junot's troops neared Lisbon, a fleet carrying the royal family, ministers, nobles, officials, soldiers, and servants sailed under escort by a British squadron. Estimates of the number of passengers range from roughly ten to fifteen thousand, and the departure was disorderly: many officials and goods were left behind, and Lisbon was occupied days later. Some Portuguese remembered the move as a flight, while others stressed that it kept the sovereign free. The royal party reached Salvador on 22 January 1808 and Rio de Janeiro in March, and for the first time a European monarch governed from the Americas.

The evidence for these events includes diplomatic correspondence, British naval reports, and the memoirs of courtiers, which describe the court's experience and rarely that of the people of Salvador and Rio. Spanish America faced the Napoleonic crisis differently, since the French captured Ferdinand VII and left no sovereign in command (Chapter 51). Portugal's crown escaped, and that difference shaped everything that followed: the monarchy survived as an institution in Brazil, and it provided an alternative center of authority.

Section 56.2

Rio de Janeiro Becomes the Center of the Portuguese Monarchy

Arriving in Rio, the court had to create the apparatus of a capital. In 1808 the regent established ministries, a high court, the Casa de Suplicação, a royal treasury, a board of trade, and a police intendancy. The first Banco do Brasil was founded that year, the Royal Press began printing in May, and the Gazeta do Rio de Janeiro appeared in September. A royal botanical garden, a military academy, medical schools, and, after 1810, a royal library followed. The Correio Braziliense, published in London by Hipólito da Costa, offered a Brazilian-born critic's view free from Portuguese censorship.

The social effects were large and uneven. The city, home to perhaps sixty thousand people in 1808, roughly doubled in the following years as nobles, officials, merchants, and servants arrived. Housing was requisitioned for courtiers by marking houses with the initials of the prince regent, which residents joked meant to put the owner into the street. Royal expenditure, pensions, honors, and offices created networks of patronage that bound Brazilian planters and Portuguese merchants to the crown. Titles and appointments were distributed, and competition between Portuguese-born officials and Brazilian-born landowners began to shape political identities.

Slavery expanded with the capital. Rio's growth depended on enslaved labor, in households, workshops, ports, and markets, and its slave trade with Africa, especially with Angola and Mozambique, grew. Enslaved Africans and their descendants were a large share of the population, and free people of color worked as artisans, soldiers, and small traders. The court's cultural projects relied on this world. Evidence includes travelers' accounts, police records, and customs data, and each reflects the viewpoint of officials and visitors, while the experiences of enslaved people appear mostly in legal and police records.

Section 56.3

Opening the Ports, British Influence, and the Transformation of Atlantic Trade

On 28 January 1808, in Salvador, the prince regent opened Brazilian ports to ships of friendly nations, ending the exclusive trade with Portugal. Advised by Brazilian-born economic thinkers such as José da Silva Lisboa, he set duties on imports and permitted the export of colonial products. In April another decree lifted restrictions on manufacturing. The measures were practical: with Portugal occupied, the crown needed customs revenue, and British ships controlled the Atlantic. They also dismantled the central legal structure of colonial status, since a colony that traded freely with the world was a colony in name only.

In February 1810, Portugal and Britain signed treaties of alliance and of commerce and navigation, negotiated with the British envoy Lord Strangford. They set a preferential tariff of about 15 percent for British goods, lower than the rate for other nations, and gave Britain special judges for its subjects. Britain also secured Portuguese commitments to limit the slave trade, but enforcement was weak. British merchants established themselves in Rio and Salvador, importing textiles and other manufactures and exporting sugar, cotton, and coffee.

Different groups experienced the changes differently. Planters and Rio merchants gained access to markets and credit, and consumers gained cheaper goods. Portuguese merchants lost their monopoly and complained, and artisans and small producers struggled against British imports. Lisbon felt that the colony now dominated the metropole's commerce. These grievances help explain why many supporters of the Porto revolution of 1820 sought to restore Portugal's commercial primacy, and why Brazilians read the Cortes' later decrees as an attack on their gains.

Section 56.4

The United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil, and the Algarves

On 16 December 1815, a royal law declared Brazil a kingdom, united with Portugal and the Algarves as the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil, and the Algarves. Motives included diplomacy, since the Congress of Vienna was reorganizing Europe and a monarch ruling from a colony sat awkwardly among European states, and politics, since Napoleon's fall removed the original reason for the court to stay in Brazil, yet João chose to stay. Maria I died on 20 March 1816, and João became King João VI, though he was acclaimed in Rio only in February 1818. Before 1816 he must be called prince regent.

Elevation changed the legal position of Brazil from colony to kingdom, but its meaning was ambiguous. For Brazilians it created a constitutional right to equal status, so any attempt to restore subordination would be a reversal. For Portuguese, it confirmed their fear of subordination to a colony, especially since the king remained in Rio while Portugal was governed by a British-dominated regency under Marshal Beresford. The reign saw military expansion, with the invasion of the Banda Oriental in 1816–17, as well as high taxes to pay for the court and army.

These tensions erupted in the revolts of 1817 and 1820. The United Kingdom was therefore both an institutional foundation of independence and a source of crisis. It had created a Brazilian political center with its own institutions and elites, yet it had left Portugal feeling reduced, and the contradiction between a European dynasty ruling from America and a European nation seeking its restoration became the central conflict of the next phase.

Division II — Regional Resistance and the Crisis of the United Kingdom, 1817–1821

Section 56.5

The Pernambuco Revolution of 1817 and Republican Opposition

The Pernambuco Revolution began in Recife on 6 March 1817, when the governor, Caetano Pinto de Miranda Montenegro, tried to arrest officers suspected of conspiracy and an army brigadier was killed in the confrontation. Within a day the governor surrendered and a provisional government took power, composed of representatives of agriculture, commerce, the army, the clergy, and the judiciary. It proclaimed a republic, abolished some taxes, and sent emissaries to Buenos Aires, the United States, and Britain seeking recognition. Leaders included the merchant Domingos José Martins and priests trained in Olinda's seminary, where Enlightenment texts circulated, and the movement drew on Masonic-linked networks of officers and professionals.

The causes were layered. Pernambuco's cotton and sugar planters resented taxes that financed a court in Rio, while merchants and officers disliked the favoritism shown to Portuguese-born officials and the concentration of the army's command. A drought and falling cotton prices sharpened tensions. Yet the republic was socially cautious: its leaders needed planter support, and they did not abolish slavery. They urged enslaved people to remain obedient, a contradiction that made the revolt's radical language coexist with the defense of property in people. The republic spread briefly to Paraíba and Rio Grande do Norte, and in Ceará Bárbara de Alencar, a landowner in Crato, took part in proclaiming it, an example of a woman acting as a political leader and later imprisoned for it.

The court responded decisively. A naval blockade of Recife was imposed in April, forces from Bahia advanced overland, and Recife was retaken on 20 May 1817. Courts and military commissions imprisoned many participants and executed others, among them Martins, who was shot in Salvador in July. The repression showed the crown's determination to prevent separation, but it also left a memory of martyrdom that fed Pernambuco's later resistance to Rio, including the Confederation of the Equator of 1824. Evidence for the revolution comes largely from the official inquiries that followed, which describe rebels through the lens of prosecution, and from later memoirs written to celebrate or criticize the movement.

Section 56.6

The Liberal Revolution of Porto, 1820

The Porto Revolution began on 24 August 1820, when army officers and merchants in Portugal's second city formed a provisional junta and demanded a constitution and a Cortes. Its grievances were Portuguese as much as liberal. The king had been absent for twelve years, British officers, notably Marshal Beresford, commanded the Portuguese army, Portuguese commerce had suffered from the end of the colonial monopoly, and the country felt reduced to dependence on Brazil. Lisbon joined in September, and elections were organized for a constituent Cortes that would also decide how the monarchy should be governed.

The news produced a chain of local pronouncements in Brazil. Pará's garrison declared for the constitution in January 1821, Bahia's followed in February, and on 26 February 1821 troops and crowds in Rio's Rossio forced João VI to promise to accept the constitution the Cortes would write. Pedro swore to it on the king's behalf, and ministers considered unpopular were dismissed. Constitutionalism initially united Portuguese-born and Brazilian-born residents against absolutism, and many Brazilians welcomed provincial juntas as a way to reduce the power of captains-general appointed from Rio. The Cortes convened in January 1821, and Brazilian provinces began electing deputies indirectly.

The revolution created a constitutional crisis within the Portuguese monarchy rather than simply resolving the problems of absolutism. The king remained sovereign but needed to bind himself to a document not yet written, the dynasty was divided between Lisbon and Rio, and the Cortes claimed to speak for the whole nation, including Brazil. Whether the Portuguese nation included equal Brazilian partners was the question that would determine whether the Porto revolution became a constitutional reform or a prelude to separation.

Section 56.7

The Lisbon Cortes and the Conflict over Brazilian Sovereignty

The Cortes' treatment of Brazil unfolded through debates and decrees between early 1821 and September 1822. Brazilian deputies arrived in stages through 1821 and were outnumbered by Portuguese colleagues, and they were divided. Antônio Carlos de Andrada of São Paulo, Diogo Antônio Feijó, and Cipriano Barata of Bahia argued for equal representation and, in some proposals, for a form of separate Brazilian administration, while some other deputies accepted closer integration with Lisbon. Proposals for a federal arrangement were rejected by the Portuguese majority, and Brazilian deputies complained that the assembly treated their provinces as dependencies.

The decrees of late September and October 1821 were decisive. They organized provincial governments through elected juntas that reported to Lisbon, placed armed forces under commanders answerable to Lisbon, abolished the central tribunals created in Rio, and ordered Pedro to leave for Europe on a tour of study. Portuguese liberals defended these measures as constitutional uniformity and as the end of an exile-era arrangement; some also argued that the 1815 elevation had been an act of royal grace rather than a permanent right. Brazilians, especially in Rio, São Paulo, and Minas Gerais, read them as recolonization, since the decrees removed the institutions that had made Rio a capital and restored Lisbon's control over trade and appointments.

The political reality was more complicated than a simple Portuguese-Brazilian division. Several provinces, including Bahia, Pará, and Maranhão, supported Lisbon, partly because elected juntas gave local elites autonomy from Rio, and commercial ties to Portugal remained important. Others, like Pernambuco, balanced between Lisbon and Rio. The dispute therefore reveals competing conceptions of sovereignty: a single Portuguese nation with equal citizens, a dual monarchy centered in Rio, and provincial autonomy. Historians have argued over whether the Cortes' program was a sincere constitutional experiment or a determined restoration of Portuguese primacy, and they distinguish its stated aims from the Brazilian perception, which helped create the movement it feared.

Cortes

Section 56.8

João VI's Return to Portugal and Pedro's Regency in Brazil

Facing pressure from the Cortes and from the Lisbon garrison, João VI decided to return to Portugal. On 21 April 1821 an assembly of electors that met in Rio's Praça do Comércio to select a regency and discuss the constitution was dispersed by troops, and several people were reportedly killed, an episode that shocked Rio and hastened his departure. On 22 April the king named Pedro regent, and on 26 April João VI sailed with several thousand Portuguese, taking with him substantial funds from the treasury and the Banco do Brasil, whose reserves were drained and whose later insolvency worsened government finances.

Pedro, twenty-two, inherited a government with a depleted treasury, Portuguese troops under General Jorge de Avilez who were loyal to Lisbon, and provinces whose juntas took orders from Lisbon, Rio, or neither. Cisplatina had been incorporated in 1821 into the United Kingdom, and its political status was unsettled. The regent was instructed to govern under the Cortes' authority, and his powers were narrowed by the decrees of September and October. A traditional story says that João told his son to take the crown himself if Brazil moved to separate; the story is repeated in later memoirs but cannot be verified from contemporary evidence.

The return of the king ended the unusual period in which the monarchy was governed from America. It also forced Brazilian elites to decide where their loyalties lay. Rio's merchants and officeholders, whose position depended on the court, had the most to lose, while provinces such as Bahia and Pará looked to Lisbon. Pedro's position was ambiguous, a Portuguese prince serving as regent in Brazil, bound by obligations to the Cortes but pressed by Brazilian petitioners to resist. That ambiguity set the stage for the events of January 1822.

Division III — The Political Break with Portugal, 1821–1822

Section 56.9

The Dia do Fico and Pedro's Refusal to Return to Portugal

The Cortes decrees ordering Pedro's return reached Rio in December 1821 and produced an organized campaign to keep him. Rio's municipal chamber, led by José Clemente Pereira, gathered thousands of signatures on a representation, while letters from São Paulo, drafted with José Bonifácio's involvement, and from Minas Gerais urged him to stay and warned that obeying the decrees would dismantle the institutions of the kingdom. On 9 January 1822 Pedro received the representation and announced that he would remain. The decision, remembered as the Dia do Fico, was a refusal to obey a specific order of the Cortes, though it was presented as service to the nation and to the constitutional monarchy rather than as a break.

The consequences were immediate. The Portuguese division in Rio under General Jorge de Avilez mutinied against the decision, and Pedro summoned Brazilian militia and regular units and the city's population, who assembled in large numbers. Avilez's troops were forced to retreat across the bay to Niterói and were eventually shipped to Portugal in February. On 16 January Pedro appointed a ministry led by Bonifácio, the first government composed of Brazilians, and it began to issue decrees that asserted the regent's authority over the provinces. The Fico therefore changed the balance of force in Rio, but independence remained undeclared, and many provinces continued to obey Lisbon.

Section 56.10

José Bonifácio, Leopoldina, Political Factions, and the Independence Movement

José Bonifácio de Andrada e Silva, born in Santos, had spent decades in Portugal as a mineralogist and public official before returning to Brazil in 1819. He favored a strong monarchy that would preserve territorial unity and avoid social upheaval, and he distrusted both democratic radicalism and Lisbon's authority. Joaquim Gonçalves Ledo, with the journalist Januário da Cunha Barbosa, led a more radical current associated with Masonic lodges, which wanted broader representation and constraints on the monarch. José Clemente Pereira, president of Rio's chamber, mobilized municipal support. Rivalry among these groups shaped the process: after independence Bonifácio's faction prosecuted its Masonic rivals.

Princess Maria Leopoldina, an Austrian archduchess who had married Pedro in 1817 and had studied natural sciences, was a political participant. Her correspondence shows that she followed events closely and urged Pedro to lead Brazil. On 2 September 1822, while Pedro was in São Paulo, she presided over the Council of State, which decided to send him letters urging immediate separation. Her role is documented in surviving letters and council records, though later narratives often reduced her to the wife of the emperor. A new institution, the Council of Attorneys-General of the Provinces, created in February 1822, gave provincial representatives a voice, but not all provinces sent delegates.

Section 56.11

Press, Provincial Governments, and Competing Visions of Independence

The end of prior censorship in 1821 produced a lively press. In Rio the Revérbero Constitucional Fluminense, edited by Ledo and Barbosa, advocated autonomy, while other papers supported Lisbon or moderation. Newspapers in Bahia and Pernambuco argued over loyalty to the Cortes and the legitimacy of Rio. Pamphlets and political songs circulated among literate men and were read aloud to wider audiences. The press shows that independence was debated, not simply proclaimed, though readers were a small minority in a population in which most were illiterate or enslaved.

Provincial politics varied. Pernambuco's junta, formed in Goiana in late 1821, was elected locally and looked ambiguously to Rio and Lisbon. Bahia, Maranhão, and Pará remained loyal to the Cortes, supported by Portuguese garrisons and merchants. Pedro travelled to Minas Gerais in March and April 1822 to secure support there. On 4 May 1822 he decreed that Lisbon's orders required his approval, the Cumpra-se, and on 13 May, at the chamber's proposal, accepted the title Perpetual Defender of Brazil, deliberately avoiding any claim to a crown. These measures asserted national authority while formally preserving the union.

Section 56.12

The Escalating Constitutional Crisis and the Road to September 1822

On 3 June 1822, responding to a request from Rio's chamber and the attorneys-general, Pedro summoned a Brazilian constituent and legislative assembly, an act that asserted that Brazilians could write their own constitution. On 1 August he decreed that Portuguese troops arriving without his consent were to be treated as enemies, and manifestos drafted by Bonifácio and Ledo, addressed to Brazilians and to friendly nations, presented Brazil as justified in defending its rights. These documents were political arguments, intended to win domestic and foreign audiences, and they stopped short of using the language of republican revolution.

Meanwhile the Cortes hardened. Decrees reaching Rio in late August annulled Pedro's acts, called for the prosecution of his ministers, and renewed demands for his return. Pedro was then in São Paulo, where he had gone to settle local disputes and shore up support. Couriers carried news from Rio, together with advice from Bonifácio and Leopoldina, to him on the road. Contemporary accounts of the sequence differ on details and were shaped by later commemoration, but they agree that the Lisbon decrees and the council's urging coincided with Pedro's decision.

Historical paraphrase, not a verbatim reconstruction: Correspondents warned that the Cortes' new orders threatened Pedro's position and Brazil's political autonomy, and urged him to act without further delay. Accounts of the meeting held under Leopoldina on 2 September are important evidence of political coordination, but do not supply a reliable transcript of what Pedro said at Ipiranga on 7 September.

Analysis: These messages make the break appear as a decision informed by ministers and the princess, not an isolated spontaneous gesture. However, some familiar dramatic formulations entered later patriotic storytelling. Students should compare the surviving correspondence, dated reports, and later memoirs, noting that letters from different days should not be presented as a single document.

Section 56.13

September 7, 1822: Independence, Acclamation, and Imperial Coronation

On 7 September 1822, as Pedro returned from Santos toward São Paulo, he received the courier with the letters near the Ipiranga stream and declared Brazil independent. Participants' later accounts differ on his words and gestures, and the famous cry of 'Independence or Death' and the painting by Pedro Américo, completed in 1888, belong to the work of later memory, which shaped the scene as a founding moment. The evidence supports a decisive political act on the road, not a staged ceremony.

Independence was followed by distinct steps. Pedro returned to Rio in mid-September, and on 12 October, his twenty-fourth birthday, he was acclaimed Constitutional Emperor and Perpetual Defender of Brazil at the Campo de Santana before crowds, with municipal authorities and military officers participating. On 1 December he was crowned in a religious ceremony. These steps gave the new regime legitimacy through acclamation, which implied popular consent, and through coronation, which implied dynastic and religious continuity. They must not be conflated with the declaration itself.

The commemorative tradition credits Pedro alone, but the process involved ministers, municipal chambers, provincial elites, and Leopoldina, and independence did not exist in much of Brazil on 7 September. Bahia, Maranhão, Pará, and Cisplatina were controlled by Portuguese forces or divided, and wars would be needed to establish authority. In Rio, factional conflict immediately followed the acclamation, with Bonifácio's group prosecuting rivals. The September act was thus the beginning, not the end, of independence.

Division IV — The Wars of Brazilian Independence, 1822–1823

Section 56.14

The War of Independence in Bahia

Bahia was the first province where independence was decided by prolonged fighting. Salvador, a major port and former colonial capital, was garrisoned by Portuguese troops under Inácio Luís Madeira de Melo, whom Lisbon had appointed military commander. In February 1822 Pedro named a Brazilian-born commander, and Madeira refused to yield. Fighting broke out in the city on 19 February, and the next day Portuguese soldiers entered the Lapa convent, where Sister Joana Angélica was killed resisting them; her death became a nationalist symbol. Many Brazilians fled Salvador and organized in the Recôncavo, the sugar and tobacco region around the bay.

Cachoeira, a town in the Recôncavo, acclaimed Pedro on 25 June 1822 and became the center of Brazilian resistance, where a provisional council coordinated militias, planters' retainers, and volunteers. Pedro sent a French officer, Pierre Labatut, who led the Pacifying Army from late 1822, and a battle at Pirajá on 8 November 1822 checked the Portuguese. Troops and volunteers also came from Sergipe, Pernambuco, and Rio. The siege shut off Salvador's supply by land. Labatut was arrested by his own officers in 1823 and replaced by José Joaquim de Lima e Silva, an indication of tensions within the army.

Naval power broke the stalemate. Thomas Cochrane's squadron reached Bahia in April 1823, fought an inconclusive engagement on 4 May, and then blockaded the port, cutting seaborne supply to Madeira's forces. Facing hunger and disease, the Portuguese commander evacuated Salvador on 2 July 1823 with troops and many Portuguese civilians aboard dozens of ships, and the Brazilian army entered the city. Bahia's population was divided: Portuguese merchants and officials, many poorer residents, and some Brazilians favored Lisbon, and the evacuation was a defeat for them. The date remains Bahia's major civic holiday, celebrated with processions that feature a caboclo, an Indigenous-associated symbol of the resistance.

Section 56.15

Maranhão, Piauí, Pará, and the Struggle for Northern Brazil

The northern provinces had commercial and administrative links to Lisbon that differed from Rio's, and they were distant from the court. In Piauí, the Portuguese governor of arms, João José da Cunha Fidié, held Oeiras, but Parnaíba on the coast proclaimed for Pedro in October 1822. Fidié marched against it, and on 13 March 1823 his troops met a larger and poorly armed force, including cattle herders and local volunteers, at Jenipapo near Campo Maior. Fidié won the battle at heavy cost to the Brazilians, but he lost time and strength and withdrew into Maranhão, where he was besieged at Caxias by forces from Piauí, Ceará, and Maranhão's interior and surrendered in the summer of 1823.

São Luís, Maranhão's capital, was dominated by Portuguese merchants and officials. It adhered to the empire on 28 July 1823, only after Cochrane's squadron appeared and the city feared blockade and the arrival of rebel forces. Cochrane's methods included bluff and rapid threats, and his actions there were later disputed. In Pará, a naval officer under Cochrane, John Pascoe Grenfell, arrived at Belém in August, and the province adhered on 15 August. In October, Grenfell had hundreds of prisoners confined in the hold of the brig Palhaço after local unrest, and most suffocated, an atrocity that shows the repression accompanying integration.

These events show that independence in the north was achieved by a combination of naval pressure, regional rebellion, and negotiation, and that adherence did not mean consent. The grievances of Pará and Maranhão, including resentment of Rio's authority, Portuguese merchants' loss of position, and the repression of 1823, fed later conflicts. The Cabanagem rebellion in Pará, which began in the 1830s, belongs to a later chapter, but historians connect it to these unresolved tensions.

Section 56.16

Naval Power, Cochrane, and the Military Consolidation of Independence

Brazil had no navy of its own in 1822. The government bought and refitted ships, including some from the Portuguese fleet, and hired foreign officers and sailors, many of them British and French. Thomas Cochrane, a Scottish officer who had commanded the Chilean and Peruvian squadrons (Chapter 54), arrived in Rio in March 1823 and was commissioned as admiral. His squadron sailed for Bahia and, after the blockade there, pursued the Portuguese convoy toward the Atlantic and then sailed north to Maranhão and Pará.

The fleet's crews were mixed. Foreign officers served alongside Brazilian-born sailors, and Afro-Brazilian seamen, both free and enslaved, formed a significant portion of the crews, though records rarely name them. Cochrane's relationship with the government was difficult. He demanded high pay and prize money, and disputes over captured ships were prolonged. Naval power was decisive because the Portuguese depended on supply by sea, but it was not sufficient. On land, local forces and the siege, as well as political defection in the provinces, were essential, and the navy's costs added to the new empire's debt.

Section 56.17

Popular Participation, Slavery, Women, and the Social Costs of War

Independence was fought with a mixed society. Brazilian forces included regular units, militias, volunteers, and irregulars, among them free people of color, Indigenous recruits, and enslaved men. In Bahia, where the armed forces had long included Black and mixed-race battalions, historians such as Hendrik Kraay have shown that men of color were central to the war and that independence affected their position in military and civic life. Recruitment of enslaved men was controversial because planters feared losing labor and wanted to prevent any link between military service and freedom, and the government's policies were inconsistent.

Women took part in many roles. Maria Quitéria de Jesus, from the Cachoeira region, disguised herself as a man to join a Bahian battalion, was discovered, and continued to serve. Pedro later awarded her the Order of the Cruzeiro, a recognition documented in imperial records. Sister Joana Angélica's death was commemorated, and women of many classes provisioned troops, nursed the wounded, and moved information. Other figures, such as Maria Felipa de Oliveira, are remembered in Bahian tradition as leading women on the island of Itaparica against Portuguese ships. Their historicity rests largely on oral memory and later accounts, which historians treat with caution.

The costs of war fell unevenly. Requisitions of food and animals, forced recruitment, and displacement burdened the Recôncavo and the north, while Salvador's residents faced shortages during the siege. Planters feared both Portuguese and popular mobilization, and the war reinforced their desire to avoid any threat to slavery. Independence did not produce emancipation, and for enslaved people the fighting could bring both opportunity and increased control. These social costs are reflected in military records, petitions, and memoirs, which are written mostly by officers and officials, so the experiences of ordinary participants are reconstructed indirectly.

Division V — Constitutional Monarchy and the Limits of Independence, 1823–1824

Section 56.18

The Constituent Assembly of 1823 and Competing Constitutional Projects

The Constituent and Legislative Assembly opened in Rio on 3 May 1823, with deputies chosen through indirect elections in provinces that had adhered to the empire, which meant that Bahia, Maranhão, and Pará were represented only later or not at all at the start. Pedro's opening speech stated that he expected a constitution worthy of Brazil and of himself, language that members read as a warning that the emperor expected a strong executive. The deputies, mostly lawyers, magistrates, clergy, and landowners, were not organized in modern parties but in shifting groups defined by loyalty to the Andrada brothers, to the emperor, or to provincial interests.

Competing constitutional projects reflected competing views of the monarchy. The draft associated with Antônio Carlos de Andrada gave the legislature strong powers, limited the emperor to a suspensive veto, and restricted his control over the armed forces. It also tied political rights to income measured in manioc flour, which led critics to call it the manioc constitution. Others feared that the draft would reduce the emperor to a figurehead or favor Brazilian-born over Portuguese-born residents, while some provincial deputies wanted greater local autonomy. These disagreements were about how far independence should limit the crown, not about whether Brazil should have one.

Conflict deepened outside the Assembly. Bonifácio left the ministry in July 1823 after court intrigues and moved into opposition, and the Andradas published newspapers attacking the emperor's circle and Portuguese-born officers. Many Portuguese-born merchants and officers, who remained influential in the army and court, felt targeted by radical nationalism. The Assembly's debates were conducted before a politicized public, and anti-Portuguese feeling in Rio increased. By autumn, the Assembly and the emperor were in open conflict over the army, the veto, and the place of Portuguese-born Brazilians.

Section 56.19

The Dissolution of the Assembly and the Night of Agony

The crisis came in November 1823. After a pharmacist, David Pamplona, was assaulted by two Portuguese-born army officers, the Assembly declared itself in permanent session on 11 November and demanded action. Pedro responded by surrounding the Assembly building with troops and, on 12 November, dissolving the body, which he accused of threatening the emperor and the nation. The night of 11–12 November, when deputies waited under military guard, is remembered as the Night of Agony. The Andrada brothers and other deputies were arrested and deported to Europe; Bonifácio remained in exile until 1829.

The dissolution was a coup against a representative body, carried out by the emperor with the army's support, but he promised to give Brazil a constitution more liberal than the Assembly's draft would have been. He created a Council of State of ten members to prepare it. Many drafters had served in the Assembly, and the text drew on its work. The episode shows how Brazilian independence produced a constitutional crisis: the emperor accepted a constitution in principle but insisted on his authority to define it, and the army's loyalty was decisive. Reactions varied: municipal chambers largely accepted the decision, while radicals in the north saw it as proof of despotism.

Section 56.20

The Constitution of 1824 and the Moderating Power

On 25 March 1824 Pedro swore to a constitution that he had granted, a document submitted for approval to municipal chambers, many of which accepted it. It established a unitary state with a hereditary monarchy and a General Assembly consisting of a Chamber of Deputies elected for four years and a Senate with lifetime members chosen by the emperor from lists of candidates elected in the provinces. Provincial presidents were appointed by the emperor, and provincial councils had limited advisory powers. The Catholic Church remained the state religion, and other faiths were permitted private worship. A Council of State advised the emperor.

The most distinctive feature was the Moderating Power, set out in Articles 98 to 101. The charter named four powers, legislative, moderating, executive, and judicial, and gave the emperor the Moderating Power as the keystone of the system, allowing him to appoint senators, convene or dissolve the Chamber of Deputies, sanction or veto laws, and appoint and dismiss ministers. The person of the emperor was declared inviolable and not subject to responsibility. The idea drew on the French theorist Benjamin Constant's notion of a neutral royal power, but in Brazil it gave the emperor extensive control.

Historians disagree about the charter. Some emphasize its authoritarian character, since it was imposed after the Assembly's dissolution and gave the crown dominance over the legislature. Others stress that it created representative institutions, an independent judiciary, guaranteed rights, and a flexible structure that lasted until 1889, the longest-lived constitution in Brazilian history. Both views have support: the charter combined liberal guarantees with centralized authority, and its practice varied with the emperor and the political context, which Chapter 61 examines.

Section 56.21

Citizenship, Representation, Religion, and the Persistence of Slavery

The constitution defined citizenship, but not equality. Article 6 recognized as citizens those born in Brazil, whether free from birth or freed, and certain Portuguese-born residents who accepted independence. Enslaved people were not citizens, and the constitution did not name slavery as an institution while guaranteeing property rights that protected it. Africans who were freed were not citizens by birth, which created a separate and more vulnerable legal position. The charter was silent on Indigenous peoples, who remained subject to local arrangements and tutelage.

Political participation was further restricted. In the first stage, citizens with a minimum income could vote in parish assemblies, a threshold that left a relatively large electorate by contemporary standards but excluded the poor, servants, and many others. Under Articles 90 to 95, these voters chose electors, who needed higher incomes and, as the charter specified, could not be freed people. Brazilian-born freed citizens who met the income requirement could therefore vote in primary assemblies, but were barred from serving as electors. Higher income requirements applied to deputies and senators, and non-Catholics could not hold certain offices.

These provisions reveal the contradiction at the heart of the new empire. The charter used the language of liberal constitutionalism while preserving the world's largest slave society. Free people of color, who were numerous in Bahia, Pernambuco, and Rio, held rights that were real but contingent, and the boundary between free and enslaved status was affected by local reputation, patronage, and the risk of enslavement. Slaveholders supported the monarchy as a guarantor of order, and British pressure to end the slave trade, which the empire agreed to in 1826, was resented. Slavery's persistence shaped the entire constitutional structure.

Section 56.22

The Confederation of the Equator and Resistance to Imperial Centralization

The Confederation of the Equator began as a challenge to Pedro's imposition of the constitution and his appointment of provincial presidents. In Pernambuco, the emperor replaced a locally supported president with an unpopular appointee, and local leaders rejected the change. On 2 July 1824 Manuel de Carvalho Paes de Andrade proclaimed the Confederation of the Equator. Frei Joaquim do Amor Divino Caneca, a Carmelite friar who had been imprisoned after 1817, supported it through his newspaper, Typhis Pernambucano, arguing that the constitution had been granted without the people's consent. The movement's leaders offered a provisional adoption of the Colombian constitution and discussed a republic, but they did not agree on a program.

Participation across the northeast was uneven. Pernambuco was the center; Ceará joined most fully, led by figures such as Tristão Gonçalves, while Paraíba and Rio Grande do Norte participated only partly and briefly. Other provinces, including Bahia, remained loyal to Rio. The movement drew on artisans, militia, and soldiers as well as planters and professionals, and its leaders hesitated over slavery, as in 1817, fearing to alienate slaveholders. The social base was therefore broad but unstable, and it lacked support from the great planters, who feared disorder.

The empire responded by force. A naval blockade under Cochrane and an army under Francisco de Lima e Silva reached Recife, and the city fell on 17 September 1824. Carvalho fled aboard a British vessel, and the Ceará movement was suppressed in the following weeks. Caneca was captured, tried by a military commission, and executed by firing squad on 13 January 1825, since local executioners reportedly refused to hang him. The repression ended the immediate threat, but the conflict showed that the compromise of 1824 was contested. Questions of centralization, local autonomy, and the nature of the monarchy remained unresolved and returned in later revolts.

Division VI — Recognition, Territorial Continuity, and the New Empire, 1824–1825

Section 56.23

Foreign Recognition, British Diplomacy, and Portugal's Recognition of Independence

Independence required recognition, and recognition came in stages. The United States was the first state outside the Americas' newly independent republics to take a formal step, receiving a Brazilian envoy and recognizing the empire in May 1824; its motives included commerce and the desire to separate the Americas from European influence, though the monarchy did not fit the republican model. Britain, whose trade and finance were deeply involved in Brazil, waited. George Canning's government wanted a settlement between Lisbon and Rio that would secure British commercial privileges, avoid a prolonged war, and lead to commitments to end the slave trade. Sir Charles Stuart, a British diplomat, mediated.

The settlement was signed on 29 August 1825 as a treaty of peace, friendship and alliance between Portugal and Brazil, in which Portugal recognized Brazil's independence. João VI was granted the honorary title of emperor, Pedro was acknowledged as the sovereign of the new empire and as heir to his father, and Brazil agreed not to accept the union of Portuguese colonies. A separate additional convention of the same date set Brazil's financial obligations, fixing a total indemnity of £2 million, to be met partly by Brazil assuming a Portuguese loan contracted in London in 1823 and partly by paying the balance. The financial terms were controversial, since they made Brazil pay for its independence and increased its debt to British lenders.

Britain then moved to formalize its own relations. The Anglo-Brazilian convention of 23 November 1826 committed Brazil to end the transatlantic slave trade within three years of ratification; this undertaking was widely resisted and proved difficult to enforce. A separate treaty of friendship, navigation, and commerce with Britain was signed on 17 August 1827. These later agreements must not be conflated with Portugal’s recognition of Brazilian independence in 1825. Other European states recognized Brazil in the following months, and the empire's diplomatic position was established. Recognition shows the importance of international politics: Britain's leverage, Portugal's reluctant acceptance, and the cost to Brazil were all part of the independence settlement. Pedro's claim to the Portuguese throne after João VI's death in 1826 would soon complicate his position, a subject for later chapters.

Section 56.24

Territorial Continuity, Provincial Integration, and the Limits of Imperial Sovereignty

By 1825 the new empire claimed almost all of Portuguese America, from the Amazon to the southern frontier. This territorial continuity was neither natural nor assured. In 1822 Pedro's authority extended mainly over Rio, São Paulo, Minas Gerais, and parts of the south, and the northern provinces and Cisplatina were held by Portuguese forces or divided. The government extended control through military action, naval pressure, negotiation with local elites, and the creation of institutions such as provincial presidencies appointed from Rio.

The southern frontier shows the limits. Cisplatina, the former Banda Oriental of the Río de la Plata that Portuguese forces had incorporated in 1821, was divided between supporters of Rio and those of Lisbon, and a siege of Montevideo ended in late 1823 when the Portuguese garrison left. Brazil held the province, but it remained restive and was claimed by Buenos Aires, and a rebellion in 1825 began a war that led to the creation of Uruguay, subjects reserved for later chapters. In the north and interior, the empire's claims to Amazonia and to lands inhabited by Indigenous peoples were far greater than its administrative reach, and many communities lived beyond effective imperial control.

Provincial integration relied on a bargain. Local elites kept control of land, militias, and councils, while the central government provided legitimacy, military support, and protection of slavery. The bargain was strained by revolts in Pernambuco in 1817 and 1824 and by resentment in the north, and later revolts in the 1830s would test it again. Unity depended on a combination of institutions, coercion, negotiation, and shared interests, and its durability was not guaranteed in 1825.

Section 56.25

Why an Empire? Brazilian Independence in Comparative Perspective

Why did Brazil emerge as a monarchy and a single large state when Spanish America fragmented into republics? Several factors worked together. The monarchy was already present in Rio, with a dynasty, a court, ministries, and a legitimate heir who could claim continuity, while Spanish America faced a vacant sovereignty after the French captured Ferdinand VII in 1808 and contested it through juntas. Brazil's elites, many of them trained at Coimbra and tied to the court, were relatively cohesive, an interpretation developed by the historian José Murilo de Carvalho, and they feared that prolonged war might unleash social revolution in a society where roughly a third or more of the population was enslaved. The example of Haiti reinforced that fear.

The contrast should not be overdrawn. Spanish Americans also considered monarchy: Mexico had an empire under Iturbide in 1822 and 1823, and some leaders in the Río de la Plata and the Andes proposed monarchical solutions. Brazil also faced wars, regional rebellions, and republican alternatives, including those in Pernambuco in 1817 and 1824. Its unity was achieved with force and negotiation, and many provinces resisted. The Spanish American wars were longer and more destructive, but they also produced new state institutions, and the new republics' fragmentation had many causes, including geography, regional economies, and the collapse of imperial administration.

Brazilian and Spanish American independence were connected. Both emerged from the Napoleonic crisis in Iberia, from Atlantic commerce and British power, and from similar debates about sovereignty, constitutionalism, and race. Observers in both regions watched one another, and Brazil's monarchy was seen with suspicion by the republics. The outcomes were not predetermined: different decisions in 1821 and 1822, a different response by Pedro, or a different constitutional settlement might have produced a different Brazil. The empire that emerged in 1825 was a distinctive compromise, with a constitutional monarchy, a continental territory, and an economy based on slavery, whose later history is traced in Chapters 61 and 66.

Chapter Summary

This chapter explained how Portuguese America became a constitutional empire between 1807 and 1825, and why it retained most of its territory while Spanish America fragmented. Its central argument is that independence grew from the institutional changes caused by the court's relocation, was shaped by a constitutional crisis between Lisbon and Rio, and was consolidated by wars, negotiation, and force.

The court's arrival in 1808 turned Rio into a capital. Open ports, British treaties, new institutions, and royal patronage gave Brazilian elites access to markets and offices, and the 1815 United Kingdom made Brazil legally equal to Portugal. These changes made a return to colonial status unacceptable to many Brazilians and intolerable to many Portuguese, who resented being ruled from America.

Regional movements showed alternatives. Pernambuco's 1817 republic combined republican ideas with defense of slavery and was suppressed, leaving a memory that shaped later conflict. The Porto Revolution of 1820 produced a constitutional crisis, and the Lisbon Cortes' decrees subordinating Brazilian governments to Lisbon were read as recolonization, though Portuguese liberals held varied aims and some provinces welcomed ties to Lisbon.

João VI's return in 1821 left Pedro as regent. The Dia do Fico, the ministry of José Bonifácio, the Cumpra-se, the constituent assembly summons, and the council led by Princess Leopoldina led to the break of 7 September 1822. Independence, acclamation on 12 October, and coronation on 1 December were distinct steps, and commemorative tradition has obscured the collective process.

Independence was also fought. Bahia's siege ended with the Portuguese evacuation of Salvador on 2 July 1823, and Maranhão and Pará adhered under naval pressure and repression. Local militias, Cochrane's squadron, free and enslaved people of color, Indigenous recruits, and women such as Maria Quitéria took part. The wars show that integration was constructed and not simply accepted.

The 1823 Assembly's dissolution and the 1824 constitution determined the monarchy's form. The Moderating Power gave the emperor wide authority, citizenship and voting rules stratified the population, and slavery was protected without being named. The Confederation of the Equator showed that centralization remained contested, and the participation of northeastern provinces was uneven.

Recognition came through a treaty and a financial convention in August 1825, mediated by Britain, with the United States having recognized Brazil earlier. Territorial continuity was imperfect: Cisplatina was restive, and much of Amazonia and the interior lay beyond effective control.

In comparison with Spanish America, Brazil had a surviving dynasty, cohesive elites, institutions in Rio, and fear of social upheaval after Haiti. The outcome was not predetermined, and the two experiences were connected. Later chapters trace the empire's reign, its slavery, and its end.

Chapter Reflection

Brazilian independence shows how a relocated monarchy, provincial wars, and elite compromise could produce a state that was continuous yet contested. The court's arrival changed Brazil's institutions before any declaration of independence, and the constitutional crisis of 1820–22 made that status impossible to reverse.

The chapter also shows that unity had a price. It was won through sieges, naval blockades, and repression, and it left slavery untouched. The empire's centralization provoked revolts, and the compromise of 1824 resolved few questions about citizenship and provincial autonomy.

Compared with Spanish America, Brazil took a different path, but the paths were connected by the Napoleonic crisis, Atlantic trade, British power, and fear of social upheaval. Neither outcome was predetermined, and each shaped how later generations remembered independence.

Chapter 57 turns to Central America and the Caribbean, where Iberian rule ended unevenly, and Chapter 61 follows the empire created here. Students should carry forward the view that independence was a political settlement, and that those excluded from it, especially the enslaved, would shape the next stage.
