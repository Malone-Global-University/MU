The Greco-Persian World: War, Diplomacy, and Cultural Contact

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Persians and Greeks were at different times subjects and rulers, enemies and paymasters, employers and employees, hosts and exiles, and the familiar picture of a single struggle between "Persia" and "Greece" captures only one strand of a relationship that lasted for generations and shaped both worlds.

Sardis, the old capital of the kingdom of Lydia in western Anatolia, is a useful place to begin, because it shows how poorly a simple map of "East" and "West" fits the evidence. After the mid-sixth century BCE it was the seat of a Persian governor, a satrapy center linked to the imperial heartland by the road system examined in Chapter 11. Around it lay Greek-speaking cities on the Aegean coast, Lydian, Carian, and Phrygian communities, Persian and other Iranian-speaking landholders and garrisons, and merchants and craftsmen from many places. A Greek resident of Ephesus or Miletus in the year 510 BCE did not live at the edge of a foreign empire. He or she lived inside one.

This chapter studies the long, uneven relationship between the Achaemenid Empire and the many Greek communities on both sides of the Aegean. It is the third of the three chapters of Part III that follow Chapter 11's account of how the empire was governed and Chapter 12's account of who lived within it. Its title assigns three responsibilities, war, diplomacy, and cultural contact, and it treats them as equals. The wars that Greek tradition called the Persian Wars, the expedition ending at Marathon in 490 BCE and the great invasion of 480–479 BCE, receive full and careful treatment. They are not, however, the whole story, and they are not even the most representative part of it.

The reason is that "Greek" and "Persian" were not unified actors. The Greeks lived in hundreds of independent communities, called poleis, that fought one another, formed shifting alliances, and disagreed sharply about Persia. Some Greek cities were Persian subjects, some submitted to Persian envoys, some fought bitterly against Persian forces, and some did all of these things at different moments. The Persian side was equally composite. The armies and fleets of the Great King were imperial forces drawn from the peoples of the empire, as Chapter 12 established, and Persians proper were only one element within them. To read the period as ethnic Greeks against ethnic Persians is to misread it.

The chapter's argument can be stated briefly. Greco-Persian relations were not a continuous war between two unified civilizations. They involved imperial domination, rebellion, major interstate wars, alliances, mercenary service, diplomacy, trade, migration, and reciprocal cultural borrowing. Persian power shaped Greek politics long after the invasions of 490 and 480–479 BCE, and Greek communities existed both outside and inside the Achaemenid imperial system. The older formulation, in which Persia tried to conquer freedom-loving Greece, Greece won, and East and West permanently separated, is itself a historical artifact that students should learn to recognize, and its origins lie substantially in Greek writing of the fifth century BCE.

That observation points to a central problem of evidence. Most continuous narrative of these events was composed by Greek authors, above all Herodotus, writing after the events and for Greek audiences. Achaemenid royal inscriptions, administrative tablets from the imperial heartland, Babylonian and Egyptian documents, coins, seals, and archaeological remains supply an indispensable check, but no comparable Persian narrative history of these wars survives. Section 13.9 examines this problem directly, and the whole chapter proceeds with it in mind.

The chapter runs from the Persian absorption of Lydia and the Ionian cities in the 540s BCE to the King's Peace of 387/386 BCE. That chronological range is deliberately broad, because a chapter that stopped at Plataea in 479 would have space for war but almost none for diplomacy and cultural contact. It also keeps the chapter within its assigned responsibility. Later dynastic history, rebellions, the reign of Artaxerxes II and his successors beyond their Greek relations, and the coming of Macedon and Alexander belong to Chapter 14, and the chapter avoids the teleological suggestion that these events made the empire's fall inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Persian rule over Ionia and Lydia placed Greeks inside the Achaemenid imperial system before any Persian expedition reached mainland Greece.
  2. Analyze the causes of the Ionian Revolt and explain why it should not be described simply as a national liberation movement.
  3. Trace the sequence from the Ionian Revolt through Marathon and the invasion of Xerxes, distinguishing what the sources establish from what they only assert.
  4. Assess the strategic significance of Thermopylae, Artemisium, Salamis, Plataea, and Mycale without treating any of them as the end of Persian power.
  5. Describe the multinational composition of the Persian forces and the divided response of Greek communities to Persian demands.
  6. Evaluate Herodotus as a source, identifying what he preserves well and what cannot be read literally.
  7. Explain how Persian policy shifted from invasion to indirect diplomacy and financial leverage after 479 BCE, and assess the debate over the Peace of Callias.
  8. Interpret Persian involvement in the Peloponnesian War and the expedition of Cyrus the Younger as evidence of entanglement rather than simple hostility.
  9. Analyze the King's Peace of 387/386 BCE as an instrument of Persian diplomatic influence.
  10. Evaluate claims of cultural borrowing between Greeks and Persians, distinguishing well-attested contact from unsupported assumptions of "influence."
  11. Critique the "East versus West" framework and explain why it distorts the evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Greeks and Persians become military enemies, political partners, imperial subjects, mercenaries, diplomats, traders, and cultural intermediaries, and why is the relationship poorly understood if reduced to "Greece versus Persia"?
  2. What does it mean to call the Ionian Greeks "subjects" of the Great King, and how did they experience Persian rule?
  3. What were the Persian expeditions of 490 and 480 BCE trying to accomplish, and how confidently can historians answer that question from Greek sources?
  4. Why did some Greek communities fight Persia, others submit, and others remain neutral?
  5. How should historians use a Greek literary source such as Herodotus when the Persian side left no comparable narrative?
  6. How did Persian strategy toward the Greek world change after 479 BCE, and why?
  7. What does the participation of Greeks in Persian dynastic politics and Persian money in Greek warfare reveal about the limits of a "civilizational conflict" model?
  8. How can historians identify real cultural exchange between Greeks and Persians without exaggerating or inventing "influence"?

Historical Context

By the time this chapter begins, the Achaemenid state described in Chapters 9 through 12 was an empire of unprecedented geographic scale. Cyrus had defeated the Median, Lydian, and Babylonian kingdoms, Cambyses had added Egypt, and Darius I had consolidated the system of satrapies, tribute, roads, and royal inscriptions that gave the empire its institutional form. The western frontier of that empire ran through Anatolia, and beyond it lay the Aegean Sea, an enclosed and island-rich waterway that already connected Anatolia, mainland Greece, Thrace, Cyprus, the Levant, and the Black Sea.

The Greek world in the sixth century BCE was not a state but a cultural and linguistic zone. Greek-speaking communities stretched from Sicily and southern Italy across the Aegean to the western Anatolian coast and the Black Sea, sharing a language, major religious sanctuaries such as Delphi and Olympia, and a body of common traditions, yet organized into independent city-states with different constitutions, institutions, and interests. Sparta, Athens, Corinth, Thebes, Miletus, and Ephesus each pursued their own policies. Greek city-states were often at war with each other, and it is anachronistic to assume that shared language automatically produced shared political loyalty.

Trade and migration had linked the Aegean coasts for centuries before Persian rule. Greek cities of western Anatolia, collectively called Ionia, stood in close contact with the Lydian kingdom of Sardis, whose kings had already subjected many of them to tribute before Cyrus. The Persian conquest therefore changed the identity of the overlord more than it created the condition of subjection. It did, however, join the Ionian cities to a far larger administrative system, one whose center lay more than a thousand kilometers to the east at Susa and Persepolis.

The plateau-centered geography emphasized in Chapter 1 matters here in one particular way. The Achaemenid center of gravity was inland and eastern, while the Aegean was a maritime periphery reached through Anatolian roads, Phoenician and Egyptian shipyards, and subject Greek harbors. Persian power in the Aegean rested on imperial organization rather than on any natural geographic advantage, a point that becomes vivid when the logistics of the invasion of 480 BCE are examined in Section 13.5.

Section 13.1

The Aegean Frontier before the Persian Wars

The history of Greek and Persian contact begins with Lydia. Under its kings, of whom Croesus is the best known, the kingdom centered on Sardis controlled much of western Anatolia and had brought most of the Greek cities of the Ionian coast into a relationship of tribute and alliance, while leaving them considerable internal autonomy. When Cyrus defeated Croesus around 547 or 546 BCE (the traditional dating, discussed in Chapter 9), Persian power inherited this position. The Persian general Harpagus, according to Herodotus, then reduced the Ionian cities, most of which resisted briefly or came to terms. Herodotus reports that Miletus had already made an agreement with Cyrus, and that some islanders, such as those of Lesbos, Chios, and Samos, came under Persian authority only later.

Persian rule on this frontier was practical rather than doctrinaire. The Ionian cities paid tribute, supplied ships and men when required, and were governed under the oversight of the satrap at Sardis. Herodotus states that the Persians supported local tyrants, men who held power in individual cities without constitutional office, as reliable intermediaries. This policy was consistent with the empire's wider habit, described in Chapter 11, of ruling through local elites, but it created a lasting vulnerability: a city's tyrant depended on Persia, and his opponents could therefore present resistance to him and resistance to Persia as the same cause.

The later sixth century saw Persian influence extend north and west. Darius I campaigned in Thrace around 513 BCE, an expedition that Herodotus links to a crossing of the Danube against the Scythians in which Ionian tyrants supposedly guarded the bridge. Darius's northern campaign against the Scythians is generally accepted, but its route, scale, objectives, and many details of Herodotus's narrative remain debated. It is reasonably clear that Persian authority was extended over parts of Thrace and that Macedon, on the northern edge of the Greek world, accepted Persian overlordship; Herodotus describes the Macedonian king as submitting the token of "earth and water." By the end of the century, the Persian frontier was therefore no longer the Anatolian coast alone but reached into the northern Aegean.

Students should notice what this account does not imply. Mainland Greek states such as Athens and Sparta had not been conquered, but they were not unaware of Persia either. Athenian and Spartan envoys, Greek exiles, and merchants moved between the mainland and the Anatolian cities, and Persian authority was a fact of diplomacy before it became a fact of war. A tyrant expelled from Athens, Hippias, later sought refuge with the Persians, an early sign that Persian power could become a resource in Greek political struggles.

Section 13.2

Greeks within the Achaemenid World

Chapter 12 described the empire as a system of differentiated communities held together by shared institutions. Greeks formed one such community, or rather several. In Old Persian royal inscriptions, Greeks appear as the Yauna, a term derived from "Ionians," and the inscriptions distinguish different groups of them, including Yauna "of the mainland," Yauna "who are by the sea," and a further group described by a term usually translated as "petasos-wearing," or hat-wearing, Greeks whose identification is debated. Persian administrative vocabulary thus already treated Greek communities as plural and geographically dispersed.

Historical Vocabulary
Yauna

Definition: The Old Persian word for Greeks, derived from the name of the Ionians, the Greek community with which the Persians first had extensive contact.

Historical context: Achaemenid inscriptions list Yauna among the subject peoples, sometimes with subdivisions. The term reflects the geography of Persian contact: Ionians on the Anatolian coast were the Greeks Persia governed, and so their name became the general term. The Greeks themselves generally used Hellenes, and the two words did not coincide in scope.

Why it matters: The term shows that Persian officials saw Greeks through imperial administration, as one group among many, rather than as a single opposing civilization.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that "Yauna" and "Hellene" meant precisely the same set of people, or that the Persian term carried the cultural weight later Greek writers attached to their own name.

Greeks lived and worked in Persian territory in many capacities. The foundation inscription of Darius's palace at Susa records that Ionians and Sardians worked stone there, alongside craftsmen from other regions of the empire. Persepolis administrative tablets, though they seldom identify workers by ethnic label, show a labor system in which specialists from many regions received rations. Greek sailors and soldiers served in imperial forces, and Greek physicians attended the court: Herodotus tells of Democedes of Croton, who treated Darius, and Ctesias of Cnidus later served Artaxerxes II as physician and wrote a Greek account of Persia that survives only in fragments and summaries.

The reverse movement is equally important. Persians and other Iranian speakers lived in Anatolia as governors, landowners, and garrison troops, and the satrapal courts at Sardis and Dascylium were places where Greeks, Lydians, and Persians met in daily administrative and social life. The Greek cities of Anatolia themselves became mixed places. Greek families intermarried with Carians, Lydians, and Persians, and some Greek communities adopted Persian-style luxuries and titles. Any account that sharply separates "Greek" and "Persian" spaces will therefore misdescribe Ionia.

Section 13.3

The Ionian Revolt, 499–494 BCE

The Ionian Revolt is known chiefly from Books 5 and 6 of Herodotus, and his account is composed of both traditions preserved by the Ionians themselves and his own judgments. According to that account, the revolt arose from a failed Persian-backed expedition against Naxos in 499 BCE, led by Aristagoras, the deputy tyrant of Miletus. Fearing punishment and Persian displeasure, Aristagoras, Herodotus says, chose rebellion. Herodotus emphasizes personal motives, including ambition and fear, and may be simplifying. Modern historians have also considered the burdens of tribute and imperial service, resentment of Persian-backed tyrants, local political rivalries, and economic pressures, although the existence of a general Ionian commercial crisis is disputed.

The revolt spread through many Ionian cities, and in several the tyrants were deposed and replaced by other forms of government, but it never enlisted all Greek communities. Some Ionian cities took little part, and some Greeks stayed on the Persian side. Aristagoras traveled to the mainland to seek help. Sparta refused, while Athens sent twenty ships and Eretria five. Their forces joined the Ionians in an attack on Sardis in 498 BCE, in which the lower city was burned, an event that later Greek tradition presented as a decisive provocation of the Persian king. The revolt also spread to Cyprus and to Caria, where fighting was severe.

Persian recovery was methodical. Cyprus was retaken, the Carian resistance was contained, and a Persian fleet, much of it Phoenician, defeated the Ionians at the naval battle of Lade in 494 BCE, in part, Herodotus says, because of the defection of Samian ships. Miletus fell that year and was sacked. Herodotus reports the deportation of many of its inhabitants to a Persian-controlled site near the head of the Persian Gulf, a report consistent with Achaemenid practice of resettling populations. Persian suppression was harsh at Miletus and elsewhere. The administrative pacification that followed, however, emphasized surveyed tribute assessments and mechanisms for arbitration between the Ionian cities: Herodotus records that the satrap Artaphernes had the land of the Ionian cities measured and the tribute fixed, and that Mardonius later supported democratic governments in place of tyrants, a claim Herodotus felt the need to defend against skeptical Greek readers.

Students should resist calling the revolt a struggle for national independence. The Ionians were not a nation in any political sense, their cities acted on different interests, and the revolt lacked a lasting common leadership. It was nevertheless a serious challenge to Achaemenid rule on the western frontier, and it created the conditions for the mainland wars, because Athenian and Eretrian intervention gave Persia a reason and an opening to act against those states.

Cause and Effect

From Ionian Revolt to Imperial Invasion

Long-term causes

Persian rule over western Anatolia; rivalries among Greek poleis; tyrannies dependent on Persian support and the factions opposed to them; Aegean commercial links; the empire's need to secure its western frontier.

Short-term causes

The failed Naxos expedition and Aristagoras's political predicament; local hostility to tyrants; the willingness of Athens and Eretria to intervene.

Immediate trigger and event

The revolt of 499 BCE and the burning of Sardis in 498, followed by Persian reconquest culminating in the fall of Miletus in 494.

Effects

Persian punitive and expansionist expeditions; Greek coalition-building; the invasion of 480–479; the growth of Athenian naval power; prolonged Persian involvement in Greek affairs.

Historical complexity: The Ionian Revolt did not mechanically cause every later Greco-Persian conflict. Choices by many actors, Persian kings and satraps, Athenian and Spartan assemblies, Ionian leaders, and the states that submitted or stayed neutral, turned one regional rebellion into a far larger confrontation, and other outcomes were conceivable at each stage.

Section 13.4

From Revolt to Marathon

Persian action after the revolt proceeded in stages. In 492 BCE Mardonius, Darius's son-in-law, led a force through Thrace and Macedon, reasserting Persian control in the north. According to Herodotus, his fleet was badly damaged by a storm off Mount Athos, and the expedition turned back. In 491 Persian heralds went to Greek states to demand earth and water, the symbolic acknowledgment of submission. Many states complied; Athens and Sparta, in Herodotus's story, killed the envoys, an episode of uncertain historicity that later Greek writers used to dramatize their resistance.

The expedition of 490 BCE, under the commanders Datis and Artaphernes, was an amphibious operation carried by ship across the Aegean. It captured Naxos, spared Delos, and destroyed Eretria, whose inhabitants Herodotus says were deported to Persian territory. The force then landed at Marathon, on the coast of Attica, accompanied by the exiled Athenian Hippias. The Athenians, joined by a contingent from little Plataea, met the Persians there, and the Athenian infantry won a clear victory. Herodotus gives the Persian dead as 6,400 and the Athenian as 192; these are his reported figures, not independently verified counts. The decisive local Athenian victory is established by the outcome of the battle, not by treating that casualty ratio as exact. The surviving Persian ships sailed toward Athens but withdrew.

Marathon deserves neither dismissal nor inflation. For Athens it became a founding memory of civic confidence, cited for generations. For Persia it was a limited defeat of an expedition that had already achieved important aims, above all the destruction of Eretria and the demonstration of Persian reach. Darius did not treat it as a catastrophe. Egypt rose in revolt in 486 BCE, and Darius died that year, leaving the western question to his son Xerxes. To say that Marathon "saved Western civilization" is a modern rhetorical claim that the evidence cannot bear, and the idea that it ended Persian ambitions toward Greece is simply wrong.

The analytical question is therefore what Darius was trying to do. Most sources present the expedition as punishment for Athenian and Eretrian aid to the Ionians. A more expansive aim, the extension of Achaemenid control across the Aegean, is also plausible. The question is explored more fully in the debate feature below.

Section 13.5

Xerxes and the Invasion of 480 BCE

Xerxes came to the throne in 486 BCE and first dealt with revolts in Egypt and, later, in Babylonia. His decision to invade Greece was therefore not an inherited reflex but a choice made after other priorities had been addressed, and his preparations, as Herodotus describes them, lasted several years. The scale of the effort depended directly on the administrative capacity examined in Chapter 11: provisioning depots in Thrace, roads and river crossings, and the mobilization of subject fleets and contingents on a schedule the empire could sustain.

The most spectacular preparations were engineering works. A pair of pontoon bridges was built across the Hellespont, the narrow strait between Asia and Europe, and a canal was cut across the base of the Athos peninsula so that the fleet could avoid the dangerous headland where Mardonius's ships had been wrecked. Traces of the canal have been identified in the landscape, which supports the core of Herodotus's report. These works show an empire able to combine engineering, forced and paid labor, and logistics on a large scale.

The composition of the invasion force follows the empire. Herodotus provides a long list of contingents, Persians and Medes, Sacae, Bactrians, Indians, Arabs, Ethiopians, Egyptians, Phoenicians, Cypriots, Ionian Greeks, and many others, each with distinctive equipment. The catalog is a literary composition and its details cannot all be trusted, but the general principle, an army and navy drawn from across the empire, matches what Achaemenid sculptures and administrative records show elsewhere. Herodotus's total of well over two million fighting men, and the round figures he gives for the fleet, are not credible as arithmetic; supply constraints alone make them impossible. Modern estimates vary widely, but they are lower by an order of magnitude or more, and none can claim certainty.

On the Greek side, a coalition was assembled in 481 BCE at a meeting on the Isthmus of Corinth, usually called the Hellenic League, with Sparta holding overall command. It was a limited alliance. Many communities did not join. Thessaly and most of Boeotia, notably Thebes, sided with or submitted to Persia, and other states, such as Argos, remained aloof. The Greek states that resisted were, in numbers and geography, a minority of the Greek world, and their eventual success owed much to strategic circumstances as well as to determination.

Historical Debate

What Were the Persian Invasions Trying to Accomplish?

The question: What were the objectives of the expeditions of 490 and 480–479 BCE? Persian sources do not say, so historians must reconstruct aims from Greek accounts and from the scale and character of the operations.

Interpretation A — Punishment and restoration of authority: The expeditions responded to Athenian and Eretrian support for the Ionian Revolt, and aimed to punish those states and reassert Persian prestige and security on the western frontier. The destruction of Eretria and the demand for earth and water fit this view.
Interpretation B — Expansion into the Aegean and mainland Greece: The scale of Xerxes's preparations, including bridges, a canal, depots, and a massive imperial mobilization, suggests a broader intention to bring mainland Greece under Achaemenid control, as Thrace and Macedon had already been brought.
Interpretation C — Aims that cannot be fully reconstructed: Objectives may have varied by stage and by decision-maker, and the maximum intended outcome may never have been fixed. A limited aim can expand if success is easy.

Evidence: Herodotus, Greek tragedy, and archaeology of the engineering works are the main evidence. Achaemenid inscriptions of Xerxes survive but do not narrate this campaign.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is chiefly one of interpretation and of how much weight to give Greek reports of Persian intentions.

Why it matters: If the aim was punishment, Salamis and Plataea were failures of a limited operation; if conquest, they were failures of a major imperial project. Either way, students should ask about Persian aims rather than assume the Greek frame.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that would best support each interpretation, and explain why each is hard to establish from the surviving sources.

Section 13.6

Thermopylae, Artemisium, and the Politics of Greek Resistance

In late summer 480 BCE the Greek allies attempted a combined defense in central Greece. A land force under the Spartan king Leonidas took position at Thermopylae, a coastal pass north of Boeotia, while an allied fleet, largely Athenian, took position at Artemisium, at the northern tip of the island of Euboea. The positions were mutually supporting, because the pass and the strait were both chokepoints on the route south.

Greek tradition remembers "the three hundred Spartans." That number, drawn from Herodotus, refers to Spartiates in Leonidas's bodyguard, but the force at Thermopylae included several thousand other Greeks, among them contingents from Peloponnesian states, Locrians, Phocians, Thespians, and Thebans. According to Herodotus, a local guide later showed the Persians a mountain track that outflanked the pass. Herodotus reports that the allies debated whether to remain and says he believed Leonidas dismissed most of them. The Spartans and the Thespians remained, together with a group of Thebans. Most of those who remained were killed, and Leonidas died in the fighting; Herodotus says the Theban contingent surrendered, though his treatment of Thebes is shaped by later hostility.

At the same time, the fleets fought inconclusively at Artemisium. A storm damaged the Persian fleet, and when news of Thermopylae reached the Greek navy it withdrew. Thermopylae was a tactical Persian success and a costly delaying action for the Greeks. Its later celebrity as a symbol of resistance should not obscure the fact that it opened central Greece, that Athens was then evacuated and its temples on the Acropolis burned, and that resistance was continuing only at sea and in the Peloponnese.

Historical Vocabulary
Medism (medizing)

Definition: A Greek term for siding with or submitting to the Persians, derived from "Mede," the name Greeks often applied to Persians.

Historical context: The term was used in Greek political argument, often as an accusation, against states and individuals who accepted Persian authority in the wars of 490 and 480–479 BCE. The word reflects the Greek habit of calling Persian forces "Medes," not any literal Median ethnicity.

Why it matters: Many Greek states submitted or remained neutral for reasons of security, local politics, or geography. "Medism" was therefore a partisan label. It shows how the Greeks themselves divided over Persia, and how later memory could turn political choices into moral judgments.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat submission as simple cowardice or treason. The label expressed the hostility of Greek opponents and reveals more about later Greek politics than about the motives of those who accepted Persian terms.

Section 13.7

Salamis: Naval War and Imperial Coalition

After the sack of Athens the Greek fleet gathered in the narrow waters off the island of Salamis, and the Persian fleet moved to engage it there in September 480 BCE by the traditional reckoning. The Athenian leader Themistocles, according to Herodotus, sent a false message to Xerxes to lure the Persian fleet into the narrows; the story already appears in Aeschylus and is widely credited, though its details may have been shaped by Greek storytelling about clever Greek strategy, and its historicity cannot be settled with certainty. What is clear is that the narrow space limited the number of ships that could fight, reducing the advantage of the larger fleet, and that the Greek triremes, closely coordinated, achieved a major victory.

The Persian fleet was not "Persian" in composition. It was drawn from the maritime peoples of the empire, especially the Phoenicians, whose cities had long-standing naval traditions, and from the Egyptians, Cypriots, and Anatolian Greeks, including Ionians and Carians. Herodotus portrays Artemisia, the ruler of Halicarnassus, as a capable commander in the fleet, an indication that Greek-speaking rulers served in the Persian system. The idea that Ionians fought against their fellow Greeks is often mentioned, and Greek sources debate their reliability in the battle; the empire evidently relied on them and on their cities' ships.

Reconstructing the numbers is difficult. Herodotus's initial fleet catalogue totals 1,207 triremes, but that is not a secure count of the ships actually present at Salamis after earlier losses and reinforcements; his Greek catalogue gives about 380 ships. These figures are literary and cannot be verified, though a larger Persian force facing a smaller Greek one is plausible. A near-contemporary source is the Athenian dramatist Aeschylus, whose play The Persians, performed in 472 BCE, presents the battle from the Persian side; even so, it is a tragedy, not a record.

Salamis was a serious strategic reversal for the Persian effort but not the destruction of the Persian navy or the empire. The fleet retained substantial strength, and the loss did not change the balance of forces on land. Xerxes withdrew from Greece with part of the army, while a large force remained in Thessaly under Mardonius for a campaign the following year. Greek sources give a dramatic account of a panicked retreat, but historians generally treat that portrayal as literary shaping and reconstruct the withdrawal as a strategic decision driven by logistics, the season, and the need to attend to other parts of the empire.

Section 13.8

Plataea and Mycale, 479 BCE

Mardonius spent the winter of 480/479 BCE in northern Greece and tried diplomacy. According to Herodotus, he sent Alexander I of Macedon to offer Athens generous terms, including restoration of its territory and autonomy in exchange for an alliance. Athens refused, according to that account, and Mardonius reoccupied Attica. This episode is important because it shows that Persian strategy included persuasion as well as force, an approach that would grow in importance later in the chapter.

In 479 a large Greek army under the Spartan regent Pausanias met Mardonius's force near Plataea in Boeotia. The battle, fought over several days, turned on Persian cavalry harassment, Greek supply difficulties, and a confused engagement in which Greek hoplites overcame the Persian infantry and Mardonius was killed. The surviving Persian forces withdrew, and the Greeks besieged and punished Thebes for its alignment with Persia. Herodotus places the battle of Mycale on the same day, a coincidence that may itself be a literary flourish. At Mycale, on the Anatolian coast, a Greek fleet landed forces against a Persian encampment and defeated it, and the action encouraged Ionian cities to rebel again.

These victories ended the invasion of mainland Greece, and over the next few years Persian control north of the Aegean shrank. The Greeks took Sestos on the Hellespont, and later operations removed Persian garrisons from parts of Thrace. Yet Persia retained its Anatolian satrapies and its much wider empire, while control of the Ionian coast became contested and a growing number of Greek cities broke from Persian authority. The empire remained a major power for roughly another century and a half, and Plataea should not be described as the cause of Achaemenid decline, a claim that presupposes an outcome the sources do not support.

Section 13.9

Herodotus and the Problem of Our Sources

Students who have read this far have encountered the same source repeatedly. Herodotus of Halicarnassus, born around 484 BCE in a city then subject to Persia, composed his Histories in the decades after the wars, probably completing them in the 420s BCE. He is the principal narrative source for the Ionian Revolt, Marathon, and the invasion of 480–479, and he preserves a huge body of material on Persian customs, geography, and political tradition. A writer who grew up in a Persian-ruled Greek city was in a good position to gather information from many sides, and his work is indispensable.

It is also a Greek literary work written after the events, for Greek audiences, and shaped by narrative and moral design. Its limits are well understood. The dialogues and speeches, including the celebrated debate at the Persian court about forms of government, are literary compositions and cannot be treated as transcripts. Troop numbers are implausible. Explanations of Persian motives are often inferred from Greek expectations, and the contrast between Greek freedom and Persian monarchy is a thematic structure, not a neutral description. Yet he is not simply a liar: many specific claims, such as the existence of the Athos canal, the general shape of the imperial contingents, and the sequence of the Ionian Revolt, are corroborated or plausible.

Other evidence corrects and supplements him. Achaemenid royal inscriptions and reliefs, including the subject-delegation reliefs at Persepolis, show how Persians presented the empire. Administrative tablets from Persepolis reveal the workings of the state. Babylonian and Egyptian documents supply local views of the same period. Archaeology at sites such as Sardis and Miletus documents destruction and rebuilding, and coins show patterns of exchange. Aeschylus and later Greek writers, including Thucydides, Xenophon, and Ctesias, provide additional views, each with distinct purposes. Historians ask of each source who wrote it, for whom, and what perspectives it leaves out. The most important absent voice is a Persian narrative of these campaigns.

Source Criticism

What Herodotus Gives Us, and What He Cannot

Reliable in general outline: the sequence of major events; the geography of the campaigns; the multinational character of Persian forces; many local traditions of Ionian, Athenian, and Spartan communities.

Not to be read literally: speeches and private conversations; enormous numbers; explanations of Persian thought and emotion; the dramatic structure that presents Persian defeat as the consequence of pride.

Absent or thin: Persian archival record of the campaigns; the experience of ordinary subjects, soldiers, and rowers; Egyptian, Phoenician, and other imperial perspectives.

Questions for students:

  1. Why does it matter that Herodotus was born in a Persian-ruled city?
  2. Which kinds of statement in Herodotus can be checked against other evidence, and which cannot?
  3. How would our understanding of the wars change if a Persian narrative history survived?
Section 13.10

From War to Diplomacy: Persia and the Greek States after 479 BCE

After 479 BCE the confrontation became a long, mostly maritime struggle. In 478/477 BCE, many Aegean and Ionian Greek states formed an alliance under Athenian leadership, later called the Delian League, to continue the war and to protect the Greek cities from Persia. Members supplied ships or, increasingly, payments, and Athens administered the treasury. In its early years the League was an alliance of states with a common enemy. Over time, Athens converted it into an instrument of Athenian power, using force against members that tried to leave, and modern historians treat this evolution as one of the central developments of the fifth century. It should not be described as an Athenian empire from its inception.

The League campaigned against Persian positions in the Aegean and along the southern Anatolian coast. A major victory at the Eurymedon River, traditionally dated around 466, weakened Persian naval power in the region. In the mid-450s, Athens supported an Egyptian revolt against Persia led by Inaros, an intervention that ended in disaster for the Athenian expedition. Fighting around Cyprus continued into the late 450s and 440s. At the same time, Persian officials began to rely on indirect approaches: supporting rival Greek factions, offering refuge to exiles, and negotiating. The Athenian statesman Themistocles, ostracized from Athens, eventually found refuge at the Persian court according to Thucydides.

Later Greek writers, especially fourth-century orators, referred to an agreement between Athens and the Persian king, the "Peace of Callias," named for the Athenian envoy Callias, dated to about 449 BCE, under which the Persians would stay away from the Aegean and Greek cities of Asia would be free. Whether such a formal treaty existed is genuinely disputed. Thucydides, who narrates the period, does not mention it. Some historians accept a formal agreement, others argue for an informal understanding or a de facto end to hostilities, and others regard the peace as a later Athenian invention or exaggeration. Students should note that the disagreement is a good test case for critical source use: the evidence is later, partisan, and inconsistent, and the Persian side left no record.

Section 13.11

Persia in the Peloponnesian War

The great war between Athens and Sparta and their respective allies, the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE), created new opportunities for Persian diplomacy. Persian satraps in western Anatolia, Tissaphernes at Sardis and Pharnabazus at Dascylium, had their own interests: to recover tribute from the Greek cities of Ionia and to weaken the Athenian empire that had encroached upon them. After Athens suffered its disaster in Sicily in 413, Sparta and its allies sought Persian money for a fleet, and in 412–411 BCE the satraps negotiated a series of agreements. Thucydides, who reports the treaty texts in his eighth book, records that the drafts recognized the King's claim to the territory that he and his ancestors had held, terms that raised difficulties for Sparta and were revised in later versions.

Persian involvement deepened in 408/407 BCE when Cyrus the Younger arrived at Sardis with wide authority over the region and a strong personal interest in supporting Sparta. Persian funds allowed the Spartan commander Lysander to pay rowers and to build a fleet that could compete with Athens, and the Athenian fleet was destroyed at Aegospotami in 405, leading to Athens's surrender in 404. Persian money was not the sole cause of the Spartan victory, but it was a major enabling condition.

The relationship was not an alliance of friends. It was an arrangement of interests, and the Persian side sought advantage without committing to permanent obligations. Persian payments were sometimes irregular, and Persian officials at times played Greek states against one another. The lesson for students is that by the late fifth century Greeks and Persians were no longer merely enemies; Persian satraps were participants in Greek interstate politics and Greek states were negotiating for Persian favor.

Section 13.12

Greek Mercenaries and the Expedition of Cyrus the Younger

When Darius II died in 405/404 BCE, his elder son took the throne as Artaxerxes II. Cyrus, the younger son and the powerful regional commander in Anatolia, plotted to seize the throne. In 401 BCE he raised an army that included a substantial contingent of Greek mercenaries, recruited from many cities and often veterans of the recent wars. Xenophon, an Athenian who joined the expedition, wrote the Anabasis, the principal surviving continuous account of the expedition, supplemented by fragments of Ctesias, who was present at Cunaxa, and later authors.

The army marched from Sardis across Anatolia and Syria and down the Euphrates toward Babylonia, where in September 401 BCE it met Artaxerxes's forces at Cunaxa. The Greeks performed well, but Cyrus was killed, ending the rebellion. According to Xenophon, Tissaphernes then invited the Greek commanders to a meeting under assurances of friendship and had them seized (other surviving traditions differ in detail), and the remaining Greek force, perhaps ten thousand or more, chose new leaders, including Xenophon, and made its way north through hostile mountainous country to the Black Sea, where it reached Greek cities.

This episode is the best single example of entanglement. Thousands of Greeks served a Persian prince in an Achaemenid succession struggle, and their employer's defeat did not make them enemies of Persia so much as unemployed professionals in a hostile country. It also demonstrates how much the Achaemenid empire depended on Anatolian satraps and how important Greek military labor had become. Yet the older claim, encouraged by Xenophon himself and by later Greek writers, that the march proved the empire to be a hollow shell, exaggerates what the evidence shows. The Greek force was an elite professional body facing an army that had lost its principal rival, and it retreated rather than conquered. The empire's core territories remained secure.

Primary Source Window

Xenophon, Anabasis (early fourth century BCE), on Cyrus and His Greek Troops

Creator: Xenophon of Athens, a participant in the expedition · Date: composed some years after 401 BCE · Type: first-person military narrative, written partly in the third person

Context: Xenophon, a young Athenian with connections to Sparta, joined Cyrus's Greek force and became one of its leaders after the seizure of the original generals. He wrote the account later, partly to explain and defend his own conduct. No verified translation of a specific passage is reproduced here; the summary below is a paraphrase.

Paraphrase: Xenophon reports that Cyrus gathered Greek forces under a series of cover explanations, telling commanders and soldiers different things about the purpose of the expedition, and that many of the Greeks realized only as the march went east that the target was the Great King himself. He then describes how Cyrus persuaded them to continue with promises of pay and reward.

Analysis: The passage shows Greeks operating inside Persian dynastic politics, motivated by wages and by relationships with a Persian prince, and it shows how a Persian commander recruited across Greek city-state boundaries. Xenophon also provides ethnographic and geographic detail on Anatolia and Mesopotamia.

Limitations: The account is the work of an interested participant writing to justify himself. It reflects a Greek view of Persian culture and often stereotypes it. It cannot establish the internal state of the Persian court, the size of the armies with certainty, or the general strength of the empire.

Perspective: A Greek soldier and later author. The perspectives of Cyrus, Artaxerxes, their Persian and other imperial officers, and the ordinary soldiers are represented only indirectly.

Section 13.13

The King's Peace and Persian Diplomacy in Greece

After Cunaxa, Tissaphernes and Pharnabazus moved to reassert Persian authority over the Ionian cities that had passed under Spartan protection, and Sparta, which had championed Ionian freedom, found itself at war with Persia. Spartan commanders campaigned in Anatolia in the 390s, including the king Agesilaus, with limited results. Persia responded partly by military means and partly through diplomacy. A Persian fleet commanded by the Athenian exile Conon and the satrap Pharnabazus defeated the Spartan fleet at Cnidus in 394 BCE, and Persian funds supported Sparta's opponents in the Corinthian War (395–387). Greek tradition, especially in Xenophon, emphasizes Persian bribery of Greek politicians, a claim that reflects partisan hostility but also a real Persian policy of financial influence.

The war ended with the settlement known as the King's Peace, or Peace of Antalcidas, of 387/386 BCE, named for the Spartan negotiator. Xenophon reports the king's terms: the cities in Asia, together with Clazomenae and Cyprus, were to belong to the king; the other Greek cities, small and great, were to be autonomous, except Lemnos, Imbros, and Scyros, which were to remain Athenian. The king threatened war, together with those who accepted the settlement, against any state that rejected it. Sparta acted as the enforcer of the settlement in Greece.

The settlement is significant because it secured explicit recognition of Persian authority over the Asian Greek cities and gave the Great King a powerful role in a Greek interstate settlement without requiring conquest of mainland Greece. It is also a warning against exaggeration. The King's Peace did not make Persia master of Greece. It shows strong Persian leverage and diplomatic influence, exercised through money, negotiation, territorial claims, and the threat of intervention, but not annexation. Later Greek writers, such as the Athenian orator Isocrates, denounced it as a humiliation, a view that reflects Greek attitudes rather than settled fact.

Historical Vocabulary
The King's Peace (Common Peace)

Definition: The settlement of 387/386 BCE sponsored by Artaxerxes II, recognizing Persian authority over the Asian Greek cities and Cyprus while proclaiming autonomy for the other Greek cities, with Lemnos, Imbros, and Scyros remaining Athenian.

Historical context: It is often called the first "common peace," a general settlement intended to bind all Greek states, though even that description depends on the evidence and definitions discussed above. It combined Persian claims in Asia with an autonomy principle that Sparta used to break up rival alliances.

Why it matters: It demonstrates Persian diplomatic power in Greek affairs and reveals the mechanisms of persuasion and leverage.

Potential misunderstanding: The term does not mean Persia ruled Greece; it means the King acted as the guarantor and beneficiary of a settlement among Greek states.

Section 13.14

A Shared Greco-Persian World

Cultural contact accompanied every phase of the political story, and it moved in both directions. Persian objects and styles are found in Greek contexts, including luxury vessels, textiles, and imagery of Persian dress, banquets, and court customs. Greek writers borrowed the Old Iranian word for an enclosed park or garden, which appears in Greek as paradeisos and later gives English "paradise." The Athenian tradition that Pericles's Odeion recalled the tent of Xerxes is late and disputed, and illustrates the kind of claim requiring caution. On the Persian side, Greek craftsmen contributed to imperial construction, as the Susa inscription indicates, and Greek styles may have shaped some Persian and Anatolian works, though attribution of any individual work is usually uncertain.

Coinage is a good field for study. The Persian gold daric and silver siglos circulated widely, Persian officials paid Greek mercenaries and allies in coin, and Greek-type coinage circulated in Anatolia. Local dynasts in regions such as Caria and Lycia, who were both Persian subordinates and participants in Greek culture, produced hybrid monuments and coins that combine Persian, Greek, and local elements. Physicians, artists, exiles, and interpreters moved between courts and cities: Democedes and Ctesias, the exiles Themistocles and Demaratus, and countless unnamed craftsmen and merchants.

Every claimed influence needs a caution. Similar artistic motifs do not automatically prove direct borrowing, and many forms belong to a broader Near Eastern and eastern Mediterranean repertoire shared by both. Similarly, "Oriental" is a vague label that obscures the specific peoples involved. Historians therefore prefer to speak of selective borrowing and hybrid forms, and to remain open about cases where the evidence is ambiguous. The traditional Greek habit of contrasting free Greeks and servile barbarians was itself partly a product of the wars, and scholars sometimes call the resulting stereotype a foundation of later Orientalist thinking.

By the early fourth century BCE, Greeks and Persians had spent generations fighting, bargaining, trading, serving one another, and borrowing from one another. Persian kings remained powerful participants in Greek interstate politics, even as new political and military conditions were emerging in the western Aegean. The next chapter turns from this connected Greco-Persian world to the later history of the Achaemenid monarchy itself and the chain of events that culminated in Alexander's conquest.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Persian Power and the Greek World, c. 500 to 386 BCE

What Changed

  • Around 500 BCE, Persia governed Greek cities in Anatolia directly and confronted independent mainland states militarily. By about 400, Greeks served Persian princes as mercenaries, Persian satraps financed competing Greek states, and diplomacy supplemented invasion.
  • By 387/386, the Great King could shape a Greek interstate settlement through diplomacy and leverage without conquering the mainland.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Ionian cities remained a contested boundary, and Persian claims to them persisted throughout.
  • The empire continued to rely on subject communities, local intermediaries, and mixed forces.
  • Greek states remained divided in their responses to Persia.

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven and shaped by choices, not a steady drift. Those on the Anatolian coast experienced the most direct continuity of Persian rule, while mainland communities felt Persia intermittently and unevenly.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary People in a Contested Frontier

The wars were experienced most heavily by ordinary people. Rowers in triremes, whether Athenian citizens or Phoenician and Egyptian subjects, formed the labor force of the navies. Farmers in Attica and Boeotia saw their fields occupied and their supplies taken, and the inhabitants of Eretria and Miletus faced deportation. Ionian tax-payers bore the tribute burden regardless of who governed their city, and craftsmen moved to where imperial and civic building projects created work.

Because the surviving sources were mostly written by male elites, they say little about women, laborers, enslaved people, and rural communities, whose experience must often be inferred from archaeology and from incidental remarks. Historians must therefore read even famous battle narratives with attention to who is missing from them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Greco-Persian relations were not a continuous war between two unified civilizations but a varied relationship of domination, rebellion, war, alliance, mercenary service, diplomacy, trade, and cultural exchange. Greeks were subjects of the Achaemenid Empire from the mid-sixth century BCE, and Persian authority was a fact of Aegean life long before Persian armies reached mainland Greece.

The Ionian Revolt of 499–494 BCE arose from tensions within Persian-ruled Ionia and was not a national independence movement. Athenian and Eretrian intervention gave Persia grounds and opportunity to act against the mainland, though the later wars were the product of many choices rather than a mechanical chain of causes.

The expedition of 490 BCE, culminating at Marathon, was a limited Persian operation with a limited setback. Xerxes's invasion of 480–479 was a far larger effort that drew on the imperial system described in Chapter 11, using subject fleets, engineering works, and multinational forces. The Greek response was divided, and many states submitted or stayed neutral.

Thermopylae, Artemisium, Salamis, Plataea, and Mycale ended the invasion of mainland Greece without ending Persian power. Each has been celebrated in later memory, but the sources are literary, later, and shaped by Greek concerns, and their numbers and dramatic details need careful treatment.

Herodotus is indispensable and unreliable in specific ways. He preserves a wealth of traditions but writes as a Greek literary author, and the absence of a Persian narrative leaves many questions about Persian aims and experience open. The chapter's debates, about the invasions' aims and about the Peace of Callias, show how much rests on interpretation.

After 479 BCE, the relationship shifted to a long indirect contest, in which the Delian League evolved from a common alliance into an Athenian instrument, and Persia increasingly relied on money, diplomacy, and support for Greek factions. The Peloponnesian War made Persian satraps arbiters of Greek warfare, and the expedition of Cyrus the Younger showed Greek soldiers inside Persian dynastic politics.

The King's Peace of 387/386 BCE crowned this diplomatic pattern. It demonstrated substantial Persian leverage in Greek affairs, though not control. Throughout, Persia's empire remained strong, and the idea that Xenophon's march or the Persian Wars revealed a hollow or declining empire misreads the evidence.

Cultural contact accompanied all of this, and involved craftsmen, physicians, exiles, merchants, mercenaries, and coinage. Historians should treat claims of influence with caution, and should avoid the simplistic frameworks of East versus West or despotism versus freedom that later ideology drew from the wars.

MGU Library Research Project

Building a Source Dossier on the Greco-Persian World

Construct a source dossier that compares Greek and non-Greek evidence for one episode of Greco-Persian relations.

  1. Choose an episode. For example, the Ionian Revolt, the invasion of 480 BCE, the Peloponnesian War negotiations, the expedition of Cyrus the Younger, or the King's Peace.
  2. Collect sources. Locate at least one Greek literary source, one Achaemenid or other non-Greek source (an inscription, relief, tablet, or coin), and one archaeological or material report, using reliable translations and reference works.
  3. Analyze each source. Identify author, date, audience, purpose, perspective, and limits.
  4. Compare interpretations. Note where sources agree, disagree, or leave gaps, and how modern historians resolve differences.
  5. Write findings. In 3–5 pages, present your dossier and conclusions using careful language about uncertainty.

Library resources to use: translated ancient histories, collections of Achaemenid inscriptions, museum catalogs of Persian and Greek objects, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on the ancient Mediterranean and Near East.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the Achaemenid Empire from its western edge, where its administrative reach met a fragmented and vigorous Greek world. Chapter 11 explained how the empire governed and Chapter 12 explained who lived within it. Chapter 13 shows how that system dealt with neighbors who were partly inside it and partly outside, and how the boundary between the two was porous.

It also reveals how historical memory is made. The story of Greek freedom against Persian despotism was shaped by Greek writers and later adopted by European traditions. Reading behind that story, historians find an empire that used force, money, and diplomacy flexibly, and Greek communities whose responses to Persia varied widely. The lesson is methodological as much as factual: the source that survives is not always the most representative one.

The developments here shaped what followed. Persian influence in the Greek world continued, Greek military labor entered Persian service, and Greek political fragmentation left the Aegean open to new powers. Chapter 14 turns to the later history of the Achaemenid monarchy itself and to the events culminating in Alexander's conquest, and Chapter 15 examines Alexander in Iran. Students should carry forward the caution developed here: later events were not inevitable outcomes of these earlier ones.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger commitment to connecting Iran to Mediterranean systems without treating Iran as either isolated or merely a stage for others' histories. The Greco-Persian world was jointly made, and understanding it requires attention to both sides and to those who stood between them.
