Susa, the Zagros, and the Rise of Complex Societies

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Somewhere beneath the modern city of Shush, in the low, hot plain of southwestern Khuzestan, archaeologists uncovered a mudbrick platform raised high above the surrounding floodplain, a cemetery holding hundreds of burials accompanied by painted pottery of remarkable technical skill, and small clay objects, tokens, sealed lumps of clay, stamped impressions, that suggest people were counting, recording, and controlling goods on a scale no single household could have needed on its own. None of this happened suddenly. It was the outcome of centuries of change across an entire regional landscape, change that this chapter, rather than any single site, must explain.

Chapter 3 left the story of southwestern Iran at the level of the household and the village: farmers cultivating wheat and barley, herders managing sheep and goats, families storing grain in pits and rooms, small communities exchanging obsidian, shell, and other valued materials across considerable distances, and the first faint signs, in some village cemeteries, that not everyone in a community was buried, remembered, or provisioned in quite the same way. This chapter asks what happens when those same underlying activities, farming, herding, storage, craft work, and exchange, become coordinated not just within a household or a single village but across a whole settlement system: when some places grow much larger than their neighbors, when certain crafts become specialized and standardized, when construction projects require more labor than any one family could supply, and when people begin inventing small clay devices to keep track of who owes what to whom.

The place where archaeologists can trace this process in the greatest detail is the Susiana plain, the lowland floodplain of southwestern Khuzestan watered by rivers descending from the Zagros Mountains. Across the fifth and fourth millennia BCE, this plain supported a changing landscape of villages, larger towns, and eventually one preeminent settlement, ancient Susa, whose early history opens a window onto processes that mattered across the wider ancient Near East. Sites such as Chogha Mish, and the fourth-millennium centers now known to archaeologists as KS-04, KS-59, and KS-108, reveal that Susiana was not simply an empty stage waiting for Susa to rise. It was a landscape of multiple substantial communities, some larger and more elaborate than others, whose fortunes shifted across centuries before Susa achieved lasting regional prominence.

This chapter is deliberately cautious about the words it uses. "Complex society," the term this chapter adopts as its central analytical category, does not mean "civilization," "state," "city," or "Elam." Archaeologists reserve those more specific terms for later, more fully documented, and more precisely defined developments. What this chapter traces instead is a set of processes, growing settlement size and differentiation, craft specialization, monumental construction, unequal access to certain goods and treatments in death, and above all the invention of administrative technologies for recording and tracking, that recur across many parts of the ancient world wherever societies moved beyond the scale of the individual village. These processes do not appear all at once, they do not appear in a fixed order, and their presence does not automatically prove that a state, in the fuller sense examined in later chapters, already existed.

Students should also resist a second, related temptation: reading this chapter's evidence as an early chapter in the history of Elam. Elam, examined fully in Chapter 5, is a specific historical civilization with named political actors, textual evidence, and documented relationships to Mesopotamian states. The societies examined in this chapter, the villages and towns of fifth- and early fourth-millennium Susiana, including early Susa itself, precede any securely Elamite historical narrative. Archaeologists and historians generally avoid retroactively labeling these populations "Elamite," since the very earliest textual and political evidence for a recognizably Elamite polity belongs to a somewhat later period. This chapter therefore functions as a bridge: it explains the archaeological processes that made a civilization like Elam possible, without assuming that Elam had already arrived.

Consider the different kinds of evidence a visitor to early Susa around 4000 BCE would have left behind, compared to a visitor to a small Neolithic village of the kind examined in Chapter 3. At the village, evidence survives mostly at the household scale: hearths, storage pits, tools, and burials tucked beneath house floors. At early Susa, by contrast, archaeologists find a large artificial platform requiring the coordinated labor of many people over an extended period, evidence of pottery production organized at a scale exceeding household need, and above all small clay sealings, the impressions of stamp seals pressed into wet clay to close containers, doors, or bundles of goods. These sealings, numbering in the hundreds at early Susa, indicate that some individuals or institutions were asserting control over specific goods, and that a broader community accepted or required this kind of marking. This is exactly the sort of evidence this chapter treats as diagnostic of social complexity, evidence of coordination and administration beyond the household, without yet constituting proof of a full territorial state.

This chapter proceeds from smaller to larger scales and from more general to more specific evidence. It begins by defining social complexity as archaeologists use the term, then moves outward from individual settlements to the regional landscape of the Susiana plain, examines the specific archaeological case of Chogha Mish and the wider network of contemporary centers, traces the intensification of craft specialization and administrative technology, and finally arrives at the rise of Susa itself as the culmination, rather than the starting assumption, of these processes. It closes by explicitly marking the boundary between what this chapter has demonstrated and what Chapter 5 must still establish before the specifically Elamite history of southwestern Iran can begin.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define social complexity as archaeologists use the term, distinguishing it from civilization, urbanism, the state, and Elam specifically.
  2. Explain how archaeologists move from studying individual households and villages to studying regional settlement systems.
  3. Describe the geography of the Susiana plain and its relationship to the Zagros Mountains and lower Mesopotamia.
  4. Trace the developmental sequence at Chogha Mish and explain what its archaeology reveals about growing organizational complexity.
  5. Evaluate evidence for a multi-center settlement landscape in Susiana rather than a single, inevitable path toward Susa.
  6. Analyze craft specialization, including pottery production and metalworking, as evidence of changing economic organization.
  7. Assess storage, monumental construction, and mortuary evidence for social differentiation without overstating what they prove.
  8. Explain how stamp seals and sealings document early administrative practices and evaluate the debated interpretation of geometric clay tokens as counting or accounting devices.
  9. Describe the rise of Susa I and identify the archaeological evidence for its scale and organization.
  10. Interpret the Susa I cemetery and painted ceramics as evidence for ritual, identity, and specialization, while recognizing the limits of mortuary evidence.
  11. Compare competing explanations for the relationship between Susiana and Mesopotamia during this period.
  12. Distinguish the archaeological evidence examined in this chapter from the specifically historical civilization of Elam introduced in Chapter 5.

Historical Context

Chapter 3 established the foundations on which this chapter builds: the long Neolithic transition across the Zagros and adjacent lowlands, during which communities in and around the Iranian plateau domesticated wheat, barley, sheep, and goats, adopted increasingly settled village life, and began storing surplus grain, exchanging valued materials such as obsidian across considerable distances, and, in some cemeteries, treating certain individuals differently from others in death. By the sixth and fifth millennia BCE, village life of this kind was well established across much of southwestern Iran and neighboring Mesopotamia, supporting communities that, while still organized primarily at the household and village scale, were becoming increasingly interconnected through exchange networks and shared technological traditions, including pottery styles associated with the broader Ubaid cultural horizon of Mesopotamia and its Iranian counterparts.

This chapter picks up that story at the point where village-scale organization began to give way, in certain places, to something larger and more differentiated. It focuses geographically on the Susiana plain, the lowland floodplain of Khuzestan fed by rivers descending from the Zagros, because this region offers an unusually well-documented archaeological sequence spanning the transition from village society to the early stages of urban and administrative life. It does not claim that Susiana was the only such region in the ancient Near East, nor that its trajectory was typical of every part of the Iranian plateau; the highland zones examined in Chapter 5's discussion of Anshan in Fars followed a related but distinct trajectory, a point this chapter will return to at its close.

Students should understand this chapter as occupying a specific and deliberately narrow place within the larger textbook. It does not attempt to narrate the full history of Elam, which Chapter 5 will undertake once the evidence permits a specifically historical and political narrative naming identifiable rulers, cities, and interactions with Mesopotamian states. Nor does it attempt to explain the origins of agriculture, sedentism, or domestication, which Chapter 3 has already addressed. Its task is narrower and, in some ways, more difficult: to explain how and why some communities in southwestern Iran grew larger, more specialized, and more administratively coordinated than others, without assuming that this growth was either inevitable or destined to produce any particular later outcome.

Section 4.1

What Is a Complex Society?

Archaeologists use the term social complexity to describe a cluster of traits that recur when human communities organize themselves at a scale and with a degree of internal differentiation beyond the individual household or small village. These traits include, but are not limited to, differences in settlement size across a region, specialization in craft production, monumental or communal construction requiring coordinated labor, evidence of administrative technologies for recording and tracking goods, and unequal access to certain kinds of goods, spaces, or treatment in death. No single trait, taken alone, demonstrates that a society has become "complex" in this sense; archaeologists look instead for combinations of evidence that, together, suggest organization beyond what a single household or an undifferentiated village could sustain.

It is essential, and this chapter insists on the point repeatedly, that social complexity in this archaeological sense is not a synonym for civilization, urbanism, or the state. A civilization, as later chapters will use the term, typically implies a durable historical tradition with cities, writing or comparable record-keeping, and a recognizable political and cultural identity that historians can trace across generations. A state implies specific institutions of centralized political authority, capable of making and enforcing binding decisions over a defined population and territory. Urbanism implies settlements of a particular scale, density, and functional diversity, typically supporting populations who do not produce their own food. The societies examined in this chapter display some, but not all, and not always all at once, of the traits associated with these later, more specific categories. A settlement can show evidence of specialized craft production and administrative record-keeping without yet functioning as a city in the fuller sense, and a region can show settlement hierarchy without yet possessing a centralized state capable of enforcing decisions across that entire hierarchy.

This caution matters because it protects the chapter, and the textbook as a whole, from two related errors. The first error assumes that once a few markers of complexity appear, a state or civilization must already exist, collapsing centuries of gradual and uneven change into a single dramatic threshold. The second error assumes the opposite, that nothing meaningful happened until a fully formed state or civilization appeared, erasing the substantial organizational achievements of the communities examined in this chapter. This textbook instead treats social complexity as a spectrum and a process, unfolding unevenly across different sites and different centuries, rather than as a single line to be crossed.

Section 4.2

From Villages to Regional Settlement Systems

Chapter 3 examined evidence primarily at the scale of the household and the individual village: hearths, storage pits, house floors, and village cemeteries. This chapter requires a different analytical scale, one that historians and archaeologists describe as the regional settlement system. Rather than asking what happened inside a single village, this approach asks how an entire landscape of settlements, some larger, some smaller, some short-lived, some long-lived, related to one another over time.

Archaeologists reconstruct regional settlement systems primarily through settlement survey, the systematic walking and recording of a landscape to identify the location, size, and approximate period of occupation of every detectable ancient site within a defined area. Combined with excavation at selected sites, survey data allow archaeologists to reconstruct site-size distributions, the pattern of how many settlements of each size existed in a region during a given period. When a settlement system contains many small sites and very few large ones, and when the larger sites show evidence of specialized functions, administrative activity, or monumental construction not found at smaller sites, archaeologists describe this pattern as a settlement hierarchy, a key indicator, though never proof by itself, of growing regional coordination.

Applying this method to Susiana allows historians to move beyond a simple story in which one heroic site, Susa, single-handedly created civilization in southwestern Iran. Instead, survey and excavation together reveal a changing landscape across the fifth and early fourth millennia BCE, in which different settlements rose and fell in relative importance, some growing into substantial regional centers before eventually declining, others enduring and eventually surpassing their neighbors. Understanding this changing landscape, rather than focusing exclusively on Susa from the outset, is essential to the argument this chapter develops.

Archaeological Method

Settlement Survey and Site Hierarchy

Settlement survey involves systematically examining a defined region, often using a combination of pedestrian walkover, surface artifact collection, and increasingly remote sensing and satellite imagery, to locate every identifiable ancient site. For each site, archaeologists record its approximate size, based on the extent of surface debris or the footprint of a mounded ruin (a tell), and its approximate period of occupation, based on datable surface pottery.

By comparing site sizes and dates across an entire survey region, archaeologists can reconstruct how the number, size, and spacing of settlements changed over time. A landscape with numerous similarly sized small villages and no larger centers suggests a relatively undifferentiated settlement system. A landscape with a few substantially larger sites, particularly when those larger sites also show evidence of specialized production, monumental architecture, or administrative objects absent from smaller sites, suggests the beginnings of settlement hierarchy and regional coordination.

This method has clear limitations. Surface survey cannot detect sites buried deeply beneath later deposits or destroyed by erosion, agriculture, or modern development; it can only estimate, rather than precisely measure, a site's population; and it depends on the survival and recognizability of diagnostic surface pottery for each period. Despite these limitations, settlement survey remains one of the most powerful tools available for reconstructing regional history in periods that left no written records.

Method Analysis Questions:

  1. Why can settlement size alone not prove that a site held centralized political authority over smaller sites nearby?
  2. What kinds of evidence, beyond size, would strengthen an argument that a large site functioned as a genuine regional center?
  3. Why might some ancient settlements be systematically underrepresented in a modern survey?
Section 4.3

The Susiana Plain and Southwestern Khuzestan

The Susiana plain, the lowland floodplain of southwestern Khuzestan, provides this chapter's principal geographic setting. Fed by rivers descending from the Zagros Mountains, including the watercourses in the vicinity of ancient Susa, the plain offered more reliable water and richer alluvial soils than much of the interior Iranian plateau, supporting agricultural productivity broadly comparable, though on a smaller scale, to the great river valleys of lower Mesopotamia examined in the parallel volume of this series. Its position, between the Zagros highlands to the east and the Mesopotamian lowlands to the west, also placed it along routes of exchange and interaction connecting highland and lowland populations across many centuries.

Students should resist treating this geography as a determining cause of Susiana's later prominence. Favorable agricultural conditions and strategic location created possibilities; they did not by themselves produce social complexity, which depended equally on the choices, labor, and social relationships of the people living in the region. Other lowland zones with comparably favorable conditions did not necessarily follow an identical trajectory, and Susiana's own history, as the next sections will show, was neither smooth nor predetermined. Nonetheless, the plain's combination of reliable water, productive soils, and connective position provided a durable environmental context within which the specific developments examined in this chapter took place.

The neighboring Deh Luran plain, a smaller lowland zone to the northwest, offers a useful point of comparison. Deh Luran's own Neolithic and early village sequence, examined briefly in Chapter 3, followed a broadly similar trajectory of agricultural intensification and village growth, but it did not develop settlement centers on the scale eventually achieved in Susiana. This contrast reminds students that similar environmental starting points did not automatically produce identical historical outcomes, reinforcing the chapter's broader caution against environmental determinism.

Section 4.4

Chogha Mish and the Growth of Early Regional Centers

Chogha Mish, located in the Susiana plain, provides this chapter's first detailed case study in growing organizational complexity. Excavations at the site revealed a long occupational sequence spanning the local Early, Middle, and Late Susiana ceramic phases, documenting continuous local development rather than a sudden, externally introduced transformation. Across this sequence, the settlement grew substantially in size, and its later phases show increasing evidence of specialized craft production, more elaborate architecture, and, by the mid-to-later fifth millennium BCE, some of the earliest administrative objects known from the region, including tokens and sealed clay lumps associated with the control of goods.

The significance of Chogha Mish lies in what its long sequence shows, and also in what it does not show: a smooth, uninterrupted rise. During the Late Middle Susiana phase, dated by one current chronological scheme to approximately 5000 to 4600 BCE, Chogha Mish grew to exceptional size and supported a massive monumental building along with specialized craft production. This phase has also produced the earliest securely attested administrative objects yet found at the site, a small number of clay tokens and a handful of clay sealings, evidence that some form of counting or control over goods was already in practice. Toward the end of this sequence, however, the monumental building was destroyed by fire, and the large settlement experienced major disruption or abandonment. Occupation during the subsequent Late Susiana phase, approximately 4600 to 4000 BCE, continued on a markedly reduced scale, though stamp seals and further sealings are known from this later occupation, even if some come from contexts whose dating is less secure.

Students should understand Chogha Mish neither as a failed predecessor eclipsed by Susa nor as a simple stepping-stone in a smooth, inevitable progression. Its long sequence, including its dramatic mid-sequence disruption, demonstrates that the organizational innovations examined throughout this chapter, specialized production, monumental construction, and early administrative technology, developed independently at Chogha Mish well before Susa's later rise, and that growth in this period could be interrupted or reversed rather than following a single upward line. Susa's later prominence should be understood as one outcome among several possible regional trajectories rather than a predetermined destination.

Section 4.5

Settlement Hierarchy, Aggregation, and Regional Competition

Excavation of the lowland Susiana landscape has substantially complicated any simple two-site narrative in which Chogha Mish gave way directly to Susa. Additional locations, designated by archaeologists using the site codes KS-04 (Chogha Do Sar), KS-59 (Abu Fanduweh), and KS-108 (Beladieh), were excavated in 2004 and 2006, and the full stratified results of that work were comprehensively published only in 2026. That publication identifies these three sites, together with Chogha Mish and Susa, as major fourth-millennium BCE polities, substantially strengthening earlier interpretations that had rested largely on surface survey. This evidence indicates that the Susiana plain supported a genuinely multi-center regional landscape across the fourth millennium BCE, rather than a landscape dominated from the outset by a single inevitable capital.

This evidence matters for how students should understand the emergence of settlement hierarchy in Susiana. Rather than picturing a single line of development running neatly from Chogha Mish to Susa, historians should picture a shifting network of substantial communities, each with its own trajectory of growth, specialization, and, in some cases, eventual decline or absorption into a larger neighbor. Some of these centers may have competed for control of agricultural land, water, labor, or access to exchange networks; others may have cooperated, exchanged goods and personnel, or shared administrative practices such as the use of similar seal designs or token types. The archaeological evidence available at present cannot fully reconstruct the specific relationships, whether competitive, cooperative, or some combination of the two, among these centers, and historians should be cautious about assuming that competition alone explains why some settlements grew while others did not.

What this evidence firmly establishes is that Susa's eventual dominance, examined later in this chapter, emerged from within an already complex regional landscape rather than from an empty or undifferentiated one. This reframing matters directly for Chapter 5: when Elam later appears as a documented political civilization, its territorial core in lowland Khuzestan inherited a landscape already shaped by centuries of shifting settlement hierarchy, not a landscape awaiting its first organized community.

Section 4.6

Craft Specialization and the Organization of Production

Craft specialization refers to the concentration of particular kinds of production, most visibly pottery manufacture, but also stone working, shell and bead production, and, by the period's later stages, early metalworking, in the hands of skilled producers working for consumers beyond their own household. Archaeologists detect specialization through several kinds of evidence: increasing standardization in the size, shape, and decoration of finished objects, evidence of dedicated production spaces such as kilns and workshops, concentrations of production debris such as wasters and tools at particular locations within a settlement, and skill levels in finished products, particularly finely painted pottery, that suggest extensive training rather than occasional household production.

The painted pottery traditions of Susiana, refined across the Early, Middle, and Late Susiana ceramic phases and reaching a particularly accomplished level in the Susa I painted wares examined later in this chapter, provide some of the clearest evidence for this kind of specialization. The consistency of certain motifs and vessel forms across many examples suggests that skilled potters, rather than every household independently, were producing a significant share of the finest painted vessels, even if simpler domestic pottery continued to be made more broadly.

Craft specialization matters historically because it can restructure economic relationships. As specialization intensified, especially where production occupied a substantial share of a producer's labor and time, specialist households or workshops may have depended increasingly on exchange with other households or on institutional provisioning for food and raw materials. Specialization, however, need not imply that every craft producer worked full time or ceased producing food altogether; part-time and seasonal specialization, combined with continued farming or herding, remained entirely possible. Where dependence on exchange did grow, it created a form of interdependence that is itself a marker of growing social complexity, since it requires mechanisms, whether informal exchange, market-like transactions, or more formal administrative allocation, to coordinate specialized producers with the wider community. The administrative technologies examined later in this chapter, tokens, seals, and sealings, may have developed in part to help manage this kind of coordination.

Section 4.7

Storage, Surplus, Labor, and Social Differentiation

Chapter 3 introduced household-scale storage as a foundation of early village economies, while cautioning against assuming that all Neolithic communities were economically or socially undifferentiated. This chapter extends that discussion to the regional scale. At larger Susiana settlements, archaeologists have identified storage facilities, and eventually monumental construction, whose scale exceeds what a single household's labor could plausibly have produced without coordination across many families.

The clearest example is the major mudbrick platform constructed at early Susa, examined in detail in Section 4.9, whose scale required the sustained, organized labor of a substantial workforce over an extended period. Comparable, if smaller, communal construction efforts are attested at other Susiana centers. Such projects imply some mechanism, whether voluntary communal labor, obligation to a religious or kin-based authority, or a more formal system of labor mobilization, for coordinating many individuals' work toward a shared goal. Archaeology alone cannot determine which of these mechanisms operated in a given case, and historians should resist assuming that monumental construction automatically implies coercive labor or a powerful ruling class; comparable communal projects are well documented among societies without centralized states, motivated by religious devotion, kinship obligation, or collective prestige.

Mortuary evidence, examined more fully in Section 4.10, offers a second line of evidence for social differentiation. Where some burials include substantially more elaborate grave goods, more finely made pottery, or more prominent placement than others, archaeologists infer that Susiana society was not perfectly egalitarian by this period. However, differentiation in death does not translate in any simple, mechanical way into precise political or economic ranks; it may reflect age, gender, ritual role, craft specialization, or membership in a particular kin group as much as it reflects wealth or hereditary status. This chapter treats storage, monumental labor, and mortuary differentiation together as convergent, mutually reinforcing evidence for growing social complexity, while insisting that none of these forms of evidence, alone or together, proves the existence of a fully stratified class society or a centralized state.

Section 4.8

Seals, Tokens, and the Beginnings of Administration

Among the most historically significant developments examined in this chapter is the emergence of small-scale technologies associated with counting, marking authority or control, and recording transactions. Tokens, small fired or unfired clay objects made in a range of standardized geometric shapes, appear at Susiana sites from an early date. They are often interpreted as counters or accounting devices, particularly when found in later, more clearly administrative contexts, but the function of many earlier examples remains genuinely debated among specialists; research on comparable objects elsewhere in the Near East has shown that such geometric clay pieces could serve multiple purposes, and counting agricultural goods should not be assumed automatically for every specimen. Stamp seals, carved stone or clay objects bearing a distinctive design, were pressed into wet clay to create an impression, either directly onto a container, door, or storage surface, or onto a separate lump of clay, called a sealing, used to close or secure a container, room, string, or bundle of goods and to indicate the authority, identity, or access associated with the seal. A bulla refers more specifically, in current scholarly usage, to a distinctive ovoid or faceted clay piece commonly shaped around a string or cord; not every sealing is a bulla, and the two terms should not be used interchangeably.

At Susa, archaeologists have recovered approximately three hundred clay sealings, including bullae, from the site's early levels, a substantial number that indicates sealing practices were used with some regularity in the management of stored, secured, or transported goods. Because breaking a sealing was necessary to open whatever it secured, sealings functioned as a simple but effective form of accountability, allowing an absent authority, whether an individual, a household, or perhaps an emerging institution, to know whether something had been opened since it was last sealed. Over the following centuries, as the discussion of Section 4.11 will note, systems of numerical notation using tokens and sealed containers developed further across both Susiana and Mesopotamia, and by the fourth millennium BCE contributed, in ways that remain actively studied and debated, to the more clearly proto-literate and eventually literate administrative technologies examined at the opening of Chapter 5.

This chapter treats the appearance of tokens, seals, and sealings as evidence of an important development in social technology: physical means of recording or securing information about goods that did not depend solely on memory or personal trust. This represents a significant organizational development in its own right, whether or not it coincided with the emergence of a formal state. Some historians of administration and information technology have argued that systems of this kind fed into the later invention of writing; this remains an influential interpretation rather than a settled fact, and this textbook reserves the term proto-literacy primarily for the more clearly numerical and incipient script-bearing technologies of the later fourth millennium BCE, rather than applying it to every early token or ordinary stamp-sealing.

Material Source Window

Clay Tokens and Sealings from Early Susa

Type of evidence: Small fired and unfired clay objects, including geometric tokens and clay sealings, some of them bullae, bearing stamp-seal impressions · Context: Recovered from excavated levels at Susa associated with the site's earliest major occupation · Nature of evidence: Material and archaeological rather than textual; no accompanying written explanation survives from this period

Description: Excavators at Susa recovered approximately three hundred clay sealings from the site's early levels, along with tokens made in a range of simple standardized geometric shapes such as cones, spheres, and discs. Many sealings preserve the impressions of carved stamp seals bearing geometric or animal designs, and some retain traces of the surface or object they once secured, such as the mouth of a jar, a woven container, a door, or a length of string.

Interpretive summary: Specialists generally interpret these sealings as evidence of an emerging system for tracking and controlling access to stored, secured, or transported goods. The tokens are often given a similar counting or accounting interpretation, though their exact function in every case remains debated, and such objects need not have served only one purpose.

Analysis: The substantial number of sealings recovered suggests that sealing practices were not rare or exceptional but had become a fairly routine part of managing goods at early Susa. The variety of seal designs suggests that multiple individuals, households, or perhaps emerging institutional roles each used a distinct seal, implying a system in which more than one authority within the community could assert control over specific goods.

Limitations: Because no accompanying written text survives from this period, archaeologists cannot know with certainty what specific goods most sealings secured, who exactly held each seal, or how disputes over sealed goods might have been resolved. Interpretations rely on analogy with better-documented later administrative systems in Mesopotamia and Iran, which carries some risk of retrospective assumption.

Perspective: This evidence reflects the activity of whoever controlled or moved goods within the community; it says relatively little directly about individuals who did not participate in sealing practices, including many ordinary farmers and herders whose economic activity may have left little trace of this kind.

Section 4.9

The Rise of Susa

Having traced the gradual development of settlement hierarchy, craft specialization, and administrative technology across Susiana, this chapter now turns to Susa I, the early phase of occupation at the site that would eventually become one of the principal lowland political centers and capitals of later Elam. Susa emerged as a major regional center during the later fifth millennium BCE and increased in regional prominence through the fourth millennium; Susa I is commonly dated to approximately c. 4350/4200 to 3800 BCE, depending on the chronological scheme used, and, as with much of this period's chronology, precise dates remain subject to ongoing refinement and disagreement.

Early Susa is distinguished archaeologically by several converging lines of evidence. Excavators identified a substantial mudbrick platform, an artificial raised construction whose scale implies the coordinated labor discussed in Section 4.7, along with associated monumental or enclosing architecture. The settlement also produced evidence of intensive craft production, including the finely painted pottery examined in the next section, and, as already discussed, hundreds of clay sealings indicating routine administrative activity. Taken together, this evidence indicates that Susa, by this period, had become one of the largest and most organizationally sophisticated settlements yet identified anywhere in Susiana, a status it appears to have achieved by drawing together, concentrating, and in some respects surpassing organizational patterns already visible at Chogha Mish and the other centers discussed in Section 4.5.

This chapter deliberately frames Susa's rise as a culmination rather than a starting assumption. Students who have followed the argument through Sections 4.2 through 4.8 should now be able to explain Susa's prominence as the outcome of processes at work across the entire Susiana region, rather than treating Susa as an isolated, self-explaining phenomenon. At the same time, historians should avoid treating Susa's eventual dominance as inevitable. Other centers, as Section 4.5 established, had followed broadly comparable trajectories, and the specific historical, political, or environmental circumstances that allowed Susa in particular to achieve lasting regional prominence are not fully recoverable from the archaeological evidence alone. What is clear is that by the close of the period this chapter examines, Susa had become the most significant node within a regional system that had itself been developing in complexity for centuries.

Section 4.10

Burial, Ritual, and Community Identity at Susa I

Excavations at Susa uncovered an extensive cemetery associated with the site's early occupation, containing many hundreds of burials. The earliest excavators, working under early twentieth-century methods and standards of documentation, originally estimated a total in the thousands, but later reconstruction based on their own recorded figures suggests a more plausible total on the order of roughly 800 to 1,000 graves, and the exact number remains genuinely uncertain because of the limitations of the original excavation records. What is not in doubt is the scale of the accompanying material: several thousand ceramic vessels, many finely painted with geometric and stylized animal motifs and demonstrating considerable technical accomplishment, were recovered from the cemetery as a whole. The scale and quality of this funerary assemblage represents one of the chapter's most striking bodies of evidence, and it invites careful interpretation regarding ritual practice, community identity, and social differentiation.

The presence of a large, apparently communal cemetery suggests that burial practice at Susa involved shared ritual conventions recognized across a substantial community, rather than purely private household observance. Variation among graves, in the quantity, quality, and type of accompanying goods, provides evidence, consistent with the broader discussion in Section 4.7, that not all individuals were treated identically in death. Some burials include unusually fine or numerous painted vessels, which specialists have interpreted variously as markers of status, wealth, craft affiliation, ritual role, or membership in a particular family or lineage group.

Students must exercise particular caution when interpreting this evidence. Differences in grave goods do not translate directly into a specific social or political rank; a finely decorated vessel might mark the deceased's own achievements, a survivor's display of grief or status, ritual requirements associated with age or manner of death, or simply the vessel's availability at the time of burial, and archaeologists often cannot distinguish confidently among these possibilities. Moreover, the skill evident in the painted pottery accompanying many burials reinforces the craft specialization discussed in Section 4.6, suggesting that specialized potters produced vessels intended, at least in part, for funerary use, which further complicates any simple equation between grave wealth and the deceased's everyday economic status. The Susa I cemetery is best understood as powerful evidence for a community with shared ritual practices and some degree of social differentiation, rather than as a precise social ledger that can be read off directly into ranked classes.

Section 4.11

Susiana, Mesopotamia, and the Problem of External Influence

No account of Susiana's development during this period can avoid the question of its relationship to neighboring Mesopotamia, where broadly contemporary developments associated with the Ubaid cultural horizon, and later the more expansive Uruk period, were unfolding along a related but distinct trajectory. Susiana's painted pottery traditions, its use of tokens and sealings, and its participation in wider regional exchange networks all show clear points of contact and similarity with Mesopotamian developments, and archaeologists have long debated how to weigh local Iranian development against the possibility of Mesopotamian influence.

Older interpretive models, prevalent in earlier twentieth-century scholarship, sometimes assumed that civilization "arrived" in Iran from Mesopotamia, treating Susiana primarily as a recipient of innovations developed elsewhere. This chapter, consistent with more recent scholarship, treats such a model as inadequate. The long, largely continuous local ceramic sequence traced through the Early, Middle, and Late Susiana phases, together with the independent trajectories visible at Chogha Mish and the other centers discussed in Section 4.5, demonstrates that Susiana possessed its own sustained developmental momentum well before the period of most intensive Mesopotamian contact. At the same time, dismissing Mesopotamian interaction entirely would equally distort the evidence; shared pottery styles, comparable administrative technologies, and, during the later fourth millennium, more intensive contact associated with Uruk-period expansion indicate genuine and significant interregional exchange, imitation, and perhaps at times more direct forms of contact or population movement.

The most defensible position, and the one this textbook adopts, treats local development and interregional interaction as complementary rather than competing explanations. Susiana's communities developed increasingly complex settlement systems, craft specialization, and administrative technologies through their own internal social and economic processes, while simultaneously participating in, contributing to, and drawing upon a wider zone of exchange and interaction that included lowland Mesopotamia and, as Chapter 5 will further examine, highland Fars. Neither explanation alone accounts adequately for the evidence; both together produce a more historically accurate picture.

Section 4.12

From Complex Societies toward Elam

This chapter closes by drawing a firm line between what it has demonstrated and what remains for Chapter 5 to establish. By the close of the period examined here, Susiana had developed a settlement hierarchy encompassing multiple substantial centers; craft specialization producing standardized and technically accomplished goods, especially painted pottery; monumental construction requiring coordinated labor beyond the household; mortuary evidence for social differentiation and shared ritual practice; and increasingly sophisticated administrative technologies, tokens, seals, and sealings, for tracking and controlling goods. Susa itself had emerged as the most prominent node within this regional system.

None of this evidence, however, constitutes a fully documented historical or political narrative of the kind Chapter 5 requires. Archaeologists cannot yet name specific rulers, political institutions, or diplomatic relationships for this period, nor can they confidently apply the label "Elamite," in its later linguistic, cultural, or political sense, to the populations examined in this chapter. The term "Elam" and its associated historical narrative depend on a later body of evidence, increasingly formal administrative and eventually written records, together with references in Mesopotamian sources, that allow historians to speak with much greater confidence about specific places, rulers, and events. Chapter 5 will take up that evidence directly, examining Elam as a civilization linking lowland Susiana, the very landscape this chapter has traced, with highland Anshan in Fars, and tracing its documented political history from its earliest securely attested phases onward.

Students completing this chapter should therefore understand Susiana's fifth- and early fourth-millennium archaeological record as the essential foundation for, but not yet the substance of, Elamite history. The regional network of settlements, the specialized economy, and above all the administrative technologies examined in this chapter created the institutional and organizational groundwork on which a historically documented Elamite civilization could later be built. Precisely how, when, and through what further developments that transition occurred is the subject to which the textbook now turns.

Historical Debate

Local Development or Mesopotamian Influence?

The question: Did the growing social complexity of Susiana during the fifth and early fourth millennia BCE develop primarily through local processes internal to Iranian communities, or did it depend substantially on influence, contact, or population movement from Mesopotamia?

Interpretation A — Primarily local development: Some archaeologists emphasize the long, continuous local ceramic sequence and the independent trajectories visible at multiple Susiana sites as evidence that organizational complexity developed chiefly through internal social and economic processes, with Mesopotamian contact playing a secondary, reinforcing role.
Interpretation B — Substantial Mesopotamian influence: Other archaeologists point to shared pottery styles, comparable administrative technologies, and, especially in the later fourth millennium, intensified contact associated with Uruk-period expansion as evidence that interaction with Mesopotamia significantly shaped, accelerated, or in some respects directed developments in Susiana.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological record, ceramic sequences, administrative objects, and architectural remains, but weigh the significance of local continuity against the significance of shared or borrowed material culture differently.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and relative emphasis rather than the existence of contact itself, which most specialists accept; scholars differ chiefly on how much explanatory weight local processes deserve relative to interregional influence.

Why it matters: This textbook's account of Susiana's rise depends directly on how this debate is resolved; treating Mesopotamian influence as the primary cause risks depicting Iranian communities as passive recipients of civilization, while ignoring interaction risks obscuring genuine and historically significant contact.

Student task: Using the evidence discussed in Section 4.11, construct an argument for the interpretation you find more persuasive, or explain why a combined explanation better accounts for the evidence than either interpretation alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in a Growing Center: Perspectives on Susiana

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have identified across the evidence. They are not quotations from any named historical individual.

From the perspective of a farming household near a growing center such as Susa: Continuing to cultivate grain and manage livestock much as earlier generations had, while increasingly exchanging surplus produce for finely made pottery or other goods produced by specialists rather than made at home.
From the perspective of a specialized potter: Devoting most working time to producing finely painted vessels for exchange or for funerary use, depending on other households or an emerging institution to supply food and raw materials in return.
From the perspective of an individual entrusted with a stamp seal: Bearing responsibility for marking specific stores of grain, textiles, or other goods as sealed, and understanding that breaking such a seal without authority would be immediately visible to the wider community.
From the perspective of a resident of a smaller Susiana settlement, or of a community on the neighboring Deh Luran Plain: Observing the growing scale of Chogha Mish or Susa from a smaller community that retained more village-like organization, and experiencing regional change unevenly rather than uniformly across the plain.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Village to Regional Center in Susiana

What Changed

  • Settlement size and regional differentiation increased substantially, producing a genuine settlement hierarchy by the later fifth millennium BCE
  • Craft production, especially fine painted pottery, became increasingly specialized and standardized
  • Monumental construction requiring coordinated labor beyond the household appeared at Susa and, on a smaller scale, elsewhere
  • Administrative technologies, tokens, seals, and sealings, developed and spread across the region
  • Mortuary practice at Susa came to display more pronounced differentiation in grave goods than is typically documented in earlier village cemeteries

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture and pastoralism, as established in Chapter 3, remained the economic foundation supporting even the largest Susiana settlements
  • Household-level production and consumption continued alongside emerging craft specialization rather than disappearing entirely
  • The fourth millennium BCE shows increasingly state-like or state-level forms of regional organization in southwestern Iran, but the precise structure and degree of centralized political authority remain debated, and no textually documented political institution can yet be reconstructed with the specificity possible in later periods
  • The region's connection to, and interaction with, neighboring Mesopotamia and the Zagros highlands continued and, in some respects, intensified

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating Susa's rise as an abrupt revolution disconnected from earlier village society, while still allowing students to appreciate the genuine organizational transformation this chapter documents.

Cause and Effect

Factors Associated with Susa's Rise

Long-term regional background

Centuries of agricultural intensification, craft specialization, and settlement growth across Susiana, including the substantial, if interrupted, development already achieved at Chogha Mish and paralleled at other fourth-millennium centers such as KS-04, KS-59, and KS-108.

Possible contributing factors

Large-scale construction, concentrated craft production, and extensive sealing practices at Susa itself, associated with, though not demonstrated to have single-handedly produced, the settlement's growing prominence during the later fifth and fourth millennia BCE.

Observed outcome

Susa emerged as an increasingly prominent settlement within a regional system that had itself been growing more complex for centuries, eventually becoming one of the principal lowland political centers of later Elam.

Limits of inference

Archaeological evidence cannot fully explain why Susa, rather than Chogha Mish or another Susiana center, ultimately achieved lasting regional prominence; this textbook presents Susa's rise as the culmination of region-wide processes while withholding the fuller historical narrative of Elam until Chapter 5 can supply the necessary political and textual evidence.

Historical complexity: The features associated with Susa's growth should not be mistaken for a demonstrated causal explanation of its dominance; historians should treat the incomplete recoverability of this outcome as a genuine limit of the evidence rather than supply a confident but unsupported account.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life in a Growing Regional Center

The organizational changes examined in this chapter reshaped daily experience for the people of Susiana, even though most residents continued to farm, herd, and manage households much as earlier generations had. A farming family near Susa might now regularly exchange part of its harvest for pottery made by a specialized potter rather than making all its own vessels at home. A household near a workshop area might spend part of each day producing standardized goods for exchange rather than solely for its own consumption. An individual entrusted with a seal bore a new kind of social responsibility, one requiring trust from the wider community and accountability for the goods under that seal's protection.

At the same time, most Susiana residents likely remained primarily engaged in agriculture and herding, and the benefits and burdens of growing organizational complexity were probably not shared equally across the population. Those buried with especially fine painted pottery in the Susa cemetery may represent individuals or families who benefited disproportionately from craft specialization, administrative roles, or ritual status, while many ordinary farming and herding families likely experienced more modest change. Because most surviving evidence comes from monumental architecture, elaborate burials, and administrative objects, all of which are more likely to have belonged to relatively prominent individuals or institutions, historians must read this evidence carefully, remaining alert to the many Susiana residents whose daily lives left less archaeologically visible traces.

Historical Vocabulary
Social Complexity

Definition: A cluster of archaeologically detectable traits, including settlement differentiation, craft specialization, monumental construction, administrative technology, and unequal access to certain goods or treatments in death, that together indicate organization beyond the scale of the individual household or undifferentiated village.

Historical context: Archaeologists developed this concept to describe gradual, uneven processes of organizational change that do not always coincide with, and should not be equated with, the emergence of a state, a city, or a named historical civilization such as Elam.

Why it matters: Using "social complexity" rather than "civilization" or "state" allows this chapter to describe genuine and significant organizational change in Susiana without overstating what the fifth- and early fourth-millennium evidence can support.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not conclude that "complex" societies are more advanced or superior to village societies in a moral or evolutionary sense, nor that complexity, once it appears, automatically continues to increase without interruption, reversal, or regional variation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transition from the household- and village-scale societies examined in Chapter 3 to the regional settlement systems and administrative innovations that emerged across the Susiana plain of southwestern Khuzestan during the fifth and early fourth millennia BCE. It began by carefully defining social complexity as a cluster of archaeologically detectable traits, settlement differentiation, craft specialization, monumental construction, administrative technology, and unequal treatment in death, while insisting that this concept is distinct from civilization, urbanism, the state, and Elam specifically.

The chapter then moved from the household scale to the regional scale, introducing settlement survey and site-size distribution as the methods by which archaeologists reconstruct regional organization. It established the geography of the Susiana plain, fed by rivers descending from the Zagros, as a productive but not deterministically privileged environment, using the comparison with the neighboring Deh Luran plain to caution against environmental determinism.

Chogha Mish provided the chapter's first detailed case study, demonstrating that craft specialization, monumental construction, and early administrative objects developed there substantially, though not smoothly or without interruption, well before Susa achieved regional prominence; its Late Middle Susiana peak, including a massive monumental building, ended in destruction by fire and significant disruption. The chapter then broadened this picture further, drawing on the fully published results of the 2004–2006 excavations at KS-04, KS-59, and KS-108 to establish that fourth-millennium Susiana supported a genuinely multi-center landscape of major polities, with several substantial settlements developing in parallel rather than a single settlement dominating the plain from the outset.

Craft specialization, particularly in painted pottery production, emerged as a central theme, restructuring economic relationships among households and creating new forms of interdependence. Storage, monumental construction, and mortuary evidence together suggested growing, though not precisely measurable, social differentiation, while the chapter repeatedly cautioned against reading any single form of evidence as conclusive proof of hereditary class distinctions or centralized political authority.

Stamp seals and clay sealings received particular emphasis as strong evidence for increasingly formal practices of securing goods and marking authority or access at early Susa. Geometric clay tokens were also present and are often interpreted as counters or accounting devices, although their precise functions remain debated. Together, these materials illustrate the expanding use of external physical devices in economic and administrative practice before writing.

Susa's own rise was presented as the culmination of these region-wide processes rather than as a self-explaining or inevitable event. Its major mudbrick platform, intensive craft production, approximately three hundred administrative sealings, and large cemetery, containing many hundreds of burials, perhaps on the order of 800 to 1,000, and thousands of finely painted ceramic vessels, together indicate that Susa had become, across the later fifth and fourth millennia BCE, an increasingly prominent center within an already complex regional system. The chapter examined this cemetery carefully, showing how mortuary evidence can suggest social differentiation and shared ritual practice without permitting confident, precise conclusions about specific social ranks, and noting that the exact burial count remains uncertain due to early excavation methods.

The chapter also addressed the long-debated relationship between Susiana and Mesopotamia, rejecting older models in which civilization was assumed to have arrived in Iran from Mesopotamia, while equally rejecting any account that ignores genuine and significant interregional interaction. It concluded that local development and interregional exchange operated as complementary explanations for Susiana's trajectory during this period.

Finally, the chapter drew an explicit boundary between its own archaeological account of growing complexity and the specifically historical, political, and textual narrative of Elam that Chapter 5 will introduce. By the close of this chapter, Susiana possessed settlement hierarchy, craft specialization, monumental construction, administrative technology, and a prominent regional center at Susa, but not yet a documented political history, named rulers, or a securely attested Elamite identity. That transition, from archaeological complexity to historical civilization, is the subject Chapter 5 now takes up.

Students should carry forward from this chapter both a substantive understanding of how organizational complexity developed in Susiana and a methodological caution against collapsing archaeological evidence too quickly into later historical categories, a caution that will remain useful throughout the textbook's subsequent treatment of Elam, the Achaemenid Empire, and beyond.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced one of the most consequential transitions in the history of southwestern Iran: the shift from village-scale society to a regional landscape of differentiated settlements, specialized production, and formal administrative technology. That transition did not happen instantly, and it did not happen at a single site acting alone. It emerged across an entire regional system, at Chogha Mish, at the fourth-millennium centers of KS-04, KS-59, and KS-108, and finally, most visibly, at Susa itself.

The habits of mind this chapter has modeled, distinguishing social complexity from civilization and the state, reading settlement patterns rather than assuming a single inevitable center, treating monumental construction and grave goods as suggestive rather than conclusive evidence, and weighing local development against interregional interaction without collapsing into either extreme, are not incidental to this chapter's subject. They are essential tools for the responsible study of any period for which the surviving evidence is archaeological rather than textual, and they will remain relevant as the textbook moves into periods with increasingly abundant written sources.

What this chapter has deliberately withheld is equally important. It has not named a single Elamite ruler, narrated a single political event, or declared that a state existed in southwestern Iran by the close of the fourth millennium BCE. That restraint is not a gap in the story; it is the story's central lesson. The organizational achievements traced in this chapter, settlement hierarchy, craft specialization, monumental labor, and above all the invention of durable, externalized administrative technology, created the conditions from which a historically documented civilization could emerge, without themselves constituting that civilization.

Chapter 5 now takes up the history of Elam directly, tracing how the archaeological landscape examined here gave way to a documented political civilization linking lowland Susiana with highland Anshan in Fars. Students who have absorbed this chapter's careful distinctions, between complexity and the state, between suggestive and conclusive evidence, and between archaeology and history, are well prepared to evaluate that civilization's origins with the same rigor and caution this chapter has modeled.
