Ismailism and the Nizari State in Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

For about a hundred and sixty-six years, a community of Ismaili Muslims governed a chain of mountain fortresses, valleys, and settlements across Iran, organized around a distinctive understanding of religious authority. Their enemies remembered them chiefly for a handful of political killings. The historical Nizari state was larger, more settled, and more intellectually serious than that memory allows, and it was destroyed in 1256 by an enemy against which its fortifications could not hold.

In the closing weeks of 1256, the Persian administrator and historian Ata-Malik Juvayni accompanied the Mongol army of Hulagu into the valley of Alamut, in the Alborz mountains north of Qazvin. The fortress above him had been the headquarters of the Nizari Ismailis since 1090, and its last lord had already ordered its surrender. Juvayni, whose family had served Mongol rulers for a generation, later wrote that he was permitted to inspect the fortress's library. He reports removing some works he thought worth keeping and destroying others he considered the products of heresy. We know of his visit because he described it himself, in a book written for a Mongol court and hostile to the people whose library he sorted. That single scene contains the problem of the whole chapter: a great deal of what survives about the Nizaris was written by their enemies, and some of what survives was preserved by the very person who helped destroy the rest.

The people whose fortress he entered were Nizari Ismailis, adherents of a branch of Shiʿi Islam that had emerged from a dispute over the succession to the Fatimid caliphate in 1094. Their founding leader in Iran, Hasan-i Sabbah, had seized Alamut four years earlier, when the Iranian Ismailis still looked to Cairo. Over the next century and a half, the Nizaris built a territorial state of unusual shape. It consisted not of one contiguous province but of fortified districts scattered across the Rudbar region of the Alborz, Qumis, Quhistan in eastern Iran, and, from a distinct center, in the mountains of Syria. The state endured against the Seljuks, the Khwarazmshahs, and a succession of local rulers who could outnumber it but could not easily dislodge it.

This chapter treats that state as a religiously organized political society. It asks how an existing missionary network became a sovereign power; how a scattered, mountainous territory survived the hostility of stronger neighbors; how Nizari leaders understood and redefined religious authority, most dramatically in the proclamation of the Qiyama at Alamut in 1164; what ordinary communities of cultivators, artisans, soldiers, teachers, and women contributed to the state's survival; and why it fell to the Mongols. It also asks students to separate the community from the legends. The notion that Nizari warriors were drugged into obedience, or that a secret garden of paradise lay behind Alamut's walls, comes from outsiders' stories, many of them composed long after the events they claim to describe.

The chapter's governing argument is that the Nizari state should be understood as a religious community that acquired territory, built institutions to defend it, and developed its own intellectual traditions, rather than as a clandestine organization whose main activity was assassination. Targeted killings were real, and Nizari fighters known as fidaʾis carried them out. They were also one instrument among several, used by a weaker party against a stronger one, and their prominence in the sources reflects the alarm they caused rather than their share of Nizari life. Most of the state's population was occupied, like other medieval Iranians, in cultivation, herding, craft work, and the maintenance of fortifications and storehouses.

The evidence for the Nizari state is among the most unevenly distributed in medieval Iranian history. The Nizaris produced their own books, and Alamut held a substantial library, but the destruction of 1256 and later losses mean that most of that literature survives only indirectly. The principal narrative accounts come from outsiders: Juvayni's history, the work of Rashid al-Din, which drew on material from Alamut and which this chapter treats critically, Sunni chronicles such as those of Ibn al-Athir, Syrian chroniclers, and Crusader writers who encountered the Syrian Nizaris. Nizari texts that survive were largely preserved in communities in Syria, Badakhshan, and elsewhere, and recovered by modern scholarship. A historian must therefore ask of every source who wrote it, in what circumstances, and with what motive.

Within the textbook, this chapter follows Chapter 38, which narrated the Seljuk conquest and the political history of the sultanate, and Chapter 39, which introduced Nizam al-Mulk and noted the disputed attribution of his assassination. Chapter 40 described the wider religious landscape and gave concise Ismaili background, and the present chapter does not repeat that survey. It anticipates Chapter 42, which treats Persian literary culture and historical memory, and Chapter 43, which treats learning and science, including the career of Nasir al-Din Tusi. The Mongol conquest of Iran belongs to Volume IV, beginning with Chapter 45, and this chapter follows the Nizari story only to the fall of Alamut and the aftermath in the surviving fortresses and communities.

Historical Context

By the 1050s, the Muslim world contained two rival caliphates. The Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad, supervised first by the Buyids and from 1055 by the Seljuks, claimed leadership of the Sunni majority, as Chapters 36 through 38 described. The Fatimid caliphs in Cairo, who had ruled North Africa from 909 and Egypt from 969, belonged to the Ismaili branch of Shiʿi Islam and claimed the same universal office on different grounds. They sustained a network of missionaries across the Islamic world, including Iran and Central Asia. Chapter 40 surveyed the wider religious landscape, including Shiʿi communities, and the sections that follow add detail only about the Ismailis.

Iran was predominantly Sunni at this date, though its religious map was varied, as Chapter 40 explained. The Seljuk sultans ruled with the recognition of the Abbasid caliph and presented themselves as defenders of Sunni Islam. Their vizier Nizam al-Mulk supported Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars through a network of colleges and treated Ismailis as a political and religious danger, as Chapter 39 described. This political order gave the Ismailis powerful opponents. It also gave them a grievance and an opportunity: a Sunni sultanate with a mobile court and limited reach into mountain districts offered gaps through which a determined, well-organized community could establish itself.

The geography of northern and eastern Iran shaped what became possible. Chapter 1 introduced the Alborz and the Caspian littoral as an environmental boundary between the arid plateau and a humid forested coast. The valleys of the Alborz, including the Rudbar region around Alamut, are enclosed by steep ridges and approached by narrow routes, and they supported agriculture in valleys and pastoralism on slopes. Other fortified sites lay in Qumis along the northern edge of the central deserts and in Quhistan in the southeast of Khurasan, regions of oases and hill country at some distance from the major cities. The Nizari territory was therefore not a continuous state but a set of defensible highland districts, each linked to the others by religious allegiance and by messengers rather than by roads that a ruler could easily control.

Politically, Seljuk authority was weakened after 1092. Chapter 38 described the deaths of Nizam al-Mulk and Malik Shah, the civil wars among Malik Shah's sons, the division of the empire, and the growing power of atabegs and regional commanders. Those developments gave the Nizaris time and political space in the decades after the capture of Alamut. They did not make Nizari success inevitable. The Seljuks and their successors campaigned repeatedly against the fortresses, and Sunni populations in several cities attacked Ismaili communities, sometimes massacring them.

Intellectually, the Ismaili tradition was old. Ismaili writers had developed elaborate philosophical and theological systems in the tenth and eleventh centuries, some of them in Iran, and the Nizaris inherited that tradition even as they altered it. They also drew on a Persian-language scholarly culture that Chapter 35 and Chapter 37 described. The Nizari state therefore belongs to the history of Persianate learning as well as to the history of political and religious conflict, a point developed in §41.12.

Section 41.1

Ismailism before the Nizaris: Imamate, Fatimids, and the Daʿwa

Ismailism is a branch of Shiʿi Islam, and its distinguishing feature is a particular answer to a question that divided Muslims from the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632: who is entitled to lead the community, and by what authority? Shiʿi Muslims in general held that leadership belonged to the Prophet's family through ʿAli ibn Abi Talib and his descendants, and that the leader, the imam, possessed a unique religious knowledge and authority. The Shiʿa divided over the identity of the imams. After the death of the sixth imam, Jaʿfar al-Sadiq, in 765, one group followed his son Musa al-Kazim and eventually formed the Twelver tradition, which Chapter 40 discussed. Another group held that the imamate had passed to Jaʿfar's son Ismaʿil, or to Ismaʿil's son Muhammad, and from this recognition the Ismailis take their name. The early history of the group is poorly documented, and the surviving accounts were largely written after the Ismaili movement had become organized and had developed its own historical memory.

For the study of Iran, the decisive development was the creation of the Fatimid caliphate. In 909 an Ismaili imam, ʿAbd Allah (known as al-Mahdi), established his rule in Ifriqiya, in what is now Tunisia, with the support of Kutama Berber communities who had been won over by Ismaili missionaries. In 969 Fatimid armies conquered Egypt, and in 973 the dynasty moved its seat to the newly founded city of Cairo. The Fatimids were therefore unusual in the history of Shiʿism: their imams were also the rulers of a major state. They claimed an authority that rivaled the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad, and they supported that claim through diplomacy, scholarship, and an organized missionary system.

That system was the daʿwa, literally the "summons" or "invitation" to the true religion. The daʿwa was both a religious teaching mission and a political organization. It was directed by the imam and his chief officials in Cairo, and it divided the Islamic world into regions, called jazaʾir ("islands"), each supervised by a chief missionary or daʿi. Below the chief missionaries worked local daʿis who taught prospective converts in stages, gradually introducing the inner interpretation of the faith to those who had proved reliable. Some missionaries acted as political agents as well, building networks of support in places where the Fatimids had no armies. The Fatimid daʿwa reached Yemen, Sind, Iraq, and Iran, and it competed with Abbasid authority in precisely the regions where the Abbasids and their Buyid and Seljuk protectors were weakest.

The daʿwa also produced an intellectual tradition. Ismaili missionaries and thinkers in the ninth and tenth centuries, many of them working in Iran and Central Asia, developed a sophisticated cosmology and philosophy that drew on Neoplatonic ideas, Islamic law, and Qurʾanic interpretation. Figures such as Abu Hatim al-Razi, Muhammad al-Nasafi, Abu Yaʿqub al-Sijistani, and Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani wrote in this tradition, and their works circulated among Ismaili communities. Their significance for this chapter is twofold. They show that Iranian Ismailism before Alamut was a learned tradition and not simply a clandestine opposition. They also show that the Ismailis of Iran had a long record of independent thinking within a larger Fatimid framework, which helps explain why Iranian communities would later accept leadership from a local missionary and not from Cairo.

Not all Ismailis accepted Fatimid leadership at any given time. In the late ninth century a group known as the Qarmatis broke with the Fatimid line, and some of them established a state centered in eastern Arabia that lasted into the eleventh century. The Fatimid and Qarmati episodes belong to a long, complex history of Ismaili divisions, and they remind students that "Ismaili" never named a single unified organization. Each time the community divided, the groups involved produced their own claims about the imamate and their own accounts of the break. The division of 1094, which created the Nizaris, is the most consequential for Iran, and §41.5 examines it.

By the middle of the eleventh century, therefore, Iranian Ismailis were part of a network that looked to Cairo for religious authority and sometimes for guidance and funds. They were also a minority in a region where political power lay with Sunni rulers, and where Seljuk authorities, supported by a bureaucracy led by Nizam al-Mulk (Chapter 39), treated the Ismailis as a danger to the state and the faith. This was the setting in which Hasan-i Sabbah began his career. Understanding the Ismailis of this period requires holding two facts together: the daʿwa was a transregional organization with a coherent theology and a tested method of building support, and its Iranian adherents lived in a world in which open profession of their faith could be dangerous.

Historical Vocabulary
Daʿwa, Daʿi, Hujja, and Imam

Definition: The daʿwa ("summons" or "invitation") was the Ismaili system of religious teaching and organization. A daʿi ("summoner") was a missionary, whether a local teacher or the chief missionary of a region. A hujja ("proof") was, in Fatimid usage, a very senior figure, often the head of a region's daʿwa, who served as the imam's proof, that is, his representative and demonstrable authority. The imam was the divinely guided descendant of ʿAli and Fatima whom Ismailis held to be the legitimate leader of the Muslim community and the authoritative interpreter of the faith.

Historical context: These terms describe an organized hierarchy that operated from the tenth century, directed from Cairo by the Fatimid imam-caliphs and carried into Iran and Central Asia by local missionaries. After 1090 and especially after 1094, the Nizari Ismailis of Iran used the same vocabulary, but its application changed. Hasan-i Sabbah is remembered in Nizari tradition as a hujja of the imam, not as an imam himself, because the Nizari imam of his day was held to be hidden. Titles such as daʿi and hujja described functions, not hereditary rank.

Why it matters: Without these four terms the chapter's central distinction cannot be drawn: Alamut was governed by missionaries acting in the name of an imam, not by an imam in residence, at least until the later twelfth century.

Potential misunderstanding: "Daʿi" is sometimes rendered as "agent" or "spy" in hostile accounts. The word describes a religious teacher and organizer. The political work that daʿis sometimes performed was real, but it was part of a larger religious role.

Section 41.2

Missionaries and Intellectual Networks in Iran and Khurasan

Before Hasan-i Sabbah, Ismaili missionaries had already built communities across the Iranian world. Fatimid daʿis worked in western Iran, in the region historians call Iraq-i ʿAjam, with Isfahan and Rayy as important centers; in Khurasan and the lands to its east; in Kirman and the south; and in the mountain districts of Daylam and the Alborz. They reached cultivators and townspeople as well as scholars, courtiers, and officials. The movement's most visible Iranian figures wrote in Persian and Arabic, and their works circulated in the same scholarly networks as those of Sunni and Twelver authors.

One figure illustrates the character of this world better than any other: Nasir-i Khusraw (born 1004, died some time after 1070). He was a Persian poet, philosopher, and traveler from the region of Balkh, who held a post in the Seljuk-era administration of Khurasan and was by his own account dissatisfied with his life. In 1045 he left Merv on a journey that took him across Iran, Syria, Palestine, Arabia, and Egypt. He arrived in Cairo in 1047 and remained there for about three years, studying under Fatimid scholars and, by his own account, becoming committed to the Ismaili cause. He returned east in 1052, was appointed to lead the Ismaili daʿwa in Khurasan, and taught and wrote there. His open teaching drew opposition from local Sunni authorities, and he was eventually forced to leave. He spent the rest of his life in Yumgan, a valley in Badakhshan in the mountains of what is now northeastern Afghanistan, under the protection of a local ruler. Ismaili communities in the Pamir and Badakhshan region have preserved his memory and his writings ever since.

His surviving works include the travel narrative Safarnama, a collection of Persian poems, and philosophical treatises such as Khwan al-ikhwan, Zad al-musafirin, Wajh-i din, and Jamiʿ al-hikmatayn. These show that Persian had become a language of Ismaili philosophy and not only of administration or poetry, and that the intellectual tradition Ismailis carried into the Alamut period was already well developed. The Nizaris later inherited that heritage, even though Nasir-i Khusraw wrote for Fatimid Cairo and belonged to an earlier generation.

Primary Source Window

Nasir-i Khusraw, Safarnama (Book of Travels), mid-11th century

Creator: Nasir-i Khusraw, Persian writer and Fatimid daʿi · Date: account of a journey of 1045–1052, written afterward · Type: Persian prose travel narrative

Context: Nasir-i Khusraw recorded his seven-year journey from Khurasan to Cairo and back, including the pilgrimage to Mecca, in the Safarnama. He wrote after returning to the east, at a time when he was serving the Fatimid daʿwa and facing opposition in Khurasan.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Nasir-i Khusraw presents the journey as a response to a personal crisis. He says he gave up a secure official career and set out for the pilgrimage, and he devotes much of the narrative to describing the cities, markets, and religious sites he saw, with particular attention to Fatimid Cairo and its order, wealth, and public administration.

Analysis: The Safarnama shows how an Iranian intellectual could encounter Fatimid Egypt as a real, functioning society and not merely as a doctrine, and it reveals a movement with scholars, institutions, and wealth at its center. Its attention to buildings, markets, taxation, and the treatment of subjects is an unusual window into Fatimid governance as perceived by a visitor who was predisposed to admire it.

Limitations: The Safarnama is a retrospective account written by a committed partisan. It was shaped by its author's religious convictions and by his later role as a missionary, and it says little about the ordinary Ismailis of Iran or about the politics of his home region. It also predates the Nizari state by more than three decades.

Perspective: A learned, urban, Persian-speaking member of the administrative and scholarly class. The perspectives of cultivators, women, and the poorer converts of the daʿwa are not represented.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the author's reasons for travelling and about what he observed?
  2. Who created it, and under what circumstances did he write after his return?
  3. What purposes may have shaped the way Nasir-i Khusraw describes Fatimid Egypt?
  4. What can historians learn from it about the intellectual and institutional world of Fatimid Ismailism?
  5. What can they not establish from this source alone about Iranian Ismailism in the generation before Alamut?

Nasir-i Khusraw's career also shows how the daʿwa depended on patronage and protection. The communities that sheltered Ismaili missionaries were often in places that outside rulers found difficult to control: mountain valleys, border districts, and oases at a distance from major cities. The same logic would shape the Nizari state's choice of fortress sites. Daʿwa networks in cities such as Rayy and Isfahan, moreover, existed under the nose of Seljuk administrators and Sunni scholars who monitored them, and the experience of repression reinforced Ismaili caution. Members who revealed their beliefs risked loss of property, imprisonment, or death. Practices of discretion, often discussed under the heading of taqiyya (precautionary dissimulation), were common to several Shiʿi communities and gave hostile writers material for accusations of hypocrisy and secrecy.

By the late eleventh century the situation of the Iranian daʿwa was therefore double. It was part of a vast international organization with ties to a powerful caliphate and a distinguished intellectual tradition. It was also a persecuted minority operating in a region ruled by a hostile sultanate. Seljuk expansion had weakened local Ismaili protectors, and the sultanate's military and fiscal structure left little room for an open Ismaili community. The question facing missionary leaders was whether the daʿwa could survive indefinitely through concealment, or whether it needed a secure base of its own. Hasan-i Sabbah's answer to that question is the subject of the next two sections.

Section 41.3

Hasan-i Sabbah: Early Life, Conversion, and Strategy

The reconstruction of Hasan-i Sabbah's life begins with a methodological warning. No contemporary biography of Hasan survives in a form that historians can use directly. An autobiographical narrative attributed to him, Sargudhasht-i Sayyidna ("The Life of Our Master"), was preserved in the library at Alamut and used by Juvayni and, through him or from independent copies, by later historians such as Rashid al-Din. The original is lost. What historians possess are summaries and extracts embedded in the works of authors writing in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, who were hostile to the Nizaris and who had their own reasons to present Hasan in a particular light. Biographical details, particularly about his youth and about stories of conspiracies among his associates, should be treated as probable at best.

With that caution, a general outline is reasonably secure. Hasan ibn ʿAli ibn Muhammad al-Sabbah al-Himyari was born in Qom, probably in the 1040s or 1050s, to a family that traced descent from Yemeni Arabs. The family moved to Rayy, where Hasan grew up in a Twelver Shiʿi household and received a conventional education. In his twenties, according to the surviving tradition, he met Ismaili missionaries and became convinced of the case for the Ismaili imamate. He was formally initiated by the chief daʿi of western Iran, ʿAbd al-Malik ibn ʿAttash, around 1071 or 1072, and thereafter served the daʿwa.

In 1078 he travelled to Egypt, where he stayed in Fatimid territory for about three years, arriving in Cairo in 1078. His activities there are obscure. Nizari tradition, recorded in later sources, holds that he met the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir and that the caliph designated his elder son Nizar as heir. Whether this occurred in the form later told, or whether the story arose after 1094 to justify the Iranian community's break from Cairo, cannot be settled from the evidence. Hasan returned to Iran around 1081 and spent the next nine years travelling through the country, working as a missionary in Isfahan, Kirman, Yazd, Khuzestan, Qumis, and the mountains of Daylam. These were the years in which he assessed possible bases.

Hasan's strategic thinking shows in his choice of terrain. Rather than rely on one of the great cities, where Ismaili communities existed but were vulnerable, he looked for a defensible highland district whose population was receptive and whose ruler or garrison could be dislodged. He examined sites in Khuzestan, Kirman, and Damghan before settling on the Rudbar region in the Alborz, a district of steep-sided valleys that had long been home to Daylami and Shiʿi populations less closely tied to the Seljuk state. The fortress of Alamut, which stood on a rocky spur in the valley of the Alamut river, offered both strong natural defenses and a population that his missionaries had already begun to reach.

It is important to describe his position accurately. Hasan was a missionary and organizer, not an imam. After 1094 Nizari tradition held that the imam, a descendant of Nizar, was alive but in concealment (satr), and that the daʿwa was led in his name by a hujja. Hasan's authority rested on his role as the imam's proof, not on any claim to be the imam himself. Hostile sources sometimes blurred this distinction, calling him "Lord of Alamut" or an "Old Man of the Mountain," and some later European writers took such titles as evidence that he was the head of a religious order with absolute personal power. The evidence does not support that reading for Hasan. It does suggest that he combined doctrinal authority and military leadership in a way that was unusual and effective.

Historical Debate

How Can Historians Reconstruct Nizari History from Hostile Sources?

The question: Much of what we know about the Nizari state comes from outsiders: Sunni chroniclers, Mongol-era administrators, Syrian and Crusader writers. How far can such sources be trusted, and what can be recovered of the Nizari point of view?

Approach A — The polemical tradition as primary framework: For a long time, scholars working from Sunni and Mongol-era narratives treated the Nizaris largely as those narratives presented them: a secret order of political killers whose beliefs were a cover for subversion. This approach was understandable because the narrative sources were abundant and the Nizari texts were largely unavailable.
Approach B — Critical use of hostile sources, supplemented by Nizari texts: Since the twentieth century, historians have recovered and edited Ismaili manuscripts, many preserved in Syria, Badakhshan, and elsewhere, and have compared them with the hostile narratives. They treat accounts such as Juvayni's and Rashid al-Din's as evidence that must be tested against other material, including the Nizaris' own writings, coins, inscriptions, and archaeology, and read for the biases of their authors.

Evidence: The approaches use overlapping materials: Juvayni's Tarikh-i Jahangushay; Rashid al-Din's Jamiʿ al-tawarikh; Sunni chronicles such as those of Ibn al-Athir; Syrian and Crusader accounts; surviving Ismaili treatises; and archaeological surveys of Nizari fortresses.

Points of disagreement: The main disagreements concern interpretation and evidence. Historians differ over how far Juvayni's and Rashid al-Din's accounts of Hasan-i Sabbah reflect Nizari material that has since been lost, how much weight to give narratives of conspiracy, and how to treat the stories of drugging and secret gardens (§41.16). They do not generally dispute that the Nizaris existed as a political and religious community or that they carried out targeted killings.

Why it matters: The Nizaris' own point of view has to be reconstructed from fragments. A history that relies only on hostile sources will tend to reproduce their categories. A history that relies only on Nizari texts, most of which were composed after the Alamut period or are doctrinal in character, will miss what outsiders observed. The most defensible approach uses each source type for what it can reliably show.

Section 41.4

The Capture of Alamut, 1090

Alamut lies in the Rudbar district of the Alborz mountains, in a valley north of Qazvin. The fortress stands on a narrow rock ridge above the river and is approached by a steep, winding path. According to local tradition recorded in later sources, it was founded in the ninth century by a Daylami ruler, and the name has been explained, in a tradition that may be folk etymology, as meaning "eagle's teaching." Whatever its origin, the site's defensive value was considerable. At the time of Hasan-i Sabbah's arrival it was held by a local ruler, an ʿAlid named Mahdi, who recognized the authority of the Seljuk sultan.

Hasan's capture of Alamut was not the sudden coup that later retellings suggest. His missionaries had been working in the Rudbar for some time, and many residents of the valley and members of the garrison had been won over. Hasan himself arrived in the area in the summer of 1090, and according to the sources, he entered the fortress in disguise or as a guest and gradually took control. Juvayni reports that Mahdi was compelled to leave and was paid a sum of gold (the figure commonly given is 3,000 dinars). The date traditionally given is 4 September 1090, the sixth of Rajab, 483 AH, though some sources disagree on the day, and the exact circumstances may have been shaped by later retellings. What is not in doubt is that by the autumn of 1090 Hasan held the fortress and that Alamut subsequently became the center of the Nizari state.

The seizure of Alamut was less a military victory than a political and religious one. It required organizers who understood local relationships and patiently built allegiance. This is consistent with the daʿwa's earlier methods and helps explain why the capture, once achieved, was hard to reverse. The new regime did not simply occupy a fortress. It integrated the surrounding population into a religious community, and it began improving the fortifications and storing grain and other supplies. Later sources describe cisterns, storerooms, and a library, though the description of the fortress's features is largely drawn from the period of Mongol conquest, and the archaeology of the ruins is limited.

The Seljuk response was initially slow and then determined. Sultan Malik Shah, who ruled at the height of Seljuk power, sent an army under the commander Arslan Tash (some sources give other names) against the valley in 1092, and the siege seems to have begun but was interrupted by the sultan's death in November of that year. Malik Shah's death, together with the killing of Nizam al-Mulk a month earlier (§41.8), threw the Seljuk state into a succession struggle, as Chapter 38 explained. In the decade that followed, no coordinated Seljuk campaign was possible. This gave Hasan time to secure the Rudbar and to extend Ismaili influence to Qumis and Quhistan.

1090 should be kept distinct from 1094. In 1090 Alamut was taken by a missionary of the Fatimid daʿwa who still looked to Cairo. The Nizari identity, defined by recognition of Nizar and rejection of the Cairo succession, came four years later. Writers who describe the 1090 capture as "the founding of the Nizari state" are using a convenient shorthand, which this chapter retains, but the people who seized Alamut did not yet think of themselves as Nizaris. This distinction matters because it shows that the territorial base was created before the doctrinal break, and that the break was, in a sense, made easier by the existence of the base: a community with its own fortress could survive the loss of Cairo's support.

Cause and Effect

Why the Seizure of Alamut Succeeded

Long-term causes

An established Ismaili daʿwa in Iran; resentment of Seljuk fiscal and military demands; the independence of Daylami highland communities; and the weak reach of the Seljuk state into mountain districts.

Short-term causes

Hasan's years of survey and preparation; a missionary network already active in the Rudbar; and a garrison and local ruler of uncertain loyalty.

Immediate context

Seljuk attention was on the sultanate's eastern and western fronts, and Nizam al-Mulk's and Malik Shah's deaths in 1092 soon deepened the political crisis.

Immediate effects

A secure base for the daʿwa; a model for taking other fortresses; and a Seljuk campaign that was interrupted before it could succeed.

Long-term consequences: A territorial community with its own institutions, able to survive the Fatimid schism of 1094 and to endure for 166 years.

Historical complexity: The success of Alamut was not the product of one man's cunning or of geography alone. It depended on local support, patient organization, a fragmented Seljuk state, and a mountain environment that rewarded defenders, and each of these conditions could have failed to hold.

Section 41.5

The Succession Crisis of 1094

The Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir died in Cairo in December 1094 after a reign of nearly sixty years. His eldest surviving son, Nizar, had long been regarded by many as the heir. But the powerful vizier al-Afdal, who controlled the army and the administration, raised to the throne Nizar's much younger half-brother al-Mustaʿli, who was married to al-Afdal's sister. Nizar left Cairo, raised a revolt in Alexandria with support from some of the city's notables, and was defeated and captured in 1095. He is believed to have died in captivity shortly afterwards. The facts of the succession are better attested than the motives of the participants, and Nizari and Mustaʿli sources later gave opposing accounts of what al-Mustansir had intended.

The consequences for the Ismaili world were permanent. The Ismailis of Egypt, much of Yemen, and Gujarat, and the Fatimid state itself, accepted al-Mustaʿli and his successors, and are known as the Mustaʿlis. Hasan-i Sabbah and the Iranian Ismailis refused. They held that Nizar had been the legitimate imam designated by his father, and they broke off their allegiance to Cairo. Ismailis in Syria and elsewhere divided in different ways, and the Syrian Nizaris eventually established an independent community (§41.15). The split ended the unified Ismaili movement that had existed under Fatimid leadership, although the two branches long continued to dispute each other's claims in polemical writings.

The break had a theological as well as a political dimension. If the imam in direct line from Nizar was the legitimate leader, where was he? The Nizari answer, developed in the following decades, was that the imam existed but was hidden, living in concealment under the protection of a trusted hujja, and that in the interim the community must follow the teaching of the imam's authorized representatives. This arrangement placed Hasan-i Sabbah in the role of proof and effectively made Alamut the visible center of the imam's authority. It also bound the community to a living authority and not merely to a past one, a point that the doctrine of authoritative teaching (taʿlim) elaborated, as §41.11 explains.

Historians disagree about the extent to which the doctrine of the hidden imam was already clearly formulated in Hasan's lifetime. The details of how Nizar's descendants were hidden and who they were come mostly from later Nizari sources, composed after the proclamation of the Qiyama in 1164, when the imamate was openly claimed again. Those sources have an obvious interest in establishing a continuous line. The sober conclusion is that the doctrine of concealment emerged from the circumstances of 1094, took shape over several decades, and acquired its later form retrospectively. This is not a reason to dismiss the Nizari account. It is a reason to treat it as a tradition produced by a community with its own purposes.

The schism also changed how outsiders saw the Iranian Ismailis. After 1094, they no longer had the Fatimid caliphate behind them, and the Seljuks and other rulers could treat them as an isolated, local threat, while the Abbasid and Sunni establishment had less reason to fear the Fatimid connection. At the same time, the Nizaris had to rely on themselves. They could not expect military aid from Cairo and could no longer present themselves as part of a larger empire. Alamut became, in practice, the head of a distinct religious and political community with its own authority and its own destiny.

Section 41.6

The Fortress Network: Rudbar, Qumis, and Quhistan

The Nizari state grew by adding fortresses and districts. In the Rudbar, Alamut was soon joined by other strongholds in the valley and surrounding mountains, including Lamasar and Maymundiz, which later became important as the state's western and southern defensive points. In the following years Nizari communities took or acquired fortresses in other parts of Iran. In Qumis, the region along the southern foot of the Alborz east of Rayy, they eventually held Girdkuh, a hilltop fortress near Damghan that became one of the longest-lasting Nizari strongholds. In Quhistan, in the southeast of Khurasan, they gained control of a group of towns and rural districts including Tabas, Qain, and others, where the leader Husayn Qaini was a significant figure in the early decades. Each of these regions had its own local circumstances, and the stages by which each came under Nizari control are not equally well documented.

The network was not a single territory. Its parts were separated by hundreds of kilometers of desert, plateau, and Sunni-controlled land. The central Rudbar block around Alamut was the core; Qumis and Quhistan were distinct regional groupings, each with local leadership and local resources. Communication ran through messengers, and unity depended on religious allegiance and shared institutions more than on a continuous frontier. Historians have debated how to describe a state of this shape. One useful way is to see it as a chain of defensible districts, each able to withstand a siege for a long time, whose security depended on mutual support and on the weakness of any single opponent's ability to besiege several of them at once.

The fortress sites themselves share certain characteristics. They were built on rock outcrops or summits, with limited approaches, and they were provisioned with cisterns, storage chambers, and fortified walls. Archaeological surveys in the Alamut valley and elsewhere have identified remains of such features, though much of the physical evidence has been damaged by time, earthquakes, and the Mongol destruction of 1256. For the many smaller strongholds the evidence is limited, and a historian must be careful not to project the descriptions of the largest sites onto the whole network.

Section 41.7

The Seljuk–Nizari Struggle, 1092–1124

After the deaths of Malik Shah and Nizam al-Mulk in 1092, the Seljuk sultanate entered a period of civil war and fragmentation that Chapter 38 described. Malik Shah's sons and other claimants contested the succession, and regional commanders gained power. In that climate the Nizaris extended their reach. They expanded their holdings in the Rudbar, took Girdkuh in Qumis, and consolidated their position in Quhistan, where local discontent with Seljuk officials helped their cause. They also established or strengthened communities in cities such as Isfahan, Rayy, and Sava, where Ismailis lived under varying degrees of tolerance and repression.

Their most ambitious early success in a major city was in the region of Isfahan. A fortress called Shahdiz, built by the Seljuk sultan Malik Shah on a hill near the city, came under the control of the Ismaili leader Ahmad ibn ʿAbd al-Malik ibn ʿAttash, the son of the daʿi who had initiated Hasan-i Sabbah. Ahmad ruled from Shahdiz and extended his influence through the countryside, collecting taxes from local landowners. Sultan Muhammad Tapar, who emerged as the dominant ruler among the Seljuk claimants, responded with a determined campaign. In 1107 his forces besieged and captured Shahdiz, and Ahmad was executed. The sources describe harsh reprisals against the Ismailis of Isfahan, including killings, and this is one episode in a pattern of retaliatory violence against Nizari communities that §41.8 considers.

In the following decade Muhammad Tapar and his commanders mounted repeated expeditions against Nizari strongholds. The most serious began in 1118, when a large army moved against Alamut itself. The siege reportedly lasted for several months and caused hardship in the fortress and its valleys, but it was lifted in 1118 when the sultan died, as Seljuk commanders disbanded to pursue their own interests. The pattern is instructive. Seljuk power was considerable, but its campaigns depended on the sultan's personal authority, and the sultan's death or competing priorities could end them suddenly. The Nizaris, by contrast, could rely on defensible sites, stored provisions, and the continuity of their institutions.

Other rulers added their own pressures. The Seljuk ruler of Khurasan, Ahmad Sanjar, who held authority in the east, conducted campaigns against Quhistan, and the relationship between Sanjar and the Nizaris fluctuated between hostility and uneasy accommodation. Local lords and atabegs, who might fear Nizari assassination or covet Nizari lands, acted according to their own interests. Sunni populations in some cities turned against Ismaili neighbors, sometimes encouraged by officials and religious leaders. In 1124, the year Hasan-i Sabbah died, the Nizaris held Alamut, Lamasar, Girdkuh, and a number of other strongholds, and no Seljuk ruler had succeeded in destroying their base. The struggle had been indecisive.

Why did neither side win? The Seljuks possessed superior manpower and resources but faced the problem of sieging well-provisioned mountain fortresses, a task that required sustained attention and large forces. Their politics, moreover, was divided among competing princes, and the same commanders who might lead a campaign against Alamut might also compete with one another for the sultanate. The Nizaris had strong defenses but limited numbers, and their rural bases could be damaged without being captured. A decisive Nizari victory was impossible, because the Seljuk state was far larger. A decisive Seljuk victory was difficult to achieve and sustain. The resulting stalemate allowed the Nizari state to develop institutions and to become, by the time of Hasan-i Sabbah's death, a stable if embattled polity.

Section 41.8

Fidaʾis, Assassination, and the Costs of Conflict

The most notorious feature of the Nizaris in the sources is their use of targeted killing. The people who carried out these attacks are called fidaʾis in Nizari and later Persian sources, a word derived from an Arabic root meaning to sacrifice or redeem oneself. Contemporary hostile writers did not necessarily use this term. They called the Ismailis Batiniyya ("those who hold to the inner meaning," a label that implied heresy), mulhids ("deviators"), or, in Syrian contexts, Hashishiyya, a term of abuse whose origin and meaning are discussed in §41.16. The word "fidaʾi" is therefore a convenient term for attackers who were identified as Nizari, but historians should note that its usage in the earliest sources is uncertain.

The documented pattern of attacks is striking. Nizari killers targeted political and religious leaders who were seen as threats to the community, including Seljuk officials, local rulers, and in some cases Sunni religious figures. Early deaths attributed to Ismaili or Nizari attackers include Nizam al-Mulk in 1092, the governor of Homs, Janah al-Dawla, in 1103, and Mawdud, the ruler of Mosul, in Damascus in 1113; the strength of the attribution differs by episode. Later, the killings of Abbasid caliphs al-Mustarshid (1135) and al-Rashid (1138) were attributed to Nizari assassins in medieval accounts, although the identity of those who directed the attacks remains uncertain. Attacks were typically carried out at close quarters, in public settings such as mosques or courts, and the attackers often did not expect to survive. Some killings were evidently ordered by the leadership at Alamut; others may have been initiated locally.

The attribution of Nizam al-Mulk's assassination requires particular care, because it is among the most cited and most consequential attributions in the whole story. According to the standard account, Nizam al-Mulk was killed on 14 October 1092 on the road near Nihavand by a man disguised as a Sufi, who stabbed him as he was being carried in a litter. Sunni sources, including Ibn al-Athir, attributed the killing to the Ismailis, and later Nizari tradition celebrated the attacker as the first of the community's fidaʾis. But Nizam al-Mulk had powerful enemies at court, including rivals within the Seljuk administration and the household of Malik Shah. Some historians have suggested that the killing may have involved court factions and that Nizari responsibility, though plausible, is not conclusively established. Chapter 39 treated the vizier's career, and the point to stress here is that the attribution is probable and widely accepted but remains a matter of inference from partisan sources.

Assassination served particular purposes. The Nizaris were outnumbered and could not match the armies of the Seljuks and their successors. The killing of a prominent figure could deter an attack on a fortress, remove a hostile governor, or create political disruption. It was a tool of the weak against the strong, and the Nizaris were not the only medieval actors to use political killing. But the tactic had costs. Each attack hardened enemies and justified reprisals. Sunni preachers called for the extermination of Ismailis, and local populations, sometimes with official encouragement, attacked Ismaili neighbors, many of whom had no involvement in the killings. The massacres at Isfahan after 1107, in Aleppo in 1113 and elsewhere were reported in Sunni and Ismaili sources, though numbers are uncertain and are best left unquantified.

The historian's task is to keep two points in view. First, targeted political killing was a real and deliberate Nizari practice, documented by multiple independent sources, and it caused suffering and fear. Second, the prominence of assassination in the sources reflects the alarm it produced, and it does not measure its place in Nizari life. A small number of attackers accounts for the killings in the narratives. The vast majority of the community lived as cultivators, artisans, and soldiers, and their daily world is the subject of Division III. Not every medieval assassination should be attributed to the Nizaris: sources sometimes blamed them for deaths that other evidence ascribes to rivals, family members, or illness, and a careful historian treats each case on its evidence.

Section 41.9

The Organization of the Nizari State: Daʿis, Hujjas, and Local Authorities

The Nizari state is difficult to describe in the vocabulary used for conventional medieval states, because it did not have a ruling dynasty in the ordinary sense, at least in its first seven decades, nor a continuous territory, nor a large standing army or fiscal bureaucracy comparable to the Seljuks'. What it had was a religious hierarchy that also functioned as a government. At the top stood the leader at Alamut, who in the early period was Hasan-i Sabbah as hujja and later his successors, who governed in the name of the hidden imam. After 1164 the lords of Alamut were themselves regarded as imams (§41.14), and from then on the leadership was hereditary within a single line.

Below the leader, authority was exercised through a network of regional and local officials. Sources speak of daʿis who headed the mission and administration in a district, and of subordinate ranks whose names and functions are reported by later writers such as Juvayni and Rashid al-Din. These authors describe a graded system of membership, from fully initiated adherents and organizers to ordinary lay followers. The details of the ranks may have varied over time and place, and hostile authors had a motive to portray the hierarchy as more rigid and sinister than it was. What the descriptions establish is that the community distinguished functions and levels of knowledge and that its leaders could assign tasks and appoint officials.

Fortresses were governed by commanders, called by Persian sources kiya or similar titles, who were responsible for defense, supplies, and relations with the surrounding population. The Rudbar fortresses were typically placed under trusted figures from the region. In Quhistan, the community was led by a local official whom Persian sources call the muhtasham, a term that had previously designated a person of rank and standing and that was used for local rulers there. This implies that the state delegated considerable authority to regional leaders, who administered their districts with a degree of autonomy while recognizing Alamut. The scholar Nasir al-Din Tusi, who lived in Quhistan and later at Alamut, dedicated a major ethical treatise to one such local ruler (§41.17).

The state also had to pay for itself. Fortresses needed garrisons, supplies, and repairs, and officials and missionaries needed support. The surviving sources say little about taxation, but the logic of the situation indicates that the Nizari authorities collected revenue in kind and in cash from the lands under their control, from the produce of fortress estates, and perhaps from contributions by followers in other regions. The evidence for coinage and fiscal administration in the Alamut period is limited, and a careful account must acknowledge that this part of the state's operation is poorly documented. The failure to find detailed records should not be read as evidence that none existed. It reflects the destruction of Nizari archives and libraries in 1256 and after.

Diplomacy was another function. The Nizaris maintained contact with Sunni rulers, Crusader leaders, and other powers. In Iran they negotiated truces and, at times, alliances of convenience with local lords. They sometimes sought recognition from the Abbasid caliphate, particularly under Hasan III (§41.17). Their religious network spanned Iran, Iraq, Syria, and the mountains of Badakhshan, and it allowed messages and personnel to move among far-flung communities. In this way the Nizari state, though territorially dispersed, remained an integrated body in religion and administration.

Historical Debate

A Religious Movement, a Territorial Polity, or Both?

The question: How should historians classify the Nizari state of Alamut? Was it primarily a religious movement, a missionary organization defending itself with arms? Was it a territorial polity, a state like others, whose religion supplied its ideology? Or was it a combination that older categories do not fit?

Interpretation A — A religious movement: Nizari leaders were missionaries, their legitimacy rested on religious doctrine, and their territorial holdings were bases for the daʿwa and not the aim of the enterprise. On this reading, fortresses were instruments of survival and the community's identity was fundamentally religious.
Interpretation B — A territorial polity: Over a century and a half, the Nizaris held land, collected resources, appointed officials, made treaties, and fought wars. These are the activities of a state, and religion supplied the vocabulary of legitimacy as it did for other medieval states.
Interpretation C — Both at once: Medieval Islamic societies did not clearly separate religious and political authority, so the question forces a modern distinction on a premodern case. The Nizari state was a religiously organized political society, and its religious and political functions were inseparable.

Evidence: The evidence includes the structure of Nizari leadership, the sources on fortress administration, Nizari doctrinal writings, and the accounts of external observers about the state's diplomacy and warfare.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is mostly about categories and emphasis. Few historians deny that the Nizaris had both religious and political functions. They differ on which provided the best framework for explanation, and on how far the political structure should be called a "state" given its dispersed and unconventional character.

Why it matters: If the Nizaris were only a movement, the legend of an assassin order fits more easily. If they were also a polity with institutions, economy, and intellectual life, the legend becomes a distortion. This chapter adopts Interpretation C, with the proviso that the state's unusual territorial form means "state" should be used carefully.

Section 41.10

Everyday Life, Agriculture, Trade, and Fortress Communities

The population of the Nizari state consisted mostly of people whose names and lives the sources do not record: farmers who cultivated valley fields and terraced slopes, herders who moved flocks to mountain pastures, artisans who worked in fortress workshops and village craft production, builders, porters, and soldiers who garrisoned the strongholds. The Rudbar valleys supported cultivation of grain, orchards, and vineyards, with irrigation drawn from mountain streams. The surviving descriptions, many of them written by outsiders who saw the region during or after the Mongol conquest, speak of ample stored provisions in the fortresses and of well-tended lands, evidence that suggests an agricultural economy capable of supplying the community's needs for long periods.

The fortress itself was a community and not merely a military installation. Residents included families as well as soldiers, and the strongholds contained living quarters, storerooms, cisterns, workshops, and places of worship and teaching. Archaeological surveys, including those carried out in the twentieth century by Peter Willey in Iran and Syria, have identified the remains of such structures, though many sites have been damaged and not all have been excavated. Where evidence exists, it shows fortifications designed for long siege: stored water, grain, and other supplies. The sources emphasize defense, but the buildings also reflect a settled life.

Trade was part of the picture, though its extent is hard to measure. The Rudbar lies between the Caspian lowlands and the interior plateau, and routes through the Alborz connected the region to Qazvin and Rayy on one side and to Caspian communities on the other. Nizari territories in Qumis and Quhistan lay along roads that linked Khurasan with central and western Iran. It would be a mistake to assume that these communities were isolated. They produced and exchanged goods, interacted with neighboring districts, and depended on wider networks for items they could not make. The evidence for specific commercial relations is limited, and any claim about trade volume or profits would exceed what the sources support.

Religious life shaped the rhythm of community. Ismaili practice combined observance of Islamic ritual and law with instruction in the inner meaning of the faith. The community's teachers, some of them high-ranking members of the hierarchy, taught initiates in stages. The role of women in Nizari society is less documented than that of men, but sources mention women of the leading families as patrons and participants in religious and political life. Women of the ruling household, for example, are reported to have made the pilgrimage to Mecca during the reign of Hasan III, a sign that the family's religious standing included women. Evidence for ordinary village women is far thinner and must be inferred.

Everyday Life and Social History

Reading Ordinary Life from Extraordinary Sources

Nearly every source for the Nizari state was written by people interested in its politics, its doctrines, or its destruction. Ordinary life must be read from side comments: the provisions Mongol and Persian observers found stored in the fortresses, the orchards and fields that a besieging army could burn, the reports of villages that submitted or resisted, and the buildings whose remains survive.

These traces suggest that fortress districts functioned as working agricultural communities whose people contributed labor, supplies, and loyalty to the state. They also suggest vulnerability. Armies moving through the valleys could burn crops and displace families, and the sources describing such devastation rarely give the names or fates of those affected. Historians should therefore treat the "fortress community" as a society of cultivators, craftspeople, soldiers, teachers, and families, whose experience of the conflict was shaped by the actions of rulers who rarely consulted them.

Section 41.11

Ismaili Theology: Imamate, Authoritative Teaching, and the Inner Meaning

The intellectual world of the Nizaris is best approached through a few central ideas, which they inherited from earlier Ismaili thought and developed in response to the circumstances of Alamut. The first is the doctrine of the imamate. Ismailis, like other Shiʿa, held that God has always provided a divinely guided leader to interpret revelation. For Nizaris, the legitimate imam was a descendant of Nizar, and his authority was necessary for understanding the faith correctly. Because the imam was in concealment during the early Alamut period, his representatives, the hujjas, acted as his voice.

The second idea is the doctrine of authoritative teaching, or taʿlim. The argument, which Hasan-i Sabbah is said to have set out in a treatise known as the "Four Chapters" (Fusul-i arbaʿa), held that human reason alone cannot reach certain knowledge of God and religious truth, and that people therefore need an authoritative teacher, the imam, to guide them. The treatise itself is lost, and its content is known mainly from a summary by the Sunni scholar al-Shahrastani. The argument was directed against rationalist and Sunni approaches that held that reason and the community's consensus could suffice. Hostile writers, particularly the theologian al-Ghazali, wrote refutations of what they called the Batiniyya and the doctrine of taʿlim, and these refutations are among the main sources for outsiders' understanding of Ismaili thought.

Third, Ismaili thought distinguished between the zahir, the outward or apparent meaning of scripture and law, and the batin, its inner meaning. The interpretation that uncovers the inner meaning is called taʾwil. Hostile writers claimed that Ismailis used the batin to dissolve the law, treating the outward commands as meaningless for initiates. Ismaili writers held that the zahir and batin are linked and that the inner meaning gives the outer practices their purpose. Historians generally accept that Ismaili thought held both levels to be real, while recognizing that the relationship between them became a matter of significant change, as §41.14 shows.

Historical Vocabulary
Taʿlim, Zahir, and Batin

Definition: Taʿlim means "instruction" or "authoritative teaching." In Ismaili thought, it referred to the doctrine that religious truth must be taught by an authorized, divinely guided teacher, the imam. The zahir is the outward, literal dimension of scripture and ritual, and the batin is the inner, spiritual dimension, accessed through taʾwil, interpretation that leads back to the original meaning.

Historical context: Muslims of many traditions distinguished outward from inner meanings, and mystical movements, including Sufism, developed their own versions of the distinction. Ismailis gave it a distinctive form by tying access to the inner meaning to the authority of the imam. Sunni opponents of the Ismailis, from the eleventh century onward, used the name Batiniyya to describe them, often to suggest that they disregarded the law.

Why it matters: These ideas explain how the Nizaris justified their leadership structure, why their missionaries taught in stages, and how they could later interpret the Qiyama as a spiritual event.

Potential misunderstanding: The term Batiniyya was a polemical label and not a self-description. The accusation that Ismailis rejected religious law came largely from opponents and does not describe how most Ismaili thinkers understood the relation of inner and outer.

Section 41.12

Books, Libraries, and Scholarship

The Nizaris valued books and learning. Alamut possessed a library, and the sources suggest that other fortresses held collections as well. The contents are known mostly from the descriptions of those who saw them at the end, particularly Juvayni, who reports that the Alamut library contained many volumes and scientific instruments. Because the library was in large part destroyed or dispersed in 1256 and after, and because Juvayni was a hostile and selective reporter, historians cannot reconstruct its holdings. They can say that the Nizaris maintained a tradition of writing, copying, and teaching and that the Alamut library was a significant institution.

The Nizari literary tradition was both Ismaili and Persianate. Works preserved by Ismaili communities in later centuries show that Persian became a major language of Nizari religious writing, alongside Arabic. Among the surviving Nizari texts are doctrinal treatises, collections of sermons and teachings attributed to leaders at Alamut, and, from the post-Alamut period, histories and religious poems that preserve memories of the state. Some of these texts, such as the Haft bab ("Seven Chapters"), attributed to Hasan-i Sabbah in the manuscript tradition, were compiled or reworked after the fall of Alamut, so their relationship to the ideas of the Alamut period is a matter for specialists.

The Nizari lands also hosted scholars who were not Ismaili by origin. The most prominent was Nasir al-Din Tusi (1201–1274), a polymath of Twelver Shiʿi background who lived in Nizari territories during the years before the Mongol conquest. Tusi composed works of ethics, philosophy, and astronomy in this period, including the Persian ethical treatise Akhlaq-i Nasiri (1235), and he also wrote a work on Ismaili doctrine. The circumstances of his residence, whether voluntary, protected, or compelled, are discussed in the sources in ways that serve different interests, and §41.17 returns to them. Chapter 43 examines his scientific career and later service to Hulagu.

Taken together, this evidence shows that the Nizaris were participants in the larger Persianate and Islamic scholarly world and not a purely military community. They had scribes, teachers, and scholars, and they sometimes sheltered intellectuals from outside the Ismaili tradition. The loss of their libraries is among the most serious gaps in the evidence for medieval Iranian history, and this chapter's reliance on outsiders' accounts reflects that loss. It would be a mistake to infer from the scarcity of surviving Nizari texts that the community produced little. It would be equally a mistake to infer that its books were destroyed in one stroke, since Nizari communities in Syria, Badakhshan, and elsewhere preserved manuscripts that only came to modern scholarship in the twentieth century.

Section 41.13

Buzurg-Ummid and Muhammad ibn Buzurg-Ummid, 1124–1162

Hasan-i Sabbah died at Alamut in May 1124 (Rabiʿ II 518 AH). The question of succession was a test of the state's institutions. Hasan's own sons are said by later sources to have been excluded from leadership, in some accounts after their execution for offenses against the community, though these stories, preserved in hostile and later accounts, should be treated with caution. The successor named by Hasan, according to the sources, was Kiya Buzurg-Ummid, a commander from the Rudbar region who had served as governor of the fortress of Lamasar. He ruled until 1138.

Buzurg-Ummid's reign was a period of consolidation. The Seljuk sultanate had not recovered from its divisions, and its princes and atabegs were preoccupied with one another. The Nizaris defended their holdings against occasional attacks and maintained their network, and the Abbasid caliph al-Mustarshid, who had attempted to restore caliphal power, was killed in 1135 in a manner that Sunni and some other sources attribute to Nizari agents, an attribution that is plausible but not proven. The state also survived the death of its founder without internal collapse, which suggests that institutions, and not only the founder's personality, held it together.

Buzurg-Ummid was succeeded by his son Muhammad, who ruled from 1138 to 1162, a long reign of nearly a quarter-century. The sources report few dramatic events for this period. It seems to have been marked by relative stability, continued fortress building and maintenance, and the gradual development of a hereditary leadership. The arrival at hereditary succession within a single family was an important change from Hasan-i Sabbah's model, since it created a ruling house in effect, and it laid the groundwork for the claims made after 1164. The Nizari state in this period also saw growth of the Syrian branch, which developed under its own leadership (§41.15).

The relative scarcity of narrative in these decades should not be mistaken for emptiness. Historians of medieval states know that long periods of institutional stability attract little attention from chroniclers, who prefer wars and dramatic deaths. The Nizari state during Muhammad's reign probably saw the maturation of the practices described in Division III: agriculture, fortress administration, scholarship, and the training of missionaries. The evidence is thin, and a responsible account of the period is correspondingly modest. What is clear is that, by 1162, the Nizari state had outlasted its founder by nearly forty years and had become an established feature of the political landscape.

Section 41.14

The Qiyama of 8 August 1164

Muhammad's son Hasan II succeeded him in 1162. Two years later, on 8 August 1164 (17 Ramadan 559 AH), at Alamut, he assembled representatives of the Nizari communities and delivered a sermon proclaiming the beginning of the Qiyama, the "Resurrection" or "Great Resurrection" (qiyamat al-qiyama). This was the most dramatic doctrinal event in the history of the Alamut state, and it has generated interpretation and controversy ever since.

Within Nizari tradition, the Qiyama was understood as an event of spiritual and doctrinal significance. The imam, whose true identity had been hidden, was now declared to be present and manifest, and the community's relationship to him changed. Nizari texts describe the Qiyama as the moment when the inner truths (batin) were disclosed to believers and when the community could recognize the imam directly. In that framework, the outward practices of the law were not abolished as meaningless but were reinterpreted: the Qiyama brought the inner meaning of religious practice to the fore, and believers who recognized the imam were said to live in a state of spiritual resurrection in which the full reality of the faith was accessible.

Hasan II, according to the Nizari sources, was now presented as the imam himself, or as the imam's direct representative, and the sources of the following generation, including those from the reign of his son Muhammad II (1166–1210), treat the family as an imamic line descended from Nizar. This is the point at which the leadership of Alamut ceased to be a delegated office and became an imamate in residence. Hasan II was killed in 1166 by a relative, and Muhammad II continued and elaborated the doctrine.

Outsiders understood the Qiyama very differently. Sunni and Mongol-era writers presented it as the abolition of the Islamic law: fasting and prayer were said to have been abandoned, wine consumed openly, and the community directed to face away from Mecca. Juvayni and later writers dwell on a ritual at which Hasan II is said to have ordered a banquet during Ramadan. These accounts are drawn from sources unfriendly to the Nizaris, and the details are not reliably established. Modern historians are cautious about both the hostile and the apologetic readings. They agree that the Qiyama was a major change in Nizari religious life and that it involved a reconfiguration of the relationship between inner and outer religion, but they differ on how far it affected the daily practice of the community and on whether the later interpretation was already clear in 1164.

For the history of the Nizari state, the Qiyama had several consequences. It strengthened the authority of the lords of Alamut by identifying them with the imam. It gave the community a distinctive and highly charged religious identity. And it made the Nizaris more vulnerable to attack on religious grounds, since opponents could cite it as proof of heresy. It also set the stage for later reversals. As §41.17 shows, in 1210 Hasan III rejected the Qiyama and brought the community back to a more conventional Sunni practice, an indication that the doctrine was not accepted by all.

Primary Source Window

Evidence for the Qiyama of 1164

Creators: Mongol-era historians (Juvayni; Rashid al-Din) and Nizari authors of the Alamut and post-Alamut periods · Date: accounts composed from the 1250s into the fourteenth century and later · Type: narrative history and doctrinal literature

Context and transmission: No text composed in 1164 survives in its original form. What we have is reconstruction. Juvayni, writing around 1260, and Rashid al-Din, writing in the early fourteenth century, report the proclamation and its sequel on the basis of materials they drew from Alamut and from Nizari informants. Their versions are shaped by hostility to the Nizaris and by the Mongol-era viewpoint. Nizari texts, including works composed under Muhammad II and Hasan III and later treatises preserved by the community, describe the Qiyama in doctrinal terms. These texts survive in manuscripts copied centuries after the events.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The hostile narratives describe a gathering at which Hasan II declared the Resurrection to have begun, set aside the observance of fasting and ritual prayer, and ordered a banquet with wine; the Nizari texts, by contrast, describe a disclosure of the inner reality of religion and the manifestation of the imam, and present the event as the fulfilment of the community's long expectation.

Analysis: The two bodies of evidence agree that a major proclamation took place at Alamut in 1164 and that it changed the community's understanding of religious authority. They differ on what it meant. The hostile sources emphasize licence and rejection of the law, while the Nizari sources emphasize spiritual disclosure.

Limitations: Neither body of evidence is contemporary with the events. The hostile accounts were written by people with reasons to depict the Nizaris as heretics, and the Nizari accounts were shaped by later doctrinal developments and by the community's need to preserve its identity after the Mongol conquest.

Perspective: The sources represent the views of scholars and leaders, with the hostile outsiders speaking from a Sunni and Mongol-court perspective. Those of ordinary Nizari followers, who had to live with the change in practice, are not directly represented.

Questions for students:

  1. What do the two bodies of evidence agree on, and where do they diverge?
  2. Who created each kind of source, and when and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have led hostile and Nizari authors to describe the Qiyama as they did?
  4. What can historians learn about the event from comparing them?
  5. What can they not establish about the event, given that neither type of source dates from 1164?
Section 41.15

The Syrian Nizaris: Masyaf and Rashid al-Din Sinan

The Nizaris of Syria are part of this chapter because they were linked to Alamut by religion and leadership, even though their history unfolded in a different political environment: the contested Syrian world of Sunni Turkish rulers, Fatimid remnants, and the Crusader states. Ismaili missionaries had been active in Syria since the Fatimid period, and after 1094 a Nizari community took shape there under leaders sent from or acknowledging Alamut. Early efforts to establish a base in Aleppo and Damascus met violent opposition, and the Nizaris suffered massacres in those cities. Over the following decades they acquired mountain strongholds in the Jabal Bahra region, among them Qadmus, Kahf, and Masyaf, the last around 1141.

The central figure in Syrian Nizari history is Rashid al-Din Sinan, born near Basra in the 1130s according to the sources, educated in the Nizari tradition at Alamut, and sent to Syria in the early 1160s. He established himself at Masyaf and led the Syrian community for roughly thirty years until his death in 1193. Under his leadership the Syrian Nizaris maintained their independence in a region where Zangids, Ayyubids, and Crusaders all competed for power. Sinan negotiated with rulers, including Saladin, who besieged Masyaf in 1176 and then came to terms; sources report that Saladin had survived attempts on his life attributed to Nizari agents in 1175 and 1176, though the circumstances and responsibility for planning them require careful source criticism.

The Syrian Nizari experience differs from the Iranian in several ways. The community was smaller and more dependent on a few fortresses. It was in contact with Latin Christian states, whose chroniclers wrote about it, and Western authors are the source of many of the "Assassin" stories discussed below. And Sinan's leadership, though linked to Alamut, appears to have been practically autonomous. The Syrian Nizaris did not necessarily follow every doctrinal development at Alamut in the same way, and their leader acted as head of a regional community with its own diplomatic strategy. Reports that Sinan held a special spiritual status are found in the sources, but their interpretation is contested.

The killing of Conrad of Montferrat, the claimant to the Crusader kingdom, in Tyre in April 1192 by two attackers identified as Nizaris is one of the best-known incidents in the Latin sources. Contemporary writers disagreed on who had instigated it, and some accused Richard I of England. The episode shows how Nizari actions could become part of wider political accusations. After Sinan's death in 1193 the Syrian community continued under other leaders and coexisted with its neighbors for several decades, until the Mamluk sultan Baybars brought the Syrian strongholds under his control between about 1260 and 1273, an event that belongs to the history of the Mamluk state.

Section 41.16

The Assassins in Historical Memory

The popular image of the Nizaris as "the Assassins" is one of the most durable and distorted in the history of the medieval Middle East. It was constructed gradually, from Muslim polemic, Crusader report, and European literary taste. The word itself derives from the Arabic hashishiyya, a term of abuse applied to Ismailis, especially in Syria, in the twelfth century. Its original sense is uncertain: it may have implied that the people so named were outcasts or social marginals, since hashish users were regarded with contempt, and it need not have meant that they were actually drugged. European travelers and chroniclers heard the term and transformed it into a word for professional killers, which entered European languages as "assassin."

The legend of the drugged killers and the secret garden developed through several stages. Crusader writers such as William of Tyre described the Syrian Nizari leader as the "Old Man of the Mountain" and attributed to his followers a blind obedience. The story of a hidden garden of earthly paradise, into which young men were taken while drugged to taste a foretaste of heaven and then sent out to kill, appears in the account attributed to Marco Polo, written at the end of the thirteenth century, decades after Alamut had fallen and based on hearsay. Polo's text does not explicitly name hashish; that link was made by later retellers. The story is not found in Nizari sources and no archaeological evidence supports it. It is best understood as a tale that explained, to outsiders, how a small community could inspire such fear.

In the early nineteenth century the French orientalist Silvestre de Sacy proposed the etymological connection between hashishiyya and "assassin," and for a long time the hashish story was treated as established fact. Twentieth-century scholarship, including the work of Marshall Hodgson, Bernard Lewis, and Farhad Daftary, took apart much of the legend and showed how it had grown from polemic and misunderstanding. The recovery of Ismaili manuscripts, associated especially with the work of Wladimir Ivanow in the 1930s and after, made it possible to study the Nizaris from the inside.

Evidence Comparison Exercise

Weighing the Evidence for the Assassin Legends

Historians evaluate a claim by asking what kind of evidence supports it, who produced the evidence, and how close the author was to the events. Apply that method to three elements of the legend.

  1. The claim that Nizari fighters were drugged with hashish. The term hashishiyya appears in hostile Muslim sources; the drugging story appears in European accounts and later retellings. Ask: Do the Nizaris' own texts mention it? Do contemporary chroniclers who observed the attacks mention it? What other explanations for the term are possible?
  2. The secret garden of paradise. The account associated with Marco Polo is set after Alamut's fall. Ask: Did the author claim to have visited Alamut while it stood? What does archaeology of the fortress show? What does the story's literary pedigree suggest about its origin?
  3. The claim that Nizari killers were fanatics who obeyed any order. Ask: What does the record of targeted killings actually show about their selection of victims and their political purposes? What do Nizari sources say about the training and motivation of the fidaʾis?

Task: For each claim, rate the evidence as strong, uncertain, or weak, and explain your reasoning. What would count as better evidence?

The legend continues to shape how the Nizaris are portrayed in popular culture, where the words "assassin" and "Alamut" serve as shorthand for secret killers and mysterious fortresses. The historical Nizaris, by contrast, were a religious community that built a state and a body of learning, and a historical account should say so. The fact that the legend is false in its details does not make the targeted killings imaginary, and the historian's task is to hold both points at once: the killings were real, and the legend about their causes was not.

Section 41.17

The Later Lords of Alamut, 1210–1255

The long reign of Muhammad II ended in 1210. His son, Jalal al-Din Hasan (Hasan III), took a startling course. He publicly rejected the doctrine of the Qiyama and declared his adherence to Sunni Islam, and his followers were instructed to follow Sunni observance. Persian sources describe him as a "new Muslim" (nawmusulman), a phrase that expresses outsiders' sense of the change. He sought recognition from the Abbasid caliph al-Nasir in Baghdad, and the caliph's acknowledgment was reported to have brought some security. Sunni jurists were brought into Nizari territory, and the ruler's mother and sister are reported to have performed the pilgrimage to Mecca. Hasan III reigned until 1221.

Historians interpret this episode in several ways. One reading treats it as a sincere religious change under a ruler seeking to bring the community into the mainstream. Another sees it as a political accommodation, a practical adjustment to a changed environment in which the Khwarazmshahs were expanding their power in Iran and Nizari security required normal relations with Sunni authority. Possibly both factors operated. The Nizari sources preserved in later centuries interpret the episode in terms of the doctrine of concealment, as a period in which the imam again withdrew from public view and the community practiced precautionary dissimulation (taqiyya), outwardly following Sunni forms. Whichever interpretation is correct, the episode shows that the Nizari state was capable of changing its public religious posture to ensure its survival, and that the Qiyama was not an irreversible turning point.

Hasan III's son, ʿAlaʾ al-Din Muhammad III, succeeded him in 1221 as a child and ruled for some thirty-four years, until 1255. Under him the community returned to the doctrines associated with the Qiyama. His reign coincided with the arrival of the Mongols in Iran. The Mongol conquests of the 1220s shattered the Khwarazmian state, a development that Chapter 44 treats, and left the Nizari fortresses in an altered and more dangerous environment. The sources for his reign describe him as a ruler of uncertain health and temper, and some of these descriptions come from hostile or later writers who had reason to stress his weakness before the fall.

These decades were also a time of notable intellectual activity in Nizari lands. The most important figure was Nasir al-Din Tusi, who lived in Nizari territory for years, first in Quhistan under the local ruler, the muhtasham Nasir al-Din ʿAbd al-Rahim, to whom he dedicated the Akhlaq-i Nasiri in 1235. This Persian work on ethics and the governance of households and states became one of the most influential treatises of its kind in the Persianate world, and its composition in a Nizari milieu is a reminder that these communities participated in broader intellectual life. Tusi's own accounts of his stay present it in ways that serve his later career at the Mongol court, in which he served Hulagu, and historians differ over whether he was a willing guest, a protected client, or a person unable to leave. His works from this period, including a treatise on Ismaili doctrine, show real engagement with Ismaili thought, though the extent of his personal conversion is disputed. Chapter 43 develops his scientific and philosophical career.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Alamut, 1090–1256

What Changed

  • The leader's status: from a missionary acting for a hidden imam (to 1164), to an imam in residence (after 1164), with a period of Sunni-style accommodation under Hasan III (1210–1221)
  • The leadership structure: from a founder to a hereditary line
  • The doctrine of the relationship between inner and outer religion, especially after 1164
  • The external environment: from a divided Seljuk sultanate to the Khwarazmshahs and then the Mongols

What Stayed the Same

  • The dispersed fortress network and its dependence on mountain terrain
  • Reliance on a religious hierarchy to govern and to hold the community together
  • The hostility of the surrounding Sunni political order and the recurring risk of retaliation
  • The intellectual life of the community and its place in Persianate learning

Pattern: Change was uneven and sometimes reversed. Doctrinal change was abrupt at moments of proclamation (1164, 1210) but unfolded over generations in practice, and institutional structures were more stable than doctrine. The leadership and the fortress elites experienced these shifts most directly, while ordinary cultivators and craft workers probably experienced greater continuity in daily labor.

Section 41.18

The Mongol Advance and Rukn al-Din Khurshah, 1255–1256

The Mongol Empire's fourth Great Khan, Möngke, who came to power in 1251, decided to bring Iran and the lands to its west under firm Mongol control. His plans, which the sources connect with his brother Hulagu, included the destruction of the Nizari strongholds and the subjugation of the Abbasid caliphate. Mongol commanders began operations against Nizari positions in Quhistan in the early 1250s, with the general Kitbuqa leading the forces. Hulagu himself set out in 1253 and crossed the Oxus in January 1256. The wider story of the Mongol conquest of Iran belongs to Volume IV, and this section concerns only its effect on the Nizari state.

The timing was unfortunate for the Nizaris. In late 1255 ʿAlaʾ al-Din Muhammad III was murdered at Alamut by a member of his entourage, an act that the sources associate with his son Rukn al-Din Khurshah, who then succeeded him, though the extent of his involvement is unclear. Rukn al-Din, a young man in his twenties, inherited an increasingly desperate situation. He faced a vast Mongol army, supported by local rulers who had submitted to the new power, and he faced it with a defensive system that had been effective against Seljuk armies but was not designed for such an opponent.

The Mongol approach differed from Seljuk campaigns in scale and method. Hulagu advanced with a large force that included siege specialists, and he combined military pressure with diplomatic demands that the Nizari ruler submit and dismantle his fortresses. Rukn al-Din vacillated. He sent envoys, offered partial concessions, and delayed. The sources describe him as caught between advisors urging resistance and others urging submission, and some accounts suggest that he hoped the winter would force the Mongols to withdraw. Nasir al-Din Tusi and other scholars in the Nizari lands, who would later join the Mongol camp, are reported to have urged submission, though the accounts of their role were written afterwards and serve the interests of their authors.

By November 1256 Mongol forces were besieging Maymundiz, where Rukn al-Din had taken up residence. After a bombardment and negotiations, he came out and surrendered in mid-November. He was received by Hulagu, and the Mongols used him to order the surrender of other fortresses. Some commanders obeyed; others refused. The collapse of the Nizari defensive system was swift in the Rudbar and prolonged elsewhere. The fortress network's strength lay in the resilience of individual strongholds, but it also meant that each fortress's fate depended on the decisions of its own commander and that a ruler's surrender might not be accepted by all.

Section 41.19

The Fall of Alamut, 1256, and What Survived

Alamut itself surrendered in December 1256 after Rukn al-Din's instructions, although its commander had initially resisted. The Mongols dismantled the defenses, and Hulagu entered the valley. Other fortresses followed, although Lamasar resisted until 1257, roughly another year after Alamut, before the Rudbar was fully brought under Mongol control. The long history of the independent Nizari state in northern Iran thus came to an end in the space of a few weeks, though the strongholds in Qumis and Quhistan did not all fall at once. The fortress of Girdkuh, in Qumis, is reported to have held out for years, until December 1270, an illustration of the staying power of the fortress system and of the care that must be taken in treating 1256 as a single moment.

Primary Source Window

Juvayni, Tarikh-i Jahangushay (History of the World Conqueror), on Alamut and its Library

Creator: ʿAta-Malik Juvayni, Persian administrator in Mongol service · Date: composed c. 1260 · Type: Persian-language narrative history

Context: Juvayni accompanied Hulagu's army and was present at the surrender of Alamut. His chapters on the Nizaris, written soon afterwards, are the most important contemporary account of the fall of the state and of the community's earlier history.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Juvayni describes how, at Hulagu's permission, he inspected the library at Alamut. He says he took out Qurʾans and other works he thought valuable, along with astronomical instruments, and that he burned books he regarded as the products of heresy. He also reports that he used a biographical narrative of Hasan-i Sabbah that he found among the books to write his own account.

Analysis: The passage tells us that Alamut had a library with works of many kinds, that Mongol officers allowed a Persian official to sort it, and that Juvayni used Nizari material as a source for his history. It makes his selection of what to save or destroy visible as a decision made by a particular person with particular convictions.

Limitations: Juvayni is the main witness to his own actions. He wrote for a Mongol court and had reason to present the conquest as a justified defeat of heresy and himself as a loyal and discriminating servant. His description of the library is selective and cannot be checked against another eyewitness. We cannot know the size or composition of the collection, how much of it he destroyed, or whether the biographical narrative he used is accurately summarized.

Perspective: A Sunni Persian official serving the conquerors. The Nizaris' own perspective on the loss of their library is not represented.

  4. What can historians learn about the Alamut library and about Mongol-era historical writing from it?

The human cost of the conquest was heavy. Mongol forces killed many Nizari defenders and civilians, and the sources describe slaughter in several places. Hulagu is reported to have ordered the execution of Nizari captives, and Rukn al-Din was sent to the great khan Möngke's court in Mongolia. Accounts report that Mongol guards killed him in 1257 in the region of the Great Khan’s court, after his unsuccessful attempt to obtain an audience; details of his final journey vary. Figures for the numbers killed in the conquest appear in the sources, but they are untrustworthy and are best left unquantified. What can be said is that the conquest involved large-scale killing, that it was part of the broader Mongol pattern of violence against resistant populations, and that it destroyed the political structure of the Nizari state in Iran.

The end of independence was not the end of the community. Nizari populations survived in Iran, in the mountains of Daylam, in Quhistan, and in other regions, though they had to live under Mongol rule and later successor states, often in concealment. The line of imams continued, preserved, according to Nizari tradition, through hidden members of the family who later reemerged, and this continuity became a focus of the community's religious identity. In Syria the Nizaris maintained their strongholds until the Mamluk conquests of the 1260s and 1270s, and Nizari communities in Badakhshan, Central Asia, and later South Asia developed their own histories, which this chapter cannot follow. Many manuscripts, preserved in these communities, survived to be recovered by scholars in the modern period. The fall of Alamut thus ended the state without ending the Nizaris, and the historical memory of the state persisted in Nizari tradition while the legend of the "Assassins" persisted in the outside world.

Section 41.20

Synthesis and Review: The Nizari State in Iranian History

The five questions that organized this chapter can now be answered together. Ismailism developed in Iran as part of an international missionary network headquartered in Fatimid Cairo and sustained by a learned tradition of Persian and Arabic writing. Hasan-i Sabbah established Alamut in 1090 because he judged that the daʿwa could not survive indefinitely through concealment in cities where it was constantly at risk, and because the Rudbar offered defensible terrain, a receptive population, and a Seljuk state unable to respond immediately. The Fatimid succession dispute of 1094 then produced a distinct Nizari community whose territorial base made it able to survive the loss of Cairo's support.

The Nizaris survived despite the fragmentation of their territory because mountain fortresses were difficult to take, because the Seljuk state was divided and its campaigns depended on the personal authority of sultans, and because the community had institutions, a religious hierarchy and a network of local administrators, that outlasted individual leaders. Their understanding of religious authority developed through three principal moments: the doctrine of authoritative teaching under Hasan-i Sabbah, the proclamation of the Qiyama in 1164, and the accommodation under Hasan III, each a response to changing circumstances and each leaving a different trace in the sources.

The state's life was carried by many people other than the fidaʾis: cultivators and herders, builders and craft workers, scholars and teachers, fortress commanders, and women of the ruling household. Targeted killings were real and consequential, and they brought reprisals against Ismaili communities that had nothing to do with them. But they were a tool of a weaker party against a stronger one and not the center of the state's life. The state fell in 1256 in the face of the Mongol army’s overwhelming resources, siege capacity, and coordinated demands for submission; Rukn al-Din Khurshah’s negotiations and the refusal of some fortress commanders to surrender also shaped the uneven outcome. What survived was the community, its manuscripts, its line of imams, and the memory of a distinctive religious experiment.

These conclusions position the chapter within the wider textbook. The Nizari state belongs beside the Seljuk sultanate (Chapter 38), the administrative order of Nizam al-Mulk (Chapter 39), and the Sunni revival and Shiʿi communities of Chapter 40 as one of the major responses to the political and religious problems of eleventh- and twelfth-century Iran. It also belongs to the story of Persianate culture, which Chapter 42 and Chapter 43 develop, and to the story of the end of the old political order before the Mongol conquest, which Chapter 44 and Volume IV continue.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Nizari state of Alamut should be understood as a religiously organized political society that held land, built institutions, and sustained an intellectual tradition for 166 years, and not merely as a clandestine organization of assassins. The argument rests on the evidence of Nizari and outsider sources read critically, and on the observation that the sources' emphasis on political killing reflects the alarm it caused and not its share of Nizari life.

Ismailism began as a Shiʿi tradition recognizing a particular line of imams. Under the Fatimids, who ruled North Africa from 909 and Egypt from 969, it became the creed of a caliphate and the base of an international missionary organization, the daʿwa. In Iran and Central Asia the daʿwa produced thinkers such as Nasir-i Khusraw, and Ismaili communities existed in cities and in mountain districts, though they lived under a Sunni political order that saw them as a danger.

Hasan-i Sabbah, born in Qom and initiated by the daʿi ʿAbd al-Malik ibn ʿAttash, travelled to Cairo in 1078 and returned to Iran to survey possible bases. In 1090 he took the fortress of Alamut in the Rudbar, in an operation that depended on local support and patient organization. He was a missionary and political founder, not an imam. The seizure created a secure base, and the Seljuk response was interrupted by the deaths of Malik Shah and Nizam al-Mulk in 1092.

The succession dispute in Cairo in 1094 created the Nizari identity. Hasan and the Iranian Ismailis supported Nizar and rejected al-Mustaʿli, and the Nizaris developed the doctrine of a hidden imam represented by a hujja. The distinction between 1090 and 1094 is essential: the territorial base preceded the doctrinal break and helped the community survive it.

Between 1092 and 1124 the Nizaris extended their holdings to a dispersed network of fortresses in the Rudbar, Qumis, and Quhistan. The Seljuks mounted repeated campaigns, winning at Shahdiz in 1107 and besieging Alamut in 1118, but they were hindered by internal division and by the difficulty of reducing mountain fortresses. The struggle ended in stalemate. The Nizaris used targeted killing as a weapon of the weak, and it brought retaliation against ordinary Ismaili communities.

The state's organization combined a religious hierarchy with regional administration. Fortress commanders and local leaders governed districts, daʿis directed missions, and the lord of Alamut governed in the name of the imam. Everyday life centered on agriculture, craft work, and the maintenance of fortresses, and the community included families and teachers as well as soldiers. Nizari thought developed the doctrines of the imamate, authoritative teaching, and the relationship of outer and inner meaning, and the community maintained libraries and welcomed scholars such as Nasir al-Din Tusi.

The proclamation of the Qiyama in 1164 under Hasan II marked the most dramatic doctrinal change, giving the lords of Alamut the status of imams in residence. Hostile sources described it as the abolition of the law, while Nizari sources described it as a spiritual disclosure; neither body of evidence is contemporary with the event. Under Hasan III the community outwardly adopted Sunni forms from 1210, showing the capacity for strategic accommodation, and under Muhammad III it returned to earlier doctrines.

The Syrian Nizaris, led by Rashid al-Din Sinan from the 1160s and based at Masyaf, developed in a different political environment, negotiating with Saladin and the Crusader states. The Latin and Muslim writers who described them created the legends of the Old Man of the Mountain, the drugged killers, and the secret garden. Those legends have no support in Nizari sources or archaeology, though the targeted killings they were created to explain were real.

In 1256 the Mongol army under Hulagu, aided by the vacillation of Rukn al-Din Khurshah, took Maymundiz, Alamut, and Lamasar, and the Nizari state in northern Iran ended. Juvayni, a Persian official in Mongol service, inspected the Alamut library and decided which books to preserve and which to destroy, and his account is both a source and an act in the history it records. The conquest involved heavy killing. Girdkuh resisted until about 1270, and the Nizari communities, their imams, and many manuscripts survived outside the borders of the old state.

The chapter's lasting significance lies in what it shows about medieval Iranian history. Religious movements could build durable institutions; small mountain states could resist stronger ones for generations; and the destruction of a community's archives could leave its story to be told largely by its enemies. Historians must therefore read the Nizaris through a combination of hostile accounts, community texts, and archaeology, and weigh each for its limitations.

The divinely guided descendant of ʿAli and Fatima whom Ismailis held to be the legitimate authority in religious interpretation.
Satr
Concealment of the imam, a condition in which the imam exists but is not publicly known.

Authoritative instruction; the doctrine that religious truth requires an authorized teacher.
Zahir
The outward, literal dimension of scripture and ritual.
Batin
The inner, spiritual dimension of scripture and ritual, accessed through interpretation.

Interpretation that leads from the outer form of a text or rite back to its inner meaning.

The "Resurrection," proclaimed at Alamut in 1164, understood in Nizari tradition as a spiritual disclosure of the imam and the inner truth of religion.

A Nizari fighter who carried out targeted attacks, often at the cost of his own life.

A term of abuse applied to Ismailis, especially in Syria, which gave European languages the word "assassin."

A fortress in the Rudbar region of the Alborz, taken in 1090 and the center of the Nizari state until 1256.

The mountainous district of the Alborz north of Qazvin that formed the core of the Nizari state.
Quhistan
A region of southeastern Khurasan where the Nizaris held towns and districts.

A title for a local ruler in Nizari Quhistan.

"The Life of Our Master," an autobiographical narrative attributed to Hasan-i Sabbah, preserved at Alamut and now lost.

A European and Muslim label for the Syrian Nizari leader, source of legends about absolute obedience.

Chapter Reflection

The history of the Nizari state matters because it shows how much of medieval Iranian history was shaped by communities that did not control the great cities or the largest armies. A network of mountain fortresses, governed by a religious hierarchy, resisted one of the strongest states of its age for decades and survived another for more than a century. The Seljuk sultanate, treated in Chapter 38, and the Sunni revival described in Chapter 40 were not the only ways of organizing political and religious life in Iran, and the Nizaris represent a serious alternative.

The chapter also shows how historical memory is made. The legend of the Assassins was built from polemic, hearsay, and literary taste and then repeated until it seemed to be fact. Students who learn to ask who wrote a source, when, and why, are better prepared to challenge such stories. The fate of the Alamut library, described by the man who sorted it, is a reminder that the shape of the surviving record is itself a historical question.

What survived the fall of Alamut was a community and its traditions. Nizari groups continued in Iran, Badakhshan, Syria, and beyond, and their manuscripts, once scattered, have enabled modern scholars to write a fuller history. The Persianate culture of learning in which the Nizaris took part, and to which Chapters 42 and 43 turn, continued under Mongol and later rulers.

Finally, the chapter connects the eleventh- and twelfth-century world of Seljuk Iran to the larger transformation that followed. The Mongol conquest, which ended the Nizari state and soon reshaped the entire region, begins a new period of Iranian history that Volume IV will examine, and the destruction of Alamut in 1256 is among its earliest and most dramatic episodes.
