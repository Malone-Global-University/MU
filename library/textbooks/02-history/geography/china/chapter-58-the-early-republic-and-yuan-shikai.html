Part X — Revolution, Republic, and War

The Early Republic and Yuan Shikai

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Abolishing a dynasty took a few months of revolution and negotiation. Building a working republic proved to be a different and far harder task, because it required agreement about who held authority, who paid for government, and who commanded the armies.

On the morning of October 10, 1913, the second anniversary of the Wuchang Uprising, Yuan Shikai was sworn in as the first formally elected president of the Republic of China. The ceremony was held in the Hall of Supreme Harmony in the Forbidden City, the building in which Qing emperors had once received their officials, and the choice of venue carried its own message. Four days earlier, members of the National Assembly who had voted for Yuan had done so while large numbers of men from a self-styled “citizens’ corps” surrounded the parliament building and, according to many accounts, prevented members from leaving until a president had been chosen. A photograph of Yuan taken that same day shows a heavy-set man in a military uniform, the trappings of a modern commander-in-chief on a man who had been trained in the late Qing army and who had spent his life moving between the court, the provinces, and the camps of the new armies. Every element of the scene — the republican title, the imperial hall, the soldiers outside the legislature — points to the central problem of this chapter.

This chapter studies the first four years of the Republic of China, from its founding in early 1912 to the death of Yuan Shikai on June 6, 1916. It asks why a state that had overthrown more than two thousand years of imperial government, and that adopted a written constitution, held national elections, and saw the rapid growth of political parties and newspapers, nevertheless failed to establish a stable constitutional order. The answer cannot be found in a single villain or a single flaw. It lies in the interaction of several things: a provisional constitution whose rules about executive and legislative power were disputed from the start; a president who controlled the most effective army in the country; provincial governments that had won their own autonomy during the revolution; a treasury that could not pay its bills without foreign loans; foreign powers with treaty rights and strategic interests; and a political class that was inexperienced, divided, and often unwilling to accept electoral defeat or constitutional restraint.

What changed in these years was substantial. The Qing court was gone, the Gregorian calendar and the Republican reckoning of years replaced the imperial reign-name system in official use, the language of citizenship and popular sovereignty entered law, and for the first time in Chinese history a national legislature was elected, however restricted the electorate. What continued was equally important: the dependence of central authority on personal networks and armies, the unresolved relationship between Beijing and the provinces, the heavy burden of foreign indemnities and treaty privileges, and the habit among many officials of treating government as a matter of personal loyalty rather than institutional rule. The same years also saw the emergence of political practices that would persist: party organization, press campaigns, telegraph politics in which provincial leaders issued public declarations for or against the government, and appeals to “public opinion” as a source of legitimacy.

The people affected by these events were many and varied. Members of the educated elite who ran for office, edited newspapers, or organized parties experienced the Republic as an opening for political participation. Provincial military men and civil officials experienced it as a struggle over who would control appointments, revenue, and troops. Soldiers, many of them unpaid for months, experienced it as an uncertain livelihood. Taxpayers saw the old levies continue under new names. Women who had joined the revolutionary cause demanded political rights and were refused. Residents of Mongolia and Tibet, where local leaders took advantage of the Qing collapse to assert autonomy, experienced the new Republic’s claim to inherit Qing sovereignty as a contested proposition. And ordinary households in the cities of the Yangtze valley, where fighting broke out in the summer of 1913, lived through the consequences of civil war.

Historians understand this period through an unusually varied body of evidence. The text of the Provisional Constitution of 1912 survives, as do the electoral laws, parliamentary records, presidential orders, and the 1914 Constitutional Compact. Telegrams exchanged among officials, published in the press and later gathered in collections, document the fast-moving politics of 1913 and 1915–1916. The terms of the Reorganization Loan were recorded in an international agreement. Newspapers of the time, including the vigorous private press of Shanghai, preserve both the optimism and the polemics of the period, while diplomatic correspondence from foreign legations records how outside observers interpreted Yuan’s rule, with all the biases of foreign diplomats. Photographs, memoirs, and diaries add the perspectives of people who were present. Each source must be read with attention to its author’s position, since almost every participant in these events had a political stake in how they were remembered.

The chapter also connects China to a wider world. The Republic was founded as the great powers moved toward the war that began in Europe in 1914, and the outbreak of World War I gave Japan an opportunity to seize German holdings in Shandong and to present China, in January 1915, with the Twenty-One Demands. The Reorganization Loan tied Chinese finances to an international banking consortium. Debates over whether China should be a republic or a monarchy were conducted in light of arguments from abroad, including those of a foreign constitutional adviser. And the Republic’s weakness in these years shaped the ideas of the younger generation, whose critiques are the subject of Chapter 60.

The chapter’s organizing question is why a republic founded with ambitions for constitutional government and national reconstruction became, within four years, a contest between parliamentary authority and military power that ended with an attempted monarchy and a renewed fragmentation of authority. Students should resist two simplifications. One treats the Republic as doomed from the start, a mere stage in a predetermined sequence leading to later revolutions. The other treats Yuan Shikai as the single destroyer of an otherwise healthy democracy. The evidence supports a more complicated account in which real institution-building, real political participation, and real choices by many actors produced an outcome that was contingent, not inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political situation inherited by the Republic in 1912, including fiscal weakness, military autonomy, foreign treaty privileges, and competing ideas of republican sovereignty.
  2. Describe the Nanjing Provisional Government under Sun Yat-sen and identify both its achievements and the limits of its authority.
  3. Analyze the Provisional Constitution of March 1912, including the roles of the president, cabinet, and legislature, and explain why its provisions were contested.
  4. Explain how Yuan Shikai became president and why his military and political position made him difficult for either supporters or opponents to bypass.
  5. Trace the formation of political parties, the elections of 1912–1913, and the opening of the first National Assembly, distinguishing electoral success from control of government.
  6. Evaluate the evidence concerning the assassination of Song Jiaoren, distinguishing established facts from disputed accusations about responsibility.
  7. Assess the role of state finance, foreign loans, and the Reorganization Loan in the struggle between the presidency and parliament.
  8. Compare the military and political resources of Yuan Shikai and his Nationalist opponents during the Second Revolution of 1913.
  9. Interpret the Constitutional Compact of 1914 and explain how Yuan’s personal government related to republican constitutional forms.
  10. Contextualize the Twenty-One Demands within World War I and Japanese strategic aims in East Asia.
  11. Distinguish the Hongxian monarchy from a Qing restoration and analyze why the monarchical project provoked the National Protection Movement.
  12. Evaluate competing explanations for the failure of constitutional government by 1916, weighing institutions, individual decisions, provincial interests, and foreign pressure.

Historical Context

Chapter 57 traced the revolutionary movements, provincial secessions, and negotiations that ended Qing rule. By the time the child emperor Puyi’s abdication was announced in February 1912, the Republic existed in two senses. It existed as a proclaimed government in Nanjing, formed by delegates from the provinces that had declared independence from the Qing. It also existed as an aspiration shared by people with very different ideas about what republican government should be. Some, like the Tongmenghui revolutionaries, understood it as the fulfilment of a nationalist program. Others, such as constitutional monarchists of the late Qing reform era, accepted it as the only workable solution once the dynasty had collapsed. Many provincial officials and military commanders accepted it because it preserved their local positions.

The new government inherited the Qing state’s problems along with its territory. The Qing treasury had been in difficulty since the indemnities imposed after the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 and the Boxer Protocol of 1901 (Chapters 55 and 56), and the revolution disrupted tax collection further. Customs revenue, the most reliable source of central income, was administered by a foreign-staffed service and, in the turmoil of the revolution, was deposited in foreign banks to guarantee payment of foreign debts. Land taxes and the salt revenue were collected through provincial and local channels that the central government did not control. The late Qing New Policies (Chapter 56) had created new provincial assemblies, new schools, and new armies, but the central government that was meant to coordinate them had collapsed. What remained was a patchwork of provincial authorities, each with its own military force, tax receipts, and political relationships.

Geographically and politically, the Republic claimed the territory of the Qing empire: the eighteen provinces of China proper, the northeastern provinces of Manchuria, and the frontier regions of Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet. The slogan of the “five peoples” (Han, Manchu, Mongol, Hui, and Tibetan) united in one republic expressed this claim, and the Provisional Constitution included all these territories within the Republic. Yet the claim was not accepted by everyone. Mongol princes declared the independence of Outer Mongolia in December 1911 under the Bogd Khan, with Russian encouragement, and the Thirteenth Dalai Lama proclaimed Tibetan independence in 1913 after Qing troops were expelled from Lhasa. The relationship between the Republic and these regions, and the role of Russia and Britain in them, form part of the story told in Chapters 48 and 76; this chapter notes them as a reminder that the Republic’s boundaries were as much a matter of negotiation as its constitution.

Internationally, the Republic operated under the treaty system described in Chapters 52 and 55. Foreign powers held extraterritorial rights, concessions in the treaty ports, leased territories, and spheres of influence. Foreign governments did not formally recognize the Republic until it had given assurances that it would honor existing treaties and debts; the United States extended recognition in May 1913, and Britain, Japan, Russia, and several other powers followed in October 1913, after Yuan had been elected president. Recognition therefore depended in part on the government’s willingness to maintain the framework of foreign privileges, a limitation on republican sovereignty that republican nationalists resented.

Socially, the early Republic was shaped by the changes of the late Qing: the growth of treaty-port cities, the spread of modern schools and newspapers, the rise of a class of returned students from Japan, Europe, and the United States, the expansion of provincial gentry and merchant organizations, and the emergence of new professions. These groups provided most of the leadership of the new parties and legislatures. They formed a small fraction of the population, however. The great majority of Chinese were farmers whose lives were shaped by the tax collector, the landlord, the local lineage, and the harvest, not by constitutional debates in Beijing. Understanding the early Republic requires keeping both groups in view.

Finally, students should note that this was a period in which the word “republic” itself had to be explained. The Chinese term gonghe (共和), used for the Republic, had its own history, including its association with a period of regency in Zhou-dynasty tradition. In 1912 it was being given a new meaning, borrowed from European and American models by way of Japan. This chapter examines the practical experience of making that borrowed form work in Chinese conditions, not whether it was suited to China in some timeless sense.

Historical Evidence

Historical Evidence

Constitutional and legal texts. The Provisional Constitution (March 1912), electoral laws, the Temple of Heaven draft constitution (October 1913), and the Constitutional Compact (May 1914) show how political actors tried to define power. They show intention, not practice; a text can be ignored.

Telegrams and official orders. The telegraph made politics public. Declarations of independence, accusations, and appeals were published in newspapers and later collected. Historians must ask whether a telegram reflects its signer’s views or was drafted by an aide, and who it was meant to persuade.

Newspapers and periodicals. The number of newspapers grew rapidly after 1911 and fell under Yuan’s press regulations; estimates of totals vary. Papers were usually tied to parties or factions, so their reports are evidence of political argument as much as of events.

Diplomatic and banking records. The Reorganization Loan agreement and foreign legation reports document the international dimension. Foreign observers often favored Yuan as a guarantor of order, and their assessments reflect their governments’ interests.

Memoirs, photographs, and trial records. Participants’ later accounts are valuable but self-serving. Photographs, such as the portrait of Yuan taken on October 10, 1913, show how authority was presented. The investigation records for Song Jiaoren’s assassination are among the most important and most disputed documents of the period.

Section 58.1

The Republic of China and the Problem of Political Reconstruction

The end of Qing rule left a gap that no single institution could fill. The dynasty had been, for all its weaknesses, the point at which provincial governors, Banner garrisons, imperial examinations, tax systems, and frontier policies were joined. When it disappeared, each of these elements continued to exist but without the center that had linked them. The Republic’s founders had to decide, almost at once, what kind of state would replace the empire, how it would raise revenue, who would command its armies, and how it would relate to the provinces that had declared independence from the Qing.

Several conditions inherited from the revolution framed every later decision. First, national integration was incomplete. The provinces that declared independence in late 1911 did so as separate units, and the Nanjing government was created by delegates from them, not by a national election. Second, military power was dispersed. The New Armies trained in the late Qing period had been organized provincially, and the revolution had been decided as much by which commanders switched sides as by popular uprising. Third, the fiscal base was weak, partly because the central government depended on foreign-administered customs revenue to service foreign debts, and partly because provinces retained funds that had formerly gone to the capital. Fourth, foreign treaty privileges remained in force, and the powers expected the Republic to honor them.

Republican leaders also disagreed about what “republic” meant. For Sun Yat-sen and many revolutionaries, it meant a government of popular sovereignty, designed to achieve national strength and social reform. For Liang Qichao and many former constitutional monarchists, the essential issue was constitutional limitation on power and orderly administration, and they were wary of mass politics. For many provincial leaders, a republic meant a federation in which provinces held substantial autonomy, an idea revived in various forms until the 1920s. For some military men, it meant a guarantee of their command and their salaries. These were not merely theoretical differences. They produced different answers to concrete questions: Should the capital be Nanjing or Beijing? Should the executive be a strong president or a premier answerable to parliament? Should provincial governors be chosen locally or appointed from the center?

Historians therefore treat 1912 as the beginning of a political reconstruction, not simply the completion of a revolution. The revolutionary coalition that won was united by opposition to the Qing, and once the dynasty was gone it split along lines that had been invisible while the enemy was common. Chapter 57 described how this coalition formed; this chapter describes how it came apart under the pressure of governing.

Section 58.2

Sun Yat-sen and the Nanjing Provisional Government

Delegates from the independent provinces, meeting first in Wuhan and then in Nanjing, elected Sun Yat-sen provisional president on December 29, 1911, shortly after his return from abroad. He was inaugurated at Nanjing on January 1, 1912, which the new government declared the first day of the first year of the Republic and the start of the use of the Gregorian calendar for official purposes. Li Yuanhong, the Hubei military leader, was vice president. The cabinet combined members of the Tongmenghui, such as Huang Xing at the Ministry of the Army, with figures from outside the revolutionary organization, including the educator Cai Yuanpei at the Ministry of Education, the diplomat Wu Tingfang as minister of justice, and the industrialist Zhang Jian as minister of industry. A Provisional Senate, made up of delegates from the provinces, served as a legislature.

The government’s achievements were largely symbolic and legislative. It issued orders proclaiming equality among the peoples of the Republic, abolishing older honorific titles and forms of address such as “Your Excellency,” encouraging the cutting of the queue that the Manchu conquest had imposed on men, and discouraging footbinding and other customs seen as markers of subordination. It sponsored educational reforms under Cai Yuanpei that removed the classics from the center of the curriculum and began to shift schooling toward a modern model. It approved the Provisional Constitution in March. These measures were announced as national policy, but enforcement depended on provincial and local officials, and the practical effect varied enormously from one place to another. The queue was cut quickly in treaty ports and army camps and more slowly in rural areas; footbinding declined unevenly over several decades.

The limits of Sun’s government were severe. It controlled little of the country directly: its writ ran in parts of the lower Yangtze region and the southeast, while the north remained under the Qing court and the commander Yuan Shikai until the abdication. Its finances were desperate. The government struggled to pay the troops that had brought it to power and attempted to raise loans from foreign banks and from Japanese business interests, including one that proposed to pledge control of the Hanyeping iron and coal company, a plan that drew strong criticism and was not carried through. Its authority over the military governors in the provinces was largely nominal.

Sun resigned in favor of Yuan Shikai for reasons that have been debated. Many Tongmenghui leaders believed the revolution could not hold the north by force and that the quickest way to end the dynasty and the threat of foreign intervention was to accept Yuan’s offer of support. Sun himself stipulated conditions: that Yuan accept the Republic, that the capital remain at Nanjing, and that the new president abide by the Provisional Constitution. These conditions show that he and his colleagues understood the risk of handing the presidency to a military man; they also show how little leverage they held. The Nanjing government is best understood as a transitional authority that proclaimed republican principles and created a legal framework but could not enforce its terms against a more powerful partner.

Section 58.3

The Provisional Constitution and Republican Institutions

The Provisional Constitution of the Republic of China, adopted by the Provisional Senate on March 8 and promulgated on March 11, 1912, was drafted rapidly under the pressure of events and amended from earlier organizational rules. It had 56 articles and was meant to govern until a permanent constitution could be drafted by a National Assembly. Its authors, among them Song Jiaoren and Wang Chonghui, were deeply conscious that Yuan Shikai would shortly assume the presidency, and the document should be read partly as an attempt to place institutional limits on a president who could not be controlled by personal trust.

The constitution declared that the Republic was composed of the people of China, that sovereignty belonged to the whole body of citizens, and that all citizens were equal regardless of race, class, or religion. It listed rights, including freedom of person, residence, speech, publication, assembly, association, religion, and correspondence, and it listed duties such as taxation and military service. It stated that the territory of the Republic comprised the provinces of China proper along with Inner and Outer Mongolia, Tibet, and Qinghai, and it recognized no distinction of legal rank among the peoples. Its guarantees were qualified: most rights could be limited by law, and the document did not specify how courts or any other institution would protect them against the executive.

Institutionally, the constitution divided power among a president, a cabinet headed by a premier, a legislature, and the courts. The provisional president was elected by the Provisional Senate, represented the Republic, commanded the armed forces, appointed civil and military officials with the Senate’s consent in specified cases, and promulgated laws. But presidential acts required the countersignature of the relevant minister, and the cabinet was responsible to the legislature. The legislature could question ministers, and the Senate could impeach the president for treason. The provisions drew on parliamentary models, the French Third Republic in particular, in place of the American presidential system that Sun Yat-sen had favored while in Nanjing. Several scholars have noted that the shift to cabinet government was a response to the expected presidency of Yuan.

These arrangements were ambiguous in practice. The constitution did not say clearly whether the premier or the president selected cabinet members, how a dispute between president and cabinet would be resolved, or whether the president could dissolve the legislature. It left open the status of the military governors, who exercised civil as well as military powers in their provinces, and it did not establish a procedure for settling disputes between Beijing and the provinces. Because it was a provisional text drafted to bridge a crisis, it left much to custom, and custom had not yet been formed.

Historical Vocabulary
Republic, Provisional Constitution, and Cabinet Responsibility

Republic (共和, gonghe). A form of government without a hereditary monarch, in which authority is claimed to rest on the people or their representatives. The Chinese term had older associations with a period of joint rule recorded in early historical tradition, and it was adopted in the 1900s to translate the Western concept. In 1912 it described both a constitutional structure and a political aspiration.

Provisional Constitution (临时约法, linshi yuefa). The temporary basic law of March 1912. The word yuefa suggests an agreement or covenant, and the choice of term reflected the idea that it was a compact among the provinces and the people, to be replaced by a permanent constitution.

Cabinet responsibility. A system in which the executive ministers answer to the legislature and the head of state acts through them. Under the Provisional Constitution, a president’s orders needed ministerial countersignature, which meant a president could not govern alone without the cooperation of a cabinet the legislature could challenge. This design explains why struggles over the premiership and cabinet became the central political fights of 1912–1913.

Section 58.4

Yuan Shikai Becomes President

Yuan Shikai was born in 1859 into an official family in Henan. After service with Qing forces in Korea in the 1880s and early 1890s, he gained prominence by training a modern army at Xiaozhan near Tianjin in 1895 and by rising through provincial offices, serving as governor of Shandong and later as governor-general of Zhili and Beiyang commissioner. He promoted police reform, schools, and telegraph and railway projects, and his army, the Beiyang forces, became the most professional in the empire. After the death of the Guangxu emperor and the Empress Dowager Cixi in 1908, the regent Prince Chun dismissed him from office in 1909. The outbreak of revolution in October 1911 led the court to recall him, and by early 1912 he held both the premiership of the Qing government and the confidence of many provincial and foreign leaders.

His appeal in 1912 rested on a combination of resources. He commanded loyal officers, the Beiyang forces were the only army capable of overcoming the revolutionaries, and foreign bankers and diplomats regarded him as the likeliest figure to preserve order and protect their investments. Revolutionary leaders in the south feared prolonged war. These calculations made him the necessary partner in an arrangement under which the Qing emperor abdicated, Sun resigned, and Yuan was elected provisional president by the Provisional Senate in mid-February. The abdication edict itself named Yuan as the person to organize a republican government, a text that republican critics and Yuan’s supporters read differently.

The agreed conditions soon came under strain. The south wanted Yuan to come to Nanjing to take office, which would have separated him from his military base. In late February and early March, mutinies broke out among troops in Beijing, Tianjin, and Baoding, apparently involving soldiers in the Third Division, and Yuan argued that his presence in the north was needed to preserve order. Whether the mutinies were staged, as many contemporaries suspected, or were spontaneous outbreaks by soldiers worried about pay, discipline, and their future, is still debated; the evidence is circumstantial. Their effect was to make Nanjing’s delegation accept Yuan’s inauguration at Beijing on March 10, 1912, and the capital remained in the north.

The result was a presidency with a Beijing base, a constitution that limited it on paper, and a Nanjing group that had accepted these terms while continuing to hold influence in several southern provinces. Historians stress that this outcome did not predetermine what followed. In 1912 Yuan worked with a cabinet led by Tang Shaoyi, a long-time associate who joined the Tongmenghui, and he made substantial concessions to parliamentary conventions. The tension between legitimate succession and concentrated executive authority was real, but its resolution depended on later conflicts over appointments, finances, and the interpretation of the constitution.

Section 58.5

The Beiyang Army, Provincial Governments, and the Limits of Central Authority

The practical structure of power in 1912 differed from the constitutional one. The Beiyang Army had grown out of the New Army reforms and the training camps at Xiaozhan. Its officers, including Duan Qirui, Feng Guozhang, Cao Kun, and Wang Shizhen, had been trained together or under Yuan’s patronage, and personal ties of loyalty, patronage, and common service linked them. The army was not a modern national force in the sense of an institution loyal to the state apart from its commanders; soldiers’ loyalty ran to their officers, who were paid, promoted, and protected through Yuan’s network. The Beiyang commanders held divisions that were stationed mainly in north China and which formed the core of the central government’s coercive power.

Beyond the Beiyang forces, the Republic had a large and varied military establishment. Provincial armies had been created during the revolution, led by officers who had declared for the Republic or who had been appointed military governors (dudu) by provincial assemblies. Veteran forces of the old Qing armies, irregular bands, and militias added to the total. By one estimate, the central government faced the problem of demobilizing and paying hundreds of thousands of troops with inadequate funds, and the resulting arrears made soldiers a source of disorder as well as of strength.

In the provinces, military governors often combined civil and military authority. Some had risen from revolutionary groups, some from provincial New Armies, some from the former Qing officialdom. Their relationship to Beijing was negotiated. The central government could appoint and dismiss governors, but only if it had the military strength to enforce its decisions and the revenue to pay them. Taxation illustrates the problem. The land tax and the salt revenue passed through provincial and local channels; the central government relied on remittances from provinces, which were frequently late or incomplete. Provinces retained revenue to pay their own troops, and some, such as Yunnan, Guangxi, and Guangdong in the south, Shanxi in the north, or Fengtian in Manchuria, were administered by leaders whose personal control was strong.

These conditions gave the early Republic a distinctive political character. Constitutional authority rested on the president, the cabinet, and the legislature in Beijing; practical authority rested on commanders and their ability to collect revenue and pay soldiers. Whenever the two diverged, the second tended to prevail. Yuan used this fact to his advantage by appointing commanders loyal to him, by sending funds selectively, and by playing provincial rivals against one another. His opponents, who controlled the legislature and a few provinces, lacked comparable military resources.

Regional and Frontier Context

The Republic as a Collection of Regions

North China and the capital region. Beijing, Tianjin, and the Zhili plain formed Yuan’s base. The Beiyang divisions, the railways, the telegraph lines, and the diplomatic quarter gave the northern capital resources no other city could match, and its revenue sources were relatively secure.

The lower and middle Yangtze. The cities of Shanghai, Nanjing, and Wuhan combined wealth, newspapers, banks, and revolutionary traditions. Guomindang influence was strong in Jiangxi, Anhui, and parts of Guangdong and Hunan, which is why these provinces were the main centers of the 1913 conflict.

The southwest and south. Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi, and Sichuan were governed by leaders with local armies, some of whom had entered the revolution through the New Armies or the provincial assemblies. Their remoteness from Beijing and the strength of their forces explain why they could resist Yuan in 1915–1916.

Frontier regions. Outer Mongolia declared independence from the Qing in December 1911 under the Bogd Khan with Russian support, and the Thirteenth Dalai Lama asserted Tibetan independence in 1913. The Republic’s claim to these regions rested on the Qing inheritance. Mongol and Tibetan leaders had their own understandings of their former relationship with the Qing, in which allegiance to the emperor as a Manchu ruler and patron of Buddhism differed from subordination to a Chinese republic. The Simla negotiations among Britain, China, and Tibet in 1913–1914, which the Chinese government declined to ratify, and the Kiakhta agreement of 1915 among China, Russia, and Mongolia, which recognized Mongol autonomy under Chinese suzerainty, illustrate how Beijing’s claims had to be negotiated with outside powers. Manchuria, where Japan and Russia held spheres of influence, was administered by provincial leaders with little central control.

Section 58.6

Political Parties, Public Opinion, and Republican Citizenship

The months after the revolution saw an explosion of political organization. Within a year of the Republic’s founding, hundreds of political associations, clubs, and parties had been formed, many of them short-lived and some little more than a name and a letterhead. They ranged from large national organizations to local societies built around a newspaper or a patron. The reasons were clear. The collapse of the Qing removed the legal barriers to association that had applied under the dynasty; the Provisional Constitution guaranteed freedom of assembly and speech; and the prospect of a national legislature made organization worthwhile. For educated men who had spent the 1900s in student societies, provincial assemblies, or reformist circles, party politics seemed the natural next step.

The press grew alongside the parties. Newspapers multiplied in Shanghai, Beijing, Tianjin, Guangzhou, Wuhan, and provincial capitals. Some were party organs, some were commercial ventures, and some reflected the views of a single editor. The telegraph allowed rapid exchange of news, and politics was increasingly carried on in public, through open letters and published telegrams addressed to “the people of the whole country.” Appeals to public opinion (yulun) became an accepted form of political argument, although the public in question was largely literate and urban. Estimates of the number of newspapers vary, and students should treat any exact figure with caution.

Citizenship was formally broad and practically narrow. The constitution made all citizens equal and protected rights, but the electoral laws of 1912 limited the vote to men over twenty-one who had resided in their district for at least two years and who met one of several qualifications: payment of a specified amount of direct tax, ownership of property above a set value, or completion of primary school. These rules enfranchised perhaps ten percent of the population by contemporary estimates, a much larger share than any earlier selection process but still a small minority. They excluded most women, most poor farmers, soldiers on active service, clergy, and others. Many officials and educators defended property and education qualifications on the ground that voters needed to understand public affairs, an argument common in many countries at the time.

Women demanded political rights. Tang Qunying, a Hunanese revolutionary who had been educated in Japan, founded the Women’s Suffrage Alliance in Nanjing in early 1912 with colleagues such as Lin Zongsu. They petitioned the Provisional Senate to include sex equality in the constitution, and when the Senate refused, groups of women confronted legislators and, in one episode, forced their way into the chamber. Their demand was rejected; the electoral laws of 1912–1913 limited suffrage to men. Women’s activism did not end. It continued through newspapers, schools, charitable organizations, and demands for education and an end to concubinage and footbinding, and it fed into the New Culture movement of the later 1910s (Chapter 60).

Multiple Perspectives

What Did the Republic Mean in 1912–1913?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named person.

A Guomindang organizer: For party members, the Republic was the result of a revolution that had required years of sacrifice, and it should be governed by a parliament in which the party’s electoral strength would give it control of the cabinet. Cabinet government was both a principle and a way of limiting a president who came from the old order.
A constitutionalist and supporter of order: Many former constitutional monarchists, who later formed the Progressive Party, accepted the Republic but feared disorder, radical politics, and foreign intervention. They tended to favor a strong executive working with a capable legislature and were more willing than the Guomindang to cooperate with Yuan.
A Beiyang soldier: For soldiers, the Republic raised practical questions about pay, discipline, and who commanded them. Their loyalty ran to their officers, and the Republic’s constitutional principles mattered less than the regular supply of rations and wages. Mutinies in 1912 arose in part from fears of disbandment.
A newspaper editor in Shanghai: Editors experienced a new freedom to criticize officials, and the press became a major arena for politics. They also faced libel suits, funding problems, and, after 1913, official suppression.
A woman seeking political rights: Activists such as Tang Qunying argued that women had participated in the revolution and deserved equal citizenship. They met opposition from male legislators, including some revolutionaries, who regarded women’s suffrage as premature. Their exclusion shows the limits of the Republic’s egalitarian language, even as their campaign helped establish suffrage as a lasting demand.

Note on balance: These perspectives were not equally powerful. Soldiers and officers could change governments by force; editors and women activists could only appeal to opinion.

Section 58.7

The Nationalist Party and Song Jiaoren

The Guomindang (国民党), usually translated as the Nationalist Party, was formed in Beijing in August 1912 through the merger of the Tongmenghui and several smaller groups, including the Unified Republican Party and other associations of former reformers and revolutionaries. Sun Yat-sen was elected its director, but in practice day-to-day leadership passed to Song Jiaoren, who served as its principal organizer and campaign strategist. The merger was an effort to turn a revolutionary organization into a party capable of winning elections, and its results were mixed. It gained many members and supporters; it also diluted the revolutionary identity of the Tongmenghui and brought together people with different ideas about policy.

Song Jiaoren, born in Hunan in 1882, had been a member of the Tongmenghui, had studied in Japan, and was a champion of constitutional government. He argued that a republic required a responsible cabinet formed by the leading party in parliament, and he drafted much of the party’s platform, which stressed national unification, local self-government, the development of industry and communications, and equality for peoples of the Republic. He toured the country in late 1912 and early 1913 to organize the elections. Many contemporaries saw him as the leading figure who might head a Guomindang cabinet, which would test Yuan’s acceptance of parliamentary government.

Sun Yat-sen’s role was different. After resigning the presidency, Sun accepted an offer from Yuan to serve as a national director of railway development, and for several months he toured the country promoting an ambitious scheme for building railroads. He regarded this as a way to pursue national development while avoiding direct conflict, and he did not take part in daily party affairs as Song did. This division of labor is important for understanding the Guomindang’s later split: Sun favored revolutionary methods when parliamentary ones failed, while Song and many other party leaders invested in the legislature.

Opposing parties included the Republican Party, led nominally by Li Yuanhong and supported by Yuan’s allies and some provincial leaders, and the Democratic Party associated with Liang Qichao, who returned from exile in late 1912. In May 1913 these groups and others merged to form the Progressive Party. It supported a strong executive and drew support from provincial elites and many former Qing officials. Yuan regarded the Progressive Party as a more reliable partner than the Guomindang, although it was never simply his instrument.

Historical Vocabulary
Guomindang, National Assembly, and Electorate

Guomindang (国民党). Literally “party of the nation’s people.” Often rendered Kuomintang (KMT) in older English-language sources. The party formed in 1912 differed from the Nationalist Party reorganized in 1923–1924 (Chapter 61), which was built along Leninist lines. Students should not read the later party’s features back into the 1912 organization.

National Assembly (国会, guohui). The bicameral legislature elected in 1912–1913, consisting of a Senate and a House of Representatives. It also had the task of drafting the permanent constitution. It must be distinguished from the Provisional Senate that sat in Nanjing and then Beijing in 1912.

Electorate. The body of citizens qualified to vote. In 1912–1913 the electorate was limited by gender, residence, age, and property or education requirements, and elections were indirect: voters chose electors who chose representatives. Even so, tens of millions of men were eligible, an unprecedented number in Chinese history.

Section 58.8

The Elections of 1912–1913 and the First National Assembly

The election laws, issued in the summer of 1912, provided for a two-chamber legislature: a House of Representatives of about 600 members chosen from the provinces and certain other districts, and a Senate of about 270 members chosen by provincial assemblies and by specified bodies representing Mongolia, Tibet, Qinghai, and overseas Chinese. Voting took place in stages. Qualified men voted for local electors, and the electors then chose representatives. Polling took place in late 1912 and early 1913, in different places at different times. The administration of elections relied on local officials, who could influence turnout and results, and contemporaries reported bribery, intimidation, and the sale of votes in some places, alongside serious campaigning in others.

The results favored the Guomindang. In round numbers, it won about 270 of roughly 600 seats in the House of Representatives and about 120 of roughly 270 in the Senate, making it by far the largest party in both chambers, although exact totals vary by source and depend on how independents and members who changed affiliation are counted. The Republican Party and its successors in the Progressive Party were the next largest groups, with smaller parties and independents making up the rest. The Guomindang therefore held a strong plurality, not an undisputed majority; to govern with parliamentary support it would still have needed allies or defections, a point students sometimes overlook.

The election showed that a large number of people in many provinces were willing to participate in national politics, and it showed that organized parties could succeed against the government’s preferences. It also showed the limits of electoral victory. The constitution gave the legislature a role in approving cabinets but left the president powers of appointment and command that were not clearly bounded. Yuan controlled the army, the central ministries, and the provincial appointments, and he could expect the support of foreign governments that valued stability. A party that held a plurality of seats but no troops and little revenue had to persuade the president to accept its claims.

The National Assembly opened in Beijing on April 8, 1913. Its tasks were to organize itself, to pass legislation, to confirm the cabinet, and to draft a permanent constitution and elect a regular president. Its early sessions were consumed by procedural disputes, by the investigation of Song Jiaoren’s assassination (below), and by the question of the Reorganization Loan. These disputes illustrated both the vitality of the new institution and its weakness. Members debated vigorously, and some Progressive and Guomindang members cooperated on specific issues. But the assembly lacked the established rules, traditions, and enforcement mechanisms that give legislatures practical weight, and it was meeting in a capital dominated by the executive’s military forces.

Section 58.9

The Assassination of Song Jiaoren and the Republican Constitutional Crisis

On the evening of March 20, 1913, Song Jiaoren was at the Shanghai railway station, preparing to board a train to Beijing to attend the opening of the National Assembly. As he walked toward the platform, a man fired a pistol at him from behind. Song was wounded in the abdomen and taken to a hospital, where he died on March 22. He was thirty years old. Before his death he reportedly dictated a telegram to Yuan Shikai urging him to respect the Republic’s constitution and to protect the rights of the people, a message whose content and authorship have been reported in different versions. His death removed the Guomindang’s leading advocate of cabinet government at the moment of its electoral success.

The investigation moved quickly. Within days, Shanghai authorities arrested the gunman, Wu Shiying (武士英), and a man named Ying Guixin who had arranged the affair. Police found papers in Ying’s possession, and in April local officials, with strong Guomindang influence in Shanghai, released telegrams and documents that appeared to show that Ying had been in communication with Hong Shuzu, a secretary of the premier, Zhao Bingjun, and, through Hong, with the Beijing government. The documents were published in the press and aroused widespread suspicion that the central government or its associates had arranged the killing. Ying and Wu Shiying were later killed or died in custody under circumstances that increased suspicion, and the legal proceedings were never concluded in a way that satisfied the public.

Historians distinguish carefully among what is known, what is probable, and what is disputed. It is established that Song was shot on March 20, that he died on March 22, that the gunman and his intermediary were arrested, and that documents linking Ying with officials in Beijing were published. It is also established that Ying and Wu died before trial could be completed. What is not established beyond dispute is who gave the order and what Yuan Shikai personally knew. Some historians conclude that officials close to Yuan, possibly including Premier Zhao Bingjun and Hong Shuzu, were responsible and that Yuan either authorized the plot or at least knew of it. Others point out that the surviving documents are open to interpretation, that Guomindang-controlled authorities had political reasons to publicize them, and that other motives, including factional rivalries, cannot be excluded. Students should treat claims that Yuan personally ordered the killing as a serious and widely held interpretation rather than as a proven fact.

Whatever the truth about responsibility, the political consequences were immediate. The Guomindang and its supporters concluded that Yuan and his cabinet were willing to use violence against parliamentary opponents. Sun Yat-sen, who returned from Japan upon hearing the news, argued for armed action, while others, including Huang Xing, favored legal methods and the use of the new parliament to demand the cabinet’s resignation. The party was divided, and Yuan benefited from the division. The crisis also hardened positions on both sides: Yuan’s supporters viewed the Guomindang as radical and willing to use the pretext of the assassination to provoke rebellion, while Guomindang leaders saw the central government as unwilling to submit to law. The assassination made compromise between the two camps far more difficult.

Section 58.10

State Finance, Foreign Loans, and the Reorganization Loan

Every government needs revenue, and in 1912–1913 the Republic’s was inadequate. Expenses included the salaries of civil servants and soldiers, the costs of disbanding or reorganizing armies, the payments owed on foreign indemnities and loans inherited from the Qing, and the costs of new administrative and educational programs. Income was uncertain. Customs duties, the most dependable source, were already pledged to foreign creditors, and surpluses after debt service were limited. The salt revenue, a second major source, was collected through a system riddled with provincial and merchant interests. The land tax was largely retained in the provinces, and the internal transit tax (lijin) was collected by provincial authorities. Provinces often withheld remittances, claiming that they needed funds for their own troops.

Foreign banks and governments were the obvious source of credit. An international banking group had negotiated with the late Qing about a large loan, and after the revolution the group, representing Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, and initially the United States, sought terms that would give foreign creditors a measure of supervision over the use of the funds and over the salt revenue. Negotiations took place in 1912 and early 1913 amid public criticism in China of the conditions. The American government under Woodrow Wilson withdrew its bankers from the consortium in March 1913, objecting that the terms infringed on China’s administrative independence, and the remaining five powers continued the talks.

On April 26, 1913, Premier Zhao Bingjun and Finance Minister Zhou Xuexi signed the Reorganization Loan agreement with the five-power group. The loan was for £25 million, bore interest at five percent, ran for forty-seven years, and was issued to investors at a discount, so that the Chinese government received considerably less than the nominal sum. It was secured on the salt revenue and on other pledged sources. Foreign officials were to be appointed to the salt administration, giving the creditors a continuing voice in a major revenue system, and the agreement specified procedures for auditing the use of the funds. The government signed it without the National Assembly’s approval, arguing that the loan was urgent and that the assembly, which opened only on April 8, had not yet organized itself.

The loan’s significance went beyond finance. It gave Yuan money to pay soldiers, retire debts, and equip troops that he would use in the summer of 1913 against his opponents, and it did so without the legislature’s consent. Guomindang members protested that the government had violated the constitutional principle that borrowing required parliamentary approval, and they also objected to the surrender of fiscal control to foreigners. Progressives and others were more willing to accept the loan as necessary. In this way a technical financial agreement became a constitutional dispute. Control over revenue and borrowing was at the core of what the legislature claimed to have a right to decide, and the executive’s ability to bypass it showed where power lay.

Cause and Effect

State Finance, Military Spending, and the Reorganization Loan

Causes

Long-term: indemnity and debt burdens since the 1890s; provincial control of the land tax and other revenue; weak central tax administration. Short-term: the revolution’s disruption of collection; the costs of paying and disbanding armies; the need for funds in early 1913.

Event

The signing of the £25 million loan on April 26, 1913, with the five-power group, secured on salt and other revenues, without parliamentary approval.

Immediate effects

Funds for the army and administration; Guomindang protests; strengthened foreign influence over the salt administration; a constitutional dispute over borrowing powers.

Long-term effects

Greater financial capacity for Yuan in 1913–1914; a precedent for executive borrowing; increased foreign involvement in revenue; a model of how loans tied to foreign creditors shaped later governments.

Historical complexity: The loan did not by itself cause the conflict of 1913. It supplied resources and a grievance to forces already in tension. It also reflects a structural problem in which any government that wanted to rule from Beijing needed money, and the available sources were foreign banks, whose terms narrowed the options of whatever government borrowed.

Section 58.11

The Second Revolution of 1913

By the spring of 1913, Yuan and the Guomindang were in open conflict. The assassination, the loan, and disputes over appointments had eroded cooperation. In June, Yuan dismissed three Guomindang-aligned provincial governors: Li Liejun of Jiangxi on June 9, Hu Hanmin of Guangdong on June 14, and Bai Wenwei of Anhui at the end of the month. The dismissals were presented as ordinary administrative changes, but the three provinces were among the party’s strongholds, and in each case a commander more acceptable to Beijing was named. For the Guomindang, the dismissals showed that the president intended to remove their provincial base. For Yuan, they were an exercise of his power of appointment and a step toward consolidating authority.

Armed resistance began in July. Li Liejun declared Jiangxi independent on July 12 at Hukou and called for the removal of Yuan. Huang Xing proclaimed independence in Nanjing on July 15, and during the next two weeks Anhui, Guangdong, Fujian, Hunan, and Sichuan made declarations of varying degrees of firmness. Sun Yat-sen, who had returned to China, called for a punitive expedition against Yuan. In Shanghai, Chen Qimei led an attack on the Jiangnan Arsenal, which failed. The movement became known as the Second Revolution (erci geming), a name its participants used to link it to the revolution of 1911, though Yuan’s government called it a rebellion.

The rising was defeated within weeks. The Beiyang army, better equipped, better paid thanks in part to the loan, and led by experienced commanders, advanced on Jiangxi under Duan Qirui and on Nanjing under Feng Guozhang and Zhang Xun, while Li Yuanhong in Hubei sided with the government. The southern forces were divided; many soldiers and officers in the supposedly independent provinces were reluctant to fight, and some provincial leaders wavered or changed sides. Commercial interests in Shanghai and elsewhere, worried about disorder, were not enthusiastic supporters. Guangdong was recovered by forces loyal to Yuan in August, and Nanjing fell on September 1 after a siege. Zhang Xun’s troops, who wore the queue as a sign of loyalty to the old order, were accused of looting and killing in the city, an episode that foreign residents reported and that damaged the reputation of the northern army. Sun, Huang Xing, and other leaders fled abroad, most to Japan.

The outcome showed the unequal resources of the two sides. The Guomindang had a plurality in parliament and a base of provincial support but no unified command, limited funds, and a movement divided about whether to fight. Yuan had a trained army, regular funding, control of the railways and telegraph in the north, foreign goodwill, and an opportunity to present himself as defender of order against rebels. The war also strengthened Yuan’s position within the Beiyang group, since his generals gained provincial posts as a reward and extended the reach of the army into the Yangtze valley, a pattern that would later work against central control when those generals developed their own interests.

Section 58.12

The Suppression of the Nationalists and the Destruction of Parliament

With the rising crushed, Yuan moved to remove the remaining constraints on his authority. The National Assembly was still in session, and its first task was to elect a permanent president. On October 6, 1913, the two houses voted in a session held while crowds, organized as “citizens’ corps,” gathered around the building demanding that the vote proceed quickly; many accounts say members were prevented from leaving until the vote was completed. Yuan was elected on the third ballot, with Li Yuanhong as vice president, and was inaugurated on October 10. Britain, Japan, Russia, and other powers recognized the Republic within days. Foreign recognition was now a source of strength for Yuan, who could claim both a legislative mandate and international acceptance.

Meanwhile the assembly’s constitution committee, meeting at the Temple of Heaven in Beijing, completed a draft permanent constitution at the end of October. Often called the Temple of Heaven Draft, it preserved cabinet responsibility, limited the president’s power to dissolve the legislature, and required parliamentary consent for important executive acts. Yuan objected. He sent telegrams to provincial governors asking for their views, and the responses from many military governors, who were allied to him or dependent on him, condemned the draft as an infringement on executive power.

On November 4, 1913, Yuan ordered the dissolution of the Guomindang, citing its role in the Second Revolution, and canceled the credentials of its members in the National Assembly. About 430 legislators were affected, along with local officials who belonged to the party. The assembly, which had lost many members, no longer had the quorum necessary to conduct business. The order was issued without any court proceeding and without legislative approval; the Guomindang’s legal existence was ended by executive decree. In January 1914 Yuan formally dissolved the remaining assembly, and in the following weeks provincial and local assemblies and self-government bodies were also dissolved. A Political Council appointed by the president replaced the parliament in a consultative role.

Press controls followed. Regulations issued in 1914 required newspapers to register, restricted reporting on certain subjects, and allowed the authorities to close publications. Editors and journalists were arrested, and many papers closed or changed tone. Sun Yat-sen and many Guomindang leaders were abroad, where they reorganized in 1914 as the Chinese Revolutionary Party (Zhonghua geming dang), a more tightly disciplined organization that required members to swear personal loyalty to Sun, an arrangement that caused disagreements among former colleagues, including Huang Xing. The destruction of parliament thus did two things: it removed the institution that had been designed to limit the presidency, and it pushed opposition politics into exile, conspiracy, and secret organization.

Historical Debate

Was Parliamentary Government Viable in 1912–1913?

The question: The early Republic’s parliament was dissolved by executive order within two years of its election. Historians ask whether the failure resulted mainly from conditions that made parliamentary government unworkable in China at that time, or from particular decisions and events that might have turned out differently.

Interpretation A — Structural conditions made the experiment unlikely to survive: Scholars who emphasize structure point to the absence of a financially independent central state, the autonomy of provincial armies, the narrow social base of party politics, the lack of experience with legislatures, the pressure of foreign powers, and the fact that real authority rested on force. In this view, even a more cautious president or a more skillful party leadership would have struggled to make parliament sovereign over armies it did not control.
Interpretation B — Individual decisions and contingency were decisive: Others stress that the political system was still being formed, that the Guomindang’s electoral success showed real support, that many officials and elites were willing to work within constitutional limits, and that decisions by Yuan, by Guomindang leaders who chose armed revolt, and by foreign lenders shaped the outcome. On this view, the assassination, the loan, and the choice to resist by force narrowed possibilities that were still open in early 1913.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite the budget and military records, the dependence of governors on their own armies, and the weakness of party organization outside cities. Supporters of B cite election results, parliamentary debates, the early cooperation between Yuan and the Tang Shaoyi cabinet, the political significance of Song’s killing, and the division among Guomindang leaders over strategy. Both sides use the same body of telegrams and press reports and differ in how much weight they give to structure versus choice. Many historians combine the two, treating structural weakness as setting limits within which individual decisions mattered.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how the whole Republican period is understood. If failure was structural, later developments appear as continuations of an underlying condition. If it was contingent, the early Republic appears as a lost opportunity, and the actions of individuals acquire greater weight.

Section 58.13

Yuan Shikai’s Personal Government and the Constitutional Compact of 1914

After dissolving parliament, Yuan reorganized the government around the presidency. A Constitutional Convention appointed in early 1914 produced a new basic law, the Constitutional Compact (Zhonghua minguo yuefa), promulgated on May 1, 1914, to replace the Provisional Constitution. The new text created a strong presidency: the president was head of state and held executive power, commanded the armed forces, declared war and made treaties, appointed and dismissed officials, and issued emergency orders. A Secretary of State, a post filled by the veteran official Xu Shichang, replaced the premier, and the cabinet was answerable to the president, not the legislature. A Council of State of presidential appointees advised him, and a legislative body was planned, to be organized later under conditions the president would determine. Rights were retained in the text but could be limited by law.

Changes in the rules for the presidency itself followed. In December 1914, revised regulations extended the president’s term to ten years, allowed unlimited reelection, and provided that the president would write the name of a successor on a document to be kept in a sealed box, with candidates chosen from a list also compiled by him. These rules made the presidency in effect a lifetime post with a designated succession, and many observers noted that the form resembled a monarchy more than a republic.

In administration, Yuan strengthened Beijing’s control. Provincial military governors were renamed generals (jiangjun), with civil governors (xunanshi) responsible for civil affairs, in an attempt to separate the two functions, and he used appointments to reward loyalty and to move potentially independent commanders. Local self-government bodies were suspended. The government also pursued reforms: a national currency law of 1914 led to a standard silver dollar bearing his portrait, the Bank of China expanded, new commercial and industrial regulations were issued, and efforts were made to reorganize the judicial and educational systems. Many foreign and Chinese observers credited the government with greater order than in 1912–1913. Yuan also revived state ceremonies associated with Confucian worship and, in December 1914, personally performed the winter solstice sacrifice at the Temple of Heaven, a rite associated with the emperors.

This pattern created a contradiction between republican forms and personal rule. The government still called itself a republic, still used republican titles, and maintained a constitution. But the constitution was written to confirm presidential supremacy, and the institutions that might have limited it had been dissolved. Yuan’s authority also remained dependent on the loyalty of generals and governors, not on any new institutional basis, and on foreign recognition and loans. A system that concentrated power in one man was vulnerable to challenges when his own legitimacy came into question.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Did Politics Reach Ordinary Households?

Taxpayers and farmers. For most rural households, the Republic changed little in the first years. The land tax, local surcharges, and the transit tax continued, and provincial and local authorities collected new levies to support armies. The political arguments about parliament were remote, but the effects of military spending and the movement of troops were not. In regions where fighting occurred in 1913, farmers experienced requisition, the destruction of crops, and flight.

Soldiers. Troops were usually recruited from poor families and paid irregularly. Mutinies, looting, and disbanded soldiers turning to banditry were recurring problems. Service in a disciplined army such as the Beiyang divisions could mean a steady wage and a career, which helps explain loyalty to commanders.

Newspapers and urban readers. In treaty ports and capitals, newspapers brought political news to literate readers, teahouses and schools became places of discussion, and students and merchants joined associations. Press regulations after 1913 reduced this variety. Ordinary readers experienced political change partly as the narrowing of what could be read.

Customs and daily practice. Haircuts, forms of address, calendars, and dress changed unevenly. The cutting of the queue was widespread in cities and among students and soldiers and slower elsewhere. The Republican calendar governed offices while most households continued to follow the lunar calendar for festivals, markets, and agricultural work. Official and everyday time coexisted.

Women and families. Girls’ schools expanded, and urban women entered teaching, nursing, and journalism in increasing numbers, but marriage, property, and legal rights continued to be governed largely by older family practices and new laws that remained to be written. Women’s political activism, discussed in section 58.6, was limited in effect but shaped later movements.

Caution: Most surviving sources were produced by educated men in cities. Evidence about rural households comes mostly from later surveys, local gazetteers, missionary accounts, and memoirs, and should be read with attention to those limitations.

Section 58.14

Foreign Relations, World War I, and Japan’s Twenty-One Demands

The Republic’s foreign relations were conditioned by treaties inherited from the Qing and by the strategic interests of the powers. After recognition in 1913, Beijing sought to maintain the existing treaty framework while pressing for modest gains, such as tariff adjustments and the recovery of some administrative rights. It also faced pressure from Russia and Britain over Mongolia and Tibet, from Japan over Manchuria, and from creditors over the salt administration and customs. Yuan’s government relied on balancing powers against each other, a practice with a long history in Qing diplomacy but one with narrowing margins as the powers’ rivalries sharpened in Europe.

When war broke out in Europe in the summer of 1914, the Chinese government declared neutrality and asked the belligerents to keep fighting out of Chinese territory. Japan, allied with Britain, declared war on Germany in August 1914 and sent forces to Shandong, landing at Longkou in early September, outside the German leased territory, and then attacking Qingdao, which fell on November 7. Japan then took over the German railway and mining rights in Shandong and extended its control along the Jiaozhou–Jinan railway. The Chinese government protested the violation of its territory but lacked the means to resist.

On January 18, 1915, the Japanese minister in Beijing, Hioki Eki, presented Yuan directly with a list of twenty-one demands arranged in five groups. The first group concerned the transfer of German rights in Shandong to Japan. The second extended Japanese leases and economic privileges in South Manchuria and eastern Inner Mongolia, including the lease of Lüshun and Dalian to ninety-nine years. The third dealt with the Hanyeping Company, a major iron and coal enterprise in the Yangtze valley, which was to become a joint Sino-Japanese venture. The fourth pledged China not to cede or lease coastal territory to other powers. The fifth group consisted of “wishes” that would have placed Japanese advisers in Chinese government, joint policing, and purchasing of arms from Japan in certain areas, and would have given Japan privileges in Fujian and elsewhere; it would in effect have limited Chinese sovereignty.

Negotiations lasted from early February until late April. The Chinese government, led by Foreign Minister Lu Zhengxiang and Vice Minister Cao Rulin, resisted the most extensive demands, and it leaked information about the fifth group to the press and to foreign diplomats, aiming to stir international concern. Public opposition grew in China, with protests, petitions, and a boycott of Japanese goods in several cities. Britain, preoccupied with the war, was reluctant to confront its ally, and the United States, through a note from Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan on May 11, declared that it would not recognize agreements that impaired American treaty rights or Chinese territorial integrity, but took no further action. On May 7 Japan issued an ultimatum, with the fifth group set aside for later discussion. Yuan’s government accepted on May 8–9, and the treaties and notes were signed on May 25. May 9 became remembered as a day of national humiliation.

The political consequences were serious. Yuan’s government was blamed for having conceded so much, although his defenders argued that no alternative to acceptance existed. Nationalist sentiment, already strong among students and merchants, took on an anti-Japanese focus that would recur in 1919 and later. The episode also affected Yuan’s calculations: it showed that Japan was a threat to the Republic and that he could not count on Western powers, and some historians suggest it contributed to his desire to strengthen his own authority.

China and the Wider World

Japan, the Powers, and the First World War in East Asia

The war in Europe reduced the capacity of Britain, France, Germany, and Russia to project power in East Asia and gave Japan and, to a lesser degree, the United States greater freedom of action. Japanese leaders, from Prime Minister Okuma Shigenobu’s cabinet, saw an opportunity to secure interests in Manchuria and Shandong that they believed necessary for security and economic development. Their methods, including the presentation of demands directly to the Chinese president and the use of an ultimatum, reflected the habits of imperial diplomacy.

China was not merely a passive object. Chinese negotiators resisted demands, used publicity, appealed to other powers, and mobilized domestic opinion. The boycott showed that merchants and students could act as political agents. The war also created economic opportunities: with European competitors absorbed in the war, Chinese textile, flour, and other industries expanded in the late 1910s, and Chinese laborers were later recruited to work in Europe. The effects of Japanese gains in Shandong would reappear at the Paris Peace Conference (Chapter 60).

Section 58.15

Yuan Shikai and the Attempt to Establish a New Monarchy

In the second half of 1915 a campaign to change the form of government began, and the role of foreign expertise in it needs careful sequencing. Frank J. Goodnow, an American professor of administrative law who had served as constitutional adviser to the Chinese government since 1913, returned to Beijing in mid-July 1915. Yuan Shikai asked him to prepare, for the president’s own information, a memorandum on the respective merits of republican and monarchical government with reference to conditions in China. In it Goodnow argued that, in theory and in the abstract, monarchy was better suited to China’s traditions and level of political development, but he expressly declined to judge whether it would be expedient to change from republic to monarchy at that time. On August 16 the newly formed Peace Planning Society (Chou’an hui), whose leading figure was the former Qing constitutional thinker Yang Du, published a manifesto declaring that Goodnow had said monarchy was better than a republic and was indispensable for China at present. Goodnow, whose memorandum was printed in full in a Beijing newspaper on August 20, regarded this as a misrepresentation; American diplomatic reports record that he worked to have a clarification published on August 28 stating that he had said nothing beyond what the memorandum contained, and that the society continued to cite him in its circulars. Historians therefore distinguish three things: Yuan’s private request for expert advice, a qualified theoretical judgment, and the monarchists’ public use of a foreign scholar’s authority to support an immediate change that he had not endorsed. The episode shows how political actors used expert opinion as a source of legitimacy. Associates of Yuan, including his son Yuan Kedin, were reported to favor the plan, and historians still debate how far Yuan directed the campaign from the start.

The arguments for monarchy were practical as well as ideological. Supporters said that the Republic had produced instability, that a hereditary or at least lifelong head of state would reduce succession disputes, that foreign powers would respect a monarchy, and that the population was accustomed to an emperor. Others, including some foreign observers, regarded the monarchical project as an expression of China’s political culture. These claims were contested by republican critics, who noted that the actual cause of instability was the dissolution of institutions. The campaign was staged to appear as a response to popular demand: the government organized “citizens’ representative” assemblies in each province to vote on the question of the national polity (guoti), and results announced in November and December reported unanimous support for monarchy. Historians have found that these votes were managed by officials and that dissent was suppressed.

The Council of State, acting as a surrogate legislature, petitioned Yuan to accept the throne in December 1915. After declining initially in the customary manner, he accepted on December 12. On December 31 he announced that the following year would be the first year of the Hongxian (洪宪, “Grand Constitution”) era. He did not restore the Qing: the Qing house was treated courteously and its privileges under the abdication arrangements were continued, but Yuan intended to found his own dynasty. A formal enthronement ceremony was planned for early 1916 but never held. Preparations went forward, including the design of robes and ceremonies and the commissioning of porcelain with the new reign name, but he did not fully assume the title.

The monarchical project proved a miscalculation. Many of Yuan’s own generals, including Feng Guozhang and Duan Qirui, were unenthusiastic, since a hereditary monarchy would have ended their own prospects of succession. Li Yuanhong refused a princely title. Japan, Britain, and Russia advised delay in October 1915, and Japan later openly opposed the change. Republicans of all kinds saw the move as the betrayal of the revolution, and many former supporters, including Liang Qichao, publicly criticized it. The attempt thus alienated the groups on which his authority depended.

Historical Vocabulary
Hongxian (洪宪) and the National Protection Movement

Hongxian. The reign title chosen by Yuan Shikai for his intended monarchy, meaning roughly “Grand Constitution.” Reign titles had been a standard feature of imperial rule; using one was a claim to a new dynasty. The term was also used afterward to refer to the monarchical episode of 1915–1916.

National Protection Movement (护国运动, huguo yundong) and National Protection War. The movement of opposition to the monarchy, beginning with Yunnan’s declaration of December 25, 1915, and the armed campaigns that followed. “National protection” meant protecting the Republic, understood as the polity established in 1912, from restoration of monarchy.

National polity (国体, guoti). The basic form of the state, such as monarchy or republic, as distinct from the form of government. Debates in 1915 turned on whether the polity was open to change by a managed vote.

Section 58.16

The National Protection Movement and the Defeat of the Monarchical Project

Resistance centered on the southwest. Cai E, a Hunanese officer trained in Japan who had served as military governor of Yunnan in 1911 and was then brought to Beijing, and Liang Qichao, his former teacher, coordinated plans with Tang Jiyao, the military governor of Yunnan. Cai slipped out of Beijing in late November 1915, traveled by way of Japan and Vietnam, and reached Yunnan in December. On December 25, Yunnan declared independence, demanded the cancellation of the monarchy and the punishment of its promoters, and organized a National Protection Army, with Cai leading troops into Sichuan and Tang Jiyao governing Yunnan. Liang Qichao drafted manifestos and moved to Guangxi to encourage provincial leaders there.

Other provinces followed over several months. Guizhou declared in January 1916. Guangxi, under Lu Rongting, declared in March after wavering. Guangdong under Long Jiguang declared in April, Zhejiang in April, and Sichuan and Hunan in May. Not all of these declarations were identical: some provinces announced “independence” from Beijing, while others declared that they would cease obeying orders from the president. Many were motivated by local interests as well as by principle, including the wish to escape Beijing’s control, which Yuan’s appointments had been extending. Sun Yat-sen’s Revolutionary Party, still in exile, organized activities in Shandong and elsewhere, but the movement was led by former Progressives and provincial military leaders, not by the Guomindang.

Militarily, the campaign was limited. The National Protection Army was small and poorly supplied, and it faced Beiyang troops sent into Sichuan. The fighting was inconclusive: the monarchy’s military failure lay not in decisive defeats but in the unwillingness of important commanders to commit their forces and in the difficulty of paying for a prolonged war. Financially, the government was in difficulty, since foreign lenders were reluctant to provide funds in the crisis, and in May the Bank of China and the Bank of Communications suspended the redemption of their notes in silver. Politically, Feng Guozhang, who held command in the Yangtze region, sought a negotiated end, and Duan Qirui, who had withdrawn from office, was widely expected to be an alternative leader.

On March 22, 1916, Yuan canceled the monarchy and abolished the Hongxian title, and he asked to be treated again as president. The decision came after the defection of Guangxi and growing pressure from his own commanders and from foreign governments. It did not end the crisis. Southern provinces demanded his resignation, arguing that a president who had attempted to overturn the Republic had forfeited legitimacy and that Li Yuanhong should assume the office under the Provisional Constitution. Yuan refused to resign. The monarchical project had failed, and its failure had broken the loyalty networks that had sustained his government.

Section 58.17

Yuan Shikai’s Death and the Breakdown of Central Authority

Yuan Shikai died in Beijing on June 6, 1916, at the age of fifty-six, still holding the presidency. The cause was generally reported as uremia, aggravated by the stress of the preceding months. The following day Vice President Li Yuanhong took the presidency, in accordance with the Provisional Constitution that the southern provinces insisted remained in force. Duan Qirui continued as the dominant military figure and soon served as premier. On June 29, Li announced the restoration of the Provisional Constitution of 1912, and in August the National Assembly of 1913 was reconvened, with Guomindang members who had been expelled in 1913 returning to their seats.

These steps appeared to restore the Republic’s constitutional order, and many contemporaries were hopeful. They did not, however, resolve the problems that had undermined that order. The army was still organized around personal commanders, among whom Duan Qirui and Feng Guozhang were the most powerful, and the provinces remained under military governors who had gained or retained autonomy during the anti-monarchy campaign. The national treasury remained dependent on loans. Foreign powers, particularly Japan, were prepared to deal with whichever faction could offer advantage. The southern provinces that had opposed Yuan had interests and leaders of their own, and former allies were becoming rivals.

A first quarrel came almost at once. The relationship between the president and the premier, left ambiguous by the Provisional Constitution, was tested as soon as Li and Duan disagreed about policy, and the question of whether China should join the war against Germany in 1917 would turn this disagreement into a crisis. This chapter ends here, because the sustained fragmentation of authority in Beijing and the provinces, the military cliques, and the competing governments of the later 1910s and 1920s are the subject of Chapter 59, and the intellectual and cultural movements of the same years belong to Chapter 60.

Yuan’s legacy has been evaluated in different ways. Some historians emphasize his role in creating a modern army, in negotiating the end of the Qing without prolonged civil war, and in attempting to build a functioning administration. Others emphasize his destruction of parliament, his suppression of opponents, and the monarchical project. A common conclusion is that he was a skilled administrator and political manager whose methods depended on personal networks, and that these methods worked against the creation of lasting institutions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Qing Imperial Authority to Yuan’s Presidential Government

What Changed

  • The hereditary emperor was replaced by an elected, though increasingly lifelong, president
  • Written constitutions and legislatures entered national politics, even though they were short-lived
  • Political parties, newspapers, and public opinion became accepted channels of argument
  • Titles, calendars, and ceremonies were redefined in republican terms, and citizenship became a legal category

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence of central authority on personal networks and loyal commanders
  • Reliance on provincial officials for tax collection and local order
  • Foreign treaty privileges and foreign-administered customs and salt services
  • The use of ceremonial symbols of rule, including the Temple of Heaven sacrifice and the reign title Hongxian, to claim legitimacy
  • The overwhelming majority of the population living in rural households governed by lineage, village, and local gentry arrangements

Who experienced change and who continuity: Urban educated men, journalists, and officials experienced the largest changes in political life. Farmers and village communities experienced greater continuity in taxation and local authority, though war and military requisition touched some regions directly.

Why the pattern matters: The early Republic changed the rules of political legitimacy more quickly than it changed the underlying structures of fiscal and military power. That mismatch helps explain why constitutional forms were repeatedly adopted and overridden in the following decades.

Section 58.18

Chapter Review and Early Republic Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the relationships among revolutionary legitimacy, electoral politics, constitutional law, military power, state finance, and personal authority in the Republic’s first four years. Revolutionary legitimacy brought the Republic into being but did not settle who should govern. Electoral politics gave the Guomindang a plurality but not control of the army or treasury. The constitution set limits on the executive but lacked mechanisms of enforcement. Military power rested largely with the president and his network. State finance depended on foreign loans, and the terms of those loans affected political independence. Personal authority, built on loyalty and patronage, proved effective for short-term control but fragile when challenged.

Students should now be able to assess the main explanations for the constitutional breakdown. An institutional explanation stresses the ambiguity of the Provisional Constitution, the absence of a neutral enforcement authority, and the weakness of party organization. A leadership explanation stresses Yuan’s decisions, the Guomindang’s resort to arms, and the choices of provincial commanders. A provincial explanation stresses the autonomy of regional armies and revenue. A foreign-pressure explanation stresses loans, treaty privileges, and Japanese demands. Most historians combine several of these, and the task for students is to decide how they weighed against one another at particular moments. A useful exercise is to compare the expectations of 1912, which included a cabinet responsible to parliament, a unified national army, and recognized borders, with the situation in June 1916, when parliament had been dissolved and restored, armies were personal, and several frontier regions were outside Beijing’s control.

Checkpoint

Core insights of this chapter: The Republic was not simply doomed from the start; it was an experiment with real participation and real constraints. Yuan Shikai was both a necessary partner and a threat to constitutional rule. Parties and elections created new political possibilities but did not control armies or revenue. The assassination of Song Jiaoren is established as a fact, while responsibility for ordering it remains disputed. The Reorganization Loan linked finance to constitutional power. The Second Revolution showed the military imbalance between Yuan and his opponents. The Hongxian monarchy was an attempt to found a new dynasty, not a Qing restoration, and it provoked a regional rebellion that ended it. Yuan’s death left a restored republican framework without a stable distribution of power.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the first four years of the Republic of China, from its founding in 1912 to the death of Yuan Shikai in 1916. Its central argument is that China succeeded in ending the imperial dynasty but found it far harder to establish a functioning republican political order. The reasons lay in the gap between constitutional design and the real distribution of military, fiscal, and provincial power, and in the decisions of individuals who acted within, and sometimes against, those constraints.

The Nanjing Provisional Government under Sun Yat-sen proclaimed the Republic, issued reforms, and approved the Provisional Constitution, but controlled little territory and few resources. The agreement that transferred the presidency to Yuan Shikai reflected the revolutionaries’ need for his army and the dynasty’s need for a negotiated end. Yuan took office in Beijing in March 1912, and the capital remained in the north. The constitution that bound him established popular sovereignty, legal equality, civil rights, and a cabinet responsible to the legislature, but it left key questions unresolved.

Real power rested on the Beiyang Army and on provincial military governors with their own forces and revenue. Constitutional authority and coercive power did not coincide, and the president’s command of the first gave him an advantage over a legislature whose authority rested on law and election. The regions of the Republic, from the Yangtze valley to the southwest and the frontier territories of Mongolia and Tibet, experienced the new government in different ways, and Beijing’s claims were negotiated, not simply enforced.

The founding years also saw real political innovation. Parties, newspapers, and associations multiplied; an electorate of tens of millions of men voted in indirect elections; and the Guomindang, organized by Song Jiaoren, won a plurality in both houses. Women demanded suffrage and were refused. The elections showed political participation and its limits, since a plurality did not produce control of the executive. Song’s assassination in March 1913, which remains a matter of disputed responsibility, removed the party’s leading parliamentary strategist and deepened mistrust.

Finance linked these political conflicts to the larger world. The Reorganization Loan of April 1913 gave the executive money without parliamentary consent and increased foreign involvement in the salt administration. When Yuan dismissed Guomindang governors in June, the Second Revolution broke out and was defeated by Beiyang forces. The president was elected formally in October, the Guomindang was banned in November, and parliament was dissolved in January 1914. The Constitutional Compact of May 1914 and later regulations gave Yuan a lifelong, unreviewed presidency in all but name.

Foreign pressure intensified in these years. Japan’s occupation of German holdings in Shandong and the Twenty-One Demands of 1915 showed the Republic’s diplomatic weakness while generating a wave of anti-Japanese protest. In this atmosphere Yuan’s supporters organized a campaign for monarchy. He accepted the throne in December 1915 and adopted the reign title Hongxian, but he met resistance from republicans, moderates, provincial leaders, and many of his own generals. Yunnan’s declaration of December 25 began the National Protection War, and Yuan canceled the monarchy on March 22, 1916.

Yuan died on June 6, 1916, and Li Yuanhong restored the Provisional Constitution and the 1913 parliament. These steps revived republican forms without resolving the conflicts that had undermined them: armies remained personal, provinces held autonomy, finances depended on loans, and foreign powers sought advantage. The period after Yuan’s death, treated in Chapter 59, would be marked by a prolonged contest among military and political groups.

The chapter’s significance for the larger history of Chinese civilization is that it shows how a major change in political form occurred without a matching change in the underlying structures of authority. It also illustrates the importance of avoiding both determinism and heroic or villainous explanations. Students should come away understanding the early Republic as a contingent, contested, and creative period whose failures were consequential for the decades that followed, and whose institutions, debates, and political practices became part of the inheritance of later movements.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how difficult it is to build new political institutions after a revolution. The end of the Qing removed a ruling house, but the Republic had to create a working system of authority from competing armies, provincial interests, political parties, and foreign pressures. The early failures of constitutional government were not simply a verdict on republican ideas; they reflected the conditions in which those ideas were first tried.

The events of 1912–1916 shaped later Chinese history in several ways. They left a legacy of distrust between parties and armies, a pattern of appeals to public opinion by telegram and newspaper, a continuing debate about how to combine unity with provincial autonomy, and a heightened concern about foreign encroachment that fed the protest movements of 1919. The dissolution of the first parliament and the experience of personal rule shaped the thinking of the next generation, including those who would turn to cultural critique in Chapter 60 and to party organization in Chapter 61.

The chapter also connects China to wider history. The Reorganization Loan and the Twenty-One Demands show the Republic operating within a global system of finance and imperial rivalry, and World War I changed the balance of power in East Asia. At the same time, Chinese actors, from negotiators to boycotting merchants, were participants in these events, not merely objects of them.

Finally, the chapter helps students see Chinese history as contested, creative, and unfinished. The early Republic was a period of institution-building, debate, and experimentation, and it was also a period of violence and repression. Its outcomes were not inevitable, and understanding them requires attention to structure, choice, region, and evidence. Chapter 59 follows the consequences into the era of military fragmentation, while Chapters 60 and 61 turn to the new ideas and organizations that sought to respond to the Republic’s unfinished problems.
