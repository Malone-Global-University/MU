Founding the People's Republic of China

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Winning a civil war and governing a country are different undertakings. Between 1949 and 1953 the Chinese Communist Party had to turn an army, a revolutionary organization, and a set of base-area experiments into a government for roughly 540 million people, most of them farmers, many of them living in places the new state had barely reached.

On the afternoon of October 1, 1949, a crowd gathered in the square in front of Tiananmen, the Gate of Heavenly Peace, in a city that had been renamed Beijing only days before. Mao Zedong, standing on the gate tower, announced that the Central People's Government of the People's Republic of China had been established. A new red flag with five yellow stars was raised, and the assembled troops and civilians who watched were present at what the Party presented as the end of a century of humiliation and civil war. For many of them it was also a moment of real relief. Wars had been fought across Chinese territory almost without pause since the Japanese invasion of 1937, and inflation had ruined the savings of millions of families.

This chapter studies what happened next. Its central question is how the Communist Party transformed military victory into an enduring system of government between 1949 and 1953, and how its early reforms, coercive campaigns, territorial expansion, and international conflicts reshaped Chinese society. The answer involves several processes unfolding at once: the construction of party and state institutions; the extension of control to Xinjiang, Tibet, Hainan, and the southwest; a land reform that redistributed roughly 43–44 percent of China's cultivated land according to official figures; a Marriage Law that challenged centuries-old family arrangements; campaigns that killed, imprisoned, or frightened very large numbers of people; the stabilization of a ruined currency; and an unexpected war in Korea that drew hundreds of thousands of Chinese soldiers across the Yalu River.

What changed in these years was extraordinary in scope. A state that had for a century been unable to collect taxes uniformly, control its currency, or protect its own borders acquired the capacity to do all three, at least in much of the country. Landlord power in most villages was destroyed, often violently. A governing party with perhaps four and a half million members built a network of cadres, peasant associations, trade unions, women's federations, and neighborhood organizations reaching down toward individual households. Foreign privileges that had defined the treaty-port era were ended. Yet much also continued: the dependence of most households on farming, the importance of local kin networks, the authority of regional military figures, and the use of campaigns of mass mobilization that the Party had practiced in its base areas.

The people affected were nearly everyone, but unequally. Poor peasants received land, tools, and sometimes political standing they had never held. Landlords, many local notables, former Nationalist officials, members of secret societies, and some religious leaders were denounced, dispossessed, imprisoned, or executed. Women gained new legal rights and sometimes paid heavily for trying to use them. Urban entrepreneurs were pressured and then disciplined. Workers gained job protections and insurance in some enterprises, but also new forms of supervision. Tibetans, Uyghurs, Mongols, and other peoples on the frontiers experienced the arrival of the People's Liberation Army and the new state very differently from villagers in the Yangtze valley, and their experiences are the subject of continuing scholarly and political debate.

Historians understand these years through an unusually varied body of evidence. Official laws and directives survive in published form, and so do speeches by Party leaders and, increasingly, internal Party documents. Foreign archives, particularly Russian and Eastern European records opened after 1991, have transformed the study of the Sino-Soviet alliance and the Korean War. Local archives in China have been partly opened and partly closed again; village studies, memoirs, interviews, and sociological surveys supply views from below. Statistics from the period, especially about executions, grain output, and casualties, are valuable but must be read as products of the system that generated them.

This chapter connects directly to the chapters on either side of it. Chapter 65 explained how the Communists won the civil war and how the Republic of China government withdrew to Taiwan. This chapter begins where that account ends and concentrates on governing rather than fighting. Chapter 67 will examine the Soviet-influenced First Five-Year Plan, collectivization, and the nationalization of industry beginning in 1953. The years covered here were different from both: a period of mixed economy and consolidation under the provisional framework of the 1949 Common Program, before the 1954 constitution and before the socialist transformation. Students should resist reading these years only as a prelude to later events, including the disasters of the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution.

The chapter also places China in wider history. The PRC was founded at the beginning of the Cold War, in a region undergoing decolonization, civil war, and partition. Its alliance with the Soviet Union, its confrontation with the United States in Korea, its recognition by some governments and exclusion by others, and its claims over Tibet, Taiwan, and the frontier regions all took shape within that setting. The early People's Republic was thus at once a national project of reconstruction, a revolutionary party's experiment in social engineering, and a participant in an emerging global conflict.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Common Program, the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference, and the Central People's Government created a provisional framework of government in 1949.
  2. Distinguish between Communist Party leadership and state institutions, and describe how cadres, mass organizations, and retained personnel connected them.
  3. Trace the extension of PLA and administrative control into the southwest, Xinjiang, Tibet, and Hainan, and identify where control remained incomplete.
  4. Analyze the 1951 Seventeen-Point Agreement and the competing Chinese and Tibetan interpretations of how it was reached.
  5. Describe the provisions, implementation, and consequences of the 1950 Land Reform Law, including regional variation and violence.
  6. Evaluate the Marriage Law of 1950 by distinguishing legal text from everyday implementation.
  7. Compare the Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries, the Three-Antis, and the Five-Antis in targets, methods, and effects.
  8. Assess how currency stabilization and public finance made economic recovery possible, and why recovery differs from socialist transformation.
  9. Interpret the Sino-Soviet Treaty of 1950 and the strategic reasoning that shaped China's decision to enter the Korean War.
  10. Evaluate the reliability of official statistics on campaigns, production, and casualties.
  11. Connect the three mechanisms of early state-building (formal institutions, mass campaigns, and military force) and explain how they reinforced one another.
  12. Contextualize the early PRC within the Cold War and avoid reading later Maoist history backward onto 1949–1953.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a revolutionary party with a base in the countryside acquire the institutions needed to govern cities, ports, and frontier regions?
  2. What was "New Democracy," and how did it shape the early government's policies toward capitalists, intellectuals, and non-Communist political groups?
  3. Why did the government pursue both inclusive institutions (a coalition conference, consultative bodies) and coercive campaigns at the same time?
  4. How did people living in Tibet, Xinjiang, and other frontier regions experience the arrival of the new state, and how do historians weigh sources from different sides?
  5. Why did land reform produce both genuine redistribution and large-scale violence, and how should historians explain the relationship?
  6. How far did legal change, such as the Marriage Law, translate into change in family life?
  7. What did the government mean by "corruption" and "bourgeois" behavior, and who was targeted by the Three-Antis and Five-Antis?
  8. What were the fiscal and political reasons that inflation ended in 1950, and what did recovery by 1952 consist of?
  9. Why did the PRC intervene in Korea, and how did the war affect politics and society inside China?
  10. In what ways was the early PRC different from the later Maoist state, and why does that difference matter?

Historical Context

The People's Republic emerged from the exhaustion of nearly four decades of political fragmentation and conflict. The Qing dynasty had fallen in 1912 (Chapter 57); the early Republic had given way to warlord politics (Chapters 58–59); the Nationalist government that came to power in Nanjing in 1928 had made real but incomplete progress in state-building (Chapter 62); the Japanese invasion of 1937 devastated eastern China and forced the Nationalists to retreat to Chongqing (Chapter 64); and the civil war of 1946–1949 ended with Nationalist collapse on the mainland (Chapter 65). By 1949 the economy was badly damaged. Railways had been cut, factories dismantled or bombed, and the Nationalist currency, the fabi and its successors, had lost nearly all value through hyperinflation. Rivers and dikes had gone unmaintained during the war, leaving areas such as the Huai River basin vulnerable to flooding. Approximately 80 percent of the population could not read.

The Communist Party that now claimed national power had spent two decades building its organization in rural base areas, especially in Shaanxi, Shanxi, Hebei, Shandong, and the northeast. It had developed methods for mobilizing peasants through land reform, for building armies and logistical networks, and for disciplining members through rectification campaigns, most notably the Yan'an Rectification of 1942–1944. Its leaders had far less experience governing large cities, managing industry and finance, or administering regions with large non-Han populations. In the spring of 1949 Mao told the Party that it was moving from the countryside to the cities and that it must learn to manage them. The Party also had to absorb enormous numbers of new people: demobilizing Nationalist soldiers, former officials and technicians, urban workers, students, and ordinary villagers with little revolutionary background.

The geography of control mattered. The Party's strongest bases were in North and Northeast China, where land reform and administration were most advanced. The south and southwest had been conquered by rapid PLA advances in late 1949, leaving large numbers of Nationalist remnants, local armies, secret societies, and bandits. Xinjiang had been governed through a combination of Nationalist garrisons and an Ili-based movement allied with the Soviet Union. Tibet had been effectively self-governing since the fall of the Qing in 1912. Taiwan and several coastal islands remained under the Republic of China. In 1949 the territory under Beijing's administration was therefore not the same as the territory that the new government claimed.

The political ideas underlying the early government drew on Mao's 1940 essay "On New Democracy," his June 1949 essay "On the People's Democratic Dictatorship," and the Party's experience with united-front coalitions. In these formulations, China would first pass through a stage of "New Democracy" under the leadership of the working class and the Communist Party, in which several classes cooperated against imperialism, "feudalism," and what the Party called bureaucratic capital, before the later transition to socialism. This theory explained why the early PRC included non-Communist parties, protected some private business, and moved cautiously against rich peasants. It also left the Party firmly in command and defined "the people" in a way that excluded those classified as enemies. Both the inclusive and the exclusionary sides of this definition shaped the policies this chapter examines.

Finally, the international setting shaped everything. In 1949 the United States had recently ended its mediation efforts in China and published a White Paper blaming Nationalist failures for the collapse; the Soviet Union had just tested an atomic bomb; Europe was divided; and the Korean peninsula, divided in 1945, was moving toward war. Mao announced in mid-1949 that China would "lean to one side," toward the Soviet Union. The new government's fears of American hostility, its need for Soviet assistance, and its ambition to restore Chinese sovereignty all influenced decisions on the frontier, in diplomacy, and in the domestic campaigns against "imperialist" influences.

As in earlier chapters, students should remember that "China" as a political claim and "China" as a governed territory did not coincide neatly in these years. The PRC claimed much of the territory of the former Qing empire but recognized the independence of Outer Mongolia in October 1949, following the October 1945 plebiscite and the Nationalist government's recognition in January 1946. It did not control Taiwan. It had not yet entered Tibet. Its authority in Xinjiang, Yunnan, and Guangxi was fresh and uneven. The chapter will return repeatedly to this gap between claim and reach.

Division I — Revolution Becomes Government, 1949–1950

Division purpose: to explain how the Communist Party developed the institutions needed to govern a large and diverse country. The PRC did not begin with the fully developed structure of later decades. Its initial system operated under the 1949 Common Program, which preceded the constitution of 1954.

Section 66.1

The Founding of the People's Republic of China

The proclamation of October 1, 1949, was the culmination of a political process that had begun months earlier. Beiping, the former Nationalist-era name for the old capital, was taken peacefully by the PLA in January 1949 and renamed Beijing ("northern capital") in late September, when the CPPCC decided to restore it as the national capital. The delegates also selected a national flag, a provisional anthem (the "March of the Volunteers," written for a 1935 film and associated with resistance to Japan), and adopted the Gregorian calendar for official use. Mao's speech opening the CPPCC on September 21 declared that the Chinese people had "stood up," a phrase that became a lasting symbol of the revolution. On October 1 the new Central People's Government Council held its first meeting, and a ceremony at Tiananmen, attended by a very large crowd (figures around 300,000 are commonly reported), announced the new state to the nation and the world.

The proclamation was not the end of the war, and it was not a moment of complete national control. PLA columns were still advancing toward Guangzhou, Chongqing, and the southwest; Xinjiang was only just coming under Communist authority; Tibet and Taiwan lay outside the new state's reach. Nor did it settle the question of legitimacy. The government's claims rested on several grounds: military victory; the Party's account of itself as the vanguard of workers and peasants; its wartime record of resistance to Japan; the coalition character of the CPPCC; and the promise to end foreign domination and economic ruin. Many ordinary people, exhausted by inflation and conflict, were willing to give the new authorities a chance. Others, including supporters of the Nationalists, wealthy landholders, and members of groups already targeted by the Party, had good reason to fear it.

Historians therefore treat the founding as both a symbolic threshold and a beginning of a longer process. It determined the name, flag, capital, and formal structure of the state, but real governing capacity had to be built through the personnel, organizations, and campaigns described in the rest of this chapter.

Section 66.2

The Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference and the Common Program

The Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference met in Beijing from September 21 to 30, 1949. It was not an elected body. Its roughly 660 delegates were chosen through consultation led by the Communist Party and represented Party and non-Party groups: the CCP, the smaller "democratic parties" (such as the China Democratic League and the Revolutionary Committee of the Kuomintang, made up of Nationalists who had broken with Chiang Kai-shek), regional delegations, the PLA, workers', women's, and youth organizations, representatives of ethnic minorities, overseas Chinese, religious figures, and prominent independents such as Song Qingling, the widow of Sun Yat-sen. The conference stood in for a national legislature until a National People's Congress could be convened, which did not happen until 1954.

It adopted three foundational documents: an Organic Law of the CPPCC, an Organic Law of the Central People's Government, and the Common Program, a roughly sixty-article statement of principles and policies that served as a provisional constitution. The Common Program described the PRC as a New Democratic or "people's democratic" state led by the working class and based on an alliance of workers and peasants, uniting "all democratic classes and nationalities." It promised land reform, protection of private property and of "national capitalist" enterprise, equality for women, regional autonomy for minority nationalities, and freedoms of speech, assembly, and religion for citizens. It also pledged to suppress counterrevolutionary activity and to abolish the privileges of foreign powers. It was thus both inclusive in its coalition language and exclusionary in defining those who could be suppressed.

Primary Source Window

The Common Program of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference (September 1949)

Creator: The CPPCC, drafted under Communist Party leadership with input from other delegations · Date: adopted September 29, 1949 · Type: provisional constitutional and programmatic document

Context: The Common Program was drafted to unite the broad coalition assembled in Beijing and to function as the basic law of the new state until a formal constitution could be written. It remained the governing framework until the 1954 constitution replaced it.

Paraphrase: The opening article describes the PRC as a state of New Democracy, or people's democracy, led by the working class, based on the worker-peasant alliance, and uniting democratic classes and nationalities; it commits the state to oppose imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucratic capitalism. Later provisions guarantee citizens' freedoms of thought, speech, assembly, and religion, promise equal rights for women and regional autonomy for minorities, and instruct the government to suppress counterrevolutionary activity.

Analysis: The document reveals how the Party framed its rule in 1949: not as socialism but as a coalition stage dominated by the working class and the Party, with protections for some private economic activity. It also shows how the same text could justify both political inclusion and repression, because the categories of "the people" and "counterrevolutionaries" were defined by those holding power. Its limits as a source are clear: it states principles and intentions, not practice. It tells us little about how the freedoms it listed were exercised in reality, how minorities were consulted, or how those outside the coalition perceived it.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the type of state the PRC claimed to be in 1949?
  2. Who drafted it, and what might the purposes of a coalition document have been?
  3. What can historians learn from the Common Program about the Party's early priorities?
  4. What can they not learn from it alone about how it was applied?
Section 66.3

Communist Party Leadership and New Democracy

The new state was led by a Party whose senior circle had worked together for two decades. Mao Zedong held the chairmanship of the Party, the Central People's Government, and the People's Revolutionary Military Commission. Liu Shaoqi, the leading figure in Party organization, Zhu De, the PLA's senior commander, and Zhou Enlai, the premier, were among the most influential colleagues, while Chen Yun directed economic planning and finance and Deng Xiaoping, Peng Dehuai, and others commanded field armies or regional bodies. Party committees at every level directed government departments, the army, and mass organizations. Decision-making was guided by democratic centralism, which in theory combined discussion with strict obedience to higher organs and in practice concentrated authority in the leadership.

New Democracy gave the early policies their character. Because the Party's theory held that China was an economically backward country emerging from "semi-feudal, semi-colonial" conditions, it planned to stay for a time with a mixed economy: state ownership of "bureaucratic capital" (the property of the Nationalist state and of those associated with it, which was confiscated), cooperatives, and private enterprise operating under regulation. Rich peasants were to keep their land, and "national bourgeois" entrepreneurs would be allowed, even encouraged, to operate. In practice the line between tolerance and pressure shifted rapidly, as Sections 66.19–66.20 show. Leaders disagreed about how long New Democracy should last. Liu Shaoqi emphasized a lengthy transition in 1949–1951, while Mao and others were more willing to hasten changes as circumstances evolved. The debate matters because it warns against assuming a fixed blueprint existed from the outset.

Historical Vocabulary
New Democracy (xin minzhu zhuyi)

New Democracy was Mao Zedong's term for a transitional political and economic order in a country that was not yet socialist. It combined leadership by the Communist Party in the name of the working class with a coalition of several classes, including peasants, urban petty bourgeoisie, and "national capitalists," united against imperialism and what the Party called feudalism and bureaucratic capitalism. Between 1949 and about 1952 it justified a mixed economy, protection of rich peasants, and non-Communist participation in government.

The term is a reminder that the early PRC defined itself as not yet socialist. After 1952–1953 the Party moved toward socialist transformation (Chapter 67), and "New Democracy" was increasingly treated as an early phase that had ended. The concept's shifting use shows that historians should avoid assuming that Party leaders had one fixed timetable in 1949.

Historical Vocabulary
Democratic Centralism (minzhu jizhong zhi)

Democratic centralism was the Leninist principle of organization adopted by the CCP. Party bodies were to be elected from below and subject to discussion, but decisions once made were binding on subordinates, individuals obeyed organizations, minorities obeyed majorities, and lower levels obeyed higher ones. The Common Program applied the term to the state as well: People's Congresses and governments at each level were to practice it.

In practice, the principle left considerable room for central leaders, especially Mao, to determine policy and for Party committees to override state organs. Democratic centralism therefore explains why Party leadership and state administration were so closely intertwined after 1949, even though they were separate institutions on paper.

Section 66.4

Central People's Government and the New State Institutions

Under the Organic Law, the Central People's Government Council, chaired by Mao with six vice-chairmen (including Zhu De, Liu Shaoqi, Song Qingling, and the non-Communists Li Jishen and Zhang Lan, plus Gao Gang), served as the highest organ of state power until the National People's Congress was called. The council appointed the Government Administration Council, essentially a cabinet headed by Premier Zhou Enlai, which supervised ministries, commissions, and the People's Bank. The People's Revolutionary Military Commission directed the armed forces. A Supreme People's Court and a Supreme People's Procuratorate were established, with Shen Junru, a respected legal figure and a member of the Democratic League, serving as president of the court. The Financial and Economic Commission, led by Chen Yun, coordinated economic policy.

Although the structure resembled a conventional government, its operation depended on the Party. Party members, including Zhou Enlai and most ministers, occupied the senior positions, and the Party's Central Committee and its departments issued directives that state organs implemented. Non-Communists held visible posts in ministries and commissions and brought professional expertise, particularly in finance, transport, engineering, education, and the law. Their presence reassured urban elites and foreign observers but did not alter where decisive power lay. Scholars often describe the arrangement as a "party-state": a system in which a ruling party's organization and the state's administration are so closely linked that policy is made in the former and executed in the latter.

The institutions were also young. Many ministries were created from nothing in late 1949 and early 1950 and staffed with a mixture of Party cadres, retained officials, and technical specialists from the Nationalist era. The legal system was provisional: the Party abolished the Nationalist legal codes in 1949 and replaced them with Party policy, new regulations, and decrees. The 1954 constitution would later give the state a more formal shape, but in 1949–1953 governance relied on directives, campaigns, and administrative regulations rather than on comprehensive codes.

Section 66.5

Regional Administration, Cadres, and Building State Authority

To govern a country of continental size, the Party divided the mainland into six large administrative regions: the Northeast, North, East, Central-South, Northwest, and Southwest. In newly conquered areas, military control committees run by PLA commanders and Party officials took over cities, banks, utilities, schools, and newspapers; they were gradually replaced by civilian people's governments. The regional governments, many of them dominated by the commanders of the field armies that had conquered them, held substantial authority over finance, personnel, and policy. Gao Gang in the Northeast and Rao Shushi in the East were among the powerful regional figures, and tensions between region and center would later contribute to a political purge in 1954 and to the abolition of the large regions. In the early years, however, they allowed the new government to function across great distances with limited communications.

The key human instrument of state building was the cadre (ganbu). Party members, veterans, and recruits from northern base areas were sent south as "southbound cadres" to run the new administrations, join work teams, and organize unions and peasant associations. They were joined by demobilized PLA soldiers, student recruits, and a very large number of "retained personnel": former Nationalist officials, police, teachers, and technicians who were kept in place because the Party lacked the people to replace them. This created a complicated relationship. Northern cadres often lacked local knowledge, language skills, and technical expertise; retained personnel had expertise but were distrusted and sometimes targeted in later campaigns. Tensions between "old" Party members from the base areas and recently recruited ones also shaped local politics.

Below the regional and provincial levels, the new government worked at the county and township levels, where it created peasant associations, militia units, women's associations, youth leagues, and, in cities, trade unions and neighborhood bodies. These mass organizations functioned as links between the state and the population and as channels for mobilization. Everywhere, local conditions mattered. In places where the Party had operated for years, administration was relatively cohesive; elsewhere, officials depended on existing village headmen, lineage leaders, and secret societies, or faced resistance from them. The reach of the new state was therefore broad but uneven, a point that will recur in discussions of land reform, public health, and frontier policy.

Division II — Territorial Consolidation and the Limits of State Power, 1949–1953

Division purpose: to show that establishing a government in Beijing did not mean immediate or uniform control of every territory the PRC claimed.

Section 66.6

The People's Liberation Army and the Consolidation of the Mainland

After October 1949 the PLA, which had perhaps five million men under arms, continued its southern and western campaigns. Guangzhou fell in mid-October, with the Nationalist government retreating to Chongqing and then to Chengdu before relocating to Taiwan in December. Chongqing was taken at the end of November, and Chengdu in December. In Yunnan, the provincial governor Lu Han transferred allegiance to the new government in December 1949. Many Nationalist commanders, particularly in the southwest, surrendered or switched sides with their troops, which accelerated the conquest but also added large numbers of former Nationalist soldiers to the new army and administration. Other Nationalist units withdrew across the border into Burma, where they created a long-lasting complication in Sino-Burmese and regional politics.

Conquest was not the same as control. In the southwest, south China, and parts of the center, former soldiers, local militias, secret societies, and bandit groups remained active. Between 1950 and 1952 the PLA and local security forces conducted "bandit suppression" campaigns, sometimes involving large operations in mountainous areas of Guangxi, Guizhou, Sichuan, and Yunnan. These operations targeted armed resistance but also blurred into the broader campaign against counterrevolutionaries (Section 66.17), since officials often labeled local opponents as bandits. Rural resistance sometimes arose from grievances over grain requisitions or the conduct of work teams rather than from loyalty to the former regime. The army also served as administrators and builders, organizing demobilized soldiers into production and construction work, which later informed the formation of frontier military-agricultural units.

Section 66.7

Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and the Northwestern Frontier

Xinjiang was a region of Uyghurs, Kazakhs, Hui, Han, Mongols, and others, with a long history of competing influences from Beijing, Russia, and the Soviet Union. During the 1940s the Nationalist administration controlled the main cities, while a movement of Uyghur, Kazakh, and other leaders in the Ili, Tacheng, and Altay districts, the "Three Districts Revolution," maintained a separate regime, the East Turkestan Republic, with Soviet backing. In 1949 several leaders of the Ili movement died in an air crash while traveling to Beijing for the CPPCC, an event that has been interpreted in different ways but which removed prominent figures and helped open the way for integration. In September 1949 the Nationalist garrison commander Tao Zhiyue and the provincial chairman Burhan Shahidi announced their allegiance to the new government, and the PLA entered Xinjiang in October. The Ili forces were incorporated into the PLA, and the regional government was reorganized. Kazakh resistance under leaders such as Osman Batur continued for some time; he was captured and executed in 1951. The Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region was formally created only in 1955.

Inner Mongolia followed a different course. Its autonomous government had been established in 1947, under the leadership of the Communist Mongol official Ulanhu, before the PRC existed, giving it the distinction of being the first autonomous region under Communist authority. It was treated as a model for the Party's nationality policy, though its territory was extended in the 1950s and its Mongol population was by then a minority within it, reflecting decades of Han settlement. In the northwest more broadly, military campaigns in 1949 defeated the Hui Ma family warlords in Gansu, Ningxia, and Qinghai, bringing Muslim-majority regions under new administration. In all these places the new government worked through a combination of military presence, alliances with local leaders, and Party-controlled institutions, while avoiding at first the full class-struggle policies applied in Han areas.

Section 66.8

Tibet, Chamdo, and the Seventeen-Point Agreement

Tibet in 1949 was governed by the Ganden Phodrang, the government headed by the Dalai Lama (then a boy of fourteen, with authority exercised by a regent until November 1950), with its Kashag, or council of ministers, and powerful monasteries and aristocratic estates. Tibet had expelled Nationalist officials in 1949 and had no formal recognition from major powers as an independent state, though it exercised practical self-government and conducted its own diplomacy at times. The PRC's position was that Tibet was an integral part of China to be "liberated" and that its leaders would be asked to cooperate. The Tibetan government, worried by developments in China, sought help from India, the United Kingdom, the United States, and the United Nations, with little result.

In October 1950 PLA forces crossed the upper Yangtze (Jinsha River) and defeated Tibetan units at Chamdo in the east; the governor, Ngabo Ngawang Jigme, was captured. The Dalai Lama was invested with full powers in November and withdrew to the border town of Dromo. A Tibetan delegation, led by Ngabo, which had been sent to negotiate, reached Beijing in 1951 and, after negotiations in which the Tibetan side had little leverage, signed an agreement on May 23, 1951. The PLA entered Lhasa in September, and the Dalai Lama endorsed the agreement by telegram in October.

Section 66.9

Hainan, Coastal Islands, and the Taiwan Strait

The PLA's inexperience in amphibious warfare became evident in late 1949, when a landing on the island of Jinmen (Quemoy) off Fujian was repulsed at Guningtou with heavy losses. Over the following months commanders gathered boats, trained troops, and learned from this defeat. In April 1950 PLA forces crossed the Qiongzhou Strait to Hainan with the help of local Communist guerrillas, and the Nationalist garrison evacuated by early May. The Nationalists also withdrew from the Zhoushan islands near Shanghai. The earlier assault on Dengbu Island in the Zhoushan group had also failed in November 1949; Nationalist forces subsequently evacuated the Zhoushan islands in 1950. The Nationalist government, relocated to Taipei, held on to Taiwan, Penghu, Jinmen, Mazu, and several smaller islands.

The PLA had been preparing an invasion of Taiwan in 1950, but several developments intervened. Taiwan's defenses and the lack of sufficient shipping posed practical problems; the Soviet Union offered limited help; and after June 1950 the Korean War changed the picture. On June 27, 1950, President Truman ordered the U.S. Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait, reversing the earlier American position, stated in January 1950, that the United States would not become involved in Taiwan's defense. The PRC condemned the fleet's deployment as aggression, and Mao's government moved forces north to the Korean border. The result was a lasting division: the PRC governed the mainland and Hainan; the Republic of China, with American protection after 1950, governed Taiwan and some offshore islands. The two governments both claimed to be the legitimate government of all China, a claim that structured the diplomatic confrontation for decades. In Taiwan, Nationalist authorities carried out their own reforms, including rent reduction in 1949 and later land redistribution, while maintaining martial law (see Chapter 65 and Appendix J).

Section 66.10

Nationalities Policy, Regional Autonomy, and Frontier Governance

The Communist Party's nationality policy drew on Soviet precedent and on its own experience in Yan'an and Inner Mongolia. In theory, the Common Program promised equality among nationalities, the right to use and develop their languages and customs, and regional autonomy within a unified state. The Party rejected the Nationalist-era notion of a single "Chinese nation" comprising all peoples as branches of one lineage, but also rejected the right of secession, which had been mentioned in its earlier documents. In practice, policy meant identifying and classifying peoples, a process that expanded in the early 1950s into an ethnic identification project that eventually recognized dozens of official nationalities, and creating autonomous areas of various levels. It also meant training minority cadres, notably at the Central Institute for Nationalities, founded in Beijing in 1951, and sending delegations to frontier regions to build relations with leaders.

The government's approach to frontier societies was often gradual. Local religious figures, aristocrats, and tribal chiefs were courted, and land reform was postponed in many minority areas, while the PLA and Han cadres provided security and administration. But this accommodation had limits. The state did not tolerate independent armed forces or alternative political authority; some local leaders resisted, and others were removed or displaced. Frontier governance also reflected economic and strategic interests, from grain supply and roads to border security. The experiences of individual communities varied enormously: Mongols in Inner Mongolia, Zhuang and Yao in Guangxi, the Yi in Sichuan and Yunnan, Hui Muslims in the northwest, Uyghurs and Kazakhs in Xinjiang, and Tibetans in several different political situations.

Section 66.11

The Agrarian Revolution and the Land Reform Law of 1950

Land reform had been central to the Communist movement since the Jiangxi Soviet period. By 1949 it had been carried out, with varying intensity, in the northern and northeastern base areas that held roughly 100 million people. The remaining "new areas" of central, southern, eastern, and western China, containing the great majority of the rural population, had not yet experienced it. The Party's wartime land policies had at times been radical, including the 1947 Outline Land Law, which treated all landholdings equally for redistribution and led to excesses that the leadership later criticized. In 1950 the Party sought a more measured approach suited to the New Democratic stage: it would confiscate landlord holdings but protect rich peasant land and "national capitalist" enterprises, in order to preserve production and keep a broad coalition.

The Land Reform Law, promulgated on June 30, 1950 (adopted June 28), declared the aim of abolishing the landlord class's feudal exploitative land ownership and establishing peasant land ownership to free the productive forces. It authorized the confiscation of landlords' land, draft animals, tools, surplus grain, and surplus housing, and the requisition of land held by ancestral halls, temples, and religious institutions, for distribution to landless and land-poor peasants. Rich peasants' land worked by themselves or with hired labor was to be left alone, and middle peasants' property was protected. Land would become the private property of the new owners, who could rent, sell, or inherit it, a feature that distinguished 1950 from the collectivization that followed (Chapter 67).

Its provision that landlords receive a share of land signaled a goal of converting them into laboring peasants, though in practice many were stripped of far more than the law specified. Section 66.12

Implementing Land Reform in the Countryside

Land reform in the new areas was carried out in phases between late 1950 and 1952–1953, starting in East and Central-South China and moving to the Northwest and Southwest. Preparatory stages included campaigns for rent and interest reduction and the suppression of "local despots" (eba) and bandits, which helped break the power of local elites before redistribution began. The central instrument was the work team: groups of cadres, often with university students and soldiers, who went to villages, lived with poor peasants, identified "activists," and organized peasant associations. The work teams, who frequently came from outside, depended on activists to give them local knowledge and legitimacy.

The core of the process was class classification. Each household was placed in a category based on its ownership of land and the extent to which it lived by exploiting others' labor: landlord, rich peasant, middle peasant, poor peasant, hired laborer, and several others. Classification was discussed in village meetings, and assigned labels could be contested; mistakes occurred, and categories sometimes depended on local politics and personal grudges. "Speak bitterness" meetings invited poor peasants to describe their sufferings, linking personal grievances to class analysis. After classification, property was confiscated and distributed through committees. Peasants received land, tools, animals, and sometimes household goods and houses. Procedures differed by region: densely populated areas of the south and southwest, where there was often little to divide, differed from northern areas with larger landholdings; in some regions lineage land, held collectively by kin groups, was a particularly important target.

Historical Vocabulary
Class Status (jieji chengfen)

Class status was an official label assigned to each rural (and later urban) household, such as landlord, rich peasant, middle peasant, or poor peasant. It was based on the household's relationship to land and labor around the time of reform and was inherited by children through the male line. The labels were not simply economic descriptions; they determined who benefited from redistribution, who was denounced, and, in later decades, who faced discrimination in education and employment. The system persisted until the end of the 1970s, which shows how land reform's classifications shaped Chinese society long after the redistribution itself.

Section 66.13

Landlords, Class Struggle, and Rural Violence

Land reform involved public denunciation of landlords and others identified as oppressors, at meetings often called "struggle sessions." Targets could be beaten, humiliated, tortured, imprisoned, or executed, and their families were expelled from homes and left destitute. Some local leaders were put on trial before people's tribunals created under regulations issued in mid-1950. The Party leadership at times urged restraint and criticized excesses, and at other times set or accepted quotas for those to be punished. Executions often followed public meetings in which villagers were encouraged to join in denunciation, binding them to the process and, scholars argue, making it harder to reverse. Some landlords committed suicide.

The scale of the violence is uncertain. Official sources give no comprehensive figures; scholars have proposed estimates that range from several hundred thousand to over two million deaths, depending on the sources and assumptions they use. Local variation was great: in some villages there were few or no executions, while in others, especially where conflicts between lineages, grievances over rents, or earlier violence had occurred, they were extensive. Part of the difficulty is that deaths from land reform, the campaign against counterrevolutionaries, and bandit suppression overlapped in time and place and were recorded under different headings. Students should regard numbers in this chapter as estimates and ask what each source's author could have known.

Historical Debate

Land Reform, Class Conflict, and Violence

The question: Was land reform primarily a response to existing rural inequality and conflict, which unleashed popular anger, or a deliberately organized political process in which violence was a method of state-building?

Interpretation A — A popular social revolution with excesses: Influenced by early observers such as William Hinton, whose book Fanshen documented land reform in a Shanxi village during the 1940s, this view emphasizes genuine peasant grievances against landlords and moneylenders, the transformation of village power, and the real material gains for poor households. Violence is seen as arising from long-standing conflicts, the dynamics of mass meetings, and local excesses that the Party at times tried to restrain.
Interpretation B — A planned political process in which terror was a tool: Drawing on archival research, historians including Julia Strauss (Morality, Coercion and State Building by Campaign, 2006) and Jeffrey A. Javed (Righteous Revolutionaries, 2022) emphasize political campaigns and the deliberate mobilization of class-based violence as means of establishing state authority. These accounts also recognize local variation and villagers' own motives rather than depicting every encounter as centrally scripted. They point to quotas, the central role of work teams, and the assignment of class labels from above as evidence of design rather than spontaneity.

Evidence: Supporters of A rely on village studies, interviews with beneficiaries, and accounts of changes in land distribution. Supporters of B rely on Party directives, internal reports, local archival material, and survivors' testimony. Both groups use overlapping evidence and differ in what they emphasize.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how to understand the relationship between social change and state power, and on whether later campaigns were extensions of a pattern established in these years.

Student task: Which interpretation is better supported by the evidence described here? What kinds of local sources would help decide between them, and what are the limitations of survivors' testimony and official reports?

Section 66.14

Land Reform's Economic and Political Consequences

According to official figures, which scholars treat as approximate, about 700 million mu (roughly 47 million hectares) of land, approximately 43–44 percent of cultivated land, was redistributed to some 300 million peasants. Before reform, landlords and rich peasants, perhaps 10 percent of rural households, were said to hold some 70 percent of the land, while poor peasants and hired laborers, more than half of the rural population, held much less than a fifth; the real picture varied greatly by region, and in parts of the south, land was scattered among many small owners with landlord holdings smaller than the national image suggests. The reform narrowed inequality in landholding, and in most places also removed the landlord class as a social force. In its place came a countryside of small owner-cultivators with new political leaders drawn from poor-peasant activists.

Economic effects were mixed but generally positive in the short term. The elimination of rent and the redistribution of land raised incomes for many poor families, and grain output recovered, reaching about 160 million tons by 1952 by official reckoning, compared with roughly 113 million in 1949. Recovery also reflected the end of war, the restoration of water-control works, and favorable weather. The government introduced a unified agricultural tax in 1950, which tied the state's revenues to the countryside. Yet the new small farms were still small, many households lacked tools or draft animals, and borrowing, seasonal shortages, and the possibility of land sales raised concerns among Party leaders about renewed inequality. These worries became a rationale for the mutual-aid teams and cooperatives of the 1950s (Chapter 67). Politically, the reform created a rural administrative base for the Party and a population of beneficiaries with a stake in the regime, but also families and communities marked for generations by hostile class labels.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household Undergoing Rural Land Reform

The following composite scenario is an illustration built from patterns in village studies and memoirs, not an account of one specific family. A tenant household in a rice-growing village in the Yangtze valley, with four adults and three children, rents two mu from a landlord and pays about half the crop in rent. In winter 1950–51 a work team arrives and calls a meeting at which neighbors describe hardships. The household is classified as poor peasant. After weeks of meetings, it receives several mu of confiscated land, a share of a buffalo, and some tools. The landlord's family loses its house and is assigned a plot, and a younger son of the landlord who studied in the city is later sent for "reform" training.

Women in the household attend meetings, sometimes for the first time, and the mother is invited to join a women's association. The poor-peasant father becomes an activist and is appointed to the village committee. But neighbors who are middle peasants worry that they will be reclassified, and the household's gain in land is real but modest. Over the next years the household's fortunes depend on rainfall, available tools, the new tax rate, and whether it can work with neighbors in mutual-aid arrangements. Households of landlords and their descendants, by contrast, carried a hereditary label that shaped their opportunities, and some did not survive the reform.

The scenario illustrates differences among households and between women and men, and underlines that outcomes depended on local circumstances.

Section 66.15

The Marriage Law of 1950 and the Reform of Family Relations

The Marriage Law, promulgated on April 30 and in force from May 1, 1950, was one of the new government's first major laws. Leaders such as Deng Yingchao, active in the women's movement, helped shape it, drawing on the Communist base areas' earlier marriage regulations and on the reform arguments of the New Culture and May Fourth movements (Chapter 60). The law declared the abolition of the "feudal marriage system" based on arranged and coerced marriage, male superiority, and disregard for the interests of women and children. It established free choice of partners, monogamy, equal rights for both sexes, and the protection of women's and children's lawful interests. It prohibited bigamy, concubinage, child betrothal, interference with widows' remarriage, and the exchange of money or gifts in connection with marriage. The minimum marriage ages were 20 for men and 18 for women, and divorce was available when both parties wished it, or by mediation and court judgment when only one did, with protection for pregnant women and for infants.

Section 66.16

Women, Households, and the Social Consequences of Reform

The All-China Democratic Women's Federation, established in 1949 under leaders such as Cai Chang, was the Party's main channel to women, mobilizing them for literacy, hygiene, production, and campaign work as well as for marriage reform. In the early 1950s divorce petitions rose sharply, and women, sometimes with the help of local women's cadres, sought to leave forced or abusive marriages. At the same time, local officials, many of them men who shared community attitudes, were sometimes unsympathetic, and families resisted. Reports from the period record violence against women who sought divorce, including suicides and killings, which the government itself treated as a serious problem. In 1953 it launched a campaign to publicize and enforce the law, partly because of such complaints and partly because the Party wanted to show that the reform was being implemented.

The Marriage Law also interacted with land reform. Women could now be allotted land in their own right, in principle, though village politics and household practice often meant that land was registered under male heads. Women's work in agriculture and in new local industries expanded, and the Party praised "women's labor heroes." Urban women found new opportunities in factories and offices, but also continued to bear most domestic work. The government's priorities sometimes limited its women's policy: when a divorce threatened family stability, or a local official feared disruption, enforcement was weak. The reforms therefore changed legal rights and gave many women new leverage, but did not end patriarchal practice or reshape the gendered division of labor.

Division IV — Political Campaigns, Coercion, and the New Political Order

Division purpose: to explain the campaigns through which the CCP established political control, disciplined officials, reorganized society, and sought to redefine acceptable political behavior. Section 66.17

The Campaign to Suppress Counterrevolutionaries

The campaign began in 1950 and peaked in 1951. Directives in March and October 1950 called for harsh action against "counterrevolutionaries," a category that included former Nationalist officials and military personnel, secret agents, members of secret societies and religious sects such as the Yiguandao, "local despots," bandits, and others accused of resisting the new order. The campaign gathered force with the Korean War, when leaders saw domestic enemies as a potential fifth column. In February 1951 the government promulgated Regulations on the Punishment of Counterrevolutionaries, which defined a range of offenses broadly and prescribed severe penalties, including death. Mass meetings were held to denounce and sentence individuals, and in many places executions were carried out publicly.

The scale was large. Leaders later gave figures suggesting several hundred thousand executions and over a million imprisoned in the early 1950s, and scholars using internal documents have proposed numbers in the same range or higher. The Party leadership set quotas in some regions and issued periodic instructions both to expand arrests and to correct excesses, such as indiscriminate killing. A system of suspended death sentences with two years of reprieve was introduced. The campaign extended the prison and labor-reform system, which would play a growing role in the economy and in political control. It also affected religious communities: Christian churches were pressed to cut ties with foreign missionaries through the Three-Self movement, and secret religious sects were targeted as subversive.

Historical Evidence

Campaign Statistics and Their Reliability

Figures for arrests, executions, and imprisonment in 1950–1953 come from several types of sources: public statements by leaders (including later remarks by Mao and by security officials), internal Party and police reports, provincial and county archives, and estimates by scholars based on these. Each has weaknesses. Leaders' numbers were sometimes given in speeches without explanation of categories. Local reports could be inflated to meet quotas or reduced to avoid criticism, and the line between executions in campaigns, in land reform, and in bandit suppression was often unclear. Archival access is partial and has fluctuated. Estimates therefore vary and should be cited as ranges, with attention to who produced them and why.

Students should ask of any figure: What was counted? Over what period and region? Who compiled it, and for whom? Can it be checked against independent evidence, such as local gazetteers, memoirs, or records of prisons and courts?

Section 66.18

Public Security, Courts, and Political Control

The Ministry of Public Security, headed from 1949 by Luo Ruiqing, built a national police and security network, with local public security bureaus, household checks, and informant systems extending into cities and villages. People's tribunals, created in 1950 to handle land reform cases, and local people's courts handled many political cases, often with little procedure and under the supervision of Party committees. Mass meetings served as venues for accusation and sentencing. The government also developed administrative punishments, including surveillance ("control") and detention, that did not require court trials. New regulations on household registration in cities from 1951 aided the monitoring of population movement.

The relationship between law and campaigns was ambivalent. The Party had abolished the Nationalist legal codes and trained a new cadre of judges, many with little legal education, but it also issued statutes and regulations that defined offenses and procedures. In practice, Party policy, quotas, and campaign priorities often determined outcomes, and the line between law and policy was blurred. Some legal professionals, including those who had joined the government from the Nationalist period, tried to maintain procedural standards and were criticized for doing so. The pattern, in which law functioned as an instrument of policy while also providing some predictability, shaped Chinese legal institutions for decades.

Section 66.19

The Three-Antis Campaign

The Three-Antis campaign, launched at the end of 1951, was aimed at corruption, waste, and bureaucratism among Party members and state personnel. It originated in concerns that the war effort in Korea, the expansion of state organs, and the arrival of former Nationalist employees and urban temptations had produced misuse of funds and lax discipline. Gao Gang's administration in the Northeast pioneered the campaign, and the Central Committee extended it nationwide in December 1951. Officials were required to write self-criticisms, report on colleagues, and undergo investigation in meetings. Some cases were publicized for deterrent effect; two senior Hebei Party officials with long revolutionary records, Liu Qingshan and Zhang Zishan, were tried and executed in early 1952, a signal that revolutionary credentials would not protect corrupt officials.

The campaign also served to tighten control over the administrative apparatus. It increased the Party's supervision of ministries and enterprises, strengthened auditing and accounting practices, and heightened suspicion of retained personnel. It also had a political logic: many of the corrupt practices were associated with private business, and the campaign's logic led directly to the Five-Antis movement directed at the private sector.

Section 66.20

The Five-Antis Campaign and the Private Business Community

Launched in early 1952, the Five-Antis campaign targeted bribery of officials, tax evasion, theft of state property, cheating on government contracts, and theft of economic information. It was directed at the urban private business community in Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangzhou, and other cities. Work teams of cadres, joined by union members, shop employees, and students, investigated firms, demanded confessions, and held mass meetings. Employees were encouraged to report on employers, which upended workplace relationships. Firms were classified into categories ranging from law-abiding to seriously or completely lawless, and many owners were fined, forced to repay taxes, or lost control of enterprises. Pressure was intense: reports of suicides among business owners were numerous, and fear of denunciation affected the entire commercial world.

The effects were far-reaching. Many private firms suffered financial damage, and the state's role in procurement, credit, and distribution increased. Prominent capitalists such as Rong Yiren in Shanghai cooperated with the authorities and kept leading roles in the new order. The campaign did not eliminate private business, which was still permitted in the mixed economy, but it demonstrated that the Party could discipline capital and set the stage for later nationalization. It also shows a consistent pattern in the early PRC: tolerance of some groups for functional reasons, followed by pressure when the Party judged their autonomy a threat.

Cause and Effect

The Three-Antis and Five-Antis Campaigns

Causes

Long-term: a Party with limited administrative experience absorbing large numbers of new officials; a mixed economy in which officials and private business interacted. Short-term: the fiscal strain of the Korean War, reports of embezzlement, and Mao's concern about bourgeois influence on cadres.

The campaigns

The Three-Antis (late 1951) disciplined officials; the Five-Antis (early 1952) investigated businesses, using work teams, employees, and public meetings.

Effects

Greater Party control of the state apparatus and of private business; fines and revenue for the state; fear and suicides among owners; new leverage over firms; and a foundation for later socialist transformation.

Historical complexity

Corruption existed and was a real problem, but the campaigns were also political tools. They cannot be explained by a single motive: fiscal needs, anti-bourgeois ideology, administrative discipline, and war mobilization all contributed.

Section 66.21

Thought Reform, Intellectuals, and Political Culture

In late 1951 the Party launched a campaign of "thought reform" among intellectuals, beginning with university teachers. Professors were asked to study Marxism-Leninism, examine their pasts, write self-criticisms, and denounce "bourgeois" and "pro-American" thinking in group sessions. The aim was to align intellectuals with the new order, and the process often combined genuine participation, social pressure, and fear. Earlier, in 1951, Mao had directed a campaign criticizing a film, The Life of Wu Xun, as an example of wrong ideas about reform and history, an early example of using criticism of a cultural work to define political boundaries.

The government also reorganized institutions. In 1952 it restructured universities along Soviet lines, merging or dissolving departments and shifting emphasis toward engineering and applied fields; Christian and private universities such as Yenching and St. John's were closed or absorbed. Foreign missionaries were expelled, and Chinese churches were pressed to reject foreign control. Newspapers, publishers, and film studios came under state or Party supervision. At the same time, many intellectuals welcomed the end of war and the chance to take part in national reconstruction, and the government recruited scientists and engineers, including some returning from abroad. The boundary between cooperation and coercion was not fixed, and individuals experienced it differently. These developments set patterns for the later Hundred Flowers and Anti-Rightist campaigns (Chapter 67).

Division V — Urban Administration, Economic Recovery, and Everyday Life

Division purpose: to explain how the new government sought to restore daily economic and social life after decades of war. Economic recovery (1949–1952) should be distinguished from socialist transformation under the First Five-Year Plan, which belongs primarily to Chapter 67.

Section 66.22

Economic Crisis and the Stabilization of Currency and Public Finance

The government inherited an economy in which prices had risen at astronomical rates, public finances were chaotic, and many people held goods or foreign currency rather than money. The Communist-issued renminbi, introduced in December 1948 by the People's Bank, circulated in the liberated areas but faced competition from silver dollars and speculation. In the second half of 1949, as the PLA's conquests extended the currency's reach, prices surged several times, particularly in the autumn and in early winter, and speculators in Shanghai and other cities hoarded grain, cotton, and other goods. The authorities responded by seizing control of the stock exchange and of speculative trading and by making use of state-owned commercial networks.

The decisive measures came in spring 1950. A March 1950 decision on unifying financial and economic work, associated with Chen Yun and his colleagues, centralized revenue, expenditure, and cash management under the central government, concentrated the holding of funds in state banks, and gave the state tighter control over the grain and cotton trades. The government used state trading companies to release goods onto the market and push down prices, issued bonds whose value was tied to the prices of basic commodities, and raised taxes, including the agricultural tax. The measures produced rapid stabilization: inflation fell sharply in spring 1950, and by 1951–1952 the budget approached balance, despite the rising costs of the Korean War. The achievement, which contemporaries such as foreign observers noted, rested on administrative reach, political power over merchants, and the postwar restoration of supply, but it also depended on tight central control that pressed on private traders and burdened some taxpayers. Its success gave the new government a measure of credibility with urban residents that political declarations alone could not.

Section 66.23

Reconstructing Industry, Transportation, and Commerce

Recovery meant repairing infrastructure first. Railways, which had been damaged in the war, were largely restored by 1950, and new lines were built, including the Chengdu–Chongqing railway, opened in 1952, which was celebrated as a project of the new government and Sichuan's labor force. Mao ordered work on flood control, and the Huai River project began in 1950 after severe floods, mobilizing millions of laborers. Industrial plants in the Northeast, much of whose equipment had been removed or damaged during Soviet occupation in 1945–1946 and the civil war, were rehabilitated with Soviet help. State enterprises, created from confiscated Nationalist and Japanese-era assets ("bureaucratic capital"), formed the commanding heights of industry, while private firms, still numerous, especially in textiles, light industry, and commerce, worked under contracts and regulation. The mixed economy of New Democracy thus combined state, cooperative, and private sectors.

Trade and commerce revived as roads and rail reopened and as the government encouraged exchanges between city and countryside. State commercial companies and supply and marketing cooperatives became important, and the government used contracts for processing and ordering to guide private firms. By 1952, official figures showed industrial and agricultural output at or above the highest prewar levels, though at low per capita levels. The government regarded this as a successful recovery and set the stage for planned industrialization. Because the figures are mostly official, students should treat them as indicative, not exact.

Historical Evidence

Economic Recovery Through 1952

Evidence for the recovery comes from central statistical reports, which were compiled later in the 1950s, from ministerial and bank records, from provincial and municipal archives, and from foreign observers' reports. The State Statistical Bureau, founded in 1952, developed national statistics, but the early series were incomplete and rely on reports from local units that had strong incentives to show improvement. Scholars have adjusted figures and compared them with prewar estimates, often finding the general direction (rapid recovery) reliable but specific numbers uncertain. The baseline year, 1949, was a year of extreme disruption, which makes recovery percentages look larger than they would from a less depressed base. Students should ask what the figures measure (gross output, per capita output, or particular goods), whose data they derive from, and whether they distinguish prices from real quantities.

Section 66.24

Urban Government, Labor, and Public Welfare

The Party's approach to cities relied on new municipal governments, trade unions, and neighborhood organizations. Unions, brought together in the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, mobilized workers for production, political study, and campaigns; a Trade Union Law was issued in June 1950, and Labor Insurance Regulations in February 1951 provided medical care, pensions, and other benefits for workers in larger enterprises. Unemployment was a pressing problem, and the government provided relief, public works, and job placement. Housing and urban services were limited, and many cities were overcrowded with refugees. Neighborhood residents' committees were developed experimentally in cities such as Hangzhou and elsewhere and were given national regulations in 1954; in the early years they helped with sanitation, security, and political mobilization. The authorities also acted against social practices they considered exploitative: Beijing closed its brothels in a single operation in November 1949, and prostitution and drug sales were attacked in other cities, with women placed in rehabilitation and job-training programs. An anti-opium campaign launched in 1950 reduced drug use sharply in some regions, though it also involved harsh punishment of dealers.

Workers' experiences varied. Skilled workers in state enterprises gained security and status, while casual laborers, rickshaw pullers, and the unemployed were less well protected. Women entered some industries in larger numbers. Private-sector workers sometimes benefited from union pressure on employers and sometimes were caught up in the Five-Antis. Political study sessions, production campaigns, and new forms of supervision became part of working life.

Everyday Life and Social History

The following composite illustration draws on patterns reported in studies of cities such as Shanghai, Tianjin, and Wuhan; it is not about a single named family. Her husband, a small shop assistant, is encouraged during the Five-Antis to report on his employer's tax practices, which leaves him uneasy about both his own safety and the shop's future.

In wealthier households, owners of private shops and factories face higher taxes, investigation, and uncertainty. Section 66.25

Education, Literacy, and the Reorganization of Schools

With roughly four-fifths of the population unable to read, expanding education was both a social goal and a political one. The government encouraged mass literacy campaigns, with winter schools for peasants and evening classes for workers, and later introduced shortcut methods such as the "rapid literacy" approach of 1952. Primary school enrollments rose substantially by official figures, roughly doubling between 1949 and 1952, though quality varied and many rural schools lacked trained teachers and buildings. Curricula were revised to emphasize political education, patriotic history, and, as relations with the Soviet Union deepened, Russian-language study and Soviet textbooks. Teachers, many of whom had worked under earlier governments, were required to take part in political study and thought reform.

Higher education was restructured in 1952: universities were reorganized, departments merged or moved, and engineering and technical institutes expanded to serve planned industrial needs. Peking University moved to the former Yenching campus, and private and missionary institutions were absorbed into the state system. The reorganization produced trained technicians but also weakened some humanities and social science disciplines. Access to education expanded for children of workers and peasants, who received preferences in admissions, while children from landlord and "bourgeois" backgrounds increasingly faced obstacles. Educational inequality persisted between cities and countryside and between regions.

Section 66.26

Public Health, Epidemics, and Social Welfare

In 1949 China had very few trained doctors for its population, high infant and maternal mortality, and widespread infectious diseases including smallpox, plague, cholera, tuberculosis, and schistosomiasis. The government's first national health conference in 1950 set the guiding principles: service to workers, peasants, and soldiers; emphasis on prevention; and cooperation between Western-trained and traditional Chinese medical practitioners. Vaccination campaigns against smallpox reached very large numbers of people, sanitation drives targeted flies, rats, and water sources, and newly trained midwives promoted hygienic childbirth. In the Northeast, quarantine and vaccination helped contain plague outbreaks. Mass mobilization through the Patriotic Health Campaign, launched in 1952, linked hygiene to political loyalty, and was accompanied by accusations, made by China and North Korea and denied by the United States, that the United States had used biological weapons in Korea. Historians continue to debate the evidence for those accusations, and students should treat them as claims requiring examination of available archives and testimony.

The reach of these services was uneven. Cities and the Northeast had more clinics and hospitals; villages in the southwest or the frontier relied on a handful of newly trained health workers, traditional practitioners, and local remedies. Relief for famine victims and refugees was organized by government and Party bodies, and flood relief expanded. Health indicators improved over the following decade, and estimates suggest that life expectancy rose significantly, though the data are imperfect. The early record shows that the state could mobilize on health issues, an ability that would be applied again in later periods, with varying results.

Division VI — Foreign Relations, the Korean War, and the Cold War

Division purpose: to explain why the new People's Republic became involved in a major international war within a year of its founding, and how that decision changed its internal and external priorities.

Section 66.27

The People's Republic and the International Order

The Soviet Union recognized the PRC on October 2, 1949, followed by the Eastern European states and North Korea. Burma and India were among the first non-Communist countries to extend recognition in December 1949, and the United Kingdom did so in January 1950, though it kept its relations with Beijing at the level of chargé d'affaires because of its stance on the United Nations seat and Taiwan. The United States refused recognition, having published its White Paper in August 1949 on the failure of its policy, and continued to treat the Republic of China as the government of China. Beijing also recognized Ho Chi Minh's Democratic Republic of Vietnam in January 1950 and began providing aid and advisers, linking China's security to developments in Indochina. The new government acted against foreign privileges: it refused to honor the old treaty arrangements, seized or took over foreign property in the course of 1950–1951, and ended the extraterritoriality and concession systems that had operated since the nineteenth century.

The question of China's seat at the United Nations was disputed. The Republic of China retained its seat, which the Soviet Union protested by boycotting the Security Council in early 1950, an absence that allowed the June 1950 resolutions on Korea to pass. The PRC would not take the seat until 1971. Throughout the early 1950s the PRC was therefore diplomatically isolated from much of the world, and its main international partners were the Soviet bloc and a small group of non-Communist governments. Chinese leaders regarded the Cold War as a struggle that required close alignment with Moscow, and they interpreted American support for Taiwan and the later Korean War as confirmation of hostile intent.

Section 66.28

Mao, Stalin, and the Sino-Soviet Alliance

Mao traveled to Moscow in December 1949 on his first journey abroad, staying until February 1950, ostensibly to attend Stalin's seventieth birthday celebrations. The visit lasted weeks, and negotiations were hard. Stalin was cautious about revising the 1945 treaty with the Nationalist government, which had given the Soviet Union special rights in Manchuria, and about committing to China's security. The talks, joined by Zhou Enlai, produced the Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance, signed on February 14, 1950, together with agreements on a Soviet loan of US$300 million over five years and on the return of the Chinese Changchun Railway and the naval base at Lüshun (Port Arthur) by 1952, though Soviet use of Port Arthur was extended in practice. Some arrangements, including secret protocols concerning Manchuria and Xinjiang, caused later resentment.

Section 66.29

The Korean War and China's Decision to Intervene

Korea had been divided at the 38th parallel since 1945, with a Soviet-backed government under Kim Il Sung in the north and a U.S.-backed government in the south. In 1949–1950 Kim sought Stalin's and Mao's approval for an invasion of the South. Archives opened since the 1990s indicate that Stalin gave his consent in early 1950 and that Mao, informed in the spring, agreed to support it, believing it would be quick. North Korean forces attacked on June 25, 1950. The United Nations Security Council, with the Soviet Union absent, called for a response, and a U.S.-led force intervened. After the Inchon landing in September, UN forces pushed the North Korean army back and, in early October, crossed the 38th parallel, moving north toward the Yalu River, the border with China.

Chinese leaders faced a difficult choice. On October 3, Zhou Enlai warned through the Indian ambassador that China would intervene if U.S. forces crossed the parallel, a warning that was not taken seriously in Washington. Within the Party, there were debates in early October about whether the country, which was still recovering and had not completed the conquest of Tibet or the preparation to take Taiwan, could afford a war with the United States. Mao favored intervention, and after delays and negotiations with Stalin about Soviet air support, which was limited, he ordered the formation of the Chinese People's Volunteers under Peng Dehuai. The first troops crossed the Yalu on October 19, and the first engagements took place in late October. The decision was announced to the Chinese public in terms of "Resist America, Aid Korea, Protect the Homeland and Defend the Country."

Section 66.30

Chinese Forces in Korea: Campaigns, Casualties, and Stalemate

Chinese forces entered Korea unannounced and moved mainly at night through mountainous terrain to avoid American air power. In the first campaign, in late October and November 1950, they struck South Korean and U.S. units and withdrew, and in the second campaign, beginning in late November, they attacked in far larger numbers, forcing UN forces into a costly retreat from the north, including from the Chosin Reservoir, where Chinese units suffered severe losses from cold and frostbite. Pyongyang was retaken in December, and Seoul fell in early January 1951. Thereafter, the front moved south and then north again. Chinese offensives in spring 1951 faced stronger UN firepower and lengthening supply lines. By mid-1951 the war had settled into positional warfare near the 38th parallel, with high casualties for limited gains on both sides.

Chinese forces had large numbers of infantry but little air power, limited heavy artillery and transport, and a fragile logistics network that relied on porters and trucks vulnerable to air attack. Soviet pilots flew MiG-15 aircraft from bases in northeast China to contest air control near the Yalu, and Soviet equipment improved Chinese capabilities over time, but supply problems persisted. Losses were heavy. Chinese official commemorative records identify 197,653 fallen People's Volunteers, while other counts of the dead and estimates of total casualties use differing categories and methods; Mao's son Mao Anying was among those killed. Peng Dehuai and Mao sometimes disagreed over strategy, and Peng argued for caution after the spring 1951 offensives. Armistice talks began in July 1951 at Kaesong and moved to Panmunjom, while the fighting continued. The question of whether prisoners of war could choose not to return home prolonged negotiations, and many Chinese prisoners eventually went to Taiwan rather than the mainland.

Section 66.31

The Korean War Armistice and Its Consequences for China

The Korean Armistice Agreement was signed at Panmunjom on July 27, 1953, by senior delegates of the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers on one side and the UN Command on the other; their commanders subsequently countersigned it. South Korea's government did not sign. The armistice suspended hostilities and created a demilitarized zone, but no comprehensive peace treaty followed. The change in Soviet leadership after Stalin's death in March 1953 and the shift in American policy contributed to the agreement. Both sides claimed success.

For China the consequences were substantial. Domestically, the war enabled the leadership to mobilize society through the "Resist America, Aid Korea" campaign, which linked patriotism to support for the state, encouraged donations, and was used to intensify the campaign against counterrevolutionaries and to restrict foreign influence, including religious ties. Military expenditure absorbed a large share of the budget in 1951–1952, constraining civilian reconstruction though not ending it. The war enhanced the PLA's prestige and its modernization, led to a larger military-industrial effort with Soviet help, and gave China a place among the major participants in the Cold War. The United States imposed an embargo and froze Chinese assets, the PRC retaliated against American holdings, and the Taiwan Strait became a more heavily militarized front. China's relations with North Korea, the Soviet Union, and the United States all took new forms, and its exclusion from the United Nations persisted. The war also deepened Chinese dependence on the Soviet Union in the short run, while contributing to the leadership's confidence in its capacity for self-reliance.

Historical Debate

Why Did China Intervene in Korea?

The question: Was China's decision primarily a defensive response to a perceived security threat, or did it also reflect revolutionary ideology, alliance obligations, and domestic political goals?

Interpretation A — A security-driven decision: Scholars such as Allen Whiting emphasized Chinese warnings and the threat that a hostile army on the Yalu posed to the Northeast, China's main industrial region, along with the presence of the Seventh Fleet at Taiwan. On this view, intervention was a reaction to American actions and a reluctant choice made after debate.
Interpretation B — A decision shaped by ideology, alliance, and domestic goals: Others, including Chen Jian, stress Mao's revolutionary outlook, the importance of the Soviet and North Korean relationships, and the usefulness of war for mobilizing society. Work based on Russian archives, such as that of Shen Zhihua, highlights Stalin's role and the negotiations over Soviet support, showing that alliance politics were central.

Evidence: Evidence includes Chinese leaders' telegrams and speeches, Politburo records, Soviet archives, American intelligence and policy documents, and memoirs. Each source set is partial, and access to Chinese archives is limited.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians understand early PRC foreign policy and the extent to which security and ideology interacted in decision-making.

Division VII — Final Review

Section 66.32

Chapter Review and Early People's Republic Checkpoint

The synthesizing question is: how did the Communist Party consolidate its authority after 1949, and what were the achievements, costs, limitations, and long-term implications of its early policies? The chapter has described three mechanisms of state-building. Formal institutions, the CPPCC, the Central People's Government, ministries, regions, courts, and banks, gave the state structure and legitimacy. Mass political campaigns, land reform, the suppression of counterrevolutionaries, the Three-Antis and Five-Antis, thought reform, and the Resist America campaign, extended Party influence into villages, workplaces, and homes, and also produced violence, fear, and widespread participation. Military force, the PLA's conquest of the mainland and frontiers and the war in Korea, extended physical control and shaped the state's priorities.

These mechanisms reinforced one another. The army enabled land reform by breaking local power; land reform supplied rural support and revenue; campaigns disciplined the cadres and private sector on whom recovery depended; and the war justified mobilization. They also produced different experiences: a poor peasant who received land, a landlord who lost everything, a woman who left a coerced marriage, a business owner under investigation, a Tibetan official negotiating under military pressure, and a Chinese soldier crossing the Yalu all lived the same founding years in very different ways.

Regional and Frontier Context

The experience of the early PRC varied widely by region. The Northeast, with its heavy industry and long Party presence, was the most developed center of state capacity, and its leaders had early influence. North China's villages had already experienced land reform; in East China and Central-South China the reform and campaigns were new and intense, and cities such as Shanghai and Wuhan were testing grounds for urban policy. The southwest, with its mountainous terrain, secret societies, and minority populations, experienced bandit suppression, land reform in Han areas, and administrative integration. The northwest combined Han farming areas with Hui, Uyghur, Kazakh, and Mongol communities. Tibet, until 1959, retained much of its pre-1950 system under the 1951 agreement, while neighboring Kham and Amdo experienced direct Chinese administration. Taiwan, under the Republic of China, followed a separate course with its own land reform and political system. Frontier regions were thus places of negotiation, coercion, migration, and cultural exchange, and their histories cannot be understood only from the perspective of Beijing.

China and the Wider World

The early PRC was shaped by and helped shape the Cold War in Asia. It joined with the Soviet Union and North Korea in the Korean War, aided the Vietnamese Communists, and confronted the United States over Taiwan, while also relating to newly independent states such as India and Burma. Chinese leaders were not passive objects of great-power politics: they negotiated hard with Stalin, decided to intervene in Korea after debate, and used international conflict for domestic purposes. At the same time, the world beyond China, from Soviet loans and advisers to American embargoes, constrained and shaped choices. The experience of the war would influence Chinese views of nuclear weapons, relations with the Soviet Union, and the role of the military for decades, topics taken up in Chapters 67 and 69.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Chinese Communist Party, after proclaiming the People's Republic on October 1, 1949, built a state and began to reshape Chinese society. Its central argument is that consolidation depended on three mechanisms, formal institutions, mass campaigns, and military force, which reinforced one another and produced strikingly different experiences across regions and social groups.

The institutional foundations were laid through the CPPCC, the Common Program, and the Central People's Government. These bodies gave the new state a coalition appearance and a provisional constitutional framework, but real power lay with the Party, which directed state organs through committees, cadres, and mass organizations. The large administrative regions allowed governance across great distances, and the reliance on retained personnel reflected the Party's shortage of administrators.

Territorial consolidation was incomplete in 1949. The PLA took the south and southwest within months, but bandit suppression and resistance continued. Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and the northwest were integrated through a mix of alliance and military presence; the Chamdo campaign and the Seventeen-Point Agreement established a framework for expanding PRC authority in central Tibet on terms whose negotiation and legitimacy remain contested; Hainan was taken, but Taiwan and several islands remained under the Republic of China, protected after June 1950 by the U.S. Seventh Fleet. Nationalities policy promised autonomy but within a unitary state, with differences of implementation across the frontier.

Land reform and the Marriage Law were the principal instruments of social transformation. The 1950 Land Reform Law redistributed a large share of cultivated land, broke the power of landlords, and created a new rural leadership, but also involved class labeling, public denunciation, and violence of uncertain scale. The Marriage Law challenged arranged marriage, concubinage, and women's legal inferiority, and gave many women leverage, but its implementation met resistance and sometimes led to violence. Both reforms show that legal change and social change are related but distinct.

The campaigns of 1950–1952 extended political control. The campaign against counterrevolutionaries combined mass participation with large-scale executions and imprisonment; the Three-Antis disciplined officials; the Five-Antis subordinated the private business community; and thought reform pressed intellectuals to align with the Party. Their numbers are uncertain and their motives complex, including real concerns about corruption and security as well as political consolidation.

The economic record was one of recovery. Currency stabilization in 1950, centralized finance, the restoration of transport, and the revival of agriculture brought output to prewar levels by 1952, within a mixed economy consistent with New Democracy. Urban government, labor insurance, literacy campaigns, and public health drives extended state services, though unevenly. These developments should be distinguished from the socialist transformation that began in 1953.

International affairs were decisive. The alliance with the Soviet Union, sealed by the treaty of February 1950, provided aid and a model but also dependencies. China's intervention in the Korean War, decided in October 1950 after debate, produced heavy casualties, strengthened the PLA, promoted domestic mobilization, and contributed to the diplomatic isolation of the PRC from the United States and its allies. The armistice of July 27, 1953, ended the fighting without a peace treaty.

Regional variation runs throughout the story. The Northeast, the villages of North China, the cities of the east coast, the mountains of the southwest, and the plateaus and deserts of the frontier each experienced the early PRC differently, and frontier peoples had their own understandings of the changes that occurred. Students should resist describing the period as uniform.

The chapter has also emphasized historical evidence and its limits. Laws and directives reveal intentions; statistics on executions, output, and casualties are partial and politically shaped; village studies and memoirs offer perspectives from below but are limited in coverage. Historical debates on land reform and on the decision to enter Korea show how historians use evidence to construct competing interpretations.

Finally, the chapter has argued that the early PRC was a distinct phase, neither a simple continuation of the civil war nor a miniature version of the later Mao era. By 1953 the state had achieved considerable capacity and legitimacy in many areas, together with extensive repression. It faced unresolved questions about sovereignty, political participation, social equality, and economic development that Chapter 67 and later chapters examine.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a revolutionary victory became a system of government. The institutions, relationships, and practices established between 1949 and 1953, including the Party's direction of the state, the use of mass campaigns, the class-label system, the regional and frontier arrangements, and the alliance structure, shaped Chinese politics and society for decades. At the same time, the early years show roads not taken: a mixed economy, a coalition-style government, and cautious policies toward some groups were features of New Democracy that did not last.

The chapter also reveals recurring themes of the textbook: the tension between state power and local society, the diversity of regional and ethnic experience, the interplay of reform and coercion, and the importance of evidence and interpretation. Land reform and the Marriage Law show how the state tried to change the most intimate structures of rural life, with results that differed by region, gender, and class. The frontier chapters show that sovereignty and identity were negotiated, contested, and remembered differently by different communities.

China's early years as a People's Republic were also part of wider Asian and global history. The alliance with the Soviet Union, the war in Korea, and the confrontation over Taiwan placed China at the center of the Cold War in Asia, while decolonization and revolution elsewhere in the region provided a context for Chinese choices. Understanding these years helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, and unfinished, and prepares them for Chapter 67, in which the government turns from recovery toward planned socialist transformation.
