Socialist Transformation and the First Five-Year Plan

China: A History of a Civilization · Principal chronology: 1953–1957

Chapter Opening

Between 1953 and 1957, the Chinese Communist Party tried to change not only what China produced but who owned it, how people worked, where they lived, what they studied, and how far they could speak. The result was real industrial growth, a new system of property and administration, and a campaign of political punishment that silenced many of the people the Party had invited to criticize it.

Imagine a peasant household in a north China village in the autumn of 1955. Three years earlier, its members received a small plot through land reform (Chapter 66). The land was theirs, and many families felt a security their parents had never known. Now a work team from the county has come to a village meeting. Cadres explain that individual farming will not feed the cities or pay for factories, and that families should pool land, tools, and labor in a cooperative. Some neighbors are enthusiastic. Others are uneasy about handing over a draft animal. Within about a year, the great majority of rural households across China would be enrolled in cooperatives of some kind.

Now imagine a young worker arriving at a steel plant in Anshan in Liaoning, in the northeast, where Japanese-built facilities had been damaged and stripped in the 1940s. He is housed in a dormitory, issued a ration, and assigned to a workshop that also runs a clinic and a canteen. A Soviet engineer may be consulting on the rolling mill. Elsewhere, in Shanghai, a textile owner learns that his mill will become a joint state-private enterprise, that he will draw fixed interest instead of a share of profits, and that a state-appointed manager will make most of the decisions. In a Beijing university, a professor who attended a 1957 meeting to criticize the Party will, within months, be dismissed and labeled a "Rightist."

This chapter studies these connected transformations. It begins with the decision, taken around 1952–1953, to move from the early recovery policies of the People's Republic toward socialist transformation: the conversion of agriculture, handicrafts, private industry, and commerce to collective or state ownership. It examines the First Five-Year Plan (1953–1957), the Soviet assistance that shaped it, and the planning institutions and constitution of 1954 that gave it a formal framework. It follows industrialization, collectivization, and nationalization in turn, then turns to cities, schools, and families, and ends with the Hundred Flowers Movement and the Anti-Rightist Campaign of 1956–1957.

What changed was profound. A mixed economy with private farms, private factories, and open markets gave way to a system dominated by planning, state and collective ownership, and administrative allocation. What continued was the Party's commitment to building a strong state, the long-standing goal of industrial modernization shared by many Chinese reformers since the late nineteenth century (Chapter 54), and the dependence of the state on peasant production. The people affected included more than 500 million inhabitants of China: peasants, workers, former business owners, artisans, students, scientists, writers, officials, and members of the non-Communist "democratic parties."

Historians understand these years through several kinds of evidence. Official statistics, plans, and Party resolutions show what the leadership intended and claimed. Speeches by Mao Zedong and other leaders reveal policy disagreements, although they were often revised before publication. Local archives, which have become more accessible to some researchers since the 1990s, show how policies worked in particular counties and factories. Memoirs, oral histories, and literary works record the experience of those who lived through the period, though they are shaped by later memory. Every one of these sources has limits, and the chapter will say so repeatedly.

This chapter also fits into larger stories. For Asia and the world, China's turn toward the Soviet model belonged to the early Cold War, when the People's Republic was diplomatically isolated from the United States and most of its allies and sought Soviet technology and credit. For the next chapters, the period created conditions that shaped the Great Leap Forward (Chapter 68): a centralized procurement system, collective rural institutions, a strengthened Party apparatus, and a political climate in which open criticism had just been punished. This chapter does not treat that later history as inevitable. Its task is to explain how the system was built, what it achieved, and which tensions were visible by the end of 1957.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between the New Democracy policies of the early People's Republic and the program of socialist transformation announced in the "general line" of the transition period.
  2. Describe the structure, goals, and adoption of the First Five-Year Plan, distinguishing the period it covered (1953–1957) from the date of its formal approval (1955).
  3. Analyze the role of Soviet loans, technology, advisers, and training in Chinese industrialization, and the limits of that influence.
  4. Distinguish the 156-project program from a simple count of completed factories, and explain why project definitions matter.
  5. Trace the sequence from mutual-aid teams to elementary and advanced cooperatives, and explain the acceleration of 1955–1956.
  6. Evaluate the connection between state grain procurement and the financing of industrial investment.
  7. Distinguish among state ownership, joint state-private ownership, and collective ownership, and explain how former business owners were treated.
  8. Explain how work units, rationing, and household registration developed in the 1950s, without attributing later features of those systems to the earlier period.
  9. Interpret the Hundred Flowers policy, the February 1957 speech on contradictions, the criticism movement of spring 1957, and the Anti-Rightist reversal as distinct but connected developments.
  10. Assess official statistics and estimates critically, distinguishing industrial achievement, agricultural performance, social change, and political repression.
  11. Compare how peasants, workers, business owners, women, intellectuals, and frontier communities experienced the same policies differently.
  12. Connect the outcomes of 1953–1957 to the conditions that preceded the Great Leap Forward without treating that outcome as predetermined.

Historical Context

The People's Republic of China was proclaimed on October 1, 1949, after decades of war, invasion, and civil conflict (Chapters 64–65). Its first three years (Chapter 66) combined consolidation with recovery. The government suppressed armed opposition, redistributed land in most of the country, launched campaigns against designated enemies, reformed marriage law, stabilized currency and prices, and entered the Korean War in late 1950. By 1952 industrial and agricultural output in official reports had roughly regained or exceeded prewar peaks. China was, however, still overwhelmingly agrarian. Most of its people lived in villages, modern industry was concentrated in a few coastal cities and in the northeast, and per capita income was low.

The economic and political thought of the Party leadership drew on several sources. Marxist-Leninist theory held that socialism required public ownership of the means of production. The Soviet Union, the only large socialist state, offered a concrete model of five-year plans, heavy-industry priority, collective farms, and a party-controlled state. Chinese reformers and nationalists since the late Qing had wanted a rich and strong country, and many non-Communist Chinese shared that aim. The Party's own experience in base areas (Chapter 64) had given it practice in mobilization, taxation in kind, and rural administration. Finally, the international situation after 1949, with the Cold War, the American embargo, and the Sino-Soviet alliance of 1950, made the Soviet Union the principal available partner.

The economic geography the new state inherited was uneven. The northeast, especially Liaoning, had the densest industrial base thanks to Japanese-era development in Manchukuo (Chapter 64), though much equipment had been removed after 1945. Shanghai, Tianjin, and other coastal cities had light industry, finance, and a commercial class. The interior, including most of the northwest and southwest, had little modern industry. Villages differed too: wheat and millet farming in the north, rice in the south, tea, cotton, and other cash crops in specialized areas. Pastoral and mountain regions with large non-Han populations, including Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, and parts of the southwest, were integrated into the new state at different speeds and often under different rules.

Social structure was equally varied. Land reform had reduced rural inequality but not eliminated it, and farms remained small and poorly capitalized. Urban society included industrial workers, shopkeepers, artisans, capitalists, a small professional and intellectual class, and large numbers of poor migrants. Families were organized by lineage and household in complex ways that the 1950 Marriage Law had begun to challenge. Women's labor was central to farming and textile production, though official rhetoric of equality did not always match lived reality.

Students should note that "China" in this chapter means the territory administered by the People's Republic of China. The Republic of China government, which had retreated to Taiwan in 1949 (Chapter 65), followed a different political and economic course, and Hong Kong and Macao remained under British and Portuguese administration. The Party's attempts to build socialism took place in a world in which the Soviet bloc, the United States and its allies, and newly independent states in Asia and Africa were all competing for influence.

This division explains the institutional and ideological decisions behind China's transition from postwar recovery to planned socialist development.

Section 67.1

From Economic Recovery to Socialist Transformation

By 1952, official reports showed that the Chinese economy had recovered from the damage of war and inflation. Prices were stable after the anti-inflation measures of 1950, the railway network had been repaired, land reform was nearly complete in most of the country, and industrial and agricultural output had reached or passed earlier peaks, according to official figures. The Party's early program, usually called New Democracy, had promised a long period in which a state-led sector, cooperatives, peasant smallholders, and private capital would coexist under the leadership of the Communist Party and in alliance with several social classes. The Common Program of 1949 had reflected that approach (Chapter 66).

Around 1952–1953, Party leaders concluded that this period would be shorter than once expected. Historians disagree about why. Several factors are commonly cited. The Korean War had increased the state's role in procurement and heavy industry. Private capital had been harnessed in some sectors but was also seen as unreliable, as the "Five-Anti" campaign of 1952 against bribery, tax evasion, and fraud had illustrated. Land reform had created many small peasant farms, and the leadership worried about the growth of rural differentiation and about the capacity of small farms to feed expanding cities. Mao Zedong and others, influenced by Soviet experience, also wanted a faster move toward socialism for ideological reasons, believing that history favored public ownership. Finally, the prospect of Soviet help for a large industrial program made central planning feasible.

The result was the general line for the transition period. Publicized in its full form in late 1953, it stated that the country would, over a period of roughly three five-year plans, achieve gradual industrialization and complete the socialist transformation of agriculture, handicrafts, and capitalist industry and commerce. In the common image, industrialization was the "main body," while the transformation of agriculture and handicrafts on one side and of capitalist industry and commerce on the other were the "two wings." Students should note that the original expectation was gradual: a period on the order of fifteen years. In practice, the transformations of agriculture and private industry and commerce were largely completed in 1955–1956, far ahead of that schedule.

The distinction between New Democracy and socialism is conceptual as well as chronological. New Democracy implied a multi-class state, protection of some private property, and a mixed economy. Socialism, as the Party defined it, meant that the main means of production would be owned by the state or by collectives, that market allocation would be replaced by planning, and that the Party would lead a "people's democratic dictatorship" in which classes designated as enemies would be excluded from political rights. How abruptly the Party moved from one to the other, and whether alternatives were realistic, remain subjects of historical discussion.

Section 67.2

The Soviet Alliance and the Transfer of Economic Models

The Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance, signed in February 1950, laid the diplomatic basis for Soviet help. Moscow offered a loan of US$300 million over five years at low interest, and further agreements followed. After Stalin's death in March 1953, Soviet leaders agreed in the same year to expand assistance, and further agreements in 1954 and 1955 enlarged the list of industrial projects. The Soviet Union also supplied equipment, blueprints, technical documentation, and, over the decade, thousands of specialists. At the same time, thousands of Chinese engineers, students, and officials went to the Soviet Union for study and training.

Students should be careful about the scale and nature of Soviet aid. Soviet credits financed only a share of China's investment, and a large part of the equipment and technology was paid for through Chinese exports of agricultural products, minerals, and other goods. The loans were not gifts, and Chinese leaders later complained about terms, delays, and Soviet attitudes. Even so, Soviet blueprints, standardized plant designs, and advice were of great importance in sectors such as steel, power equipment, and machine building, where Chinese domestic design capacity was thin.

The transfer was also institutional. Chinese planners adopted Soviet concepts such as material balances, in which planners compared the supply and demand of key goods; ministries organized by industrial branch; and enterprise management by a director responsible to higher authority. Soviet textbooks were translated, and Russian became a leading foreign language in schools and universities. The Soviet model was not copied mechanically. Chinese officials adapted it to a much poorer, more rural, and more populous country, and Chinese leaders, Mao in particular, expressed reservations about Soviet methods even as they relied on them.

China and the wider world. The alliance reflected the Cold War. The United States and many of its allies maintained an embargo on trade with China from the Korean War, making the Soviet bloc the main source of advanced equipment. Eastern European states, such as East Germany, Czechoslovakia, and Poland, also supplied machinery and technical help. Chinese leaders treated the relationship as a partnership and as a source of leverage. In 1956, events in the socialist world, including Khrushchev's criticism of Stalin and unrest in Poland and Hungary, would affect Chinese politics (Section 67.26). The relationship would deteriorate in later years (Chapter 69).

Historical Evidence

Sources on Soviet Assistance

Historians use treaties and credit agreements, Chinese ministry and planning documents, Soviet archives opened after 1991, memoirs of Chinese engineers and Soviet specialists, and translated technical manuals. Chinese and Russian archival records do not always agree on the number of specialists, the value of aid, or the terms of exchange, and published totals depend on how "aid," "credit," and "trade" are counted. Students should therefore treat precise totals as estimates and ask which source and definition each number comes from.

Section 67.3

The Making of the First Five-Year Plan

Planning for the First Five-Year Plan began in 1951, and a leadership group under Li Fuchun, working with the State Planning Commission and consulting Soviet advisers, produced successive drafts. The plan took shape while the period it covered was already underway. In 1953 the government began to implement its annual targets, and draft versions circulated within the Party. The finished plan was formally approved by the First National People's Congress in July 1955. Students should therefore distinguish between the period covered by the plan (1953–1957) and the date of its legislative adoption (1955).

The plan's central objective was to build the base for a heavy-industry economy. A very large share of state capital construction was assigned to industry, and within industry the overwhelming majority of investment was for heavy industry, including iron and steel, coal, electric power, machinery, chemicals, and defense-related production. Agriculture, light industry, and consumer goods received much smaller shares. The plan set targets for gross output of steel, coal, electricity, machine tools, cotton cloth, and grain, and it designated many new projects in the interior and the northeast.

Disagreements about priorities were present even at this stage. Some leaders, including Chen Yun and Zhou Enlai at various moments, emphasized balance and caution and worried about inflation, supply shortages, and the capacity of agriculture to bear the burden of investment. Others pressed for faster expansion. These disagreements would resurface in 1956 (Sections 67.10 and 67.26). Soviet advice also influenced the plan, but Chinese planners adjusted targets repeatedly, and the quality of data on which planning rested was poor in a country that lacked reliable statistics, experienced statisticians, and complete surveys. The State Statistical Bureau, established in 1952, worked to improve coverage, and the first modern census was conducted in 1953, reporting a population of roughly 580 million.

The Gao-Rao affair of 1953–1954 affected the planning apparatus. Gao Gang, who headed the State Planning Commission and had led the northeast, and Rao Shushi, a senior Party official in East China, were accused of forming an "anti-Party alliance." Gao died in 1954, reportedly by suicide, and the affair strengthened central control over regional authorities. It also revealed that the Party's top leadership was not free of personal and factional conflict and that planning institutions were entangled in politics from the start.

Section 67.4

Central Planning Institutions and Economic Administration

Central planning in China was an administrative system, not a single act. At its core was the State Planning Commission, which coordinated long-term and annual plans and allocated scarce materials and investment among sectors. The State Council and its ministries, many organized by industry such as metallurgy, machine building, coal, and railways, translated national targets into instructions for provincial departments and enterprises. The State Statistical Bureau gathered data, and a banking system under the People's Bank, which had been centralized in the early years, financed state enterprises and accounted for their funds.

Planned production was organized through quotas and material allocation. Enterprises received output targets, supplies, wage funds, and in many cases investment allocations. Prices for many goods were set administratively. Procurement agencies bought agricultural goods and cotton, and the state distributed grain and cloth through its own commercial network. Managers answered upward to ministries rather than to market demand. This arrangement made it possible to channel resources into priority sectors, but it also created typical problems: bargaining over targets, hoarding of materials, over-reporting of output, and weak incentives to cut costs or improve quality.

The system never became as complete as the formal plan suggested. Many goods were still traded in markets, rural households retained private plots and sideline production, and local governments and enterprises developed informal ways to obtain supplies. During 1953–1957 China also kept a mixture of centralized and decentralized elements. Regional administrative bureaus, which had governed large regions in the early 1950s, were abolished in 1954, increasing central control. In 1956–1957, as officials debated the weaknesses of excessive centralization, some authority was devolved to provinces. These adjustments show that the planned economy was constantly modified.

Historical Vocabulary
Central Planning and the Planned Economy

Central planning is a method of organizing an economy in which government agencies, rather than markets, decide what is produced, how resources are allocated, and often what prices are charged. In the Chinese case of the 1950s, planners set output targets and investment priorities in a Five-Year Plan, supplemented by annual plans. The term "planned economy" (jihua jingji, 计划经济) was used by the Party in contrast to "market anarchy," a phrase used to criticize capitalist competition.

Historians use the term with care. Planning was never total, even at its height, and prices, black markets, and informal exchanges continued to exist. In the First Five-Year Plan period, the planned sector was growing rather than complete, as many private and rural activities remained outside direct state allocation until 1956 or later.

Section 67.5

The Constitution of 1954 and the Developing Socialist State

In September 1954, the First National People's Congress met in Beijing, with deputies chosen through indirect elections that followed local elections and a national census, and adopted the first Constitution of the People's Republic. It replaced the Common Program of 1949, which had served as a provisional framework (Chapter 66). The Constitution defined China as a people's democratic state led by the working class and based on the alliance of workers and peasants. It described the NPC as the highest organ of state power, established a Standing Committee, created the post of Chairman of the People's Republic (held by Mao Zedong), and made Zhou Enlai Premier of the State Council with Liu Shaoqi leading the NPC Standing Committee.

The text also described the economy. It recognized several forms of ownership: state ownership, cooperative ownership, ownership by individual working people, and capitalist ownership. At the same time, it declared the transition to socialism as the state's goal and said the state would restrict and transform capitalist industry and commerce. This combination showed that the Constitution was a document of transition, written at a time when the Party still expected a gradual transformation. Within two years, the practical balance of ownership shifted dramatically.

The Constitution affected governance in several ways. It regularized the state apparatus, provided a legal basis for the plan, affirmed the system of regional ethnic autonomy, and guaranteed a list of civil rights to citizens, including speech, publication, and assembly. These formal provisions coexisted with the continuing authority of the Communist Party, which the Constitution recognized as leading the state and which held effective control over appointments and policy. Students should therefore distinguish between formal state institutions and the Party's actual role. In practice, important decisions were made in Party bodies and then implemented through state organs. Civil rights provisions were limited in practice by political campaigns and by the classification of "enemies" who were denied political rights.

Division II · 1953–1957Industrialization, Infrastructure, and the First Five-Year Plan

This division explains how central planning and Soviet assistance accelerated industrialization while also establishing structural imbalances.

Section 67.6

Soviet-Assisted Industrial Projects and the 156-Project Program

The core of the plan's industrial program was a set of large projects built with Soviet technical assistance. The "156 projects" is the usual name for this program, but the number does not refer to 156 identical factories completed in 1953–1957. Agreements in 1953, 1954, and 1955 added successive batches of projects: an initial group of around 90 in 1953, further additions in 1954, and a later round in 1955. Scholars reporting the total differ depending on whether they count by agreement, by plant, or by project and on revisions made over time. Some projects were canceled, merged, or changed, and many were completed after 1957. Students should therefore read the figure as the name of a program that evolved.

The projects covered several sectors: iron and steel, nonferrous metals, coal and oil, electric power plants, machine building, chemicals, aircraft and defense-related factories, and others. They were concentrated in the northeast, where existing infrastructure made expansion easier, and in selected inland cities such as Wuhan, Baotou, Taiyuan, Xi'an, Lanzhou, Luoyang, and Chengdu. The inland emphasis reflected a goal of reducing regional imbalance and, in part, strategic concerns about vulnerability in the Korean War and Cold War context. Soviet specialists helped with site selection, design, and equipment installation, and Chinese planners adjusted plans to local resources.

Even a project built with foreign designs required enormous Chinese effort: surveying sites, building roads and rail spurs, recruiting construction workers, producing local materials, and training personnel. The projects drew on domestic labor and capital, drawn largely from agricultural surplus and state revenue. Reliance on Soviet technology also created dependence: when relations deteriorated around 1960 and Soviet specialists withdrew, many projects were affected (Chapter 69).

Section 67.7

Steel, Coal, and the Expansion of Heavy Industry

Steel was the emblem of the plan. The Anshan Iron and Steel Company in Liaoning, which had been developed under Japanese control and then damaged, was rebuilt and expanded in the early 1950s and became the largest steel complex in the country. New facilities, including blast furnaces, rolling mills, and seamless tube and wire-rod plants, were built with Soviet designs. Other new steel centers were planned at Wuhan and Baotou, though they came into production later in the decade or after. Official reports state that national steel output rose from roughly 1.3 million tons in 1952 to about 5 million tons in 1957. These are official figures and should be treated as approximate.

Coal remained the main energy source. Existing mines in Fushun, Kailuan, and elsewhere were mechanized and expanded, and new mines were opened in the northeast, the north, and the interior. Official output approximately doubled over the period. Coal mining was dangerous, labor-intensive, and often located in remote areas, and expansion drew on large numbers of workers, many of them recruited from rural areas. Mine safety and conditions varied, and local reports show frequent accidents even as output grew.

Behind heavy industry lay a strategic logic. Planners believed that steel, coal, power, and machinery were the basis for everything else, including military strength, railways, and farm equipment. Heavy industry was capital-intensive: it required large investments and created relatively few jobs per yuan invested, and it did not directly produce consumer goods. This was one reason for tension between investment and living standards, discussed in Section 67.10. Heavy industry also required skilled workers and engineers, which were scarce, and the training system described in Section 67.9 was designed to supply them.

Section 67.8

Machinery, Energy, Chemicals, and Transportation

Machine building was the second pillar. The First Automobile Works at Changchun, begun in 1953 with Soviet assistance, produced China's first domestically assembled trucks in 1956. Harbin became a center for electrical machinery, turbines, and boilers. Luoyang was developed for tractor and mining machinery. These plants let China produce equipment it had formerly imported, though they depended on Soviet designs and imported components for years. Quality varied and early output was small.

Electric power capacity grew rapidly from a low base, with new thermal plants and early hydroelectric projects. Chemical industries, including fertilizer, basic chemicals, and plastics, were expanded, though fertilizer supply remained inadequate for agriculture. Oil production was small: China had limited known reserves in the 1950s, and its large later fields were developed afterward.

Transportation was essential for linking new industrial centers. Railways were the main means of moving goods and people, and the state expanded the network into the interior and the west. The Chengdu–Chongqing line opened in 1952, the Baoji–Chengdu line was connected by completed track in July 1956 but did not open to through traffic until January 1958; construction also pushed westward toward Lanzhou and Xinjiang. The Wuhan Yangtze River Bridge, opened in October 1957, linked the north-south railway across the river at a key location. These projects employed large labor forces, including soldiers and mobilized peasants, and met strategic and economic goals.

Section 67.9

Industrial Workers, Engineers, and New Industrial Centers

The industrial program needed people. The number of workers and staff in state enterprises rose sharply during the plan, drawing on rural migrants, urban unemployed, demobilized soldiers, and graduates of new technical schools. Training programs, apprenticeships, and short courses expanded, and enterprises provided on-the-job instruction. Soviet specialists and Chinese engineers trained in the USSR formed a technical elite, and the government sought to create a "red and expert" workforce that was both politically reliable and skilled, though these two goals did not always align.

Industrial cities changed rapidly. New districts were built around factories, with worker housing, schools, hospitals, and shops, and the population of cities such as Anshan, Wuhan, Taiyuan, Changchun, and Baotou grew. Housing was often crowded and inadequate, and migrants faced shortages of services. Workplace conditions ranged from relatively good in showcase plants to hazardous in mines and construction. Wages depended on a grade system modeled in part on Soviet practice, with differences by skill, industry, and region, and many workers received benefits such as medical care and subsidized food through their enterprises.

Regional and Frontier Context

Industry and Development Beyond the Core

Development was uneven by region. The northeast became the heartland of heavy industry, while the coastal cities retained much of the light industry and commerce. Inland cities received new plants, but large parts of the southwest and northwest were still primarily agrarian. In Xinjiang, the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps was established in 1954, organizing demobilized soldiers and migrants for farming, construction, and defense, and it changed the region's economic and demographic landscape. Historians discuss this development from different perspectives: official narratives emphasize development and border consolidation, while other scholars and many members of local communities emphasize migration, land use, and changing ethnic balance.

In pastoral and minority regions, the Party generally proceeded more cautiously than in Han farming areas, using terms such as "peaceful consultation" and gradualism in the 1950s, and Tibet was not subjected to the same land and collectivization programs during this period. Frontier peoples therefore experienced the First Five-Year Plan differently, through new roads, factories, migration, and shifts in administration, rather than through uniform national policies.

Section 67.10

Achievements and Limitations of the First Five-Year Plan

By official reports, the plan's results were striking. Gross industrial output value was reported to have grown at an average annual rate of about 18 percent during 1953–1957, and the output of steel, coal, electricity, machine tools, and other goods grew substantially. New industries, including automobiles, aircraft, and heavy machinery, were established. The country built a stock of industrial plants, trained engineers, and expanded the railway network. These achievements were real in a broad sense, even if exact figures are uncertain, and they laid a base on which later industrial development built.

The limits were also visible. Agricultural gross output value grew much more slowly than industrial output, at an official average annual rate of about 4.5 percent, while grain output rose from approximately 164 million to 195 million tonnes—roughly 3.5 percent per year. Grain production per person increased only modestly as population grew. Because agriculture supplied food, raw materials for light industry, and exports, slow growth constrained the whole economy. Consumer goods and housing remained scarce. Investment in heavy industry created few jobs relative to China's large labor force, and urban unemployment persisted. Regional inequalities remained or widened in places, since the best-equipped industrial areas continued to receive resources. Planners faced shortages of steel, cement, and other inputs, and in 1956 an attempt at rapid expansion led to strains that officials corrected in an "opposing rash advance" adjustment.

Official statistics themselves require care. Data were gathered by local officials who had incentives to report success, and early statistical systems had gaps. Outside scholars have produced revised estimates that sometimes differ from official figures, particularly for agriculture and for per capita consumption. The overall judgment of historians, however, is that industrial output expanded rapidly, that the structure of the economy shifted toward industry, and that these gains came with an imbalance between sectors.

Historical Debate

Industrial Growth and Structural Imbalance

The question: Was the First Five-Year Plan a successful foundation for industrial development, or did its structure create imbalances that undermined later growth?

Interpretation A — A successful foundation: Supporters point to the rapid growth of output, the creation of new industries, the training of technical personnel, and the effective use of Soviet assistance. They argue that a poor country with limited capital had to prioritize heavy industry and that the plan delivered high growth.
Interpretation B — Growth with serious imbalances: Critics emphasize slower agricultural growth, the burden on peasants, weak consumer supply, capital-intensive projects that created few jobs, and inefficiency in planning. They argue that the model's emphasis on heavy industry and extraction from agriculture produced strains that later policies aggravated.

Evidence: Both views use output statistics, investment data, and archival reports, but interpret them differently. Comparisons with other developing countries and with Soviet experience also matter.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how to judge the Soviet model, the relationship between industry and agriculture, and the road toward the Great Leap Forward.

Division III · 1951–1957Agricultural Collectivization and the Transformation of Rural Society

This division shows that collectivization was a major transformation of rural property rights, economic organization, and political authority. It was neither the land reform of Chapter 66 nor the people's commune movement of Chapter 68.

Section 67.11

From Land Reform to Agricultural Mutual-Aid Teams

Land reform gave tens of millions of rural families land, but it also created a landscape of very small, scattered holdings. Many households lacked draft animals, plows, seed, or enough labor, especially widows' households, families that had lost a male worker in war, and the poorest peasants. Some who received land soon fell into debt or sold it, and the Party worried that differences would reappear. Rural officials observed that a minority of "rich peasants" and prosperous middle peasants were accumulating land and capital, while poorer households struggled.

The Party's answer was cooperation. Mutual-aid teams were the simplest form: a few neighboring households agreed to exchange labor, tools, and animals, usually at busy seasons, while each household still owned and farmed its land. Many such arrangements had existed in the base areas and in traditional village practice, and the Party promoted them widely from 1951. Mutual-aid teams could be temporary or year-round, and they varied from informal swaps to organized units with leaders and work records.

Communist arguments for cooperation combined economic and ideological claims. Pooling labor and tools, it was argued, would raise productivity, make it possible to build irrigation and use better methods, and allow the state to guide production. Cooperation was also seen as a path away from rural capitalism. Many peasants had their own reasons to participate, such as shared labor needs and the desire for security, though others valued the independence of family farming and distrusted cadres. The Party insisted that participation be voluntary, but local pressures varied.

Section 67.12

Elementary Agricultural Producers' Cooperatives

The next stage, formally encouraged in a Central Committee resolution of December 1953, was the elementary agricultural producers' cooperative. In these, households pooled land and often tools and animals under a common management, work was organized collectively, and income was distributed partly according to the land and other assets contributed (often called land shares) and partly according to labor. Members retained legal ownership of their land in principle, and small private plots remained. A cooperative typically included a few dozen households, and members elected leaders, though cadres exerted influence.

Elementary cooperatives raised questions of fairness and management. How should the value of different plots be compared? How should work be measured and rewarded? How much should be set aside for a common fund, and who decided? Disputes arose over land valuation, the pricing of animals and tools, and the pay of cadres. Where management was capable and trusted, cooperatives could improve use of labor and allow joint projects, but where leaders were inexperienced, favored relatives, or imposed unrealistic plans, members lost confidence.

The relationship between peasants and cadres was central. Village cadres, who often came from poor peasant backgrounds, were responsible for forming cooperatives, meeting grain procurement quotas, and reporting upward, and they were rewarded for success and criticized for failure. This created incentives to expand quickly and sometimes to pressure households. In the early phase, from 1953 to early 1955, the number of cooperatives grew steadily but under a policy of selective consolidation, and in some areas the Party actively cut back overly rapid formation, a "stop, contract, and develop" approach in 1955.

Section 67.13

Mao's 1955 Collectivization Offensive

In spring 1955 the leadership was divided. Deng Zihui, who headed the Party's rural work department, and others saw problems in a number of cooperatives, including poor management, peasant discontent, and slaughter of animals in some areas, and favored consolidation before further expansion. Mao Zedong believed that this caution was holding back a rural enthusiasm that could be released. In July 1955, in a speech to provincial and regional Party secretaries titled "On the Question of Agricultural Cooperation," Mao criticized those who he said were "tottering along like a woman with bound feet" and argued that the pace should be accelerated. Deng Zihui and others were criticized, and an enlarged plenum of the Central Committee in October 1955 endorsed rapid expansion.

What followed was a political campaign. Provincial and county leaders were pressed to set higher enrollment targets, and reports of "high tide" enthusiasm filled the press. A widely circulated compilation of cases titled The Socialist Upsurge in China's Countryside, with comments added by Mao, publicized examples of rapid advance. Cadres competed to show progress, and the pace of change exceeded the earlier plan. Students should recognize that this was a top-down political decision, and that the scale of change depended on both policy pressure and on varying local responses.

Section 67.14

Advanced Cooperatives and the Socialist Transformation of Agriculture

The pace of change after July 1955 was remarkable. According to Kenneth Walker's study, the proportion of peasant households belonging to cooperatives rose from about 14 percent in mid-1955 to over 91 percent by May 1956, though students should distinguish between the types of cooperatives counted. The shift to advanced agricultural producers' cooperatives, in which members contributed land to the collective without land-share payments and received income according to labor, was especially important, since it ended the link between rewards and land ownership. Official reports state that by the end of 1956 most households were in advanced cooperatives, which were typically larger, with a hundred or more households.

Advanced cooperatives were organized into production teams and brigades with assigned fields and workers, and work was recorded and paid in "work points." Private ownership of land, large animals, and major tools was largely replaced by collective ownership, though households kept small private plots, some livestock, and sideline activities within limits. Model regulations issued in 1956 set out the rules for membership, management, and distribution. The change was a profound alteration of property rights and economic organization.

Implementation varied by region. In the north, where families had practiced mutual aid and the Party had deeper roots, the transition could be smoother, whereas in parts of the south and southwest, local conditions, rice-growing practices, and the greater importance of small-scale commerce and handicrafts made it more complicated. In minority regions, policies were more gradual. The speed of change also increased the burdens on local cadres, many of whom lacked experience managing large units, and the rapid scale-up produced mistakes in planning, record-keeping, and distribution.

Section 67.15

State Grain Procurement, Agricultural Taxes, and Rural-Urban Supply

Collectivization was linked to the state's need for grain. In late 1953, after poor harvests and rising urban demand left the state short of supplies, the government introduced a system of unified purchase and sale (tonggou tongxiao, 统购统销) of grain. Peasants were required to sell surplus grain to the state at fixed prices, private grain trading was restricted, and the state distributed grain to cities and to rural areas that lacked enough. Cotton and other crops were later brought under similar controls, and urban rationing, including cloth rationing from 1954 and grain coupons from 1955, followed.

The system served several purposes. It guaranteed food for urban workers and the army, kept prices low for industrial workers, provided raw materials and exports, and let the state capture agricultural surplus for investment. In addition to procurement, peasants paid an agricultural tax, which remained a significant source of state revenue. Because procurement prices were set below what the market would have paid, scholars often describe this as a form of resource transfer from agriculture to industry, although they disagree about its scale and about how much of the burden fell on different households and regions.

Procurement and collectivization reinforced one another. It was easier for the state to buy grain from a few thousand cooperatives than from millions of households, and cooperatives gave cadres more control over production plans and delivery. Peasants, in turn, bore the responsibility of meeting quotas that were sometimes set on the basis of overestimated harvests. The mechanism would remain central to rural life and, in the Great Leap Forward, would become a major factor in the severity of the famine (Chapter 68), but students should not attribute that outcome to the policies of 1953–1957 alone.

Cause and Effect

Grain Procurement and Industrial Investment

Causes

Short-term: grain shortages and urban demand in 1953, private trading and price pressure. Long-term: the Party's commitment to heavy industry, limited sources of capital, Soviet-style planning, and a poor, small-farm economy.

Transformation

The state monopolized grain purchase and distribution, set prices, and rationed supply, and cooperatives became the unit through which delivery was organized.

Effects

Immediate: stable urban supply and low food prices; peasant discontent in some areas. Long-term: a lasting structure of state control over agricultural surplus and a rural-urban divide in access to food.

Complexity

The system cannot be explained by one cause: it resulted from ideology, circumstances, planning, and the availability of other sources of capital, and its results varied by region and by household.
Section 67.16

Rural Life, Resistance, and the Results of Collectivization

How did villagers experience collectivization? Evidence points to a wide range. Some poorer peasants welcomed cooperatives as a source of security and tools, and some cadres and activists saw them as a path to a better life. Others, especially better-off middle peasants, worried about the loss of land and animals and about the value assigned to what they contributed. Reports from 1955–1956 describe households slaughtering livestock or selling tools before joining, disputes about compensation, and quiet noncompliance. Some regions saw open protests. After the quick enrollment, a wave of withdrawal requests in 1956–1957 emerged in parts of the country, and the Party responded with a mixture of persuasion, consolidation, and in some places pressure. The leadership also criticized "commandism," in which cadres used coercion.

Production results were mixed. In some areas, pooling labor and tools and carrying out irrigation works improved output and let farmers use methods such as improved seed. In others, poor management and confusion about work points reduced effort. Grain output grew moderately over the period, according to official figures, while the total demands of procurement increased. Collectivization also changed rural politics: cadres gained authority over land use, labor, and distribution, and the individual household's economic autonomy diminished. Women's work in cooperatives was recorded in work points, often at lower rates than men's, though official policy stressed equal pay for equal work.

Division IV · 1950–1956Nationalization of Industry and Commerce

This division explains how the government moved from regulating a mixed economy toward state and collective ownership of production and commerce.

Section 67.17

Private Enterprise and the Limits of New Democracy

In 1949, private industry and commerce accounted for a large share of industrial output and nearly all retail trade. The Party's early policy was to use private capital under state guidance, because the government needed its production, management skills, and employment. Private industrialists in Shanghai, Tianjin, and elsewhere were encouraged to resume operations, and the government gave them processing and ordering contracts. The Common Program had protected legal private ownership and described capitalist enterprises as part of the New Democratic economy.

The environment gradually tightened. The state expanded its own enterprises, took over foreign firms during the Korean War period, and increasingly controlled raw materials, credit, and markets, so that private firms depended on state orders. In 1952, the Five-Anti campaign targeted bribery, tax evasion, theft of state property, cheating on government contracts, and theft of economic information, and it subjected many business owners to investigation, fines, and public criticism. Some owners were driven to bankruptcy or suicide. The campaign demonstrated the state's power over private business and shifted bargaining power in the state's favor.

Official policy toward private ownership therefore shifted from use and regulation, to restriction, and then to transformation. Many business owners had supported the new government and hoped to continue to participate in national development, and some viewed joint management as a way to keep their enterprises going. Others worried about the long-term future. The tension between the Party's promise of a place for patriotic capitalists and its goal of eliminating capitalism shaped the experience of this class.

Section 67.18

Joint State-Private Enterprises and Public-Private Management

A joint state-private enterprise (gongsi heying, 公私合营) was a business in which the state held a share or took part in management alongside the former private owner. This differed from state ownership, in which the enterprise belonged wholly to the state, and from collective ownership, in which a cooperative or collective unit owned the property. Joint enterprises began on a small scale in the early 1950s, mostly with firms heavily dependent on state orders. From 1954, they expanded and were adopted as the main form of transformation for larger private industry.

In these firms, state representatives, often Party members or officials, joined or directed management. Owners usually continued to work in the firm in some capacity, but real authority shifted to state-appointed managers and the plan. Compensation for capitalists took the form of dividends that, from 1956, became fixed interest at a rate (commonly stated as 5 percent annually) on the assessed value of their assets, initially for a limited number of years. Profit was divided among tax, welfare funds, reserves, and the owners' share. Owners also usually received a salary. These arrangements were an alternative to outright confiscation, and the Party described them as peaceful "redemption."

The change in management affected firms in different ways. Experienced managers were sometimes retained for their skills, and some firms benefited from state orders and supplies. Others experienced friction between owners and Party cadres and between technical and political criteria. Workers in such firms often gained access to state-enterprise benefits and wage systems, though the change also meant stricter oversight.

Section 67.19

The Socialist Transformation of Industry and Commerce, 1955–1956

As in agriculture, the pace accelerated sharply late in 1955. In the autumn and winter, after Mao and the leadership decided to speed up, urban authorities organized "high tide" campaigns to bring entire trades and cities into joint operation. A dramatic moment came in January 1956, when Beijing declared that its private industry and commerce had entered joint state-private operation in a mass celebration, and Shanghai, Tianjin, and other cities followed within days or weeks. Official reports claimed that by the end of 1956 nearly all private industry and most private commerce had been transformed, including through trade-wide reorganization in which entire sectors were merged and firms consolidated.

Reorganization often meant that firms were consolidated, and small, weak, or redundant businesses were closed or absorbed, shifting employees. The change brought increased state control over distribution and retail, and it lessened competition. Business owners had little room to refuse: in the atmosphere of the campaign, resistance could be labeled as hostility to socialism. Some, like Rong Yiren in Shanghai, continued to hold official posts and were presented as examples of cooperation, and many professional managers and technicians moved into state employment.

Multiple Perspectives

Business Owners, Managers, and Workers in 1956

Business owners: Some saw joint ownership as the end of independence but accepted it as unavoidable and hoped to keep a role. Others accepted the official language of patriotic contribution and hoped for protection. All faced loss of control over their firms, regardless of their attitude.
Managers and technicians: Experienced managers could gain secure employment and in some cases new responsibilities, but faced oversight from Party cadres and uncertainty about the value of their expertise in political terms.
Workers: Many welcomed better benefits, job security, and the removal of owners' authority, while others faced reorganization and transfers. Their position gave them more political status, though not necessarily autonomy.
Party and state officials: Officials were charged with achieving rapid transformation and often felt pressure to meet targets, which sometimes resulted in disruption of service and supply.

Note: These perspectives are not equally powerful. The state set the terms, and the former owners' participation took place under pressure.

Section 67.20

Handicraft Cooperatives, Small Shops, and Commercial Reorganization

Millions of artisans, small shopkeepers, peddlers, and household producers were part of the economy. Handicrafts produced tools, cloth, furniture, and goods for rural and urban markets, and their transformation took the form of cooperatives, which ranged from supply-and-marketing groups to full producers' cooperatives in which members pooled tools and shared income. In 1955–1956 enrollment accelerated, and by the end of 1956 official reports stated that most artisans had joined cooperatives. Small shops and peddlers were similarly organized into cooperative stores or placed under state supply-and-marketing systems.

The effects were mixed. Cooperatives provided tools, raw materials, and a degree of security, but consolidation of small trades meant the disappearance of many neighborhood services, specialized crafts, and local varieties of goods. Reports during 1956–1957 complained of a decline in service quality, a shortage of everyday repairs and goods, and the loss of traditional crafts. In response, officials in 1956–1957 acknowledged some of these problems and allowed some small-scale independent activity to resume. Households that relied on small trades, including many women and older people, faced particular adjustments.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Property Ownership Before and After 1956

What Changed

  • Farmland moved from household ownership (after land reform) to collective ownership and management.
  • Private industry and commerce moved to joint state-private operation, with owners receiving fixed interest rather than profits.
  • Artisans and small traders moved into cooperatives and state supply networks.
  • Resource allocation shifted from markets toward plans and administrative systems.

What Stayed the Same

  • Household and family remained the basic units of daily life, with private plots and sidelines in the countryside.
  • Many local markets and informal exchanges persisted, even when restricted.
  • The state continued to depend on agricultural surplus and on the labor of peasants.
  • Many former owners and managers continued to work in their firms in new roles.

Who experienced change most directly? Business owners and better-off peasants lost the most control; poorer peasants and many workers gained status or security. Why does it matter? The transformation changed who controlled economic decisions, and it set the stage for later economic debates.

Division V · 1950sUrban Society, Education, and Everyday Life under Socialism

Socialist transformation involved more than ownership and output. It reorganized the institutions through which people worked, obtained necessities, received education, and took part in society.

Section 67.21

Urban Work Units and the Organization of Labor

The work unit (danwei, 单位) became the basic organization of urban life. A factory, school, hospital, government office, or research institute provided employment and often much more: housing, canteens, clinics, childcare, schooling, and access to scarce goods. Employees' wages and benefits were set by grade, and the unit managed political study, discipline, and personnel records. As state and joint enterprises multiplied after 1955, more urban residents came under this system. Students should view the danwei in the 1950s as a developing institution. Many of its features, including the extensive housing and welfare provision and the thick personnel files of the later Maoist decades, were still being built, and not all urban workers belonged to full work units. Small collectives, informal workers, and the unemployed stood outside them.

The unit linked labor and politics. Party branches operated within workplaces, trade unions served as transmission belts as well as representatives, and political campaigns were organized through units. A worker's status, whether state employee or collective member, skilled or unskilled, Party member or not, influenced income and opportunity. Women made up a growing share of the workforce, especially in textiles and light industry, though they were concentrated in lower-paid roles. State employment meant relative security and benefits, which made it highly desirable, and also meant dependence on the unit.

Section 67.22

Urbanization, Migration, Rationing, and Household Registration

The new industrial program drew migrants from the countryside, and the urban population grew quickly in the early 1950s. This created pressures on housing, food, and employment, since the number of jobs in state industry was limited. Officials began to restrict movement. A directive of 1953 warned against "blind" flows of peasants into the cities, and further measures in the following years sought to return migrants to their villages, control recruitment, and regulate who could draw on urban supplies. Urban grain supply certificates, introduced in 1955, tied access to subsidized food to urban residence, and cloth rationing had begun in 1954.

Registration of households had earlier origins. Urban household registration regulations were issued by public security authorities in 1951, and in 1955 the State Council instructed that a system of permanent household registration be established in both city and countryside. Students must note, however, that the key national regulation on household registration (hukou, 户口), which sharply restricted rural-to-urban movement, was adopted in January 1958. In 1953–1957, registration, rationing, and employment controls were developing and enforcement was uneven. The later, more rigid division between rural and urban status should not be read back into these years.

Historical Vocabulary
Danwei, Hukou, and Agricultural Cooperative

Danwei (单位, "unit") is the workplace organization through which urban residents were employed and often housed and supplied. Hukou (户口) is the system of household registration that records where a person belongs and, in its later form, restricts movement and access to benefits. An agricultural producers' cooperative (nongye shengchan hezuoshe, 农业生产合作社) was a rural organization in which households pooled land and labor. All three were institutions that connected individuals to the state and shaped social status. All three took shape in stages during the 1950s and reached more rigid forms in later years.

Section 67.23

Education and the Reorganization of Higher Learning

The government expanded schooling quickly but unevenly. Primary enrollment grew greatly and literacy campaigns, including evening and spare-time classes for adults, reached millions, though quality and access differed between city and countryside, and many rural children, especially girls, still received little schooling. Secondary schools and technical secondary schools increased, with a focus on producing technicians for industry. Admission and advancement increasingly considered political background as well as academic results, and children from families classified as landlord, capitalist, or otherwise suspect could face discrimination.

Higher education was restructured in 1952–1953 in what is often called the readjustment of departments. Following the Soviet model, authorities reorganized universities, converting comprehensive universities into smaller institutions focused on specific fields and creating specialized institutes for engineering, geology, medicine, teacher training, agriculture, and other applied fields. Some private and missionary-founded universities had been absorbed into the state system in the early 1950s. Tsinghua University, for example, was reshaped into a polytechnic institution, and some humanities and social science programs were reduced or merged. The aim was to train specialists for the plan, though the changes disrupted established academic traditions and affected the careers of many scholars.

Students entered curricula that blended technical education with political study, and many were assigned jobs by the state after graduation. The system created new opportunities for children of workers and peasants, and enrollments in higher education grew, but it also reinforced central direction of careers. Russian-language instruction and Soviet textbooks were prominent. Intellectuals' perspectives on this restructuring varied: some welcomed the expansion of technical training, while others criticized loss of academic autonomy.

Section 67.24

Science, Engineering, and Cultural Transformation

The Chinese Academy of Sciences, founded in 1949, was expanded with new institutes, and scientific and technical personnel were enlisted for national goals. Soviet models influenced the organization of research, with academies and institutes separate from universities and a stress on applied work. Chinese scientists who had trained in Europe, the United States, or Japan, and some who returned to China in the early 1950s, were integrated into the new system, often at the cost of political study and campaigns of ideological reform of intellectuals launched in 1951–1952.

In January 1956 Zhou Enlai gave a speech at a conference on intellectuals declaring that most intellectuals had become part of the working class and calling for their fuller use. This was followed by the Twelve-Year Plan for the development of science and technology (1956–1967), which set priorities for fields including atomic energy, electronics, computers, and automation, with some Soviet assistance. The speech and plan represented a moment of relative recognition of expertise. The tension between "red" (political loyalty) and "expert" (technical skill) was a continuing theme, and it helps explain both the encouragement of intellectual criticism in 1956–1957 and the harsh reaction that followed.

Culture was also organized by the state. Publishing, film, theater, and the arts were placed under Party guidance, with writers expected to serve socialism. The campaign against the critic Hu Feng in 1955, in which his writings were condemned as counterrevolutionary and many associates were arrested, warned writers of the limits of dissent and was an immediate prelude to the debates of 1956 (Section 67.26). At the same time, there was extensive publication of classical and translated works, mass literacy efforts, and new popular forms.

Section 67.25

Workers, Women, Families, and Everyday Socialist Life

Everyday life in the mid-1950s combined new opportunities with shortages and controls. Urban workers in state enterprises enjoyed job security, subsidized services, and rising status, while housing remained crowded and consumer goods limited. The state encouraged frugality and diligence, rationed staples, and promoted new customs such as public celebrations and political study. In the countryside, daily life revolved around cooperative work, work points, and private plots, with the household still central.

Women's lives changed in several ways. The Marriage Law of 1950 (Chapter 66) gave legal support to free choice of partner and divorce, and it was promoted through campaigns that produced a rise in divorce petitions in some areas, as well as backlash and in some cases violence. Women entered paid work and cooperative labor in greater numbers, and the state promoted women's political participation through the All-China Women's Federation. Yet women often did the same or more labor for lower work points, carried the burden of household tasks and childcare, and were underrepresented in leadership positions. Official ideals of equality coexisted with persistent inequalities, and family structures, including patrilocal residence and son preference, remained strong, particularly in rural areas.

Social mobility was real for some groups. Children of workers and peasants gained access to schools, jobs, and Party membership. Others experienced downward mobility: former landlords, rich peasants, and business owners, as well as their children, faced discrimination, and the class-label system, which classified people according to their pre-1949 economic position, shaped life chances. Historians therefore describe the period as one of both opportunity and exclusion. Differences between official ideals and lived experience also appear in shortages, local corruption, and tensions between cadres and ordinary people, evident in the criticisms voiced in 1957.

Section 67.26

Intellectuals, Political Authority, and the Crisis of Socialist Governance

The year 1956 was a turning point in the socialist world. In February, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev delivered a "secret speech" criticizing Stalin's abuses and the cult of personality, and the speech's content spread among Communist parties. Chinese leaders reacted cautiously, partly supporting de-Stalinization and partly worrying about its implications for their own authority. In autumn, unrest in Poland and the Hungarian uprising, crushed by Soviet forces, showed that Communist governments could face serious opposition. Chinese leaders drew different lessons: some urged more openness to prevent a similar crisis, and others feared that open criticism could lead to instability.

Within China, the speed of socialist transformation had created problems. Local cadres had bullied or misled peasants and business owners, bureaucratic procedures were rigid, and the Party's rapid expansion had made many officials arrogant or incompetent in the eyes of critics. Shortages and inefficiencies strained patience. In April 1956, Mao's speech "On the Ten Great Relationships" argued for better balances between heavy industry and agriculture and light industry, between the center and localities, and between Han and minority peoples. The Eighth Party Congress in September 1956 declared that the main contradiction was between an advanced socialist system and backward productive forces, which implied a shift in emphasis from class struggle to economic development, and it endorsed collective leadership. Deng Xiaoping became General Secretary of the Party.

The Party had a complex relationship with intellectuals. It needed their skills, but had subjected them to ideological campaigns, and many intellectuals felt constrained by Party supervision of their work, by political study, and by the dominance of Party members with limited education. Non-Communist parties, such as the China Democratic League and the China Democratic National Construction Association, existed as junior partners and had little real influence. Around 1956, some leaders concluded that greater room for expression might reduce tension and improve governance. This is the context for the Hundred Flowers policy.

China and the wider world. The leadership's thinking in 1956 reflected both domestic problems and the international environment. China was a major voice in the socialist camp, and its leaders saw an opportunity to position themselves as independent-minded partners of Moscow. The Bandung Conference of April 1955, where Zhou Enlai represented China among newly independent Asian and African states, had also raised China's international profile. The later tension with the Soviet Union (Chapter 69) had roots in the way each party interpreted the events of 1956.

Section 67.27

The Hundred Flowers Policy and the Invitation to Criticize

The slogan "let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend" drew on the language of the Warring States period (Chapter 12). Mao Zedong used it in 1956, and Lu Dingyi, head of the Party's propaganda department, elaborated it in a speech on May 26, 1956, applying it to science, the arts, and scholarship and suggesting that debate should be allowed on matters of academic and artistic disagreement. For several months, the policy was cautious, and many intellectuals were skeptical, recalling the Hu Feng case and earlier campaigns. Some wrote or spoke in 1956, but widespread public criticism remained limited until spring 1957.

Historians disagree about the Party's intentions. Some argue that Mao sincerely wanted open criticism to correct bureaucratism and to win intellectuals' support, while others suggest that he and other leaders expected only limited, constructive comments. The uncertain boundaries of criticism were central: speakers were told that criticism of specific cadres and practices was welcome, but the Party's leadership and the socialist system were meant to be off limits. In practice, those boundaries were unclear, and there was disagreement within the leadership about how much openness was wise, with some, such as Liu Shaoqi and Zhou Enlai, reportedly more cautious than Mao.

In April 1957, the Party issued a directive launching a rectification campaign, a drive to correct "three evils": bureaucratism, sectarianism, and subjectivism. Non-Party people were invited to offer criticism of Party members and organizations, and newspapers and meetings were opened to such comments. The message was repeated by officials and in the press, and it gradually convinced many intellectuals, journalists, and democratic-party members that this time speaking out was safe.

Section 67.28

Mao's Theory of Contradictions Among the People

On February 27, 1957, Mao delivered a speech at an enlarged session of the Supreme State Conference, a gathering of several hundred officials and prominent non-Party figures, titled "On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People." He distinguished between two types of contradictions: antagonistic contradictions between the people and their enemies, to be handled by coercion, and non-antagonistic contradictions among the people, to be handled through persuasion, discussion, and criticism. The speech argued that contradictions continued to exist under socialism, including between leaders and led, and that criticism and debate were appropriate means of resolving non-antagonistic ones. It also addressed questions of the pace of economic development, relations between peasants and the state, and the role of intellectuals.

A critical point of source criticism: the text now widely read was not the speech as delivered. The speech was given with only partial notes, and audience members' recollections and an unrevised transcript differ from the version published in the People's Daily on June 19, 1957, after Mao had revised it substantially. By then the Anti-Rightist reversal was underway, and the published text added criteria for distinguishing "fragrant flowers" from "poisonous weeds," including requirements that speech should help socialism and Party leadership. Students should therefore not assume that the published text reproduces what the original audience heard, and they should identify which version they are reading.

Section 67.29

The Spring 1957 Criticism Movement

In May 1957, criticism poured out in universities, newspapers, and meetings. At Peking University, students put up wall posters and held open forums, and on May 19, a "democracy wall" movement gained momentum, with speakers such as the student Lin Xiling gaining attention. Students in other cities held similar events. Teachers, writers, journalists, lawyers, scientists, and democratic-party members offered comments at meetings convened by the Party. Many criticisms were specific and practical, focusing on bureaucratism, privileges of Party members, poor management, shortages, and the unqualified appointment of Party loyalists to technical posts.

Some criticisms went further. Zhang Bojun of the China Democratic League and Luo Longji proposed institutions such as a review committee to oversee campaigns, which was read as challenging Party control. The newspaper editor Chu Anping was reported to have said that the Party regarded the country as its own possession, using a phrase often rendered "the Party's world." Some speakers questioned the Party's monopoly on power, the Soviet model, or the treatment of earlier campaigns. Students and others also raised complaints about shortages and about events in Hungary. Not all criticism was radical; most speakers concentrated on specific grievances and often affirmed support for socialism. Evidence about the range of views comes from newspapers of the time, which were themselves selectively published, from the files used later to prosecute critics, and from later memoirs.

The scale and tone of criticism surprised Party leaders. Workers' strikes and rural disturbances in 1956–1957, including withdrawals from cooperatives and some protests in factories, also fed leaders' concerns about stability, and the intellectual criticism was read in this light. The leadership became alarmed that the movement might get out of control, and internal discussion shifted from encouraging criticism to responding to it.

Section 67.30

The Anti-Rightist Campaign and the End of the Hundred Flowers

In early June 1957, the leadership reversed course. A People's Daily editorial of June 8 titled "What Is This For?" signaled that "Rightists" were attacking the Party. Mao and the Party leadership, with Deng Xiaoping responsible for organizing the campaign, directed that those who had gone beyond permissible criticism be identified and punished. The revised text of Mao's contradictions speech was published on June 19, and a nationwide Anti-Rightist Campaign followed through 1957 and into 1958. Work units, universities, and government offices were told to find a quota of Rightists, and in many places, local officials classified people to meet expected percentages.

Those labeled Rightists were publicly denounced in meetings, forced to write self-criticisms, dismissed from jobs, expelled from the Party if members, demoted, or sent to labor reform or "reeducation through labor," often in remote areas. The label affected spouses and children and could last for twenty years. Targets included professors, scientists, writers, students, journalists, officials, and members of the democratic parties, some of them well-known figures such as Zhang Bojun, Luo Longji, and Chu Anping, and many obscure individuals. Some classifications were based on a single remark or on personal disputes with local leaders.

The consequences were severe and lasting. The campaign silenced criticism, discouraged independent thought among intellectuals, strengthened Party control over universities and the press, and altered the relationship between intellectuals and the Party. It also deprived the state of the skills of many trained people. Later in the Mao era, the climate of 1957 meant officials and experts were reluctant to report problems or disagree with the leadership, a factor historians connect to the failures of reporting in the Great Leap Forward (Chapter 68), though that connection is an interpretation and not an automatic result.

Historical Evidence

Anti-Rightist Classification and Punishment Estimates

A commonly cited retrospective official total is about 550,000 people classified as Rightists. Some post-Mao tallies distinguish 552,973 people classified in 1957–1958 from 552,877 whose classifications were subsequently corrected, leaving 96 not formally corrected; the latter number should not be mistaken for the initial classification total. Most classifications were corrected after 1978, and the official account described the campaign as wrongly expanded. These precise retrospective figures remain subject to the limitations of official reporting. The classification total measures people assigned the Rightist label, not the number imprisoned, assigned to labor, or killed; these categories must be distinguished. Some scholars argue that the actual number of people affected, including those given lesser punishments and family members, was larger than the official count. Reliable numbers for deaths among those sent to labor camps are not firmly established, and some of the worst mortality occurred during the famine years that followed (Chapter 68). Students should always check whether a number refers to classification, labor assignment, imprisonment, or death, and who produced it.

Sources include official statements in the late 1970s, scholarly studies, memoirs, and local archival materials. Each has limitations: official figures may be rounded or selective, memoirs depend on memory, and archives remain restricted in some areas.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Hundred Flowers Movement End in Repression?

The question: Did the leadership plan the Hundred Flowers policy to trap critics, or did it change course because the scale of criticism was unexpected?

Interpretation A — A deliberate trap: Some observers, drawing on Mao's later remarks about "luring snakes out of their holes" (a phrase he used in internal statements after the campaign began), argue that the invitation was meant to draw out opponents for punishment.
Interpretation B — A genuine experiment that went wrong: Other scholars argue that the leadership sincerely sought constructive criticism, was surprised by the depth of hostility, and reacted to what it saw as a threat. They cite the earlier cautious policy, the internal disagreements, and the rapid change of position in early June.

Evidence: Both views rely on internal Party documents, Mao's speeches and remarks (in different versions), press coverage, and memoirs. The retrospective nature of some of the evidence makes it difficult to establish intentions at the time. Some scholars emphasize elements of both, for example that Mao's intentions shifted as events unfolded.

Why it matters: The question concerns whether the political system in the 1950s allowed genuine debate and how leaders understood criticism, and it affects interpretations of later Maoist politics.

Section 67.31

Chapter Review and Socialist Transformation Checkpoint

This chapter has asked how successful the First Five-Year Plan was, how socialist transformation changed economic and social relationships, and why the end of the period produced new political and developmental tensions. Students should answer by distinguishing five things that are easily blurred: industrial achievement (rapid growth in output and new industries), agricultural performance (slower growth under rising state demands), institutional consolidation (planning, collectivization, joint ownership, work units, and the 1954 state structure), social inequality (rural-urban divisions, class labels, and gender differences), and political repression (campaigns culminating in the Anti-Rightist Campaign).

By late 1957 there was an unresolved debate about development strategy. Leaders who favored balance and caution, including those who had urged opposition to "rash advance" in 1956, were in tension with others, including Mao, who were impatient with the pace of growth and the constraints of planning and who believed that mass mobilization could overcome shortages. The political climate after the Anti-Rightist Campaign made open disagreement riskier. These tensions form the background to Chapter 68, but students should avoid treating the later events as the necessary result of this chapter.

Chapter Summary

Between 1953 and 1957, the Chinese Communist Party moved the People's Republic from a recovering mixed economy toward a Soviet-influenced system of central planning and public ownership. The general line for the transition period set the political direction: socialist industrialization combined with the transformation of agriculture, handicrafts, and private industry and commerce. The shift from New Democracy reflected ideological commitment, the demands of the Korean War period, the availability of Soviet assistance, and concerns about rural differentiation and private capital.

The First Five-Year Plan covered 1953–1957 and was formally approved in 1955. It was built on heavy industry, supported by Soviet loans, equipment, designs, and advisers, and organized through the State Planning Commission and industrial ministries. The 156-project program was an evolving set of Soviet-assisted projects, not a count of identical factories finished in the plan years. Steel, coal, power, machinery, vehicles, and railways grew substantially in official reports, and new industrial centers appeared in the northeast and the interior. These achievements depended on Chinese labor and capital as well as Soviet help.

The costs and limits were visible. Agriculture grew much more slowly than industry, consumer goods remained scarce, and the capital-intensive strategy created few jobs. The state financed industry in large part by drawing on the agricultural surplus through unified purchase and sale of grain and agricultural taxes. Official statistics are valuable but must be handled critically, and historians debate whether the plan created a sound foundation or built-in imbalances.

Collectivization transformed the countryside. Mutual-aid teams led to elementary cooperatives, and after Mao's July 1955 intervention, enrollment accelerated so quickly that by 1956 most rural households were in cooperatives, many in advanced ones with collective ownership of land. The change altered property rights, income distribution, and the authority of cadres. Peasants experienced it variously, with enthusiasm, accommodation, and resistance, and the long-term effect on production was mixed. It should be distinguished both from land reform and from the people's communes of 1958.

The ownership of industry and commerce changed in a similar way. Private firms were incorporated as joint state-private enterprises, with owners receiving fixed interest and losing control, and artisans and shopkeepers were organized into cooperatives. State ownership, joint ownership, and collective ownership were distinct forms. The transformation was rapid and carried out amid political pressure, and it affected business owners, managers, workers, and small traders differently.

Urban life was reorganized through work units, rationing, and developing migration and registration controls. Education and science were restructured on Soviet lines to train specialists for the plan. Women gained legal rights and employment, but faced continuing inequality. The household registration system, which would later divide rural and urban populations rigidly, was still taking shape; its main national regulation dates from 1958.

The political story of 1956–1957 follows from this transformation. International shocks, domestic problems of bureaucracy, and the need for expert cooperation led the leadership to encourage criticism in the Hundred Flowers policy. Mao's February 1957 speech, later revised and published, set conditional limits, and in spring 1957 the criticism that emerged ranged from specific complaints to challenges to Party authority. In June, the leadership reversed course and launched the Anti-Rightist Campaign, in which roughly 550,000 people were officially classified as Rightists, with lasting consequences for their lives and for intellectual life.

Regional variation and frontier policy matter throughout. The northeast became the center of heavy industry, while minority regions and Tibet were treated more gradually, and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps reshaped the northwest. Experiences differed by region, class, gender, and occupation.

By the end of 1957, China had a significantly larger industrial base and a consolidated socialist economic system, and also faced imbalances and political constraints. Disagreement over the proper pace of development was unresolved, and the Anti-Rightist Campaign had made open criticism more dangerous. These conditions would influence the policies and crises of the following years, but the transformation of 1953–1957 should be understood in its own right, not as a prelude.

MGU Library Research Project

A Case Study in Socialist Transformation: One City, County, or Enterprise, 1953–1957

  1. Choose a case. Select a city (such as Anshan, Shanghai, or Wuhan), a county, or an enterprise, and state a historical question about how it changed.
  2. Gather primary sources. Use translated documents, official statistics, local gazetteers, memoirs, or oral histories, and record the creator, date, and edition of each.
  3. Gather secondary sources. Use scholarly studies of the First Five-Year Plan, collectivization, or the Hundred Flowers, and note how historians interpret the same material differently.
  4. Analyze. Identify which changes were driven by national policy, which by local conditions, and which groups gained or lost.
  5. Present. Write a 5–7 page essay or build an annotated timeline and map that distinguishes plans from outcomes and official claims from independent evidence.

Library resources: documentary collections, scholarly monographs, and reference works on the early PRC (see also Appendix L and Appendix M).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the years 1953–1957 built many of the institutions that defined economic and social life in Maoist China: planning agencies, collective farms, state and joint enterprises, work units, a state grain system, and a Party-led intellectual and educational order. They also produced real industrial growth that shaped China's later economy.

The chapter reveals recurring themes in modern Chinese history: the ambition to build a rich and strong country, the tension between state goals and the interests of households and regions, the dependence of industrialization on agricultural surplus, and the difficulty of reconciling expertise, criticism, and political authority. It also shows that outcomes depended on choices made by leaders, cadres, peasants, workers, and intellectuals, not on fixed historical laws.

The period connects China to wider Asian and global history through the Cold War alliance with the Soviet Union, the exchange of technology and people, the influence of events in Eastern Europe, and China's emerging role among postcolonial states. It also shows how a poor, large, regionally varied country sought to industrialize under particular international constraints.

Understanding this period helps students see Chinese history as diverse, contested, and unfinished. The same transformation was a source of security for some and loss for others, achievement and constraint together. Chapter 68 will examine how the next phase of Maoist policy built on these foundations and what followed.
