Part X — Revolution, Republic, and War

Japanese Invasion and the Second Sino-Japanese War

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

How did China endure eight years of full-scale war against Japan, how did invasion transform Chinese government and society, and why did victory in 1945 leave the country both internationally strengthened and internally devastated?

In the winter of 1937–1938, hundreds of students and a small number of teachers from Peking University, Tsinghua University, and Nankai University set out from Changsha on foot for Kunming, nearly two thousand kilometers away by the routes they took. Their institutions had already fled once, from the occupied north to central China, and the approach of Japanese armies forced them to flee again. The marchers crossed Hunan and Guizhou in roughly two months, carrying notebooks and surveying equipment as well as bedding. When they arrived, the three universities merged into the National Southwest Associated University, which became one of the most respected centers of learning in wartime Asia. The journey captures much of the chapter's subject: a society under invasion that did not simply collapse, but moved, improvised, and rebuilt institutions in unfamiliar places at enormous human cost.

This chapter studies the Japanese invasion of China and the war that followed. It begins in 1931 with the seizure of Manchuria, follows the erosion of Chinese sovereignty in the north through 1937, and gives its fullest attention to the full-scale war of 1937–1945, known in China as the War of Resistance against Japan (Kangri zhanzheng). The war is treated here as several overlapping histories: a military conflict between a modern imperial army and a poorly industrialized but vast country; a political contest among rival Chinese governments and movements; a colonial and occupation history in which millions lived under Japanese or Japanese-sponsored rule; a humanitarian catastrophe of massacre, flood, famine, bombing, and displacement; and a global story in which China's resistance became tied to the wider Second World War.

The war matters because it transformed almost every institution examined in the preceding chapters. It destroyed much of the coastal urban and industrial society described in Chapter 63. It ended the Nanjing Decade state-building project of Chapter 62 and forced the Nationalist government into the interior. It allowed the Chinese Communist Party, whose survival had seemed doubtful after the Long March, to expand its armies, base areas, and organizational reach. It produced some of the worst mass violence of the twentieth century in Asia. It also ended with a Chinese government seated among the victorious Allies and with the abolition of the unequal treaty privileges that had defined China's international position since the Opium Wars (Chapter 52).

What changed was the scale and character of political authority, the geography of Chinese society, and the position of China in the world. What continued included the regional variation, local power networks, and rural poverty described in earlier chapters, as well as the rivalry between the Nationalist and Communist parties. The people affected included soldiers and conscripts, peasants in occupied and unoccupied territory, refugees, industrial workers, students and professors, women subjected to sexual violence and women who sustained families and workplaces, collaborators and the officials who resisted them, and minorities and colonial subjects, including Koreans and Taiwanese, whose positions in Japan's empire were complicated and often coerced.

The evidence for this war is abundant and contested. It includes Japanese military records and Foreign Ministry documents, Nationalist and Communist archives, League of Nations and diplomatic reports, American, British, German, and Soviet records, newspapers, photographs and film, the diaries of missionaries and relief workers, court records from postwar trials, and large collections of survivor testimony. Because the war remains politically significant in China, Japan, Taiwan, Korea, and elsewhere, historians must read these sources critically. Where scholars disagree, for example over casualty figures, this chapter explains the nature of the uncertainty. This does not imply that well-documented atrocities are matters of opinion; the historical record on those is extensive.

This chapter respects the sequence of the textbook. Chapter 62 covered the Nationalist state before 1937, the Long March, and the Xi'an Incident, and Chapter 63 covered Republican society and culture in peacetime. Chapter 64 introduces only the background needed to explain how those institutions changed under war. It ends with Japan's surrender and the immediate significance of victory in 1945, leaving the postwar struggle for Chapter 65. Students should resist reading the Communist victory of 1949 back into the war: both major Chinese parties emerged from 1945 changed, and neither the outcome of the civil war nor its timing was fixed by 1937.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  4. Evaluate evidence for the Nanjing Massacre, distinguishing documented facts from contested casualty estimates and later denial.
  5. Assess the military rationale and human consequences of the 1938 Yellow River flood.
  6. Describe how the Nationalist government governed, financed, and supplied the war from Chongqing, and why its capacity declined.
  7. Compare Nationalist conventional warfare with Communist guerrilla warfare and rural government without reducing them to a contest over who fought Japan.
  8. Analyze occupation, collaboration, forced labor, sexual violence, and famine as central parts of the war's human history.
  9. Explain how China's relations with the Soviet Union, the United States, and Britain changed from 1937 to 1945.

  11. Distinguish China's enhanced diplomatic standing in 1945 from the condition of its society and economy.

Historical Context

By 1931 China had been a republic for nearly two decades, but state authority remained uneven. The Nationalist government at Nanjing, established in 1927–1928 after the Northern Expedition (Chapter 61), controlled the lower Yangtze region and nominally claimed the whole country. Regional commanders, including those in Guangxi, Shanxi, Yunnan, Sichuan, and the Northwest, retained substantial armies and fiscal autonomy. The Communist movement, driven from the cities in 1927, maintained rural base areas until the Nationalist encirclement campaigns forced the Long March (Chapter 62). The Nationalist government's priorities in the early 1930s were to suppress the Communists and consolidate central control, a policy often summarized in the formula of "first internal pacification, then resistance to external enemies." That policy was widely criticized at the time and became a source of political conflict, culminating in the Xi'an Incident of December 1936.

Japan's interests in China had deep roots. After the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 (Chapter 55), Japan acquired Taiwan, and after the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 it inherited Russian rights in southern Manchuria, including the Kwantung Leased Territory and the South Manchuria Railway. Japan's population pressures, its dependence on imported raw materials, and the economic shocks of the late 1920s and Great Depression strengthened arguments within the Japanese military and political elite for securing resources and markets on the Asian mainland. Japanese politics in the 1930s was not unified: army field officers, general staff planners, civilian ministers, business groups, and the emperor's court held different views, and officers in the field repeatedly acted ahead of, or against, instructions from Tokyo. Understanding this fragmentation helps explain why escalation in China so often took the form of faits accomplis.

Geography shaped the war. The North China Plain and the Yangtze valley, the most densely populated and economically developed regions, were reachable by rail and river from Japanese bases in the northeast and from the sea. Japan's army and navy could move quickly along railways and rivers and by coastal landings, but they could not easily control the vast countryside between the lines. China's interior, including Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, and the Northwest, was mountainous, poorly connected, and agriculturally constrained, but it was far from the coast and difficult to conquer. Retreating into this interior gave the Nationalist government survival; it also meant abandoning the regions that had supplied most of its revenue and its modern industry.

China's international position was also unusual. China had been a founding member of the League of Nations and, by 1931, was still bound by the unequal treaty system (Chapter 52): foreign concessions at Shanghai and elsewhere, extraterritoriality for foreign nationals, and foreign gunboats on Chinese rivers. It had few close allies. The Soviet Union, the United States, Britain, France, and Germany each pursued interests that did not always align with Chinese hopes. The League offered investigation and condemnation but not enforcement. This isolation forms part of the background for 1937, and the change from isolation to alliance after 1941 is a central theme of Division VI.

Finally, the war cannot be understood only by the geography of modern borders. Manchuria, Inner Mongolia, Taiwan, and the Northwest had distinct political histories (Chapters 45–48, 55). Japanese planners exploited these differences, claiming to liberate Manchus, Mongols, or local populations from Chinese rule, while Chinese nationalists framed these regions as integral parts of China. The reality involved local identities, competing claims of sovereignty, and, above all, military power. Students should keep these competing frames in view without confusing Japanese colonial propaganda with historical explanation.

Purpose: to explain why the war developed, rather than treating July 1937 as an isolated beginning.

Section 64.1

Japanese Imperial Ambitions and Northeast China

Northeast China, historically called Manchuria in foreign usage and Dongbei in Chinese, was a region of fertile plains, forests, coal, iron ore, and soybeans. Under the late Qing, it had been the Manchu homeland and, after the 1890s, a zone of intense Russian and Japanese competition. Chinese settlement there grew enormously in the early twentieth century as millions of migrants from Shandong and Hebei crossed or sailed to the northeast, making Han Chinese the large majority of the population, alongside Manchus, Mongols, Koreans, Russians, and others. By the late 1920s the region was governed by the warlord Zhang Zuolin and then his son Zhang Xueliang, who in December 1928 pledged allegiance to the Nationalist government.

Japan's position there rested on treaty rights and economic investment. The South Manchuria Railway Company operated not only trains but mines, ports, hotels, research institutes, and rail-zone towns, and a Japanese garrison, the Kwantung Army, protected the railway. Japanese businesses dominated parts of the region's trade and heavy industry. By the late 1920s, Chinese nationalism, a campaign to build competing Chinese rail lines and ports, and Nationalist claims to sovereignty threatened what many Japanese officials regarded as a vital economic and strategic position. Army planners also saw Manchuria as a buffer against the Soviet Union and as a base of raw materials for national mobilization. The assassination of Zhang Zuolin in 1928 by Kwantung Army officers, who hoped to provoke a crisis, showed how far field officers would go and how limited Tokyo's control was.

The conditions for expansion thus combined long-term strategic planning with a political culture in which radical officers, convinced that diplomacy had failed, believed that bold action would be rewarded. The global economic depression undermined Japan's civilian parties and export economy, and the Nationalist government, still consolidating its authority, was unable to match Japanese military power. Historians therefore generally explain the crisis of 1931 not as a Chinese provocation or a single conspiracy but as the product of economic anxiety, institutional indiscipline inside the Japanese military, and a regional balance of force that favored an aggressive local army.

Section 64.2

The Mukden Incident and Conquest of Manchuria

On the night of September 18, 1931, an explosion damaged a section of the South Manchuria Railway track near Mukden (modern Shenyang). Kwantung Army officers, in a plot developed by Ishiwara Kanji and Itagaki Seishiro, had staged the explosion; the damage was minor, and a train passed over the track shortly afterward. The army immediately blamed Chinese soldiers and attacked the Northeast Army barracks and Mukden itself. Within days Japanese forces had taken major cities in Liaoning and Jilin, and by early 1932 they controlled Heilongjiang and most of the region's major towns. The original cabinet in Tokyo sought to limit the conflict but was unable to restrain the army; the government that followed accepted the occupation as a fact.

Chinese response was shaped by weakness and by political calculation. Zhang Xueliang's best troops were stationed south of the Great Wall for domestic conflicts, and the Nationalist government, facing Communist insurgency and floods along the Yangtze, instructed Northeast commanders to avoid escalating the conflict while appealing to the League of Nations. Many Chinese, especially students and urban associations, protested against this policy of non-resistance, organized boycotts of Japanese goods, and demanded war. Zhang Xueliang later bore much of the blame in popular memory, although the decisions involved the Nanjing leadership. The loss of Manchuria was a national shock, removing a region of significant industry and tax revenue and putting Japanese forces within reach of Beiping and Tianjin.

The League of Nations responded by dispatching a commission of inquiry under Lord Lytton (see the Primary Source Window in 64.3). Its report in October 1932 rejected Japan's claims that the army had acted in self-defense and that Manchukuo reflected a local independence movement, yet it proposed a compromise that would have left Japan with extensive economic privileges. When the League adopted the report in February 1933, Japan walked out and formally left the League in March. No state imposed sanctions. The lesson drawn by many observers, in China and elsewhere, was that international institutions could condemn aggression without stopping it. Chinese resistance continued inside Manchuria through volunteer armies, bandit-turned-guerrilla forces, and, increasingly, Communist-led units, but it was suppressed by Japanese counterinsurgency campaigns by the mid-1930s.

Section 64.3

Manchukuo and Japanese Colonial Rule

In March 1932, Japan proclaimed the independent state of Manchukuo, with Puyi, the deposed last Qing emperor, as chief executive. In 1934 Puyi was elevated to emperor under the reign name Kangde. Japanese propaganda described Manchukuo as a multiethnic state of five peoples, Manchu, Han, Mongol, Japanese, and Korean, ruled according to a "kingly way" (wangdao) of harmony. In practice, Japanese officials, especially from the Kwantung Army and the General Affairs Board, held decisive authority at every level. Chinese and Manchu officials held office but acted on "guidance" from Japanese advisers who held the real power. Japan recognized Manchukuo in September 1932; a small number of states, including Germany and Italy later, followed, but most of the world, including the United States and the League majority, did not.

Historical Vocabulary
Puppet State

A puppet state is a government formally independent but in practice controlled by an outside power. Manchukuo had a flag, an emperor, ministries, and a constitution-like organic law, yet Japanese officers and advisers determined policy, and its armed forces and economy served Japanese strategic needs. Historians use the term analytically, not only as an insult: it focuses attention on who held real authority, while leaving room to study the many local people who worked inside such regimes for varied reasons, as discussed in Sections 64.23 and 64.24.

Manchukuo was also a colonial economy. Japanese planners promoted heavy industry through bodies such as the Manchurian Heavy Industries Development Company, expanded coal, steel, and chemical production, and built railways, hydroelectric projects, and cities such as Changchun (renamed Hsinking) as showpieces of planned modernity. Industrial output grew rapidly, and this development has led some scholars to emphasize the productive capacity Japan built in the region, which would matter to both the postwar Soviet occupation and Chinese industrial planning. But growth was oriented toward Japanese military needs, depended on resource extraction, state monopolies, and coercive labor recruitment, and often displaced Chinese farmers and workers. Opium and narcotics were also tolerated or promoted as sources of revenue and social control in some areas.

Settler colonialism was a major element. Beginning in the mid-1930s, Japanese authorities sponsored agricultural emigration, aiming eventually at a million households; in practice several hundred thousand Japanese settlers arrived, frequently on land acquired at low prices or confiscated from Chinese and Korean farmers, a source of enduring resentment. Resistance took many forms, including the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army, associated with commanders such as Yang Jingyu, who was killed in 1940. The Japanese response combined military sweeps with the relocation of villagers into "collective hamlets" to cut off guerrillas. Manchukuo's population experienced colonial rule differently by class, ethnicity, and place: Korean farmers, themselves subjects of the Japanese empire, occupied an ambiguous and often precarious position, while Chinese city dwellers, Manchu aristocrats, and Mongol nobles faced different mixtures of cooptation and coercion.

Primary Source Window

Report of the Commission of Enquiry (Lytton Report), League of Nations, October 1932

Creator: Five-member League commission chaired by Victor Bulwer-Lytton (Britain), with members from the United States, France, Germany, and Italy · Date: October 1932 · Type: International investigative report

Context: After China appealed to the League, the Council appointed a commission to investigate the Manchurian crisis. The commissioners traveled in Japan, China, and Manchuria, interviewed officials and others, and produced a lengthy report.

Paraphrase: The report concluded that Japanese military operations after September 18, 1931 could not be regarded as legitimate self-defense, and that Manchukuo had not arisen from a genuine and spontaneous independence movement but depended on Japanese military and political support. It also recognized that Japan had real treaty rights and economic interests in the region and proposed an autonomous Manchuria under Chinese sovereignty with international advisers and protections for Japan's interests.

Analysis: The report is valuable as a contemporary, relatively independent assessment and as evidence of international attitudes. Its limits are equally important. The commissioners worked under Japanese supervision in Manchuria, drew on a limited range of Chinese testimony, and were committed to a negotiated compromise rather than to enforcement. Its recommendations balanced Chinese sovereignty against Japanese privileges in ways neither party accepted. The perspectives of ordinary residents of Manchuria are largely absent.

Section 64.4

Japan's Advance into North China, 1932–1936

The seizure of Manchuria did not end Japanese ambitions. In January 1932 fighting broke out in Shanghai after a boycott campaign and an incident involving Japanese Buddhist priests, and Japanese naval forces attacked Chinese districts of the city. The Nineteenth Route Army, a Cantonese force led by Cai Tingkai, resisted for weeks and became a symbol of Chinese determination before a ceasefire in May 1932 created demilitarized zones around the city. In early 1933 Japanese forces invaded Rehe province, which was incorporated into Manchukuo, and moved south of the Great Wall. The Tanggu Truce of May 1933 established a demilitarized zone in eastern Hebei that Chinese troops could not enter, effectively accepting Japanese control of the Manchurian frontier.

Japanese strategy in North China then shifted toward "autonomy" movements: pressing local officials to separate the five northern provinces from Nanjing's control. The He–Umezu Agreement and the Qin–Doihara Agreement of mid-1935 forced Nationalist forces and party offices to withdraw from Hebei and parts of Chahar. In late 1935 Japanese-backed authorities set up the East Hebei Anti-Communist Autonomous Council, whose territory became a center of smuggling that undermined Chinese customs revenue. Nanjing negotiated, delayed, and conceded, creating the Hebei–Chahar Political Council as a compromise buffer. Chiang Kai-shek privately regarded China as unready for war and sought time to build the army, rail network, and industry, while continuing campaigns against Communist forces.

These concessions provoked intense domestic opposition. In December 1935, students in Beiping staged the December 9th demonstrations, calling for resistance and national unity, and a wave of "National Salvation" associations developed across urban China, including among women, journalists, and professionals. The Communist Party, after the Long March, shifted toward calling for an anti-Japanese united front. In late 1936, Chinese forces in Suiyuan repelled a Japanese-backed Mongol and Chinese puppet force, a victory widely celebrated. Then, in December 1936, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng detained Chiang Kai-shek at Xi'an to press for an end to the civil war, as examined in Chapter 62. By early 1937 a wary political compromise was developing, and with it a growing Japanese concern that China was becoming harder to coerce.

Section 64.5

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident and the Outbreak of Full-Scale War

By 1937 Japanese garrison forces in the Beiping–Tianjin region, stationed under rights arising from the Boxer Protocol of 1901 (Chapter 56), held night maneuvers near the Marco Polo Bridge (Lugou Bridge) at Wanping, southwest of Beiping, near a rail line connecting the city to the south. On the night of July 7, shots were fired in the dark, and a Japanese soldier was reported missing, later returning to his unit. The origin of the shots remains uncertain; historians have found no convincing evidence that either side's high command planned the incident. The Japanese demanded to search Wanping, Chinese forces of the Twenty-Ninth Army under Song Zheyuan refused, and fighting broke out and spread.

Local officials negotiated a ceasefire within days, as had happened after earlier incidents. This time the local arrangement failed because of decisions at higher levels. The Japanese cabinet and general staff debated how much force to send, and the army leadership, hoping for a quick strike to force China to concede, authorized reinforcement. On the Chinese side, Chiang Kai-shek, at Lushan in Jiangxi, announced on July 17 that China could not accept further compromise of its sovereignty, saying in effect that the point of no more concession had been reached. Meanwhile Japanese forces fought for Beiping and Tianjin in late July, and local settlement became impossible. Historians emphasize that full-scale war resulted from a series of choices on both sides, in a context where both believed the other would back down.

Students should be cautious with the label "beginning" for July 1937. For Manchuria, the war had begun six years earlier, and some Chinese historians use 1931 as the start of a fourteen-year War of Resistance. The year 1937 marks the point at which hostilities became a general war across much of China, the central government committed to resistance, and the Communist and Nationalist parties moved toward cooperation. Japanese leaders usually referred to the conflict as an "incident," a term that avoided triggering restrictions under American neutrality law and the legal consequences of declaring war.

Section 64.6

The North China Campaigns and the Fall of Beiping and Tianjin

Japan entered the war with a modern army and navy, extensive air power, mechanized units, and a developed arms industry. China's forces were numerous but unevenly equipped and trained, and many units remained loyal to regional commanders rather than to Nanjing. A small core of German-trained and German-equipped divisions was the best of the Nationalist army, supported by a weak air force and little heavy artillery. China's industrial base could not supply sustained modern war, and its fiscal and transportation systems were fragile. These imbalances explain the rapid Japanese successes of 1937 but not the outcome of the longer war.

Beiping fell at the end of July 1937, and Tianjin soon after, when the Twenty-Ninth Army withdrew after heavy fighting in which General Zhang Zizhong's units and others suffered serious losses. Japanese forces then moved along the rail lines toward Baoding, Shijiazhuang, Taiyuan, and Jinan. In Shanxi, the campaign included the Chinese victory at Pingxingguan in September, won by the Communist Eighth Route Army's 115th Division under Lin Biao in an ambush on a Japanese supply column. It was a small battle but was widely publicized as proof that Japan could be beaten. A larger Nationalist defense at Xinkou and Taiyuan, with Shanxi's Yan Xishan, ended with the fall of Taiyuan in November 1937 after heavy Chinese casualties.

The northern campaigns showed patterns that would recur. Japan could win set-piece battles and seize cities and rail corridors but lacked the numbers to garrison the countryside. Chinese forces could stop or delay columns only at serious cost. In the north, a political vacuum arose as the Nationalist state withdrew, and into it moved Japanese-sponsored administrations, collaborationist officials, and, in rural Shanxi, Hebei, and Shandong, Communist-led guerrillas, as discussed in Division IV. Japanese authorities installed the Provisional Government of the Republic of China at Beiping in December 1937.

Section 64.7

The Battle of Shanghai

Fighting broke out in Shanghai on August 13, 1937, after weeks of tension. Chiang Kai-shek chose to fight there rather than only in the north, for reasons that historians still discuss. Shanghai was China's main commercial and financial center, home to foreign concessions and press attention. Fighting there could draw foreign interest, lure Japan into battle on terrain where China held some advantages, and shift the war's axis from the northern plains, where Japanese mechanized forces were strongest, toward the Yangtze valley, nearer the Nationalist heartland. It also risked the loss of the best-trained Chinese divisions in a costly urban struggle.

The battle lasted about three months and became one of the largest of the war. Chinese divisions assaulted Japanese positions in the Hongkou area and held their ground in Luodian, Dachang, and the Suzhou Creek area against naval gunfire, bombing, and Japanese reinforcement. Civilians in the city faced bombing: on August 14 and 23, errant bombs killed large numbers of people in the International Settlement and French Concession, and the images that circulated worldwide, including the photograph of an injured infant at the South Station, shaped foreign opinion. Casualties were heavy; estimates of Chinese losses commonly exceed 200,000, including large numbers of junior officers from the best units, while Japanese casualties were in the tens of thousands. After a Japanese landing at Hangzhou Bay in early November threatened the Chinese rear, Chinese forces retreated, sometimes in disorder.

Shanghai had large strategic effects. The Soviet Union signed a non-aggression pact with China on August 21, 1937 and began military aid. Western public opinion shifted somewhat toward China, although governments mostly stayed out. The destruction of the Nationalist army's best divisions made the later war more dependent on poorly trained conscripts and regional armies, and the loss of Shanghai cost China its leading economic city and customs and tax base. At the same time, the battle upset Japanese expectations of a short campaign: Japan, which had meant to coerce Chinese concessions, now faced a prolonged war.

Section 64.8

The Fall of Nanjing and the Nanjing Massacre

After Shanghai, Japanese forces advanced quickly up the Yangtze delta toward Nanjing, the Nationalist capital, which the government had begun evacuating. The government's leadership, including Chiang Kai-shek, left, and General Tang Shengzhi was charged with a defense that was widely seen as hopeless. Japanese troops entered the city on December 13, 1937, after Chinese defenders were overwhelmed and many soldiers fled or tried to cross the Yangtze, where large numbers were killed. In the following weeks, Japanese soldiers killed civilians and disarmed soldiers, raped women on a large scale, looted, and burned parts of the city. These events are called the Nanjing Massacre or the Rape of Nanjing.

A group of foreign residents, including the German businessman John Rabe, the American missionaries Minnie Vautrin, Lewis Smythe, and John Magee, and others, organized the International Committee for the Nanjing Safety Zone. They created a refuge in the western part of the city where many tens of thousands of civilians found at least partial protection, administered food and medical relief, and documented violence in letters, diaries, and reports to Japanese and foreign diplomats. Magee filmed footage of victims in hospital. Chinese survivors, including women and men who saw relatives killed, later gave testimony to postwar tribunals and, in recent decades, to oral-history projects. Some Japanese soldiers' diaries and later memoirs also corroborate the killings.

Historical Evidence

How Historians Know About Nanjing, and What Remains Uncertain

Types of evidence. Contemporary foreign eyewitness records (the Safety Zone Committee's documents, diaries, letters, and Magee's film); diplomatic dispatches from German, American, and British officials; Japanese military records and soldiers' diaries; Chinese burial-society records and survivor testimony; and trial records from the International Military Tribunal for the Far East (Tokyo, 1946–1948) and the Nanjing war-crimes tribunal (1947). Later collections include thousands of Chinese survivor interviews and institutional archives of recorded testimony.

What is established. Japanese forces killed large numbers of Chinese prisoners and civilians in and around Nanjing over a period of weeks; there was widespread rape; there were arson and looting. These facts are supported by many independent sources, including Japanese ones, and are accepted by the great majority of historians in Japan, China, and elsewhere.

What is debated. Estimates of the number killed vary widely, depending on how the geographic area (the city proper or surrounding counties), the time period, and the categories of victims (civilians, disarmed soldiers) are defined. The Tokyo tribunal found that more than 200,000 were killed, the 1947 Nanjing tribunal arrived at a figure above 300,000, and the figure of 300,000 is the official Chinese number; many scholars in Japan and elsewhere propose lower figures, from tens of thousands to around 200,000, according to their definitions and sources. Estimates of rape victims run to tens of thousands. A small minority in Japan has denied or minimized the massacre; their claims have been rejected by mainstream historians on documentary grounds.

Limits. Wartime chaos, deliberate concealment, the destruction of Japanese records in 1945, and the political uses of the event after 1945 complicate numerical reconstruction. Uncertainty about numbers is a methodological problem, not evidence that the events did not occur.

The massacre had no single cause. Historians cite the brutality of Japanese army culture and training, the frustration of soldiers after the hard fighting at Shanghai, supply problems that encouraged looting, a command structure that tolerated or failed to restrain atrocity, and a dehumanizing view of Chinese opponents. Japanese commanders, including Prince Asaka, and General Matsui Iwane, who commanded the expedition, bear differing degrees of responsibility; Matsui was convicted and executed after the Tokyo trial. Nanjing was not the only site of large-scale violence in 1937–1938, but it became central to Chinese and international memory, and remains a contested subject in Chinese–Japanese relations.

Section 64.9

Taierzhuang, Xuzhou, and Chinese Resistance in 1938

After the fall of Nanjing, Japan sought to connect its northern and central armies along the Tianjin–Pukou railway. Chinese politics shifted toward a united resistance: the Nationalist government reorganized, the Communist armies had joined the national command, and a commander from Guangxi, Li Zongren, took charge of the Xuzhou front, which brought together units from Guangxi, Sichuan, central Nationalist divisions, and others. In March and April 1938, Japanese divisions advancing south from Shandong attacked the town of Taierzhuang, a canal town in southern Shandong. Chinese forces, including those under Sun Lianzhong and Tang Enbo, fought house to house, encircled part of the Japanese attack, and forced a retreat in early April.

Taierzhuang was a limited victory, with perhaps tens of thousands of casualties on each side, but its symbolic importance was large. It was the first major Chinese success against a Japanese division of the regular army, and it was widely celebrated at home and abroad. It encouraged the idea that China could resist effectively and showed that cooperation among regional forces could succeed under good local leadership. But Japanese reinforcements arrived, and by May the Chinese forces around Xuzhou faced encirclement. Li Zongren withdrew his troops in a skillful retreat, and Xuzhou fell on May 19, 1938. Japanese armies could take territory, but they could not destroy the Chinese armies or end the government's will to continue.

For Japanese planners, this was a strategic dilemma. In January 1938, Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro had announced that Japan would "not deal with" the Nationalist government, closing the door on negotiation after the failure of German mediation in late 1937. Without a negotiated end and without enough force to compel one, Japan faced an open-ended war on the Asian mainland, at the same time as the Soviet Union and, later, Western powers loomed as strategic competitors.

Section 64.10

The Yellow River Flood of 1938

In early June 1938, after the fall of Xuzhou, Japanese forces moved west along the Longhai railway toward Zhengzhou, a rail junction that controlled access to Wuhan and the central plains. Chinese commanders, with Chiang Kai-shek's approval, ordered the breaching of the Yellow River's southern dike near Huayuankou, north of Zhengzhou, to flood the plain in front of the advancing enemy. An earlier breach attempt at Zhaokou failed to cause serious flooding; the Huayuankou breach, around June 9, succeeded. The river poured south into Henan, Anhui, and Jiangsu, spreading across the plain and into tributary river systems.

Cause and Effect

The 1938 Yellow River Flood

Causes

Short-term: the Japanese advance toward Zhengzhou and Wuhan after Xuzhou; shortage of Chinese forces able to hold the line. Long-term: the Yellow River's dependence on dikes, which made the river a possible weapon; a tradition of military use of water in Chinese warfare; Chinese material weakness against mechanized armies.

Event

Chinese engineers and troops breached the dike near Huayuankou in early June 1938. Floodwater inundated a vast area, and the river changed its southern course, flowing into the Huai system.

Effects

Immediate: the advance on Zhengzhou was delayed by weeks or months, giving Wuhan time to prepare; many villages and some Japanese positions were flooded. Human: estimates of deaths range from roughly 400,000 to nearly 900,000, and millions were displaced. Long-term: agricultural land was ruined by silt, hunger and disease persisted, and the river was not returned to its northern course until 1947.

Historical complexity

The flood resulted from Japanese aggression and Chinese decisions at the same time. Residents were not adequately warned, and the government initially blamed the Japanese for the breach. The military gains were temporary; the civilian costs were permanent. Historians debate how much the flood saved militarily and whether the Chinese government weighed civilian costs.

The flood is a stark example of the choices total war imposed. Defenders of the decision argue that it was a desperate measure when China had few other means to stop a mechanized enemy and that it bought precious time for the Wuhan campaign and the evacuation of industry. Critics argue that its military effect was brief, that the casualties could have been reduced by warning, and that the policy reflected a willingness to impose enormous costs on peasants for tactical benefit. Both sides acknowledge that the human cost fell chiefly on rural families in a region already vulnerable to hunger. Records of the planning and the number of victims are incomplete, and statistics vary, so students should treat figures as estimates.

Section 64.11

The Battle of Wuhan and the Retreat into the Interior

From June to October 1938, the Battle of Wuhan was the largest Chinese defensive campaign to that point. Nationalist forces fought along the Yangtze, in the Dabie Mountains, and around Lake Poyang, using terrain and rivers to delay the enemy, with Soviet air support for part of the campaign. Japanese forces advanced with heavy use of naval and air power and with chemical weapons in some operations, drawing heavy casualties on both sides. Meanwhile, Japanese forces landed in Guangdong and took Guangzhou on October 21, cutting off a major supply route through Hong Kong. Wuhan fell in the last days of October.

The government had already begun retreating. In late 1937 it had moved to Chongqing and, in 1938, the wartime capital was effectively established in Sichuan. Hundreds of factories, schools, hospitals, and government offices moved west and southwest by river steamers, trucks, and carried by hand through the Yangtze gorges, an effort led in part by the National Resources Commission and civic and business groups, notably the shipping magnate Lu Zuofu, whose company ferried evacuees and machinery. Millions of civilians moved with them or independently. This "great retreat" preserved some industrial capacity and administrative continuity, although much equipment was lost.

After Wuhan the war entered a new phase. Japan held the major cities, ports, and rail lines, but not the countryside, and the Nationalist government held the interior but little of the coast. Chiang Kai-shek's government declined to surrender, and Japan's declared "new order in East Asia" of November 1938 offered terms China would not accept. In December 1938, Wang Jingwei left Chongqing and, in 1940, would lead a government under Japanese sponsorship (Section 64.24). Chinese leaders now spoke of a long war of attrition in which time and space would trade for strength, an idea also developed in Mao Zedong's 1938 lectures on protracted war.

Division IIIThe Nationalist War Effort and the Wartime State, 1938–1945

Purpose: to explain how the Nationalists continued to govern and fight after losing major cities and industrial areas, balancing endurance against coercion and declining capacity.

Section 64.12

Chongqing and the Nationalist Wartime Government

Chongqing, a hilly river port at the junction of the Yangtze and Jialing rivers, was a modest city before the war. It became the capital of Nationalist China in the interior, often called "Free China" or "Nationalist-held China," home to ministries, foreign embassies, universities, newspapers, and a swollen population of several hundred thousand to over a million. Sichuan had been controlled by regional militarists until 1935, and the arrival of the central government shifted the province's political and fiscal balance, creating friction between local elites and the outsiders who demanded grain, labor, and taxes.

The wartime regime was centered on Chiang Kai-shek, who held the post of Generalissimo and chaired the Military Affairs Commission, while also leading the Nationalist Party. The Supreme National Defense Council, set up in 1937 and later reorganized, concentrated emergency authority. In 1938 the Kuomintang created the People's Political Council, an advisory body that included members of other parties, independents, and Communists, and that gave some public voice, although it lacked real power. The party also established the Three Principles of the People Youth Corps to mobilize young people. Critics saw the regime as increasingly authoritarian: secret police and censorship expanded, and non-Nationalist parties such as the Democratic League, formed in 1941, complained of repression.

Administrative capacity was limited. The government extended its presence into provinces such as Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi, and the Northwest, but depended on negotiation with local commanders. Provinces like Yunnan under Long Yun and Guangxi under Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi retained substantial autonomy. Civil–military relations were complicated by Chiang's personal control of promotions and funds and by competing factions in the party and army. The government's achievements nonetheless deserve attention: it kept an administration and an army in the field for eight years, relocated much industry and education, built roads and airfields, and sustained diplomatic relations that eventually brought allied support.

Section 64.13

Nationalist Military Strategy and the War of Attrition

Nationalist strategy after 1938 aimed to survive, preserve strength, wear down Japan, and wait for international change. It emphasized defense in depth, the use of terrain and distance, harassing attacks, and limited counteroffensives. Chinese forces fought a series of battles around Changsha, an important rice and rail center in Hunan: in 1939, 1941–1942, and 1944 (during Ichi-Go), where Chinese commanders used elastic defense to draw Japanese columns in and counterattack. The Winter Offensive of 1939–1940 and the Battle of Zaoyi in 1940, in which General Zhang Zizhong was killed, are among other major actions. Chinese forces also fought at Kunlun Pass in Guangxi in late 1939, and Yichang and the Yangtze gorges were held to protect Chongqing.

Command and logistics were persistent problems. The Nationalist army consisted of central divisions loyal to Chiang and regional armies with varying loyalty and quality, and Chiang often distrusted regional commanders and kept the best units for strategic reserves or for watching the Communists. Troops were paid inadequately and supplied poorly; rations often came late, and the army lived partly off local communities. Ammunition and artillery were limited; Soviet supplies dwindled after 1941, and the closing of the Burma Road by Japan and its allies cut off much of the foreign aid until the opening of air supply across the Himalayas, as described in Section 64.30. These shortages shaped the type of war China could fight: a war of positional defense, withdrawal, and attrition, not of strategic offensives.

Historians disagree on how to evaluate this strategy. Supporters emphasize that China tied down large Japanese forces, perhaps a million troops, in a theater where Japan could not win, and that endurance was the core achievement. Critics argue that Chiang's reluctance to risk his best units, combined with political caution, reflected concern about the Communists and regional rivals, and that the stalemate corroded the army's morale and discipline. The war's operational history shows both: serious and costly resistance, and organizational weaknesses that Japan's 1944 offensive would expose.

Section 64.14

Soldiers, Conscription, and Military Society

The Chinese army's rank and file came overwhelmingly from the countryside. Before 1937 many soldiers were volunteers or men hired by commanders, but the war required mass mobilization. The government introduced conscription through a National Military Service Law and relied on the baojia system, in which local headmen were responsible for supplying quotas of men. Estimates suggest that around fourteen million men were conscripted during the war, although figures are uncertain. The system was widely abused. Wealthy families could pay for substitutes or bribe officials; poor households, lacking connections, supplied the men. Press-gang practices, men tied together and marched in columns, and deaths in transit from hunger and disease were common, especially in 1941–1943 and in provinces like Sichuan and Henan.

Military life was hard. Recruits were often malnourished and had little training; field hospitals and medical care were scarce; desertion was frequent. Soldiers' families lost labor in the fields and sometimes had to sell land or borrow; the government promised preferential treatment to families of servicemen, but delivery was inconsistent. Some units nonetheless fought with great determination, and the war had real patriotic content: soldiers and civilians often understood the conflict as a defense of the country. Women served as nurses and workers and in some units, and organizations such as the New Life Movement women's associations, led by Soong Meiling (Madame Chiang), aided wounded soldiers and war orphans.

The conscription system illustrates the gap between the wartime state's ambitions and its capabilities. Its coercive practices bred resentment in rural areas and contributed to a lasting distrust of central government. When retreating Chinese soldiers were disarmed by villagers during the 1944 Henan collapse, as reported by observers, they were reaping the consequences of years of requisitions and mistreatment. The long-term effects on rural attitudes toward the Nationalist state are an important factor in the postwar political situation that Chapter 65 examines. Total Chinese military deaths are commonly estimated at between three and four million, although the figure is uncertain and depends on how battlefield losses, disease, and deaths in custody are counted.

Section 64.15

The Bombing of Chongqing and Life in the Wartime Capital

Japan conducted air raids on Chinese cities from the start of the war, including Shanghai, Nanjing, Wuhan, Guangzhou, and many smaller towns, but the campaign against Chongqing from 1938 to 1943 was the longest and most systematic. Japanese army and navy aircraft flew from bases in occupied Hubei and elsewhere, hoping that terror and destruction would force surrender. The heaviest raids came in May 1939 and during the summers of 1940 and 1941, when attacks on a crowded city of wooden houses caused fires that destroyed large districts. Bombing killed and injured many thousands, with estimates of the Chongqing dead varying, and destroyed property and public buildings. Across China, bombing killed many tens of thousands of civilians.

Everyday Life and Social History

Air Raids in Chongqing

Residents learned to read warning signals, often lanterns or balloons raised on hills and then sirens, and to head for shelters dug into the rock. Rock-cut tunnels and official shelters protected some but not all; wealthy residents and officials could use better shelters, and many poor residents depended on crowded, poorly ventilated tunnels. In June 1941 a long raid trapped thousands of people in a tunnel in the Dahongzhi area, and many suffocated or were crushed; the exact number is disputed, but it was one of the worst civilian disasters of the bombing. After raids, residents rebuilt with bamboo and mud, markets reopened, and government offices moved outward to the countryside or into caves.

Daily life was shaped by shortages and by crowding. Refugees from the east, including professors and writers, lived beside Sichuan residents in cramped quarters; rents rose; food, fuel, and cloth were scarce. Teahouses became places for conversation and news, newspapers were censored, and theaters and wartime drama groups staged patriotic plays. Foreign diplomats, journalists, and relief workers lived in the city and recorded their observations, which are important sources, though they often reflect the views of a small expatriate community. Urban residents and rural migrants experienced the bombing differently according to income, housing, and access to shelters.

The bombing did not break Chinese resistance. Historians generally conclude that strategic bombing in this period, like the Blitz in Britain, hardened rather than weakened resolve, while also imposing real damage and hardship. It did, however, add to the burdens of a government already stretched by inflation, and it concentrated attention on air defense, an area in which China depended on foreign help. The experience of Chongqing, with its tunnels and fires, became a major element of wartime and later Chinese memory, and, in recent years, of local historical commemoration.

Section 64.16

The Wartime Economy, Finance, and Inflation

The war cost the Nationalist government its most productive regions. The coastal provinces, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Wuhan had supplied most of the modern industry, customs revenue, and taxes. The government and private entrepreneurs moved a significant share of machinery, estimated in the hundreds of factories, into the southwest, and the National Resources Commission, led by technocrats such as Weng Wenhao, built mines, steelworks, power plants, and oil production in the interior. The Chinese Industrial Cooperatives movement, supported by foreigners such as Rewi Alley and Chinese leaders, set up small cooperative workshops to produce goods for civilians and the army. Southwest China, previously a poor and remote region, saw growth in industry, education, and infrastructure, a legacy that endured after the war.

Finance was a severe problem. With customs revenue and the land tax from lost provinces gone, the government relied on foreign loans, new taxes, and the printing of money. In 1941 it shifted the land tax to collection in kind (grain) to secure food for troops and officials and brought grain and salt under greater state control. Collection was uneven; local officials and landlords shifted burdens onto poorer farmers, and graft was widespread. Inflation worsened every year after 1939: by the end of the war prices had risen thousands of times above prewar levels. Salaried groups such as teachers, civil servants, and soldiers suffered severely, while speculators, hoarders, and some merchants prospered. Black markets, smuggling across the front lines, and hoarding were common.

Inflation changed the political landscape. It eroded the loyalty of the urban middle class and educated groups that had supported the Nationalist government, weakened the army's cohesion, and fed criticism of corruption in government. Historians debate how far the economic collapse stemmed from avoidable policy failures, such as heavy reliance on printing money, weak tax administration, and the failure to tax wealthier groups, as opposed to unavoidable losses from invasion and blockade. Most agree that both were important, and that the Nationalists' inability to finance the war without inflation was a decisive weakness.

Section 64.17

Operation Ichi-Go and the Crisis of 1944

By 1944 Japan was losing ground in the Pacific, and American bombers based in southern China were beginning to threaten Japanese shipping and the home islands. Japanese commanders in China launched Operation Ichi-Go (also "Continent Crossing"), the largest Japanese offensive of the war, from April to December 1944. Its aims were to open a continuous rail corridor from north China to Indochina and to capture the airfields used by American forces. About half a million Japanese troops took part. The offensive began in Henan in April, where Chinese forces collapsed quickly, then moved into Hunan, where Changsha fell in June and Hengyang held out for weeks before falling in August, and in the autumn into Guangxi, taking Guilin and Liuzhou and approaching Guizhou before it stalled.

The offensive exposed the Nationalist army's decay: unpaid, underfed, poorly led troops, local hostility, and corruption in supply. In Henan, reporters and witnesses described peasants attacking retreating soldiers, a result of years of requisition and abuse. The loss of East China airfields forced the Americans to relocate, and hundreds of thousands of Chinese soldiers and civilians were killed, wounded, or displaced. The disaster provoked a political crisis inside the Nationalist government, in the Chinese press, and between Chiang and the American commander Joseph Stilwell, who demanded control over Chinese forces. In October 1944 President Roosevelt recalled Stilwell at Chiang's insistence, and Patrick Hurley and Albert Wedemeyer took over American roles.

Ichi-Go had mixed results for Japan. It achieved its territorial goals, but did not defeat China, and the new rail line could not be used effectively because of Allied air and naval power. It stretched Japanese forces thin, leaving them vulnerable to Chinese counteroffensives in 1945 and giving Communist forces opportunities to expand in the countryside behind the Japanese advance. For China, 1944 was not simply a prelude to 1945; it was a crisis that damaged the Nationalist army's prestige and strength and weakened its position vis-à-vis both the Allies and the Communists, a point discussed further in Section 64.33.

Division IVCommunist Resistance and the Strained United Front

Purpose: to examine the Communist war effort as both military resistance and political institution-building, distinct from the Nationalist regular-army war.

Section 64.18

The Second United Front and Nationalist–Communist Cooperation

After the Xi'an Incident (Chapter 62), negotiations between the two parties continued through spring 1937. After the Marco Polo Bridge Incident, the Communists declared support for national resistance, and in September 1937 the Nationalist government acknowledged the Communist declaration of cooperation, a step usually described as the founding of the Second United Front. The Communist main force, around forty to fifty thousand men after the Long March, was reorganized nominally as the Eighth Route Army (later the Eighteenth Group Army) under Zhu De and Peng Dehuai, and in southern China remaining guerrilla units were consolidated into the New Fourth Army under Ye Ting. Communist base territory in the Northwest became the Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region, officially a local government under the Republic.

The alliance was one of necessity and suspicion. The Communists accepted Nationalist leadership in name and the Three Principles of the People as a national program, while maintaining their own party, army, and territory. The Nationalists accepted Communist participation in the national war effort while seeking to limit Communist growth through restrictions on funding, supplies, and recruitment. Zhou Enlai and other representatives negotiated in Chongqing and elsewhere, and cooperation was real in some areas: in the Wuhan period, there were joint political work, propaganda, and some military coordination, and Communists participated in the People's Political Council. But mutual distrust grew as the war dragged on, as the Communists expanded behind Japanese lines, and as Nationalist forces blockaded Communist territory. The Comintern's influence also shaped Communist strategy; Wang Ming, returning from Moscow in late 1937, argued for subordinating Communist autonomy to the united front, a position Mao Zedong and others resisted.

The Second United Front therefore did not create a unified command or a shared political program. It created a framework within which two rival organizations pursued the war on different terms. Understanding this prevents a common simplification: that the war was either one of unified national resistance or one in which the parties only plotted against each other. The cooperation was real but limited, and it declined after 1939–1941, as the sections below show.

Section 64.19

Guerrilla Warfare Behind Japanese Lines

When Japanese armies took cities and rail lines in North China, the countryside between them was left lightly held. Communist units, sent into Shanxi, Hebei, Shandong, and later other provinces, moved into this space. Commanders such as Nie Rongzhen, Liu Bocheng, Deng Xiaoping, and He Long built base areas in mountain regions, including the Shanxi–Chahar–Hebei Border Region and the Taihang Mountains, recruited local volunteers, and organized village militias. They raided rail lines, ambushed convoys, gathered intelligence, and attacked small garrisons. In central China, the New Fourth Army operated in the Yangtze delta and Jiangsu–Anhui region, and in the south, smaller guerrilla units worked in Guangdong, notably the East River column.

Historical Vocabulary
Protracted War (chijiu zhan)

In lectures delivered in Yan'an in 1938, Mao Zedong argued that China could defeat a stronger Japan only through a long war in three stages: a strategic defensive during which Japan advanced, a stalemate in which guerrilla warfare grew in importance, and a final stage of Chinese counteroffensive. He rejected both the view that China would be quickly subjugated and the hope of a quick victory. The concept expressed an idea, also held in different forms by Nationalist leaders, that time and space could be traded for strength. It also integrated political mobilization with military strategy, which is why historians treat it as a political as well as military doctrine.

Guerrilla warfare was not only tactics but a social organization. Success depended on local support: food, intelligence, hiding places, and recruits. Communist cadres worked to win peasants through measures such as reductions in rent and interest and the formation of village associations of farmers, women, and youth. In some places the Communists tapped existing grievances against landlords and local strongmen; in others they worked with local elites, and their policies varied by region. Guerrilla forces also competed with Nationalist guerrilla units, local militias, secret societies, and bandit groups, and Communist expansion sometimes led to violent conflict with these. The extent of Communist control depended on Japanese strength in each locality; "base areas" and "guerrilla zones" shifted constantly.

The military significance of guerrilla warfare is debated. It tied down Japanese units, disrupted communications, and denied Japan control of the rural areas that were supposed to provide resources for its war effort. But it rarely defeated Japanese forces in set-piece engagements, and it provoked severe reprisals against civilians, as the next sections show. Its larger significance lay in building a political and organizational network across much of North China, a network that later shaped the civil war.

Section 64.20

Communist Wartime Base Areas and Rural Government

Yan'an, in northern Shaanxi, was the Communist capital from 1937 to 1947. It was a small town surrounded by loess hills, with caves for housing, and it attracted thousands of students, writers, intellectuals, and idealistic young people from across China, many traveling through Nationalist-controlled territory. Yan'an hosted the party's schools, publishing houses, and training programs, such as the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University, and foreign visitors, including journalists and, in 1944, an American observer mission, gave generally favorable accounts that shaped outside perceptions. Those reports, though valuable, often reflected carefully managed visits.

Border-region governments tried to combine mobilization with moderate social policy. The Communists adopted a "united front" approach to rural society: rather than confiscating land as in the early 1930s, they promoted reductions in rent and interest (commonly a 25 percent rent reduction), progressive taxation, and, in the "three-thirds" system, allocation of one-third of government seats to Communists, one-third to left-leaning progressives, and one-third to middle-of-the-road figures. Elections were held in some areas, with limited meaning given Communist leadership. In 1940 Mao's essay on "New Democracy" described a transitional political order that included multiple classes under Communist leadership.

Economic pressure was severe, especially after 1941, when Japanese sweeps, Nationalist blockade, and reduced Nationalist subsidies cut the border region's resources. Authorities responded with the Great Production Movement of 1942–1943, in which soldiers, officials, and students raised grain and cotton, produced cloth, and ran workshops; the 359th Brigade's land reclamation at Nanniwan became a famous example. Revenue also came from taxes and, according to scholarship using Chinese and other sources, from the production and trade of opium in some areas. Historians evaluating these base areas must balance Communist accounts of popular support and reform with evidence of coercion, material hardship, and variation between places. Border regions held perhaps close to a hundred million people by 1945 in Communist claims, a figure that should be treated as approximate.

Section 64.21

The Hundred Regiments Offensive and Japanese Counterinsurgency

In August 1940, forces of the Eighth Route Army under Peng Dehuai launched a coordinated offensive against railways, mines, and garrisons in North China, involving, in Communist accounts, around a hundred regiments, hence the name Hundred Regiments Offensive. The campaign damaged the Zhengtai (Shijiazhuang–Taiyuan) railway and the Jingxing coal mines and temporarily disrupted Japanese communications. It was celebrated as a major Communist contribution to the war and as evidence of Communist military effectiveness. It also had costs: the Japanese North China Area Army responded with intense counterinsurgency, and the offensive revealed Communist strength and positions to the enemy. In later years, some Communist leaders, including Mao, criticized Peng for the offensive.

Japanese counterinsurgency in 1941–1942, under commanders such as Okamura Yasuji, combined military sweeps with blockade lines, trenches, and walls, strongpoints, and the relocation of villagers into guarded settlements. Chinese sources term the policy of "kill all, burn all, loot all" the Three Alls (sanguang); Japanese records refer to "mopping up" and "pacification." Whether a formal order using these words existed is a matter of historical debate, but the practice of destroying villages and killing civilians suspected of supporting guerrillas is documented in Chinese testimony, Japanese soldiers' accounts, and war-crimes records. In the Jizhong plain and other regions, large areas were turned into zones of devastation; Communist base areas shrank, and the Eighth Route Army lost manpower and territory. Villages caught between guerrillas and occupiers suffered repeated violence from both sides, as raids for grain and conscription accompanied Japanese reprisals.

These events show how guerrilla war and counterinsurgency drove each other upward in violence. Rural communities had few options: to hide, flee, collaborate, or join the resistance, depending on circumstance. By 1942–1943 Communist leaders concluded that they needed to rebuild with a more cautious strategy emphasizing small-scale warfare, local militia, political work, and economic self-reliance, a shift that helped the party survive the hardest period of the war.

Section 64.22

Communist Party Consolidation and the Breakdown of Cooperation

In January 1941, in the New Fourth Army Incident (also called the Southern Anhui Incident), Nationalist forces attacked units of the New Fourth Army that were moving north across the Yangtze, after an order to leave the area south of the river. Thousands of Communist soldiers were killed or captured, the commander Ye Ting was imprisoned, and Xiang Ying was killed. Chiang Kai-shek declared the New Fourth Army disbanded. The Communists reconstituted it north of the Yangtze under Chen Yi and Liu Shaoqi's political leadership, and the incident became a symbol of Nationalist betrayal. Although the united front formally continued, the incident ended meaningful military cooperation and deepened Nationalist blockade of the Shaan-Gan-Ning region.

Within the Communist Party, the war years saw the consolidation of Mao Zedong's leadership. In 1942 the party launched the Yan'an Rectification Movement (zhengfeng), a campaign of study, criticism, and self-criticism directed against dogmatism, subjectivism, and sectarianism, targeting intellectuals and officials trained abroad or in urban settings. Mao's talks at the Yan'an Forum on Literature and Art in May 1942 required writers to serve the revolution and the masses. The campaign included persecution: the writer Wang Shiwei was criticized and later executed, and in 1943 the "Rescue Campaign" under security chief Kang Sheng, aimed at finding spies, produced coerced confessions and abuses that Mao later acknowledged as excessive. In 1945 the Seventh Party Congress confirmed Mao's leadership and wrote "Mao Zedong Thought" into the party constitution; membership had grown from around forty thousand in 1937 to roughly 1.2 million, and Communist forces to roughly a million regular troops plus militia, figures that should be treated as approximate.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Resistance War

Note: these are analytical composites synthesizing patterns found in many sources, not quotations from named individuals.

A Nationalist conscript from a poor Sichuan or Henan village: Taken by baojia headmen and marched to a training camp, often underfed and sick, he fought in a war that was fought on behalf of a distant government. Wealthy families escaped the draft. Patriotism existed, but so did resentment, and desertion was an act of both survival and protest.
A Communist guerrilla or village cadre in North China: Part of a political and military organization that provided training, purpose, and a program of rent reduction and village organization. Danger was constant, because Japanese reprisals targeted both fighters and villages. Discipline and indoctrination were intense, and the party's demands, from grain to labor, could be heavy.
A peasant household in a contested rural zone: Visited by Japanese patrols, puppet troops, Communist cadres, and Nationalist tax collectors in turn, each demanding grain or loyalty. Survival depended on avoiding the wrong allegiance at the wrong time. Families sometimes sent members to different sides as insurance.
A woman in a base area or in Free China: Her labor in farming, spinning, and household production sustained the war effort, and she might serve as nurse, teacher, activist, or factory worker. Wartime opened some new roles, but traditional expectations and wartime violence also persisted.

Note on balance: These perspectives are not equivalent in power, but they illustrate that the war was not one experience.

Historical Debate

Who Fought Japan? Nationalist and Communist Contributions

The question: Both parties claimed to be the true leader of resistance, and after 1949 the competing claims became part of political legitimacy on both sides of the Taiwan Strait. How should historians assess the relative contributions of the Nationalist regular army and Communist guerrilla forces?

Interpretation A — Nationalist forces bore the main burden of the conventional war: The Nationalist army fought nearly all of the large battles, suffered the vast majority of military casualties, held down most Japanese troops, and maintained a government in the interior. On this view, Communist contributions were limited and often conserved for postwar competition.
Interpretation B — Communist forces made a distinctive and important contribution: Communist guerrilla warfare and rural mobilization contested Japanese control of the North China countryside, made occupation costly, and preserved resistance where Nationalist forces had withdrawn. On this view, the Communist role was central to the war's character and to the political mobilization of peasants.

Evidence: Casualty figures, battle records, Japanese military archives, Nationalist and Communist documents, and foreign observers' reports. Each type has limits: official histories on both sides emphasized their own role, Japanese records record only encounters they considered significant, and foreign visitors saw limited areas.

Why it matters: The debate bears on historical memory in mainland China and Taiwan, where official narratives long differed. Many recent scholars argue that the two forces fought different kinds of wars, conventional and irregular, with different costs and effects, and that treating them as a simple competition obscures both.

Student task: What evidence would be needed to compare the military effect of guerrilla operations with the effect of conventional battles? Why is the comparison itself difficult?

Division VOccupation, Collaboration, and Civilian Survival

Purpose: to put civilians at the center of the war, treating collaboration analytically without excusing atrocity.

Section 64.23

Governing Japanese-Occupied China

Japanese occupation was not a single system. Japan established multiple regimes, varying with local conditions and shifting policy. In North China, the Provisional Government of the Republic of China, headed by Wang Kemin, was set up at Beiping in December 1937; in 1940 it was reorganized into the North China Political Council. In central China, the Reformed Government, led by Liang Hongzhi, was set up at Nanjing in March 1938. In Inner Mongolia, Prince Demchugdongrub (Demchukdongrub, known in Chinese as De Wang) headed the Mengjiang regime, which Japan promoted as a national home for Mongols. The Japanese army ran these through advisers, and after 1940 many were folded into the Wang Jingwei government. Occupation authorities in cities relied on local police, auxiliary armies, and registration systems, and in rural areas on fortified points and rail lines rather than continuous control.

Economic extraction was central. Japanese companies such as the North China Development Company and the Central China Promotion Company took over mines, railways, utilities, and factories, sometimes through seizure and sometimes through partnership with Chinese owners. Japanese-sponsored banks issued currency, such as Federal Reserve Bank notes in the north and Central Reserve Bank notes in the Nanjing regime's area, which pushed out the Nationalist currency. Occupiers also promoted opium and narcotics in some regions as a source of revenue and control. The extraction of coal, iron, cotton, and food, and the diversion of transport, caused shortages and inflation in occupied cities as well.

Everyday life under occupation involved constant calculations. Residents had to carry identity documents, bow to sentries, and pass checkpoints; schools used Japanese-approved textbooks and Japanese-language instruction; newspapers were censored. Many people simply tried to keep working and feed their families, a form of compliance not the same as support. Resistance ranged from sabotage, assassination, underground party organizing, and strikes to quiet acts such as hiding people, passing information, or refusing to teach propaganda. The same person might move between categories over time. Historians therefore avoid binary labels in favor of attention to context and pressure.

Regional and Frontier Context

One War, Many Regions

Northeast (Manchukuo): occupied from 1931, with heavy industrial colonization and settler migration; long experience of Japanese rule before 1937. North China plain: contested ground, with Japanese control of cities and rails and Communist guerrillas in the countryside. Lower Yangtze and Shanghai: China's wealthiest region, heavily damaged and occupied, with a collaborationist government from 1938 and 1940. Southwest (Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi): a refuge and rear base, transformed by migration and facing conscription, taxation, and bombing. Northwest: Shaan-Gan-Ning Border Region and Nationalist-controlled Gansu, Qinghai, and Ningxia, including Hui Muslim commanders in Nationalist service; Xinjiang under Sheng Shicai, who aligned with the Soviet Union until 1942 and then turned toward Chongqing. Inner Mongolia: Mengjiang under Japanese sponsorship. Southeast coast and Taiwan: Taiwan had been a Japanese colony since 1895 and was mobilized for Japan's war, with Taiwanese conscripted into Japanese forces; Hong Kong fell to Japan in December 1941. Tibet remained outside Japanese control, with its own relationship to Chongqing and to British India.

Section 64.24

Wang Jingwei and the Collaborationist Nanjing Government

Wang Jingwei was among the most senior figures in the Nationalist Party, a close associate of Sun Yat-sen, and long a rival of Chiang Kai-shek. He had advocated negotiation with Japan and left Chongqing in December 1938 after Prince Konoe's statement offering terms based on "good-neighborly relations, anti-Communist cooperation, and economic cooperation." In Hanoi and then Shanghai, he negotiated with Japanese representatives, and on March 30, 1940, he inaugurated a "Reorganized National Government" at Nanjing, claiming to be the legitimate continuation of the Nationalist government. Japan recognized it formally in November 1940, and it was subsequently recognized by Germany, Italy, and other Axis-aligned states.

Historical Vocabulary
Hanjian (漢奸)

The term hanjian, literally "traitor to the Han," became the standard Chinese word for collaborators with Japan. Its use was moral and political as well as legal, and after 1945 the Nationalist government prosecuted many people as hanjian, while the Communists also used the label. Historians use it carefully because the range of conduct it covered was very wide: from senior officials of puppet regimes to police, clerks, businesspeople, and ordinary people who had to deal with occupation authorities. Its use in postwar trials was often shaped by political calculation as well as justice.

The Wang regime had limited authority. Its territory consisted mostly of cities and rail corridors in the lower Yangtze region, the Japanese army retained effective control, and its Peace Army was small and often unreliable. Wang's regime supported a "New Order" ideology and a "Greater Asianism," promoted anti-communism and anti-Western propaganda, and ran an education and police system, but it never won broad legitimacy. In January 1943, as Japan sought to bolster it, the regime declared war on the United States and Britain, and Japan returned some concessions to it. Wang died of illness in Japan in November 1944, and the regime collapsed in 1945. Motives of collaborators varied widely: ideological commitment to peace or pan-Asianism, anti-communism, ambition, opportunism, fear, family protection, and coercion all appear in the sources. Explaining these motives does not excuse the regime's participation in repression and in a system that served Japanese war aims.

Section 64.25

War Crimes, Forced Labor, and Occupation Violence

Atrocities were not confined to Nanjing. Japanese forces massacred civilians in many places during and after campaigns and in counterinsurgency operations, including mass executions, burning of villages, and killing of prisoners. The use of poison gas, in violation of international agreements, is documented in Japanese and Chinese records. Aerial bombing of cities killed large numbers. Japan also took Chinese prisoners and civilians for forced labor in mines, construction, and fortifications in Manchukuo, North China, and, from 1943–1944, in Japan itself, where tens of thousands of Chinese laborers were shipped and thousands died; the Hanaoka incident of June 1945, in which Chinese laborers at a mine in northern Japan rose in revolt and many were killed, became a notable example.

Sexual violence was widespread. In addition to mass rape in the course of conquest, the Japanese military established "comfort stations" throughout occupied territories, to which women were coerced or deceived. Victims came from Korea, China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Indonesia, and other places, and included some Japanese women. Estimates of the total number of women range widely, from tens of thousands up to higher figures that remain debated; Chinese women formed a large part of those in stations in China. Survivors began to speak publicly in the early 1990s, and their testimony, with Japanese military documents and postwar investigations, forms the basis of the historical record. Questions of responsibility, apology, and compensation remain disputed in the politics of Japan and its neighbors, but the existence of the system is documented.

Japan also developed biological and chemical warfare programs. Unit 731, headquartered at Pingfang near Harbin in Manchukuo and led by Ishii Shiro, conducted lethal experiments on prisoners, including Chinese, Russians, Koreans, and others, and tested weapons. Japanese forces used plague-infected fleas and other agents against Chinese cities, including Ningbo in 1940 and Changde in 1941, and during the Zhejiang–Jiangxi campaign of 1942, with deaths estimated in the tens of thousands or more, though numbers are uncertain. After the war, many involved were not prosecuted by the United States, which wanted their data. Sources include Japanese trial records from the 1949 Khabarovsk proceedings, Chinese archives, survivor testimony, and later scholarship. These topics remain sensitive, and students should note both the strength of evidence for the main facts and the uncertainty of numbers.

Section 64.26

Refugees, Displacement, Education, and Cultural Survival

The war displaced an extraordinary number of people. Estimates of those who fled their homes at least temporarily run to tens of millions, with some scholars suggesting figures of eighty to ninety million or more; precise counts are impossible. People moved by rail, boat, cart, and on foot, from the coast and the north toward the southwest and northwest, and within regions away from front lines and flood zones. Refugees faced hunger, disease, bandits, exploitation by landlords and merchants, and the hostility of host communities competing for scarce resources. Many never returned. Others settled permanently in cities such as Chongqing, Kunming, and Guiyang, changing the demography and culture of the southwest.

Everyday Life and Social History

Refugee Families and Displaced Universities

More than a hundred institutions of higher education relocated, often several times. National Southwest Associated University in Kunming, formed from Peking, Tsinghua, and Nankai, operated in makeshift buildings with thatched roofs and borrowed labs, yet educated leading scientists, writers, and later public figures. Other universities moved to places such as Leshan, Zunyi, and Chengdu. Professors' salaries lost value to inflation, and many taught while farming or selling possessions. Students lived in crowded dormitories, studied by oil lamp, and sometimes joined protests against corruption or the government. Refugee families often split: men left for service or work, women and children traveled with relatives or took jobs as nurses, teachers, domestic workers, and factory laborers. Orphans were gathered by relief organizations, including the Chinese Industrial Cooperatives, the China Defense League led by Soong Ching-ling, and missionary and international groups. Source materials include diaries, memoirs, school records, and relief reports, which tend to favor the literate and the relatively well-connected.

Cultural life continued under severe conditions. Writers such as Lao She, Mao Dun, Ba Jin, and Guo Moruo produced novels, plays, essays, and poems in Chongqing, Guilin, and Yan'an, with debates over the role of literature in national resistance. Wartime drama, woodcut prints, cartoons, and songs mobilized popular opinion, and journalists, from the Dagong Bao in Chongqing to foreign correspondents, documented the war. Scientists and engineers moved to the interior and conducted research; the Academia Sinica and other institutions relocated. Wartime scholarship in anthropology, geology, and history, in regions like Yunnan and Sichuan, expanded knowledge of the southwest. Cultural production was shaped by censorship and political pressure in both Nationalist and Communist areas, and in occupied cities, writers faced pressure to cooperate, to go silent, or to flee.

Section 64.27

Famine, Rural Devastation, and the Human Cost of War

War brought hunger across China. Flood, drought, locusts, requisitions, the disruption of trade, and the destruction of crops and transport interacted. The most severe famine occurred in Henan in 1942–1943, after drought and locust plagues struck a province that had already been devastated by the 1938 flood, been a major battleground, and contributed heavily to army grain requirements. Peasants ate leaves, bark, and seed grain, sold land, livestock, and children, and fled in large numbers, many toward Shaanxi. Estimates of deaths range from about 1.5 to 3 million, and millions more became refugees. The government continued to collect taxes in kind and requisitions in many places, and relief was inadequate and unevenly distributed. Local officials and army units sometimes diverted supplies; some local elites hoarded grain.

Hunger and disease were also gendered and generational. Women and children were often most vulnerable: families sold daughters, widows and abandoned wives struggled to keep land, and malnutrition and epidemic disease such as cholera, typhus, and malaria killed many. Women also sustained farms, took on men's labor, cared for the sick, and created networks of mutual aid. Ecological effects accumulated: flooded fields in the Yellow River plain, deforestation, soil erosion, and the destruction of irrigation works reduced productivity for years. Scholars estimate Chinese deaths in the war, military and civilian, at somewhere between about fourteen and twenty million or more, with civilians the large majority; the figure of 35 million casualties, including wounded, is officially cited in China. These numbers differ because of different definitions and sources, but all indicate a catastrophe on a vast scale.

Division VIChina, the Global War, and Victory, 1937–1945

Purpose: to establish China's importance in the wider Allied victory while explaining the difficulties of its relationships with the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union.

Section 64.28

Foreign Assistance and Diplomacy Before Pearl Harbor

From 1937 to 1941 China fought largely without major allies. The first substantial help came from the Soviet Union, which, after the August 1937 non-aggression pact, supplied aircraft, artillery, and vehicles on credit, sent advisers, and for a time sent pilots; Soviet motives combined concern about Japan's threat to the Soviet Far East with ideological interest and a desire to keep Japan engaged in China. Soviet aid declined after the Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact of April 1941 and the German invasion of the Soviet Union that summer. Germany had previously provided military advisers and trade to China, but its alignment with Japan led to the withdrawal of advisers in 1938 and recognition of Manchukuo, and the Tripartite Pact of 1940 made the Axis opposition explicit.

The United States and Britain were sympathetic but cautious. American public opinion generally favored China, missionaries and journalists shaped views, and Roosevelt condemned aggression, but neutrality laws, isolationist sentiment, and concern about Japan limited action. The Panay incident of December 1937, when Japanese aircraft sank an American gunboat on the Yangtze, was settled diplomatically. From 1938 the United States extended modest loans to China, such as those backed by tung oil, and in 1939 gave notice that it would end its commercial treaty with Japan; larger loans followed in 1940. Britain opened the Burma Road in 1938 as a supply line from Rangoon to Kunming, built by Chinese laborers, but, under Japanese pressure in 1940 when Britain was fighting Germany, closed it for three months. After Japan occupied northern French Indochina in 1940 and southern Indochina in 1941, the United States tightened economic sanctions and in 1941 extended Lend-Lease aid to China.

Chinese diplomacy was active in these years. Chinese envoys, including Hu Shih as ambassador to Washington from 1938, T. V. Soong as financial negotiator, and Wellington Koo in Europe, sought loans, arms, and moral support, and the Chinese government and its supporters lobbied the American public through organizations such as the American Committee for Non-Participation in Japanese Aggression. In 1941 the American Volunteer Group, the "Flying Tigers," organized by Claire Chennault with American pilots in Chinese service, began operations from Kunming and Burma, a project that symbolized growing cooperation. Foreign assistance up to 1941 was not decisive, but it helped China survive, and Japan's inability to end the war contributed to its decision to expand the conflict into Southeast Asia.

Section 64.29

China Becomes a Major Allied Power

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor and on British and Dutch territories in December 1941 turned the Sino-Japanese conflict into part of a global war. Chiang Kai-shek's government formally declared war on Japan, Germany, and Italy on December 9, 1941, after more than four years of undeclared war. Chinese leaders were relieved, since they now had allies, but they also recognized that Allied strategy emphasized the defeat of Germany first, which would limit resources for China. Hong Kong fell to Japan on December 25, 1941, and Japanese forces swept through Malaya, Singapore, the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies, and Burma, closing the Burma Road by mid-1942.

In January 1942 Chiang was named Supreme Commander of the Allied forces in the China Theater, which included parts of Indochina and Thailand, and American General Joseph Stilwell became his chief of staff, an arrangement that seemed to put China on an equal footing but whose meaning was contested. Roosevelt, with the idea of a postwar order in which China would be one of the "four policemen," treated China as a great power in diplomacy, while many American and British military planners doubted its military value. The strategic contribution of China was real: its resistance kept large Japanese forces on the Asian mainland, possibly over a million troops, which could not be used elsewhere. It also offered air bases for strikes against Japanese shipping and, potentially, the home islands.

The alliance was also fraught with tension. Chinese leaders felt neglected and resented the low priority given to China in lend-lease allocation; American officials complained about corruption, hoarding, and Chiang's reluctance to commit troops to offensives. Chiang wanted air power and supplies, and believed the Americans underestimated Chinese conditions; Stilwell wanted to reform and retrain Chinese forces and to use them aggressively. Cultural misunderstanding and personal antipathies compounded policy differences. Understanding these conflicts requires recognizing that each side held legitimate concerns: China's resource constraints and political calculations, and American desire for effective use of aid and a successful campaign against Japan.

China and the Wider World

China as Actor, Not Only Object

China was not merely a recipient of foreign aid. Its government negotiated loans and treaties, its soldiers fought in Burma and India, and its diplomats shaped the language of Allied war aims, including the abolition of extraterritoriality and the formal return of lost territories. Chinese communities overseas, in Southeast Asia, the United States, and elsewhere, raised funds and sent supplies, and thousands of overseas Chinese volunteered as drivers and mechanics for the Burma Road. Within Asia, Chinese resistance inspired nationalists in Korea, whose Provisional Government operated in Chongqing, and linked to the wider decolonization movements that followed the war. At the same time, Japan's "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" propaganda sought to present its conquests as liberation from Western imperialism, and China's experience was a counterexample of what Japanese rule meant in practice.

Section 64.30

Burma, the Hump, and Allied Military Cooperation

Control of supply routes was crucial. After Japan's conquest of Burma in early 1942, the Burma Road was cut. The Chinese Expeditionary Force, sent into Burma in early 1942 to help the British, fought at Toungoo and other locations but, after the collapse of the Allied position, was forced to retreat. One group retreated into Yunnan; another, with Stilwell, marched out on foot through the jungle to India in May 1942, losing many men to disease and hunger. Stilwell, bitter after the defeat, then built a new Chinese force in India. Chinese troops evacuated to Ramgarh in India were trained and equipped by Americans (known as X-Force), and a second force in Yunnan (Y-Force) was also trained, a program that reflected American hopes for reforming Chinese forces.

Meanwhile, supplies reached China by air over the eastern Himalayas, a route known as "the Hump." Starting in 1942, American and Chinese transport pilots flew from bases in Assam, India, to Kunming and other airfields, over high mountains and in difficult weather. The airlift transported hundreds of thousands of tons of fuel, ammunition, and equipment by the war's end, with considerable losses of aircraft and crews. Chennault's Fourteenth Air Force, successor to the Flying Tigers, flew from Chinese bases against Japanese targets and shipping. The Ledo Road, built by American engineers and local laborers, including Chinese, India, and Burmese workers, from Ledo in Assam toward Burma, was completed in early 1945 as Allied forces cleared northern Burma, with Chinese troops taking part in operations around Myitkyina and the Salween.

Disagreements shaped these operations. Stilwell wanted priority for the reconquest of Burma and the retraining of Chinese armies, while Chennault argued that a smaller air campaign could damage Japan faster and at lower cost. Chiang tended to support Chennault's view and resisted giving Stilwell command of Chinese forces. After the disaster of Ichi-Go and a confrontation over command, Stilwell was recalled in October 1944. The Burma and Hump operations illustrate both the possibilities and the strains of the Sino-American partnership: they produced real military results, helped keep China in the war, and left both sides with grievances that persisted into postwar politics.

Section 64.31

Wartime Diplomacy and China's International Status

On January 11, 1943, the United States and Britain signed treaties with China relinquishing extraterritorial rights and related privileges in China, ending the system of unequal treaties that dated from the Opium Wars (Chapter 52). The Chinese government and public welcomed these treaties as the long-sought end of "national humiliation," although some privileges, such as Hong Kong's status and British Kowloon, were not resolved. In December 1943 the United States repealed the Chinese Exclusion Acts, though keeping tight immigration quotas. These measures were partly symbolic and partly practical, intended to strengthen Chinese morale and counter Japanese propaganda about Western imperialism.

In November 1943 Chiang Kai-shek, accompanied by Soong Meiling, met Roosevelt and Churchill in Cairo. The meeting, which took place before the Tehran Conference with Stalin, was Chiang's only wartime summit with the other two leaders. Its public statement, issued on December 1, set out Allied aims against Japan, including stripping Japan of Pacific islands taken since 1914 and the return of territories taken from China.

Primary Source Window

Analysis: The declaration shows that by 1943 the Allies were willing to commit publicly to restoring Chinese sovereignty over Taiwan and Manchuria. It is a statement of intention, not a treaty: it was a press release, with no single signed text, and later disputes among governments, including debates in the postwar period about its legal force and the status of Taiwan, rely on interpreting it alongside the Potsdam Declaration and the Japanese surrender. Chinese and American records of the conference, which show that Chiang's other requests were not all accepted, are essential for understanding its limits. The perspective of Taiwan's residents, Koreans, and Manchurian populations was not part of the conference.

China was also part of planning for the postwar order. Representatives took part in the Dumbarton Oaks talks of 1944 that sketched the structure of the United Nations, and China was designated one of the permanent members of the Security Council. China was a founding signatory of the UN Charter at San Francisco in June 1945. At the Yalta Conference in February 1945, however, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin agreed in secret on Soviet entry into the war against Japan in return for Soviet rights in Manchuria, including Port Arthur and the Manchurian railways, without Chinese participation; the terms were later incorporated in a Sino-Soviet treaty in August 1945. China thus gained status as a great power on paper while remaining subject to decisions made by stronger states, a pattern students should keep in mind in Chapter 65.

Section 64.32

The Defeat of Japan and the End of the War in 1945

By 1945 Japan was losing the Pacific war: American forces had taken the Philippines and Okinawa, bombed Japanese cities heavily, and blockaded its shipping. In China, Japanese forces still held large areas, but the Chinese army, with American training and equipment, was recovering from the defeats of 1944 and launched offensives in Hunan and Guangxi in spring 1945. On August 6 and 9, American atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. On August 8–9, the Soviet Union declared war and attacked the Japanese Kwantung Army in Manchuria with overwhelming force. Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's surrender on August 15. Japan signed the formal surrender on September 2, aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, and on September 9 General Okamura Yasuji surrendered Japanese forces in China to General He Yingqin at Nanjing.

Historians debate the relative importance of the atomic bombs, Soviet entry, and the existing naval and economic collapse in producing Japan's surrender, and, for China, the importance of the long attrition on the mainland. What is clear is that the sudden end of the war found China unprepared for the situation that followed. Japanese forces were widely dispersed, and the question of who would accept their surrender, and thus control cities and weapons, became a major political issue. Nationalist forces, with American air and sea transport, moved to take over major cities; Communist forces moved into northern rural areas and Manchuria, and the Soviet army in Manchuria removed industrial equipment as war booty and delayed its withdrawal. Taiwan was returned to Chinese administration in October 1945 after fifty years of Japanese rule, under Governor-General Chen Yi, an event with complicated consequences discussed in Chapter 65.

Celebrations in Chongqing, Kunming, and other cities were enormous, but the sense of victory was mixed with exhaustion and uncertainty. Millions of refugees wished to return home, but many homes, farms, and rail lines were destroyed. Prices were inflated, and trust between the government and citizens had been strained. The Japanese settlers in Manchuria, many of them women and children, faced chaos and death in the Soviet occupation and the journey home, while Chinese families took in or adopted some of the orphans left behind. The Chinese casualties of war, with millions dead and tens of millions displaced, and the immense property destruction made recovery a monumental task, even as China held an exalted diplomatic position as one of the victorious Allies.

Chapter Review and War of Resistance Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the Second Sino-Japanese War transformed China through military devastation, occupation, mass displacement, economic disruption, and prolonged resistance. The Nationalist government survived the loss of its capital and its most productive regions and, from the interior, helped bring China into the Allied camp; the Communist movement expanded its rural organization; Japanese occupation inflicted violence, forced labor, and deprivation, but also generated a wide variety of responses; and by 1945 China was both victorious and ruined. The principal analytical task for students is to explain how a country could suffer such losses while emerging with a stronger diplomatic position.

Continuity and Change Over Time

China Before and After the War

What Changed

  • The coastal, urban economy of the Nanjing Decade was shattered, and the southwest and Northwest gained population, industry, and schools.
  • China's international status changed from a semi-colonial state under unequal treaties to a victorious Allied power and a UN founding member, though dependent on foreign aid.
  • The Communist Party grew from a small, embattled group into a mass organization with armies and administrative experience in rural areas.
  • The Nationalist government's fiscal capacity, army cohesion, and social support were seriously weakened, particularly among the urban middle class and peasants burdened by requisitions.
  • Mass displacement and mass death changed local societies, and the memory of atrocity became part of national identity.

What Stayed the Same

  • Regional diversity and the importance of local elites and power networks, even under wartime centralization.
  • Rural poverty, landlord–tenant relations, and the vulnerability of villages to flood, famine, and tax pressure.
  • The rivalry between the Nationalist and Communist parties over the political direction of the country.
  • The Japanese presence in Chinese history as both an imperial power and a source of cultural influence, memory, and conflict.

Who experienced change most directly: refugees, rural villagers in contested areas, urban salaried workers, and women in occupied zones. Why it matters: the wartime changes shaped the balance of forces and expectations of 1945–1949 without determining their outcome.

Historical Debate

How Did the War Reshape the Prospects of the Nationalists and Communists?

The question: The Communist victory in 1949 occurred only four years after the war ended. Did the war make that outcome likely, or was it one of several possible outcomes?

Interpretation A — The war decisively favored the Communists: Nationalist losses of territory and revenue, inflation, conscription abuses, and army demoralization alienated many Chinese, while the Communists built organizations, armies, and rural support. On this view, the war created the conditions for the Nationalist collapse.
Interpretation B — The outcome remained open: The Nationalists emerged in 1945 with international recognition, a large army with American equipment, control of major cities, and wide popular goodwill as victors, while the Communists were still militarily weaker and geographically limited. On this view, the war weakened both sides, and the civil war's course depended on postwar decisions, including in Manchuria, on economic policy, and on foreign involvement.

Evidence: Party membership and army figures, economic data, accounts by Chinese and foreign observers, archival documents from both parties and from the United States and Soviet Union, and local studies of rural communities. Local studies show great variation between regions.

Why it matters: The debate concerns how historians avoid teleology: knowing the outcome can make earlier developments appear to point inevitably toward it.

Student task: Which evidence supports each view, and what kind of evidence from 1945 itself, not from later outcomes, would be most useful in assessing it?

Students should be able to compare the wartime strategies of the two movements. The Nationalists sought to preserve a state and army while relying on foreign support and a long-term strategy of attrition; they governed through central ministries, regional compromises, and coercive extraction. The Communists, with a smaller army and a base in rural areas, combined guerrilla war with village-level organization, party discipline, and an ideology of mass mobilization. Neither strategy was costless or purely successful, and both movements used violence against internal critics and opponents. Both emerged from 1945 altered, and the struggle that followed is the subject of Chapter 65.

Chapter Summary

Between 1931 and 1945, China experienced one of the most destructive wars in its history. The conflict began with Japan's seizure of Manchuria, a region where Japan had long held economic and strategic interests and where army officers, acting beyond the control of Tokyo, created a pretext for conquest. The League of Nations condemned Japan but could not enforce its decision, and Manchukuo became a Japanese-controlled state that served as a model of colonial rule and resource extraction.

Japanese pressure on North China continued through the mid-1930s, using truces, local agreements, and autonomy movements to erode Chinese sovereignty, while Chinese public opinion increasingly demanded resistance. The Marco Polo Bridge Incident of July 1937 began as a local clash, but decisions by leaders on both sides made it the start of full-scale war. The early campaigns, at Beiping and Tianjin, Shanghai, and Nanjing, were marked by Japanese military superiority, heavy Chinese casualties, and the atrocities at Nanjing, which are documented by multiple kinds of evidence even though numerical estimates remain debated.

China's defeat in the first campaigns did not produce surrender. Chinese victories at Taierzhuang, the long defense of Wuhan, and the decision to flood the Yellow River plain delayed Japan's advance but at great human cost, and the government retreated to Chongqing, relocating industry, schools, and administration to the southwest. Japan, lacking the resources to conquer the whole country and unable to negotiate an end to the war, was drawn into a prolonged conflict.

The Nationalist wartime state survived through improvisation and coercion. It built an interior economy, conducted a strategy of attrition, and conscripted millions of men through a system that burdened the poor, while inflation and heavy taxation eroded its support among urban salaried groups and peasants. Bombing of Chongqing and other cities killed many civilians, and the 1944 Ichi-Go offensive exposed the army's weaknesses and strained relations with the United States.

The Communists, formally allied with the Nationalists in the Second United Front, expanded their armies and base areas behind Japanese lines. They combined guerrilla warfare, rural reform, and party-building, and they faced Japanese counterinsurgency and Nationalist blockade. The New Fourth Army Incident of 1941 revealed the limits of cooperation, and the Yan'an Rectification Movement consolidated Mao Zedong's leadership while involving coercion and persecution. The two forces fought different kinds of wars, and the question of relative contribution remains a subject of historical debate.

Occupation shaped the lives of tens of millions. Japanese-sponsored governments in Beiping, Nanjing, and Inner Mongolia, and the Wang Jingwei regime from 1940, ruled in cooperation with Chinese officials whose motives varied and whose authority was limited. Occupation brought economic extraction, forced labor, massacre, sexual enslavement, biological warfare, and repression, but also strategies of survival, evasion, and resistance. Refugees fled in vast numbers, universities and cultural institutions relocated, and famine, especially in Henan in 1942–1943, killed millions.

Diplomatically, China moved from near isolation to alliance. Soviet aid helped early on, American and British support grew in 1940–1941, and after Pearl Harbor China became a formal Allied power. Burma, the Hump airlift, and the Sino-American partnership produced both cooperation and conflict. The 1943 treaties ended extraterritoriality, the Cairo Declaration promised the return of territories taken by Japan, and China became a founding member of the United Nations, even as decisions at Yalta and elsewhere showed the limits of its influence.

Japan's surrender in August–September 1945 ended the war in China, but not the country's crisis. The war left millions dead, tens of millions displaced, the economy distorted, and the central state weakened, while also giving the Communist Party a mass base and army. Students should hold two truths together: China was a victor with international standing, and China was a society devastated by war.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, the war was a turning point in several ways. It changed the geography of Chinese society, advanced the integration of the southwest, ended the unequal treaty system, and established memories of invasion and resistance that continue to shape politics and international relations. It also prepared the terrain on which the civil war of Chapter 65 would be fought, without making its result inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

The Second Sino-Japanese War is central to modern Chinese history because it changed the conditions under which every later political choice was made. It destroyed much of the urban and industrial society of the Republican era, forced millions into new regions, and left a legacy of memory that remains important to national identity and to relations between China, Japan, and other societies. It also ended the unequal treaty regime and created a new international standing for China, even as the country's domestic foundations were severely damaged.

The chapter reveals larger themes of this textbook: the interaction of state power and local society, the importance of regional variation, the role of violence and coercion alongside creativity and endurance, and the difficulty of drawing simple lines between resistance and collaboration. It shows Chinese actors making hard decisions under extraordinary pressure, and it shows ordinary people sustaining households, schools, farms, and communities in conditions of danger and hunger.

The war also connects China to wider Asian and global history. It was part of Japan's imperial expansion, an early front of the Second World War, and a setting for the transformation of Allied diplomacy, colonial empires, and postwar order. Chinese resistance shaped Japanese strategy, Allied planning, and the politics of Korea, Taiwan, and Southeast Asia, and the war's end introduced the Cold War in East Asia.

Students should leave the chapter with a sense of unfinished history. In 1945 China's future was uncertain; the war had changed the balance of forces without settling it. Chapter 65 takes up the failed negotiations, civil war, and Communist victory, but does so as a new history with its own contingencies, not as the inevitable conclusion of the war years.
