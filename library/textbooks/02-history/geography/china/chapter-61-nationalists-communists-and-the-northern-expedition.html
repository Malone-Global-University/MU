Part X — Revolution, Republic, and War
61

Nationalists, Communists, and the Northern Expedition

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1927, two revolutionary movements that had marched north together through half of China turned their weapons on one another. How rival organizations came to cooperate, why that cooperation achieved so much, and why it ended in bloodshed is the subject of this chapter.

On the morning of 21 March 1927, the sound of whistles, gongs, and gunfire spread across the working-class districts of Shanghai. Several hundred thousand workers, by the estimates of organizers, put down their tools in a general strike. Armed picket corps, most of them equipped with a motley assortment of pistols and captured rifles, attacked police stations and the North Station of the railway, while the warlord army that had controlled the city, that of Sun Chuanfang and his subordinate Bi Shucheng, crumbled under pressure from the south. Within a day the National Revolutionary Army, which had set out from Guangdong the previous summer, entered a city whose workers had already seized much of it. Three weeks later, on 12 April, many of those same workers were being disarmed, arrested, and shot by men who, officially, belonged to the same revolution. The two scenes, only twenty-two days apart, frame the central problem of Chapter 61.

This chapter studies the formation and interaction of two organized revolutionary movements, the Chinese Nationalist Party (Guomindang, or GMD) and the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), between roughly 1921 and 1928. It examines how the intellectual ferment of the New Culture and May Fourth movements (Chapter 60) gave way to new forms of political organization; how the Nationalists under Sun Yat-sen and the Communists under Chen Duxiu came to cooperate in the First United Front with the assistance of Soviet Russia and the Communist International; how a party-led army was built at the Whampoa Military Academy; how workers, peasants, students, and women were drawn into politics; how the Northern Expedition of 1926–1928 overthrew or neutralized the principal military coalitions in the Yangtze valley and the north; and how, in 1927, the revolutionary coalition shattered in a series of purges, ruptures, and massacres.

The chapter matters for several reasons. It explains the origin of the two parties that would dominate Chinese politics for the rest of the twentieth century, one governing mainland China from 1949 and the other, after 1949, the government of Taiwan. It shows how the Republic of China moved from the fragmented politics of the warlord era (Chapter 59) toward a new, if incomplete, national government centered at Nanjing. It demonstrates how military organization, mass mobilization, and foreign assistance could reshape a political landscape in a few years. And it poses, in an unusually sharp form, questions of historical explanation: Was the 1927 split the inevitable result of incompatible goals, or the contingent product of particular decisions, personalities, and pressures?

Much changed in this period. In 1920 China had no national government commanding broad obedience, and neither the Nationalists nor the Communists controlled a disciplined army. By mid-1928 a Nationalist government at Nanjing was recognized, with varying degrees of enthusiasm, by most foreign powers and exercised influence over much of the country, even though regional militarists retained armies and the Communists had been driven underground and into remote rural areas. A political culture of mass mobilization, anti-imperialist protest, and party discipline had taken root, and the idea that political legitimacy could derive from a revolutionary party, not simply from an emperor, a constitution, or a general, had become widely accepted.

Much also continued. Regional militarism, foreign extraterritorial privilege, deep rural inequality, and weak fiscal capacity persisted through the revolution and beyond it. Many militarists simply changed banners. The Nationalist victory was real, but it was a victory of coalition-building and military pressure that left social conflicts unresolved and some of them aggravated. The people affected were not confined to party leaders and generals. Shanghai factory workers, Guangdong tenant farmers, Hunan village schoolteachers, women activists, merchants who funded or feared the new regime, conscripted soldiers, and foreign residents in the treaty ports all experienced the revolution differently, and often painfully.

Historians understand this period through a mixture of sources. Party congress resolutions, manifestos, and organizational reports; Comintern and Soviet diplomatic documents opened to scholars after 1991; diaries and memoirs of participants such as Soviet adviser Mikhail Borodin’s associates and Chinese party members; the Shanghai Municipal Police files; contemporary newspapers; foreign consular reports; and the field investigations of activists such as Mao Zedong and Peng Pai all provide evidence. Each has limits. Participants wrote to persuade, to justify, or, in later decades, to fit a retrospective political narrative, and the loser in each factional dispute often had less opportunity to be heard. Reading these sources critically is a central skill of this chapter.

Finally, the chapter connects China to the wider world. The revolution unfolded in the shadow of the Russian Revolution, of Stalin’s rise to power and the Soviet debates over how to treat colonial and semi-colonial nationalism, of British, Japanese, and American interests in the treaty ports, and of an Asia-wide wave of anti-colonial politics. It was a Chinese revolution, driven by Chinese choices, but it was never only Chinese in its setting or its consequences.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the intellectual and political currents of the New Culture and May Fourth eras contributed to the formation of organized revolutionary parties.
  2. Compare the origins, social bases, organizational methods, and objectives of the Guomindang and the Chinese Communist Party in the early 1920s.
  3. Analyze the roles of Soviet Russia and the Comintern in Chinese revolutionary politics while recognizing the agency of Chinese participants.
  4. Distinguish cooperation within the First United Front from organizational merger, and explain why the alliance was interpreted differently by its participants.
  5. Describe the establishment and functions of the Whampoa Military Academy and the National Revolutionary Army, and assess the concept of the party-army.
  6. Trace the development of labor and peasant movements, including the May Thirtieth Movement and the Canton–Hong Kong strike, and explain their social and political consequences.
  7. Evaluate the experiences of women, students, workers, peasants, merchants, landlords, and officers within the revolutionary movement.
  8. Reconstruct the course of the Northern Expedition from 1926 to 1928, using maps to connect military operations to regional political and economic conditions.
  9. Differentiate the stages of the 1927 rupture: Chiang Kai-shek’s April purge, the competing governments at Nanjing and Wuhan, and the later Wuhan break with the Communists.
  10. Interpret primary sources from the period with attention to authorship, purpose, and perspective.
  11. Assess competing historical interpretations of why the First United Front collapsed.
  12. Distinguish nominal national reunification from effective state consolidation by 1928.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did many Chinese activists after 1919 turn from cultural criticism toward organized party politics and military action?
  2. What did the Nationalists and Communists each hope to gain from cooperation, and what did each fear?
  3. How did Soviet assistance shape the Chinese revolution, and in what ways did Chinese actors direct, resist, or reinterpret that assistance?
  4. How did the construction of a party-led army change the relationship between politics and military power in China?
  5. Why did strikes, boycotts, and peasant associations both strengthen the revolution and divide it?
  6. How did military victories in the Northern Expedition depend on political, financial, and social conditions as well as battlefield tactics?
  7. How do historians distinguish the several stages of the 1927 rupture, and why does the distinction matter?
  8. To what extent did the Northern Expedition achieve national unification, and in what senses did it not?
  9. How should historians weigh structural causes against contingent decisions in explaining the collapse of the First United Front?

Historical Context

The revolution of 1911 (Chapter 57) had ended the Qing dynasty but had not created a stable republic. Yuan Shikai’s presidency, his attempt to restore monarchy, and his death in 1916 (Chapter 58) were followed by the breakdown of central authority described in Chapter 59. Between 1916 and 1927 the Beiyang government in Beijing, internationally recognized and entitled to collect customs revenue and receive foreign loans, was controlled in turn by rival military cliques: the Anhui clique under Duan Qirui, the Zhili clique under Cao Kun and Wu Peifu, and the Fengtian clique under Zhang Zuolin, based in Manchuria. Other regional leaders, such as Feng Yuxiang in the northwest, Yan Xishan in Shanxi, and the commanders of Guangxi, Yunnan, Sichuan, and the lower Yangtze, held their own territories. Taxation was often collected years in advance, and soldiers were frequently recruited from desperate rural populations. This was not simple chaos, as Chapter 59 emphasizes, but it was a political order in which no authority could claim to speak for the nation.

Foreign privilege added a second grievance. Treaty-port concessions, extraterritoriality, foreign-run customs administration, and foreign gunboats in Chinese waters, all legacies of the nineteenth-century treaty system (Chapter 52), remained in place. The Paris Peace Conference’s decision in 1919 to transfer German rights in Shandong to Japan, rather than return them to China, had triggered the May Fourth protests (Chapter 60). For many politically active Chinese, parliamentary experiments appeared to have failed, Western liberal powers appeared to have betrayed China, and the question became how to build a state strong enough to end both militarism and foreign privilege.

Geography shaped the revolution. Guangzhou (Canton), in Guangdong province, was a large commercial city on the Pearl River delta, close to Hong Kong, the British colony and a major entrepôt. It was a center of overseas Chinese capital, a seat of labor organizing among seamen, mechanics, and printers, and a refuge for Sun Yat-sen’s movement because it was far from Beijing. Shanghai, the largest industrial and financial city in China, contained the International Settlement and the French Concession, where foreign authorities administered territory and where the Chinese Communist Party was founded. The middle Yangtze region around Wuhan, formed by the three cities of Wuchang, Hankou, and Hanyang, was a strategic hub of railway, river, and industrial activity. North China and the northeast were dominated by militarists with different strengths and foreign patrons. Understanding these places as distinct social and political environments is essential for understanding how the revolution unfolded.

Intellectual and social transformation formed the third background. New Culture critics had attacked Confucian family ethics and demanded science and democracy; students and some workers had learned that mass protest could force political concessions; Marxist ideas, spread through translations and the example of the Russian Revolution of 1917, offered an analysis of imperialism and class that appealed to part of this generation; and trade unions, student associations, and women’s organizations multiplied in treaty-port cities. Yet most Chinese lived in villages, where landholding, rent, debt, and local power relations structured daily life, and where the language of national politics only slowly arrived. The tension between urban political activism and rural society runs through this chapter.

Finally, readers should remember that “China” in this period meant a contested political space. The Republic of China existed in international law, but the Beijing government governed only part of the territory, Tibet and Outer Mongolia were outside effective Chinese administration, and the northeast was dominated by Zhang Zuolin with Japanese influence. The Nationalist movement sought to rebuild a national state, but it did so in a country whose regions had very different economies, armies, and loyalties.

Division I

The Formation of Revolutionary Political Movements

Section 61.1

From May Fourth to Organized Revolutionary Politics

The May Fourth era (Chapter 60) produced a generation of activists who had learned to criticize, but not yet how to govern. Students in Beijing and Shanghai had demonstrated against the Shandong settlement of 1919; merchants had closed shops in sympathy; and in Shanghai a general strike of workers and shopkeepers in June 1919 had helped persuade the Beijing government to dismiss pro-Japanese officials and decline to sign the Treaty of Versailles. These events convinced many that urban mass action could affect national politics. Yet the same events revealed limits. The protests dispersed once their immediate aims were met, the Beijing government remained in the hands of militarists, and the foreign powers remained in the treaty ports.

Three disappointments pushed activists toward organization. The first was the failure of parliamentary and constitutional experiments. Parliament had been dissolved and revived several times, its members were often bought or coerced by military patrons, and the elections of the early republic (Chapter 58) had not produced stable governments. The second was disillusionment with the Western powers. President Woodrow Wilson’s rhetoric of self-determination had raised hopes that were dashed at Paris, and many Chinese concluded that the liberal democracies would not voluntarily end their privileges in China. The third was the persistence of militarism: ideas alone, however bold, could not disarm a general with ten thousand soldiers.

At the same time the Russian Revolution offered a model that, to some Chinese, seemed relevant. After the Soviet government announced in 1919 and 1920 that it was renouncing tsarist special privileges in China (statements whose practical effect was more limited than their rhetoric suggested), Soviet Russia acquired a reputation as the one great power that treated China as an equal. Marxist-Leninist theory also offered a vocabulary that linked foreign imperialism, domestic militarism, and social inequality in a single analysis, and a model of a disciplined party able to organize workers, peasants, and soldiers. This was not the only intellectual option; anarchism, guild socialism, liberal reformism, educational reform, rural reconstruction, and provincial self-government all found advocates, and many activists moved among them. But for a significant minority, the Leninist party and the Nationalist military strategy appeared to answer the question that criticism alone could not: how to create a force strong enough to change China.

Organization also mattered because the political environment was changing. By 1920 and 1921 modern industry, though small, employed some one to two million workers in cities such as Shanghai, Tianjin, Wuhan, and Guangzhou, in textile mills, railways, docks, and mines. Workers experienced long hours, low wages, labor contractors, and in foreign-owned enterprises, discrimination. Mutual-aid societies, craft guilds, and secret societies already organized many of them, and young intellectuals began to establish night schools and clubs to reach them. In the countryside, rural elites, militias, and secret societies held local power, but peasant resentment over rents, taxes, and soldiers’ exactions was widespread. These conditions created potential constituencies for movements that were prepared to organize them.

Students should keep in mind that the shift to organized politics was neither universal nor linear. Many intellectuals remained skeptical of parties; many never joined either movement; and the choices of those who did join were shaped by personal networks, regional ties, educational backgrounds, and even chance meetings. The Nationalists and Communists drew, in part, on overlapping groups of educated young people who had once read the same journals. Their later enmity makes it easy to forget how close they sometimes were in 1923 or 1924.

Section 61.2

Sun Yat-sen and the Reconstruction of the Nationalist Movement

Sun Yat-sen had been a revolutionary for nearly three decades by 1921. Born in Guangdong in 1866 and educated partly in Hawai‘i and Hong Kong, he had founded the Revive China Society in 1894 and the Tongmenghui (Chinese Revolutionary Alliance) in 1905, and he had been briefly provisional president of the Republic in early 1912 (Chapter 57 and 58). After Yuan Shikai’s suppression of the Nationalist Party following the Second Revolution of 1913, Sun organized the Chinese Revolutionary Party in Japan in 1914, which demanded personal loyalty to himself. In October 1919 he reorganized it as the Zhongguo Guomindang, the Chinese Nationalist Party, in the wake of the May Fourth ferment. Sun was famous, but his party was small, loosely organized, and financially dependent on overseas Chinese donors and on the goodwill of regional commanders.

Sun’s strategy depended on establishing a territorial base. In 1917 he had led a “constitutional protection” government in Guangzhou in opposition to the Beijing government, but it relied on southwestern military leaders who were only intermittently committed to his goals. In 1921 he was elected “extraordinary president” by a rump parliament in Guangzhou, and he planned a northern campaign. His principal military partner in Guangdong was Chen Jiongming, a reform-minded commander who favored provincial self-government and local development over Sun’s plans for a military unification of China. In June 1922 Chen’s troops turned against Sun, shelled his residence, and forced him to flee to Shanghai on a gunboat. The episode taught Sun a bitter lesson: a revolutionary movement without its own loyal army would always be hostage to its allies.

Sun returned to Guangzhou in early 1923 with the help of troops from Yunnan and Guangxi, who drove Chen Jiongming eastward, and assumed the title of generalissimo of a new military government. But the situation remained precarious. The Guangzhou area was controlled by a patchwork of armies, many of them from other provinces and each living off local revenues. Merchants complained of taxes and levies; the city’s police were unreliable; and the Guomindang itself remained, in Sun’s own assessment, undisciplined and ineffective. Sun had tried to pursue alliances with northern militarists such as Zhang Zuolin and Duan Qirui, and he sought foreign support from Britain, the United States, and Japan, all of which declined to treat his movement as more than a regional faction. The Western powers continued to recognize the Beijing government, and in 1923 foreign naval forces even threatened to intervene when Sun’s government sought a share of customs revenue collected in Guangzhou.

These obstacles help explain why Sun turned to Soviet Russia. This was not a conversion to Marxism: Sun consistently rejected class struggle as a central principle and described his own program as one that would avoid the social conflicts of Europe. Instead it was a pragmatic search for a source of organizational expertise, funds, and weapons from the one major power willing to offer them without demanding recognition of the unequal treaties. At the same time, Sun was ambivalent: he sought Soviet assistance while remaining wary of Communist domination, and he never abandoned his ambitions to be the sole leader of the revolution. The party that resulted from the 1924 reorganization (section 61.5) bore the imprint of this ambivalence.

Historical Evidence

Sun’s own writings and lectures. Much of what historians know of Sun’s thought comes from lectures delivered in Guangzhou in 1924 and published as San Min Chu I (the Three Principles of the People), speeches, letters, and his posthumously published plans for national reconstruction. These sources reveal his intentions but were often improvised, revised in transcription, and later canonized by the Nationalist party-state, which turned them into quasi-scripture. Historians therefore read them alongside contemporary reports by Guangzhou merchants, foreign consuls, and Soviet advisers, who often gave less flattering portraits of Sun’s organizational capacities.

Section 61.3

The Founding of the Chinese Communist Party

The Chinese Communist Party grew out of study circles organized by intellectuals associated with the New Culture movement. Chen Duxiu, the editor of New Youth, and Li Dazhao, librarian and professor at Peking University, had both been drawn to Marxism by about 1919. Li Dazhao praised the Russian Revolution as a harbinger of a new era and encouraged Marxist study groups in Beijing, where young students such as Mao Zedong, then an assistant in the university library, encountered his ideas. Chen Duxiu, who moved to Shanghai in 1920, helped organize a Marxist study group there and cooperated with representatives of the Comintern such as Grigori Voitinsky. Small communist groups also formed among Chinese students in Japan and, notably, among work-study students in France, where future leaders such as Zhou Enlai, Zhao Shiyan, and Deng Xiaoping would later be associated with the movement.

In July 1921 delegates from these groups met in Shanghai for the First National Congress of the CCP. About a dozen delegates, representing a total membership usually estimated at roughly fifty to sixty, attended, along with two Comintern representatives, Henk Sneevliet (known as Maring) and Nikolsky. After police agents interrupted the meeting, the delegates reconvened on a boat on South Lake in Jiaxing, Zhejiang. Neither Chen Duxiu nor Li Dazhao attended, though Chen was elected the party’s first secretary. The congress declared the party’s goals of overthrowing capitalism and establishing a dictatorship of the proletariat; it also adopted a hostile stance toward other political parties. The exact date of the final session is uncertain because records are incomplete and later accounts differ, though the party later settled on 1 July as its anniversary.

The party that emerged was tiny and largely intellectual. In 1921 almost no members were industrial workers, and the Communists’ initial strategies centered on propaganda, labor night schools, and union organizing. They achieved some early successes: in 1921 and 1922 Communist-led unions were involved in strikes among Hunan miners, Shanghai textile workers, and railway workers on the Beijing–Hankou line. The Anyuan coal mine and railway strike in Jiangxi and Hunan in September 1922, in which Li Lisan and Liu Shaoqi played prominent roles, became a model of Communist labor organizing. But the movement suffered a severe setback on 7 February 1923, when warlord Wu Peifu’s forces violently suppressed a railway workers’ strike on the Beijing–Hankou line, killing dozens of strikers. The experience persuaded many Communists that an independent labor movement could not survive without political allies and armed protection.

Students must distinguish between the small, relatively marginal party of 1921–1923 and the larger organization of 1926–1927. By the Fourth Congress of January 1925, the CCP had perhaps one thousand members; by the Fifth Congress in the spring of 1927, it claimed close to 58,000, a figure that reflects the growth that came with the Nationalist alliance and the mass movements of 1925 to 1927 but that should be treated as an approximation, since organizational records were fragmentary. The party in 1927 was far more diverse, including workers, peasants, students, and soldiers, but also far more exposed to the repression that followed.

Section 61.4

Soviet Russia, the Comintern, and Revolutionary Strategy in China

Soviet interest in China had several sources. The Bolshevik government, struggling in civil war and diplomatic isolation, sought security on its eastern border and recognition from China and other states. It also saw the colonial and semi-colonial world as a potentially revolutionary frontier, since weakening imperialism in Asia might relieve pressure on the new Soviet state in Europe. In 1920 the Comintern’s Second Congress had debated how Communists should treat nationalist movements in colonial countries; Lenin favored temporary cooperation with “bourgeois-democratic” nationalists while preserving the independence of the Communist movement, whereas the Indian Communist M. N. Roy argued for greater emphasis on social revolution. The resulting compromise left room for varied interpretations that would recur in China.

The Comintern’s agent Maring, who had worked in the Dutch East Indies, where Communists had cooperated with the mass Islamic movement Sarekat Islam, concluded in 1922 that the young CCP was too small to lead a revolution alone and that the Guomindang, despite its weaknesses, was the most promising national organization. He urged the CCP to cooperate. Many Chinese Communists, including Chen Duxiu, resisted the idea of subordinating their party to a bourgeois-led organization, but the Comintern’s authority, and its funding, were persuasive. At a special meeting of the CCP Central Committee at Hangzhou in August 1922 Maring pressed his case, and at the party’s Third Congress in Guangzhou in June 1923, delegates adopted a policy of Communists joining the Guomindang as individuals while preserving their own organization. Chen Duxiu continued to harbor doubts, and Zhang Guotao opposed the policy at the 1923 Congress; these dissents are a reminder that Chinese Communists were not mere executors of Moscow’s instructions.

Meanwhile Soviet diplomats negotiated with Sun. On 26 January 1923 Sun and the Soviet representative Adolf Joffe issued a joint statement in Shanghai. The statement declared that conditions in China were not yet suitable for communism or the Soviet system, that the most pressing tasks were national unification and independence, and that Soviet Russia sympathized with these aims. It also referred to the Chinese Eastern Railway, a question Joffe handled cautiously. The statement did not commit Sun to Communist doctrine, and it did not establish an alliance of parties, but it opened the way to assistance. In October 1923 the Comintern sent Mikhail Borodin to Guangzhou as Sun’s political adviser; Borodin had a talent for organization and a seat at Sun’s table. In 1924 the Soviet military adviser Vasily Blyukher, known in China as “Galen,” arrived and helped plan military training and campaigns. Soviet shipments of arms, including a cargo that reached Guangzhou in October 1924, and a flow of funds supplemented Nationalist resources.

Interpreting this assistance requires care. Soviet advisers contributed significantly to the Guomindang’s organizational reform, to the technical design of the Whampoa Academy, and to Nationalist propaganda methods. But they did not control the Nationalists. Sun and, later, Chiang Kai-shek chose which advice to accept; Chinese Communists often disagreed with Comintern representatives; and within Moscow, leaders such as Stalin, Trotsky, and Zinoviev contested China policy as part of their broader struggle for power after Lenin’s death in January 1924. Directives from Moscow were often delayed, ambiguous, and based on incomplete information about local conditions. Chinese historiography in the People’s Republic, for decades, emphasized Chen Duxiu’s alleged “right opportunism,” while Nationalist historiography in Taiwan emphasized Soviet manipulation; recent scholarship, aided by Russian archival access after 1991, has stressed the interplay of Chinese initiatives, Soviet interests, and the flawed communication between them.

Historical Vocabulary
Comintern (Communist International)

The Comintern, founded in Moscow in 1919, was an international organization of Communist parties that claimed to coordinate a world revolution. In practice its executive committee, dominated by the Soviet party, sent advisers, money, and directives to national parties. Chinese Communists joined the Comintern as a “section,” bound in principle to accept its decisions, though in practice implementation depended on local conditions and communications that could take weeks.

Historians use the term to remind students that the CCP in the 1920s belonged to a transnational movement, but they also warn against assuming that the Comintern issued a single coherent policy. Its representatives in China, such as Maring and Borodin, sometimes disagreed with one another, and the advice that reached them from Moscow shifted as Soviet factional politics evolved.

China and the Wider World

The Chinese revolution was entangled in global politics. Britain, with its Hong Kong colony and large commercial interests in Shanghai and the Yangtze, saw Soviet influence in Guangzhou as a threat; Japan, with railway interests and troops in Manchuria and Shandong, viewed Chinese unification warily; and the United States, though more flexible, remained committed to the treaty-port system. Revolutionary activism in China was also connected to anti-colonial movements elsewhere in Asia, as Indian, Korean, and Vietnamese revolutionaries sojourned in Guangzhou in the mid-1920s, and Ho Chi Minh worked there for a period as a Comintern agent. The Chinese revolution thus belonged to a broader Asian moment, not merely a national one.

Section 61.5

The Reorganization of the Guomindang and the Nationalist Revolutionary Program

In January 1924, the Guomindang held its First National Congress in Guangzhou, from 20 to 30 January. Delegates numbered about 165, including Communists such as Li Dazhao, Tan Pingshan, and Mao Zedong, who was elected an alternate member of the Central Executive Committee. The congress adopted a party constitution modeled in part on Leninist organization: a hierarchy of congresses and committees from local branches to a central executive committee, a standing committee, and a central supervisory committee, together with discipline provisions and requirements that members submit to party decisions. Borodin served as an adviser in the drafting, and Wang Jingwei and Liao Zhongkai, among others, helped draw up the text.

The congress also reinterpreted the Three Principles of the People. Sun’s earlier formulation, drawn from his Tongmenghui days, had emphasized nationalism, democracy, and people’s livelihood. In 1924 nationalism acquired a more explicit anti-imperialist content, including opposition to unequal treaties and a commitment to equality among China’s peoples; democracy was tied to a period of party tutelage in which the party would guide the people toward self-government; and people’s livelihood was associated with equalizing landownership and regulating capital, which was open to both moderate and radical interpretations. Sun delivered a series of lectures on the principles throughout 1924, which remain the standard statement of the doctrine. Historians note that the ambiguity of the formula enabled it to serve divergent purposes: Communists could emphasize land reform and the support of workers and peasants, while conservative Nationalists emphasized national unity and social harmony.

The reorganization changed how the party recruited and operated. The old Guomindang had relied on personal ties and military patrons; the new one created local branches, held study sessions, and established departments for propaganda, peasant affairs, labor, women, and youth. Members were supposed to undergo training, pay dues, and accept discipline. The party founded newspapers and published its doctrines in simple language. By 1926 it claimed several hundred thousand members, though the numbers should be treated cautiously, since many were nominal and rapid expansion in 1926 and 1927 admitted large numbers of opportunists. A critical principle was yi dang zhi guo, “governing the state through the party,” which placed party organs above the formal institutions of government and prefigured the party-state of the Nanjing period (Chapter 62).

Division II

Constructing the First United Front and the Revolutionary Base

Section 61.6

The First United Front: Cooperation Between Rival Movements

The cooperation between the Guomindang and the Chinese Communist Party between 1923 and 1927, called the First United Front or, in Chinese, the First Nationalist–Communist Cooperation (diyici guogong hezuo), was never a single, stable arrangement. It was a set of institutional compromises, political bargains, and mutual expectations that each side understood differently. Students should avoid picturing two parties marching side by side under a shared program. Instead, they should picture a Nationalist organization that admitted Communists as individuals, a Communist party that maintained its own committees, discipline, and newspapers, and a series of tensions over who controlled what.

The formula adopted in 1923 and 1924 is often called the “bloc within.” Under it, CCP members joined the Guomindang in their individual capacities. They accepted the Guomindang’s constitution and Sun Yat-sen’s leadership, and many held party offices; at the same time they remained members of the CCP and answered to its Central Committee. When the Guomindang Congress considered the issue of dual membership in January 1924, some Nationalist delegates objected that Communists would use the Guomindang as a vehicle for their own party. Sun replied that the Guomindang was strong enough to absorb them, and that Communists who joined must obey Nationalist discipline. This was a political decision rather than a legal fiction: Sun believed that bringing the Communists inside the party would give him control over them and would draw on their energy, organizational skill, and connections to the Soviet Union. The Communists, for their part, regarded entry as a way to gain access to mass work and legitimacy, while building their own organization.

The alliance was founded on shared immediate goals. Both parties opposed the militarists, the unequal treaties, and foreign privilege. Both supported mass organization of workers and peasants, at least in principle. Both sought Soviet assistance. Yet they differed about the ultimate purpose of the revolution. For the Nationalists, the aim was a strong, unified, independent China under a party-led government, with social reform subordinate to national reconstruction. For the Communists, the Nationalist revolution was the first stage in a longer process that would end in socialism; class struggle might have to be restrained temporarily, but it could not be abandoned. The Comintern’s formula of a “national revolution” joined by workers, peasants, urban petty bourgeoisie, and national bourgeoisie, a “bloc of four classes,” was ambiguous enough to conceal these differences.

Authority within the alliance was contested from the beginning. In 1924 and 1925 Nationalist conservatives such as Deng Zeru, Feng Ziyou, and later Hu Hanmin and the so-called Western Hills group complained about Communist “cells” within the party and criticized Borodin’s influence. In June 1924 several Central Supervisory Committee members presented an impeachment motion against the Communists; Sun rejected it. Communists complained in turn that conservative Nationalists obstructed peasant and labor organizing. Scholars such as David Barrett have shown that participants held strikingly different conceptions of the alliance’s nature and purpose: some Nationalists viewed Communists as junior partners to be absorbed, some Communists viewed the Nationalists as a temporary vehicle, and Soviet advisers viewed both as instruments of a broader anti-imperialist strategy. This variety of understanding is part of why the alliance functioned effectively when external threats were great and deteriorated when power was at stake.

Historical Vocabulary
United Front (tongyi zhanxian) and “Bloc Within”

A united front is an alliance among distinct political forces that cooperate for specific goals while retaining separate identities. In Chinese Communist usage it came to describe a recurring strategy: cooperating with non-Communist forces against a principal enemy while preserving the party’s independence. The “bloc within” (sometimes translated “bloc inside”) was the particular form used in 1923–1927, in which Communists became individual members of the Guomindang rather than negotiating as one party with another.

The term matters because it shows that the First United Front was not a merger. Later, when the Communists and Nationalists cooperated again in the 1930s and 1940s (Chapters 64–65), the arrangement was different in form. Students should always ask what exactly was being united, who agreed to what, and how each party understood the alliance.

Section 61.7

The Whampoa Military Academy and the Creation of a Revolutionary Army

The experience of 1922 convinced Sun that his movement needed a loyal army. In the spring of 1924, with Soviet advice and funds, the Guomindang established the Whampoa (Huangpu) Military Academy on an island in the Pearl River below Guangzhou. Its formal opening ceremony took place on 16 June 1924. Chiang Kai-shek, who had spent several months in the Soviet Union in late 1923 studying the Red Army, became the academy’s commandant; Liao Zhongkai served as party representative; and Soviet advisers, including Blyukher, helped design the curriculum. Early staff included men who would hold senior positions in both parties, among them the Communist Zhou Enlai, who returned from France and became deputy director of the political department in late 1924.

Cadets were recruited from across China through party networks and public examinations; the first class numbered several hundred, and later classes grew far larger. Applicants were drawn disproportionately from middle-class families of the interior provinces, such as Hunan, Zhejiang, and Guangdong, and included both committed activists and young men seeking a career. The academy offered an intensive program combining military drill, tactics, and weapons instruction with courses in the Three Principles, imperialism, and political organization. Instructors emphasized discipline, loyalty, and indoctrination as well as technical competence. Whampoa’s alumni, among them future Nationalist generals such as Hu Zongnan and Du Yuming and future Communist commanders such as Lin Biao and Xu Xiangqian, would shape both armies for decades; their shared training at Whampoa created personal networks that survived their later enmity.

Whampoa was also the nucleus of the National Revolutionary Army (NRA). In late 1924, Sun’s government formed “teaching regiments” of academy cadets and newly recruited soldiers, which fought effectively in the campaigns of 1925. Soviet arms strengthened them, as did new organizational practices. The most striking innovation was the system of political officers, or party representatives, modeled on Soviet commissars, attached to military units. They conducted political instruction, supervised loyalty, boosted morale, and monitored commanders. By the July 1926 launch of the Northern Expedition, the NRA had been organized into eight armies, though many of these were formerly independent regional forces nominally incorporated into the new structure, and only the First Army, built from Whampoa units under Chiang, was truly a party creation.

Historians stress that the NRA was a hybrid. It combined disciplined, politically indoctrinated cadre units with allied provincial armies that retained their own commanders and loyalties. It paid its soldiers poorly and unevenly; it relied on local requisitions; and it commonly absorbed defeated enemy troops, whose political loyalty was uncertain. Its reputation for relatively good discipline toward civilians, an advantage in propaganda, depended on the quality of particular units and the efforts of political workers. The distinction between the politically trained core and the larger body of absorbed forces would become critical in 1927, when generals commanding the latter chose sides in the party conflict according to their own interests.

Historical Vocabulary
Party-Army (dang jun)

A party-army is an army that is organized, trained, and controlled by a political party rather than by a state, a monarch, or a commander’s personal following. In the Whampoa system, political officers, party cells, and political education aimed to ensure that soldiers owed loyalty to the party and its program, not simply to the general who paid them. The slogan that “the party commands the gun” is later associated with Mao Zedong, but the principle was introduced in China through the Whampoa model.

The idea mattered because it changed how political legitimacy was imagined. In the militarist era a general’s authority came from his army; in the party-army model, in theory, an army’s authority came from the party. In practice, as Chiang Kai-shek’s career showed, the military could also dominate the party.

Section 61.8

Revolutionary Government, Party Institutions, and Political Mobilization in Guangzhou

Guangzhou under Sun and his successors was less a unified state than a laboratory in which new institutions grew alongside older, more powerful ones. A government existed, with ministries of finance, foreign affairs, and military affairs; it had a mayor and city departments that built roads, tore down old city walls, and widened streets. Alongside it, the party organization created committees for propaganda, peasant affairs, labor, women, youth, and military training. In July 1925 the Nationalist Government was formally established, with a committee structure headed by Wang Jingwei as chairman, which asserted the party’s authority over administration and the army. The relation between party and government organs was never fully clarified, and personal rivalry often determined who held real power.

Finance was perennially precarious. The government depended on taxes on opium, gambling, and commerce, on loans from Guangzhou merchants, on levies in kind, and on Soviet subsidies. Its efforts to rationalize taxation, led by officials such as T. V. Soong (Song Ziwen), who became finance minister in 1925, included establishing a Central Bank in Guangzhou in 1924 and taking control of tax collection from local militarists. These reforms met resistance from merchants and local powerholders, as the Merchant Corps confrontation (section 61.9) revealed. The relationship between the revolutionary government and the commercial elite was thus a mixture of cooperation and extraction: merchants wanted order, protection from militarists, and relief from arbitrary levies, but they feared radical labor movements and the government’s demands.

Propaganda was an important tool. The party published newspapers, pamphlets, and posters, and sent speakers into the countryside and into army units. It organized memorial days, anniversaries, and rallies that publicized its symbols: the Nationalist flag with its white sun on a blue field and red ground, Sun’s portrait, and the Three Principles. Schools and training institutes, including the Peasant Movement Training Institute (opened in Guangzhou in July 1924 and later directed for a time by Mao Zedong) and Sun Yat-sen University in Moscow for Chinese students, trained organizers. These institutions produced a corps of politically committed young people who would staff the party’s propaganda, labor, and military work during the Northern Expedition.

Regional and Frontier Context

Guangdong was not typical of China. Its coastal commerce, links with Hong Kong, and connections with overseas Chinese communities in Southeast Asia, Hawai‘i, and the Americas provided capital, ideas, and recruits that inland provinces lacked. Its population was linguistically distinct: Cantonese speakers in the delta, Hakka in the hills, and Chaozhou-speaking communities in the east, whose identities shaped local politics. Communication between Cantonese-speaking activists and organizers from Hunan or Zhejiang often required interpreters or common use of Mandarin. Revolutionary success depended on translating the movement’s message into local idioms and on managing the interaction of outsiders with local society.

Section 61.9

Military Consolidation and the Guangdong Campaigns

The consolidation of a revolutionary base in Guangdong required defeating or neutralizing several armed forces: the remnants of Chen Jiongming’s army in the eastern part of the province, the troops of Yunnan and Guangxi commanders who had helped Sun retake Guangzhou but who often acted on their own, and the private armed forces of Guangzhou’s commercial class. The campaigns of 1924 and 1925 thus involved both military operations and political bargaining with regional leaders.

The first major test came in October 1924. The Canton Merchant Volunteer Corps, a privately financed militia under the leadership of Chen Lianbo, a compradore of the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, had imported a large shipment of arms. The Sun government, suspecting the Corps of preparing a coup supported by British interests, seized the weapons. The merchants responded with a commercial strike that closed shops throughout the city. On 15 October forces under the Revolutionary Committee, including Whampoa cadets, Yunnanese and Hunanese troops, and workers’ and peasants’ militias, attacked the merchants’ militia in the commercial district of Xiguan. The fighting and a subsequent fire destroyed a considerable part of the neighborhood. The victory strengthened the government’s authority, but it left a legacy of bitterness among merchants, and the British consul-general in Guangzhou had to restrain a threatened intervention by foreign warships. Contemporary accounts differ about the extent of the destruction and about who set the fires.

In 1925 the NRA conducted two “Eastern Expeditions” against Chen Jiongming’s forces in the East River region. The first, from February to March, took Huizhou and Chaozhou, with Whampoa units and Soviet advisers playing prominent roles; Peng Pai’s peasant associations in Haifeng and Lufeng provided local support. After Sun’s death in March, the Guomindang government faced a revolt in June by the Yunnan commander Yang Ximin and the Guangxi commander Liu Zhenhuan, who had occupied Guangzhou’s revenue-producing districts and enjoyed some links with conservative Nationalists. The government, with Chiang’s troops, crushed them. A second Eastern Expedition, in October and November 1925, eliminated Chen Jiongming’s remaining power in eastern Guangdong. In 1926 the remaining Guangxi forces under Li Zongren and Huang Shaohong, who controlled the province next door, agreed to join the NRA, bringing Guangxi under the revolutionary banner. These victories turned the NRA from a small party force into a regional army and gave Chiang Kai-shek prestige and a loyal core of officers.

Students should note what these campaigns did not do. They did not create a fully centralized Guangdong, since local militias, secret societies, and landlords’ private forces persisted. They also relied on the willingness of regional commanders to incorporate their armies into the NRA while maintaining their authority over them, a bargain that would shape the Northern Expedition and the politics of 1927 and afterward.

Section 61.10

Sun Yat-sen’s Death and the Struggle for Revolutionary Leadership

In autumn 1924, after the Zhili–Fengtian War reshaped northern politics, Feng Yuxiang’s coup in Beijing overthrew the Zhili government and invited Sun to the capital for negotiations on national reunification. Sun, already ill, traveled north, stopping in Japan and Tianjin, and arrived in Beijing in December 1924. His illness was diagnosed as liver cancer, and he died on 12 March 1925 at the Peking Union Medical College hospital. Before his death he signed a brief political testament urging his followers to continue the revolution and to cooperate with the Soviet Union; a separate letter addressed to the Soviet government also circulated. The authenticity and drafting of these documents are matters of record rather than doubt, although the circumstances in which they were prepared, and the roles of Wang Jingwei and others, have been debated.

Sun’s funeral and memorial ceremonies created a cult of the founder that outlived the divisions among his followers. His body was placed temporarily in a temple in the Western Hills near Beijing, and later, after 1929, moved to a mausoleum on Purple Mountain in Nanjing. His portrait would hang in schools, party offices, and government buildings; recitation of his will became a ritual for civil servants and students. The invocation of Sun became a source of legitimacy for all claimants, including Communists, who also honored him.

Sun’s death left an unresolved question: who would lead the movement? The Guangzhou government in July 1925 appointed Wang Jingwei, an eloquent orator and Sun’s long-time associate, as chairman of the new Nationalist Government. Hu Hanmin, a conservative party theorist, served as foreign minister and acting generalissimo for a time. Liao Zhongkai, finance and labor chief, who was strongly supportive of the alliance with the Communists, was assassinated on 20 August 1925, outside the party headquarters. The investigation implicated associates of Hu Hanmin, who was sent out of Guangzhou, and also touched on conservative Guomindang circles; it removed from the leadership one of the main advocates of cooperation. Chiang Kai-shek, using the occasion of the inquiry, strengthened his own position. By the end of 1925 Chiang, as commander of the First Army and head of Whampoa, had emerged as the key military figure; Wang Jingwei remained the leading civilian leader.

Meanwhile, conservative Guomindang members who opposed the alliance with Communists organized outside Guangzhou. In November 1925 a group including Lin Sen, Zou Lu, and Xie Chi met in the Western Hills near Beijing, held a purported meeting of the Central Executive Committee, and demanded the expulsion of Communists and the dismissal of Borodin. The “Western Hills faction” won little support in Guangzhou, where Wang and Chiang still needed Soviet help, but it signaled that opposition to the alliance existed within the party and would grow with the mass movements of 1925 and 1926. The strains within the Guomindang were as important as those between the parties.

Division III

Workers, Peasants, and Revolutionary Social Mobilization

Section 61.11

The May Thirtieth Movement and Anti-Imperialist Protest

The May Thirtieth Movement of 1925 began as a labor dispute. In Shanghai’s Japanese-owned cotton mills, workers, many of them young women and girls recruited from the countryside through labor contractors, faced long shifts, low pay, physical abuse by foremen, and arbitrary dismissal. In February 1925 a strike erupted in several Japanese mills. On 15 May, at a mill in the Western district, Japanese foremen confronted workers demanding wages during a lockout; a foreman shot Gu Zhenghong, a Communist-linked worker, who died the following day. His death inspired mass funerals and student protests, and the arrests of students distributing leaflets in the International Settlement led to a large demonstration on 30 May.

Thousands of students and workers marched through the Settlement’s Nanjing Road, delivering speeches and calling for the release of those arrested. When a crowd gathered outside the Laozha police station, British-commanded police, under Inspector Edward Everson, opened fire. Estimates of the dead vary, but about a dozen protesters were killed and many wounded. The shootings produced outrage. Within days a general strike paralyzed Shanghai; the Shanghai General Labor Union, organized under Communist leadership on 1 June, claimed hundreds of thousands of members at its height; student, merchant, and professional associations formed a coalition, the Shanghai General Chamber of Commerce reluctantly joined the strike, and protesters demanded the end of extraterritoriality, the return of the Mixed Court, and the right to organize. Boycotts of British and Japanese goods spread to other cities.

The movement spread nationwide. Anti-foreign demonstrations occurred in Beijing, Hankou, Nanjing, Chongqing, and dozens of smaller cities. On 23 June 1925 a demonstration in Guangzhou, including Whampoa cadets, students, and workers, marched toward the foreign concession of Shamian (Shameen); British and French troops fired, killing some fifty Chinese and wounding many more. The incident provoked the strike in Hong Kong and Guangzhou examined in section 61.12. Foreign governments responded unevenly: British authorities blamed Bolshevik agitation and sent reinforcements, while the Beijing government appointed a commission of inquiry that produced no lasting changes. The Shanghai strike was gradually settled in the autumn, with some wage concessions and limited reforms in the Settlement’s governance, but the protest had changed Chinese politics.

The movement revealed how nationalism and social grievance could reinforce each other. Workers’ demands for wages and dignity became fused with demands to end foreign privilege; students and urban professionals who had earlier been concerned with culture and national salvation found themselves in common cause with factory workers. It strengthened the credibility of both parties. The Communists emerged with a far larger urban base; the Guomindang gained prestige as the party that could lead a national struggle against foreign imperialism. At the same time, divisions appeared. Merchants, who suffered from the strike’s disruption, often distanced themselves from the labor movement once their immediate aims were met, and in Shanghai the business community’s fear of radical unionism would eventually influence its stance toward Chiang Kai-shek.

Section 61.12

Labor Unions, Strikes, and the Canton–Hong Kong Strike

The Shamian shootings inspired the largest and longest strike of the 1920s. Beginning in June 1925, tens of thousands of Chinese workers left Hong Kong, where they worked as seamen, dockers, servants, tram workers, printers, and artisans, and traveled to Guangzhou and the surrounding region. Estimates of the number of strikers range widely, from well over one hundred thousand to as many as 250,000, and reflect the imprecision of contemporary reporting. Strikers organized a Strike Committee in Guangzhou, led by activists such as Su Zhaozheng, a Communist seaman, and Deng Zhongxia, with the cooperation of the Nationalist government. The committee organized pickets, which enforced a boycott of British goods and shipping, ran a system of food and lodging for the strikers, and established courts, schools, and hospitals. Its pickets, numbering about two thousand and armed, patrolled the waterways and the border with Hong Kong, searching vessels and detaining suspected violators.

The strike devastated Hong Kong’s trade. Shipping fell sharply, the colony’s port traffic dropped, and its government had to import labor, subsidize food supplies, and pay for relief. British merchants sought to resume trade, and Hong Kong banks lost income. Yet the strike also strained the Guangzhou government. It had to subsidize the strikers, depriving the government of revenue, and the strike committee’s armed pickets acted at times as a state within the state. Local merchants, who were hurt by the boycott’s disruption of trade, grew restless. By late 1926, with the Northern Expedition under way, the Nationalist leadership decided to end the strike. In October 1926 the strike was formally called off. The government replaced the boycott with a surtax on imports, which helped finance the army. The strike had shown the power of organized labor, but also the costs.

Elsewhere, unions grew rapidly. The All-China Federation of Trade Unions (also translated in some English sources as the All-China Labor Federation), founded in May 1925 at the Second National Labor Congress in Guangzhou under Communist leadership, claimed several hundred thousand members; by early 1927 Communist and Nationalist-aligned unions together claimed millions, though these figures included many nominal members and should be treated skeptically. Railway workers, seamen, textile workers, printers, rickshaw pullers, and artisans formed unions, often drawing on older guild and native-place institutions. In Wuhan, in particular, the labor movement flourished during the Northern Expedition, expanding into the shops, schools, and neighborhoods of the three cities.

Nationalists and Communists approached labor differently. Communist organizers such as Deng Zhongxia, Li Lisan, and Liu Shaoqi saw the working class as the leading force of the revolution and aimed to develop its independent political consciousness. Nationalist leaders, including Wang Jingwei and Liao Zhongkai before his death, supported labor organization as a means of strengthening the anti-imperialist movement, but many Nationalists, especially generals, businessmen, and conservatives, regarded strikes and wage demands as disruptive and, if unrestrained, a threat to property and order. This difference would become increasingly sharp as the revolution moved into industrial cities.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work and Household in Treaty-Port Factories

A woman worker in a Shanghai cotton mill in the mid-1920s might begin her day well before dawn, walking from a rented room in a crowded tenement. Many workers were migrants from northern Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shandong who lived in dormitories, rented shared rooms, or lived in shantytowns. Shifts of twelve hours were common. Wages varied by gender, age, and skill; women and children were paid less than men, and labor contractors, known as “foremen” or “heads,” often took a part of workers’ pay and decided who was hired. Some mills employed searches and body checks, and workers recalled beatings and fines. Household budgets were tight: rice, cheap vegetables, and fuel consumed most income, and illness or unemployment pushed families into debt to moneylenders.

Yet workers were not simply victims. They built networks around native-place associations, secret societies such as the Green Gang, mutual-aid funds, and night schools. These networks could be used for solidarity and strikes, but they could also divide workers by region and gender and subject them to the patronage of gang bosses. Historian Elizabeth Perry has emphasized the importance of such networks and of different regional workforces in shaping Shanghai labor politics. When Communist organizers joined these communities, they had to adapt, and their success depended on understanding workers’ daily concerns more than on abstract doctrine.

Section 61.13

Peasant Associations and Rural Revolutionary Mobilization

Most Chinese lived in villages, so the rural question was central. In southern and central China, a large proportion of farmers were tenants or part-owners who paid rents that often consumed half or more of their crops, along with surcharges, deposits, and labor services. Taxes were collected by local officials and militarists, often in advance and in excess of formal rates. Indebtedness, usury, and the sale of land under distress were widespread. Rural elites, including landlords, gentry, clan leaders, and the heads of local militia (tuanlian or min tuan), exerted political power through landholding, lineage organization, and control of local institutions. Villagers who protested faced armed retaliation.

Peng Pai, a landlord’s son from Haifeng in eastern Guangdong who studied in Japan and became a Communist, was the pioneer of rural organization. In 1921 and 1922 he began forming peasant associations, which combined mutual aid with demands for rent reductions and later for the abolition of exploitative practices. By 1923 the Haifeng association claimed tens of thousands of members and was suppressed by Chen Jiongming’s forces, then revived after the Eastern Expeditions. The Guomindang established a Peasant Department headed by Liao Zhongkai and in July 1924 opened the Peasant Movement Training Institute in Guangzhou, which trained several hundred organizers, including a sixth class directed by Mao Zedong in 1926, who taught about rural classes, rents, and the organization of peasant self-defense. By the end of 1926, Guangdong peasant associations claimed on the order of several hundred thousand members, and in Hunan, as the Northern Expedition arrived, membership grew explosively.

Rural mobilization raised the question of land. Sun had spoken of “land to the tiller” and equalization of landownership, but the Guomindang platform of 1926 offered only moderate measures, such as rent reduction of 25 percent. Communists wanted more far-reaching redistribution, but the party’s official policy during the United Front was cautious, since many Nationalist officers came from landlord families and the Comintern advised against alienating them. The peasant associations themselves often moved faster than their leaders wished: they fined or paraded landlords, seized grain, restricted exports of rice, abolished tax surcharges, closed gambling, and challenged local militia. Resistance was fierce: landlords organized armed bands, and some local commanders, whose families were affected, turned against the movement.

Primary Source Window

Mao Zedong, “Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan,” early 1927

Brief paraphrase and excerpt: Mao reports that peasant associations have become, in many counties, the real organs of local power, and that peasants have overthrown the authority of landlords and gentry, humbled them in public, and taken control over taxation, courts, and local security. He defends these actions, including those that critics called “going too far,” as necessary to overturn centuries of rural domination, and states plainly that “a revolution is not a dinner party.” He lists measures the associations enacted, from fixing prices and banning gambling to forcing landlords to contribute funds and grain, and argues that the Party should lead rather than restrain the peasants.

Analysis: The report is an important eyewitness account of peasant mobilization at its height, written by an organizer sympathetic to the movement. Its vivid reporting of what villagers said and did cannot be dismissed, but it should be read with caution. Mao selected evidence that supported his argument, the sample of counties was limited, and his figures on membership are impressionistic. He was writing to persuade party leaders whose cautious policy he thought mistaken; and the text circulated and was edited later, so students should consult scholarly editions to see what was changed. The report reveals little of the views of landlords, of ordinary peasants who stayed out of the associations, or of women. Contemporary conservative sources, including complaints from Hunan landlords and Nationalist officers, offer a very different picture of the same events.

Section 61.14

Women, Students, Youth, and the Social Reach of Revolution

Revolution reached beyond workers and peasants into schools, households, and everyday social expectations. Students, who had been prominent since May Fourth, were among the most active participants: they organized lectures, boycotts, demonstrations, and night schools; they staffed propaganda teams that accompanied the NRA; and they joined the party in large numbers. Student associations linked schools in different cities and provinces. Yet student radicalism also generated conflict with school authorities, missionary institutions, and parents; anti-Christian and anti-imperialist campaigns in 1924 and 1925 targeted mission schools, whose Chinese staff and students were forced to choose between their institutions and the nationalist movement.

Women participated in the revolution in significant numbers. The Guomindang established a Women’s Department in 1924 (headed by He Xiangning, widow of Liao Zhongkai, after her husband’s assassination), and the CCP created its own women’s organizations, in which activists such as Xiang Jingyu and Deng Yingchao were prominent. Women students, teachers, nurses, factory workers, and some peasants joined unions, associations, and demonstrations. Women trained as propagandists and nurses with the NRA, and a small number gained access to Whampoa-related military training; the Wuhan Central Military and Political Institute, a Whampoa branch, then recruited a much larger cohort of women in late 1926 and early 1927. Revolutionary platforms called for equality of men and women, the end of footbinding and arranged marriage, women’s education, and women’s suffrage, in some cases connecting these demands to the Three Principles.

The consequences varied. Young urban women gained opportunities for public roles, and the act of cutting one’s hair short, wearing simple clothes, and speaking publicly became a political statement. In rural areas, women’s associations sometimes campaigned against footbinding and for divorce rights, provoking resistance from families and local conservatives. In Hunan in 1927, as the political climate turned, women with bobbed hair and women activists were targeted for attack, including in cases of sexual violence and murder. The movement’s leaders, mostly men, often subordinated women’s issues to the immediate demands of the national struggle, and some, including Xiang Jingyu, criticized this tendency. Xiang Jingyu was later arrested and executed by the Nationalists in 1928.

Everyday Life and Social History

Political Activism and the Household

For a young woman in a county seat in Hunan or in a Guangzhou girls’ school in 1926, joining a women’s association meant negotiating with her family. Attending evening meetings could damage a family’s reputation; refusing a marriage arranged by parents could lead to disinheritance or confinement; wearing a short haircut might invite ridicule. For some, the movement offered a route to literacy and paid work as teachers or organizers; for others it brought social isolation or danger. Men, too, found their household authority challenged when wives, sisters, or daughters acquired new roles. The evidence for these experiences comes largely from memoirs, newspaper accounts, and organizational reports, which tend to emphasize activists and exceptional cases; the lives of the many women who did not participate, or who opposed the new ideas, are less well documented.

Section 61.15

Class Conflict, Property, and the Contradictions of Mass Revolution

By the end of 1926, the revolution rested on a coalition whose members held different, and in some respects incompatible, expectations. Strikes raised wages and challenged managerial authority; peasant associations challenged landlords; merchants hoped for lower taxes and security; officers wanted pay, promotion, and the stability of their units; and party leaders wanted both mass enthusiasm and discipline. The tension was built into the strategy. Without mass mobilization, the NRA could not have advanced so rapidly or so cheaply, since workers and peasants gave information, carried supplies, sabotaged enemy communications, and sometimes joined the fighting. But the same mobilization alarmed the groups on whom the revolution also depended for finance, local administration, and military leadership.

Many Nationalist officers came from families of landlords, officials, or merchants, and they heard reports from their home villages of tenants refusing rent, associations confiscating grain, or relatives being fined or paraded. Officers’ pay was often in arrears and their units lived off local requisitions, which brought them into conflict with unions and peasant associations that sought to regulate prices or prevent seizures. Employers and shopkeepers complained that unions disrupted business, that picket corps acted as private police, and that labor demands were unreasonable in a depressed economy. Foreign firms and governments voiced similar worries. Meanwhile, Communist and left-wing organizers argued that restraining the movement would betray its social base and weaken the revolution.

The result was a politics of escalating conflict. Violence arose on all sides: landlords and their militias killed peasant activists; associations punished landlords; troops broke strikes; unions fought gangs hired by employers. The Comintern, uncertain about how far to push, issued contradictory advice: it urged continued cooperation with Nationalist generals while also calling for radical agrarian measures. Chinese leaders had to navigate these instructions amid dramatic local events. Historians who emphasize these tensions do not conclude that rupture was predetermined; they stress that the coalition’s stability depended on the continued willingness of key actors to subordinate their conflicts to the goal of military victory, and that such willingness diminished as victory approached.

Multiple Perspectives

The Revolution of 1926–1927 as Experienced by Different Groups

Industrial workers (Shanghai, Wuhan, Guangzhou): For many workers the revolution meant higher wages, shorter hours, protection against arbitrary dismissal, and a sense of dignity that unions provided. It also exposed them to dangers: employers locked out strikers, gangs attacked union offices, and as the political climate shifted in 1927 union members were arrested and killed. Workers were divided by region, gender, and political affiliation, and some belonged to Green Gang networks or unions aligned with Nationalist rather than Communist leaders.
Tenant and poor farmers (Hunan, Guangdong): Peasant associations offered the first real opportunity to challenge landlords, rent collectors, and local militia. In several counties they reduced rents, cancelled debts, and humiliated those who had dominated village life. Yet peasants bore the brunt of retaliation when the political tide turned, and many were conscripted, taxed, or exposed to the violence of passing armies whatever their politics.
Landlords and local gentry: Landowning families saw associations as threats to property, status, and personal safety. Some were fined, driven from their villages, or killed; many fled to county seats and provincial capitals, where they lobbied officers and officials to restrain the associations. Their testimony, which often describes disorder and sexual and physical abuse, is partisan, and some claims were exaggerated, but it also documents real violence and fear.
Merchants and industrialists: Merchants wanted an end to militarist levies and to foreign privilege, and many financed the Nationalists in the hope of stability. They simultaneously feared strikes, boycotts, and radical unions. In Shanghai, bankers and Chamber of Commerce leaders provided funds to Chiang Kai-shek in the weeks before 12 April, though how much influence they had on his decisions is debated.
Military officers and soldiers: Officers in the NRA included committed party members, professional soldiers seeking advancement, and former militarists who had changed sides. Many wanted order and regular pay, and many came from propertied families. Ordinary soldiers were typically poor recruits whose pay was uncertain; they responded to political education unevenly and to the example of their commanders.
Division IV

The Northern Expedition and the Struggle for National Power

Section 61.16

Preparations for the Northern Expedition and Emerging Political Divisions

Sun Yat-sen had long planned a northern campaign, but in 1925 and 1926 the question was practical rather than aspirational. The Guangdong base had been secured, the NRA had expanded, and the mass movements had created enthusiasm. Yet the strategic problem was daunting. Three major militarist coalitions dominated the territory to the north. Wu Peifu, once the strongest commander in China, held Hunan and Hubei and the railway from Wuhan northward; Sun Chuanfang controlled the five provinces of the lower Yangtze region, including Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Fujian, and Jiangxi, including Shanghai; and Zhang Zuolin, based in Manchuria, dominated the Beijing government and much of North China. Estimates of troop strength vary, but together these coalitions fielded several hundred thousand men, far more than the NRA’s roughly one hundred thousand, though many of their units were poorly motivated and loyal chiefly to their commanders. A fourth force, Feng Yuxiang’s Guominjun (National People’s Army) in the northwest, had been defeated by Zhang Zuolin and Wu Peifu in late 1925 and early 1926 and sought assistance, including Soviet supplies.

Nationalist planners, including Blyukher before his departure in 1926 and later Chinese staff officers such as Bai Chongxi, chose a strategy of defeating enemies one by one. The NRA would first attack Wu Peifu in Hunan and Hubei, where the Guangdong forces could rely on the support of Tang Shengzhi, a Hunanese general who had broken with Wu’s ally, and where the Yangtze and railway system offered strategic advantages. Then it would turn east against Sun Chuanfang in Jiangxi and the lower Yangtze. Feng Yuxiang would hold the northwest. Local mobilization would support the advance; propaganda teams, advance parties, and union and peasant networks would soften resistance. Financing relied on a combination of Guangzhou taxation, the import surtax collected after the Hong Kong strike, loans, and requisitions along the way. The Soviets continued to supply arms and advisers, although Moscow was divided about the wisdom of the venture; Stalin and Borodin supported it, whereas some other Soviet advisers feared it would provoke Britain and Japan and threaten the base.

Political divisions complicated preparation. On 20 March 1926, in the Zhongshan Warship Incident, Chiang Kai-shek claimed that a gunboat, the Zhongshan, commanded by a Communist officer, Li Zhilong, had moved suspiciously toward Whampoa, which he interpreted as a plot to kidnap him and carry him to the Soviet Union. He declared martial law in Guangzhou, arrested Communist political workers and Li Zhilong, surrounded the residence of Soviet advisers, and disarmed the strike pickets of the Hong Kong strike committee. Wang Jingwei, who was either implicated in the alleged plot or was a rival Chiang wished to sideline, left for France. The truth of the incident remains disputed: many historians consider the evidence of a plot weak and regard the episode as a deliberate move by Chiang to assert control, while others suggest that mutual suspicion and misinformation drove both sides. Whatever the cause, the result was clear.

Chiang then negotiated from strength. Borodin, who was away from Guangzhou at the time, accepted Chiang’s demands rather than risk a rupture before the Northern Expedition: Communist political officers were removed from the First Army, and Soviet advisers were limited in their roles. In May 1926 the Guomindang Central Executive Committee adopted a resolution on “Reorganization of Party Affairs,” which restricted Communists to no more than one-third of the seats on party committees, barred them from heading central departments, required that a list of Communist members be provided to the Guomindang, and forbade criticism of Sun’s doctrine. Chiang assumed the chairmanship of the Standing Committee and the Military Council, and in July he was appointed commander-in-chief of the NRA. The Communist leadership, informed by Moscow of the importance of the Expedition, accepted these limitations. The incident thus demonstrated both the dependence of the Communists on the alliance and Chiang’s increasing dominance.

Section 61.17

The Northern Expedition Begins in 1926

The Northern Expedition (Beifa) was formally launched on 9 July 1926, when Chiang, as commander-in-chief, took the oath in Guangzhou, though advance units had already entered Hunan in May and June to support Tang Shengzhi. The NRA marched in several columns: a central column through Hunan toward Changsha and Wuhan, a western column through the mountainous borderlands, and an eastern column through Jiangxi and Fujian. The army included Whampoa-trained units of the First Army, the Fourth Army of Li Jishen, which had a strong reputation and included an independent regiment commanded by the Communist Ye Ting, the Seventh Army of Guangxi under Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi, and Tang Shengzhi’s Eighth Army from Hunan.

The campaign was more than military. Propaganda and political work teams, staffed by Whampoa graduates, university students, and union and peasant organizers, preceded or accompanied the troops. They posted slogans calling for the overthrow of imperialism and militarism, held mass meetings, promised relief from heavy taxes, and organized local associations. Wuhan and other cities saw unions and peasant associations form as the army approached. Enemy troops sometimes defected after local uprisings or agitation, or after their commanders received promises of rank and payment. Chiang and other Nationalist leaders also used money, negotiation, and personal contacts with defecting officers, an ordinary practice of warlord politics that seems at odds with the party-army ideal but was often decisive.

Logistics were critical. Marching armies moved on foot and depended on porters, local officials, and civilian cooperation. In Hunan, villagers carried supplies and acted as guides in many areas, whether from conviction or because associations demanded service. In other regions, requisitioning provoked resentment. Soldiers suffered from disease, heat, and the demands of fast marches. Military historians such as Donald Jordan have emphasized that the NRA’s relatively small size, the disunity of its enemies, and the breakdown of opposing alliances amplified its successes; the NRA was not an overwhelming force, but it faced a divided and demoralized opposition and had the advantages of better political preparation and local support.

Section 61.18

Military Victories in Hunan, Hubei, and Jiangxi

The first phase unfolded rapidly in Hunan. Tang Shengzhi’s forces and the NRA’s vanguard entered Changsha on 11 July 1926. The advance continued into northern Hunan, where Wu Peifu’s troops made a stand in August at Tingsi Bridge and Heshengqiao, on the approaches to Hubei. In these battles the Fourth Army, including Ye Ting’s independent regiment, earned a reputation as the “Ironsides” by breaking through well-defended positions at heavy cost. Wu Peifu rushed north to rally his forces, but his army disintegrated. By early September the NRA had reached the Yangtze; Hanyang and Hankou fell on 6 and 7 September, and Wuchang, the walled city on the south bank, resisted a siege until 10 October, when the garrison surrendered.

The capture of the Wuhan cities gave the Nationalists control of one of China’s major industrial and communication centers: arsenals and ironworks at Hanyang, the commercial and financial districts of Hankou, with its foreign concessions, and railway lines to Beijing and Guangzhou. The Wuhan area was also a center of labor activism, where railway workers, dockers, and arsenal workers had suffered the repression of 1923 and quickly organized unions as the army arrived. Hubei and Hunan peasant associations expanded dramatically. Wuhan offered resources, but it also brought the revolution into a social environment of intense class and political conflict.

Meanwhile the eastern columns advanced into Jiangxi against Sun Chuanfang’s forces. The campaign here was harder. Nationalist assaults on Nanchang in September failed, with heavy losses, and the Fourth Army and others were checked. Reinforced and joined by units that had been engaged at Wuhan, the NRA attacked again in October and November; Nanchang fell on 8 November and Sun Chuanfang’s armies in Jiangxi collapsed. Chiang established his headquarters at Nanchang. In December and January the NRA moved into Fujian, aided by local commanders who changed sides, and in February 1927 entered Zhejiang, taking Hangzhou on 18 February. By then the NRA had expanded from roughly 100,000 men to some 250,000 or more, by estimates that include the absorbed forces of defeated or defecting commanders, a rapid growth that diluted its political cohesion.

Section 61.19

Revolutionary Mobilization and the Advance Toward Shanghai

By early 1927 the revolutionary movement faced a choice of strategic direction. The Wuhan cities provided a political base with strong labor and peasant organizations; the lower Yangtze, centered on Shanghai and Nanjing, promised the richest prizes, since Shanghai was China’s financial and industrial capital and a vital source of revenue. Chiang Kai-shek, based at Nanchang, favored moving east, in part because it would give him access to the financial resources of Shanghai and Zhejiang and distance him from Wuhan, where the left wing of the Guomindang and the Communists were influential. The Nationalist leadership, meanwhile, had begun in late 1926 to relocate the government from Guangzhou to Wuhan. A joint council of party and government leaders began to function there in early 1927, headed by figures sympathetic to the alliance. A conflict between Chiang, who wanted the capital at Nanchang, and the Wuhan leaders was building.

In Shanghai, the Communists and the General Labor Union prepared for an uprising. Earlier attempts, in October 1926 and February 1927, had failed, in the latter case after a general strike in support of the NRA’s advance was crushed by Sun Chuanfang’s forces, resulting in arrests and executions. A third attempt, planned under the direction of Zhou Enlai, Luo Yinong, Zhao Shiyan, and Wang Shouhua, began on 21 March 1927 as the NRA’s forces under Bai Chongxi approached. Estimates of the strike’s size, sometimes put at around 600,000 workers, derive from union claims and should be treated as approximate. Workers’ picket corps, numbering a few thousand armed members, fought police and warlord troops in several districts, and by 22 March they had taken most of the Chinese-administered districts, including the railway station and the Zhabei district. The General Labor Union established provisional authority in some areas, and a citizens’ government was proclaimed. The NRA entered the city largely without a fight.

The uprising demonstrated how civilian mobilization and military advance could reinforce each other. It also raised questions about what kind of authority would govern Shanghai. The union’s pickets possessed arms and a measure of popular legitimacy, and the Communists, with the assistance of Comintern advisers, hoped to retain an independent presence. The foreign powers, which had assembled forces in the International Settlement and the French Concession, including thousands of British, American, and Japanese troops and numerous warships, watched warily. Chiang arrived in Shanghai on 26 March and began consulting with bankers, merchants, Green Gang leaders, and foreign officials. Their interests, including those of the businessmen who had long felt the pressure of labor demands, shaped the next steps.

Analysts should note what was and was not achieved by civilian action. The workers’ uprising of March 1927 was an extraordinary organizational feat, and the arrival of the NRA was eased by the collapse of local defenses. But the uprising was not the sole cause of Nationalist control of Shanghai: Sun Chuanfang’s subordinate had already been weakened by defections, and the NRA was approaching in force. The relative contribution of workers and soldiers is therefore a matter of interpretation, with Communist accounts emphasizing the former and Nationalist accounts the latter.

Section 61.20

Wuhan, Nanjing, and the Fragmentation of Revolutionary Authority

By the beginning of 1927 two rival centers of Nationalist authority were emerging. At Wuhan, a Joint Council of party and government representatives, with Borodin as adviser and politicians such as Eugene Chen (the foreign minister), Sun Fo, Xu Qian, and Tan Pingshan, claimed to be the legitimate Nationalist government. It enjoyed the support of the CCP, the unions, and the peasant associations, and of generals such as Tang Shengzhi and Zhang Fakui, who had their own reasons for supporting a government that would not threaten their autonomy. Chiang at Nanchang built a power base independent of Wuhan, supported by the First Army, Whampoa graduates, and the conservative wing of the party. Disputes over the location of the capital, over the authority of the party versus the army, and over labor and peasant policy mingled with personal rivalry. In March 1927 the Third Plenum of the Central Executive Committee at Wuhan voted to curb the powers of the commander-in-chief, reduced Chiang’s positions in the party, and called for Wang Jingwei, who was still abroad, to return. Chiang, for his part, rejected the plenum’s authority.

Foreign relations compounded the divisions. In January 1927 crowds in Hankou, led by workers, occupied the British Concession, and the British, outnumbered, withdrew and negotiated; in February, Eugene Chen and British representative Owen O’Malley concluded an agreement that placed the concession under Chinese administration. This was the first transfer of treaty-port territory by force of Chinese popular action, and it alarmed the other powers. At Nanjing, which the NRA took on 24 March, soldiers and civilians attacked foreign consulates and residences, in what became known as the Nanjing Incident. Six foreigners were killed and others wounded and robbed; British and American gunboats on the Yangtze shelled parts of the city in response, killing and injuring Chinese people, with casualty figures uncertain. Who was responsible is disputed. Some accounts blamed Communist agitators, a view promoted by Chiang; others blamed undisciplined or defeated soldiers or looting by local elements; others noted that Nationalist officers, including those aligned with Chiang, had a role. The incident intensified foreign pressure: the powers demanded apologies and compensation, and the United States, Britain, and Japan hesitated over whether to intervene. Chiang used the episode to portray the Wuhan radicals as a danger to China’s relations with the powers.

Thus by early April 1927, the revolution had split in all but name. The Wuhan government, with its radical labor and peasant base, controlled Hunan and Hubei but lacked finance and military unity; Chiang controlled Nanjing and Shanghai with their financial resources; and the foreign powers waited. Neither side was ready to abandon the Northern Expedition, since its completion would legitimize whoever led it. The stage was set for a confrontation in which the distinct interests of all these actors, rather than a single plot, shaped the outcome.

China and the Wider World

The powers were not united. Britain, whose commercial stake in the Yangtze was the largest, urged a firm response; Japan under Foreign Minister Shidehara Kijūrō favored non-intervention and accommodation with moderate Chinese forces; the United States stressed negotiation while sending warships. The Soviet Union, whose embassy compound in Beijing was raided by Zhang Zuolin’s police on 6 April 1927 with the permission of the diplomatic corps, saw its documents seized and published. Each power’s calculations affected Chiang’s options, and Chiang, in turn, sought recognition and loans by presenting himself as a bulwark against radicalism.

Division V

The 1927 Rupture and the Limits of Revolutionary Unification

Section 61.21

Chiang Kai-shek, the Shanghai Massacre, and the Anti-Communist Purge

In the early hours of 12 April 1927, armed men in civilian dress, drawn largely from Green Gang networks and operating under the banner of the revived “China Mutual Progress Association,” attacked the headquarters of the General Labor Union and the pickets’ posts in Shanghai. Troops of the 26th Army and other NRA units then intervened, nominally to restore order, and disarmed the pickets, who were largely taken by surprise. On 13 April, a protest march of workers and citizens toward the headquarters of the garrison command was fired on by soldiers in Zhabei, killing and wounding many. In the following days, unions were closed, offices raided, and activists arrested, with many summarily executed. Estimates of the number killed vary considerably: contemporary Communist sources spoke of hundreds killed in the first days and of thousands killed, imprisoned, or missing in the following weeks, while other estimates are lower or more cautious. All estimates are uncertain, because of weak records, the number of unrecorded executions, and political interest in exaggeration or minimization; it is safe to say that several hundred people were killed in the first days and that the repression continued for weeks.

Preparation preceded the attack. After arriving in Shanghai on 26 March, Chiang met with leading figures of the Green Gang, including Du Yuesheng, Huang Jinrong, and Zhang Xiaolin, who commanded networks of informers, enforcers, and labor contractors with strong influence in the foreign concessions and Chinese districts. He also negotiated with bankers and merchants, among them Yu Xiaqing and Chen Guangfu, who agreed to provide substantial loans and subscriptions to the government he would create, and he consulted Nationalist elders including Wu Zhihui, Zhang Renjie, and Li Shizeng, who on 2 April had supported a Central Supervisory Committee motion calling for the “purification” (qingdang) of Communists from the party. Foreign authorities did not command the purge, but evidence indicates that some officials in the French Concession and International Settlement facilitated or tolerated the movement of gang forces; the extent and nature of formal coordination remain debated. On 5 April Wang Jingwei, who had returned to Shanghai, and Chen Duxiu issued a joint statement that denied rumors of Communist plans to seize power and called for continued cooperation, a sign of the Communist leadership’s reluctance to confront Chiang.

The violence was not limited to Shanghai. Within days, purges began in Guangzhou on 15 April, where Li Jishen’s troops arrested and killed numerous Communists, union activists, and strikers; in Zhejiang, Fujian, and Jiangsu; and in Sichuan, where earlier clashes had occurred at Chongqing. In Beijing, on 28 April, the authorities of Zhang Zuolin executed Li Dazhao and nineteen others after the raid on the Soviet embassy compound. On 18 April, Chiang announced the establishment of a national government at Nanjing, with Hu Hanmin as chairman, in opposition to Wuhan, which expelled Chiang from the party and dismissed him from his posts. The political outcome was thus a split between two rival Nationalist governments.

Students should be careful about how they describe these events. The purge was not a routine factional dispute or a customary suppression of strikes. It involved organized killing of people because of their political affiliation, carried out in coordination between military officers, party conservatives, businessmen, and gang leaders. At the same time, explaining it requires attention to Chiang’s own calculations: he sought control of the party and army, resources for his regime, and the security of a base in the lower Yangtze; he viewed the Communists as rivals and the Wuhan government as a threat; and he found allies who shared his fear of labor and peasant radicalism. It also required choices by others, including the decisions of the Communist leadership not to resist armed attack, given instructions from Moscow and Chen Duxiu’s hope for compromise.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The April 1927 Rupture

Long-term causes

Differences between Nationalist and Communist goals; the party-army’s hybrid composition; landlord and merchant fears of social revolution; Chiang’s ambitions and control of the First Army; foreign pressure; and the contradictory advice of the Comintern.

Short-term triggers

The Shanghai uprising of 21 March and the Communists’ armed pickets; the Nanjing Incident; the Wuhan Plenum’s curbs on Chiang; the offer of bankers’ funds; the Green Gang’s cooperation; the “purification” motion of 2 April.

Immediate effects

Hundreds killed and thousands arrested or driven underground; the destruction of Shanghai’s union movement; rival governments at Nanjing and Wuhan; a wave of repressions across the east and south; and the loss of the Communists’ urban base.

Long-term effects

The end of the First United Front; Chiang’s consolidation of the Nanjing regime; the Communist shift to armed insurrection and rural bases; lasting mutual distrust that shaped the Civil War and the 1930s–1940s cooperation.

Historical complexity: The April purge cannot be reduced to Chiang’s personal ambition, Soviet error, or class interest alone. It resulted from the interplay of military power, party factionalism, business and gang interests, foreign pressure, and decisions by Communist and Wuhan leaders that left their supporters exposed.

Section 61.22

The Wuhan Rupture and the Collapse of the First United Front

After the April purge, the Wuhan government continued to claim to be the legitimate revolutionary government and maintained its alliance with the Communists. In April and May the Fifth Congress of the CCP met at Wuhan (late April to early May), reaffirmed Chen Duxiu’s leadership, and claimed some 58,000 members. The Wuhan government approved the “Second Northern Expedition” against the northern militarists and sent Tang Shengzhi’s and Zhang Fakui’s armies, including Ye Ting’s Communist-led units, north into Henan, where they fought costly battles against Zhang Zuolin’s forces in May. At the same time, the Wuhan regime faced insoluble problems: an economic blockade, financial crisis, factory closures, and growing disorder in the countryside.

The most pressing was the land question. As peasant associations expanded in Hunan and Hubei, many Wuhan officers, whose families owned land, grew alarmed. On 21 May 1927, in the “Horse Day Incident,” Xu Kexiang, a regimental commander in Changsha, led his troops in attacking the provincial headquarters of the peasant and labor associations and killing or arresting activists; similar violence followed elsewhere in Hunan and Hubei. Moscow, meanwhile, sent contradictory instructions. A telegram attributed to Stalin in June urged the Chinese party to carry out agrarian revolution from below, to reform the Guomindang leadership, and to build a reliable army, but its message conflicted with the Wuhan government’s dependence on its generals, and the Communists found it difficult to implement. M. N. Roy, the Comintern delegate, showed the telegram to Wang Jingwei, who had returned to Wuhan in April, an act that confirmed Wang’s fears that the Communists and Soviet advisers were conspiring against him.

In June, Feng Yuxiang, whose army had advanced into Henan from the northwest, met with Wang Jingwei at Zhengzhou and then with Chiang Kai-shek at Xuzhou; he declared himself in favor of ending cooperation with the Communists. Pressure from generals, from Chiang, and from the remaining party conservatives led the Wuhan leaders to expel the Communists. In mid-July, after the Communists withdrew their members from the government, the Wuhan authorities declared a formal separation (fengong), arrested and expelled Communists, closed unions and peasant associations, and began to repress them. By late July the First United Front had completely collapsed. Borodin and other Soviet advisers left China.

The Communist leadership scrambled to respond. At an emergency conference in Hankou on 7 August, the party removed Chen Duxiu as general secretary, blamed his “opportunism,” and adopted a policy of armed insurrection. The new interim leadership under Qu Qiubai, with Comintern advisers, organized uprisings, including the Nanchang Uprising of 1 August 1927, led by Zhou Enlai, He Long, Ye Ting, and Zhu De, and the Autumn Harvest Uprising in Hunan and Jiangxi under Mao Zedong in September; both were suppressed, and survivors retreated to remote mountain areas. These events, treated in Chapter 62, mark the start of the Communists’ armed struggle and of their reorientation toward rural base areas. Within the Nationalist camp, Chiang resigned in August under pressure and went abroad briefly, but the Wuhan and Nanjing groups reconciled in September and Chiang returned to power in early 1928. Chiang’s Nanjing government thus emerged as the survivor.

Historical Debate

Was the Collapse of the First United Front Inevitable?

The question: Historians ask whether the Nationalist–Communist alliance was bound to fail because of structural differences, or whether different decisions might have prolonged or altered it. The question has political significance, since each party has told the story to justify its own course.

Interpretation A — Structural incompatibility: Some scholars argue that the alliance was doomed because the two parties pursued incompatible goals. The Nationalists, whose leaders came largely from military, landed, and commercial backgrounds, sought a strong centralized state and social order; the Communists sought class struggle and ultimately socialism. Peasant and labor movements threatened the interests of landlords and employers on whom the Nationalist army and finances depended. Given this conflict, the alliance could last only as long as it served the interests of both sides, and once the NRA reached the wealthy lower Yangtze, its break was almost certain.
Interpretation B — Contingency and decisions: Other scholars stress choices and circumstances. They point to the dependence of the Nationalists on Soviet aid until 1927; the divisions between Chiang, Wang Jingwei, and other Nationalist leaders, who did not all favor a rupture; the contradictory and delayed directives from Moscow, shaped by Soviet factional disputes; Chen Duxiu’s hesitation and the Communists’ decisions to disarm or restrain their own forces; and the influence of foreign powers and Shanghai financiers. On this view, the alliance might have continued longer, or broken in a different way, had key actors chosen otherwise.

Official and later narratives: For decades, People’s Republic historiography attributed the defeat largely to the “right opportunism” of Chen Duxiu and to Chiang’s betrayal; Nationalist historiography, particularly in Taiwan before the 1990s, emphasized Soviet subversion and defended the purge as a defense of the revolution. Post-1991 access to Russian archives and more critical scholarship on both sides have complicated both narratives.

Evidence: Party resolutions, Comintern correspondence, participants’ memoirs, diaries (such as those of Wuhan officials), contemporary reports, and analyses of financial and military records all bear on the question, but the sources are limited and partisan, and counterfactual reasoning cannot be proven.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how later conflicts are understood, and illustrates the difference between explaining why something happened and treating it as predetermined.

Section 61.23

The Northern Expedition’s Completion and the Limits of Unification

After the reconciliation of the Nanjing and Wuhan factions, the Northern Expedition resumed in April 1928 under a four-part coalition. Chiang Kai-shek led the First Army Group; Feng Yuxiang, whose Guominjun controlled much of the northwest and Henan, led the Second; Yan Xishan, the governor of Shanxi who had declared for the Nationalists, led the Third; and Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi, the Guangxi leaders, led the Fourth. These commanders were allies of convenience, each retaining his own army and territorial interests. Against them stood Zhang Zuolin’s Fengtian army, which controlled Beijing and the northeast, and which was weakened by earlier defeats and by rivalry.

The campaign advanced through Shandong, but in May it encountered a new complication when Japan, which had landed troops in Shandong to protect Japanese nationals and interests, clashed with NRA units at Jinan on 3 May. Fighting and the Japanese occupation of Jinan resulted in many Chinese deaths; figures vary widely by source and are contested. Chiang chose to avoid a wider confrontation and directed his forces around Jinan, an approach that angered critics but kept the campaign moving. On 3–4 June, as Zhang Zuolin withdrew by train toward Mukden, his carriage was blown up near Huanggutun; he died of his wounds. The Japanese Kwantung Army officer Kōmoto Daisaku was later identified as responsible, an event that anticipated further Japanese military initiative in Manchuria (Chapter 64). Nationalist troops, mainly from Yan Xishan’s forces, entered Beijing on 8 June 1928, and the city was renamed Beiping (“Northern Peace”) when the capital was formally placed at Nanjing.

The last piece fell into place in the northeast. Zhang Zuolin’s son Zhang Xueliang, who succeeded him, resisted Japanese pressure to declare independence and, on 29 December 1928, announced that the northeastern provinces would fly the Nationalist flag. This “changing of banners” (yiqi) gave the Nanjing government nominal sovereignty over the whole of China proper and Manchuria. In July 1928 Chiang and other leaders held a ceremony before Sun Yat-sen’s coffin in the Western Hills, reporting the completion of the Northern Expedition. Most foreign powers extended recognition to the Nanjing government, and in 1928 and 1929 the United States and several European countries negotiated tariff autonomy agreements, a first step in revising the unequal treaties.

Students should distinguish different meanings of unification. Nominal reunification was achieved: the Nationalist flag flew over almost all provincial capitals, and the Nanjing government was internationally recognized. Military authority was far less complete. The NRA now included the armies of Feng, Yan, and Li and Bai, and Chiang’s own forces were only one of several; within two years Chiang would fight a civil war against these allies (Chapter 62). Territorial control excluded Tibet, Outer Mongolia, and vast areas where local power persisted, and the Communists survived in remote areas. Social revolution had been checked and partially reversed: the unions and peasant associations had been crushed or placed under party control, and landlord power was largely intact. National integration, the construction of an administrative, fiscal, and legal system that functioned across regions, was a task only beginning in 1928.

Section 61.24

Chapter Review and Nationalist–Communist Revolution Checkpoint

Chapter 61 has traced the formation of two organized revolutionary movements, their cooperation, the construction of military and political institutions, the growth of mass movements, the Northern Expedition, and the rupture of 1927. The central argument is that the revolution of the 1920s was a coalition whose achievements and eventual disintegration both followed from the same process. The alliance made possible the creation of a party-army, the mobilization of workers and peasants, and the defeat of major militarist armies; the social conflicts it released, together with differences of purpose, personal rivalry, and external pressure, produced the violent split of 1927.

Students should be able to distinguish several developments that are often blurred. The First United Front was a bloc within the Guomindang, not a merger. Soviet assistance was important but did not determine Chinese decisions. The April purge, Chiang’s Nanjing government, and the Wuhan government’s later break with the Communists were related but separate stages. And the achievements of the Northern Expedition, the nominal unification of the country under a recognized national government, should be distinguished from the effective consolidation that Chapter 62 will examine, in which Nationalist state-building, regional rivalry, and Communist rural insurgency would shape the next decade.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 61 has examined how two revolutionary movements, the Guomindang and the Chinese Communist Party, emerged from the political disappointments and intellectual ferment of the May Fourth era, cooperated in the First United Front, and then violently divided. The central argument is that the revolution of 1921–1928 was a coalition whose achievements and whose collapse both arose from the same conditions: the need for organization and arms to overcome militarism and foreign privilege, and the social conflicts that mass mobilization released.

The two parties began from different positions. Sun Yat-sen led a loosely organized movement with a long revolutionary history but no reliable army; after the humiliation of 1922 he sought organizational help and arms from Soviet Russia. The CCP, founded in 1921 by a few dozen intellectuals, was tiny and at first had little connection with the workers it claimed to represent. The Comintern, driven by Soviet security concerns and by an evolving theory of anti-colonial revolution, encouraged cooperation, but Chinese leaders made their own decisions, and Chinese Communists such as Chen Duxiu and Zhang Guotao dissented from Moscow’s advice.

The First United Front was a bloc within the Guomindang rather than a merger. Communists joined the reorganized party as individuals while maintaining their own organization, and the arrangement rested on shared opposition to militarism and imperialism rather than agreement on ultimate goals. Participants understood the alliance differently, and the resulting ambiguity allowed rapid cooperation and contained the seeds of conflict. The reorganization of 1924 introduced Leninist organization, the doctrine of party tutelage, and a reinterpreted Three Principles of the People, which could be read in conservative or radical ways.

Institution-building transformed the revolutionary movement. The Whampoa Military Academy and National Revolutionary Army created a politically trained officer corps and the concept of the party-army, though the NRA was a hybrid that absorbed many regional forces. The Guangzhou government, with all its financial weaknesses and its tense relations with merchants, provided an administrative base. Campaigns against Chen Jiongming, the Merchant Corps, and the Yunnan and Guangxi armies consolidated Guangdong. Sun’s death in 1925 left a leadership contest in which Chiang Kai-shek, controlling the army, gained advantages over civilian leaders.

Social mobilization widened the revolution. The May Thirtieth Movement and the Canton–Hong Kong strike linked labor grievances to anti-imperialism; peasant associations in Guangdong and Hunan challenged landlord domination; students and women entered politics in unprecedented numbers. These movements gave the revolution speed and legitimacy, but they also provoked fear among landlords, merchants, and officers, and their demands outran the cautious policies of the party leaders. Different groups, from factory workers to landlords, experienced the revolution in sharply different ways, and many paid heavily for their participation.

The Northern Expedition, begun in July 1926, defeated Wu Peifu and Sun Chuanfang and brought the NRA to the Yangtze valley, aided by disunity among its opponents, defections, political work, and civilian support. Its progress also magnified internal divisions: the NRA grew rapidly by absorbing other forces, the Nationalists divided between Wuhan and Nanjing, and foreign powers, alarmed by events at Hankou and Nanjing, pressed for a more conservative course. The Zhongshan Warship Incident of March 1926 had already shown Chiang’s willingness to restrict Communist influence.

The year 1927 saw a sequence of ruptures that students should distinguish. In April, Chiang, supported by conservative party leaders, financiers, and Green Gang networks, launched a violent purge of Communists and unions in Shanghai and other cities and established a government at Nanjing. The Wuhan government continued to cooperate with the Communists until July, when, under pressure from its generals, the Comintern’s disruptive instructions, and fear of peasant radicalism, it expelled them. The Communists, devastated, abandoned the policy of cooperation, removed Chen Duxiu, and turned toward armed insurrection and rural bases. Historians continue to debate whether the collapse was inevitable.

In 1928 the Northern Expedition, resumed with a coalition of Chiang, Feng Yuxiang, Yan Xishan, and the Guangxi leaders, took Beijing, and Zhang Xueliang’s acceptance of the Nationalist flag completed nominal unification. This was a major achievement and brought international recognition, but it did not end regional militarism, resolve rural inequality, remove foreign privileges at once, or eliminate the Communist movement. The Nanjing government thus began with large claims and limited capacity. The chapter’s lessons, concerning coalition politics, the relation between armies and parties, the dynamics of mass mobilization, and the difference between military victory and state-building, are essential background for Chapters 62 through 65.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 61 matters because it explains how the political landscape of twentieth-century China was assembled. The Guomindang and the Communist Party, with their armies, mass organizations, and ideologies, emerged from the same intellectual environment, cooperated for a few years, and then became enemies whose rivalry shaped the Nanjing decade, the war with Japan, the Civil War, and the division of the Taiwan Strait. Understanding that the rupture was not simply predetermined allows students to see the choices and contingencies that mattered.

The chapter reveals themes that recur throughout this textbook: the relationship between military force and political authority; the tension between centralized state-building and regional power; the mobilization and disciplining of social movements; the interplay between domestic politics and foreign pressure; and the difficulty of turning a military victory into a durable political order. It also shows the importance of reading sources critically, since each party and each observer wrote to explain, defend, or condemn.

The revolution was also connected to the wider world. Soviet advisers, British gunboats, Japanese troops in Shandong, overseas Chinese donors, and anti-colonial activists from across Asia were part of its story, alongside Chinese workers, peasants, students, merchants, officers, and women whose experiences varied widely. Treating them as participants, not merely as background, helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, regionally varied, and unfinished. The next chapter turns to the task Nanjing now faced: building a state from a victory that was real but incomplete.
