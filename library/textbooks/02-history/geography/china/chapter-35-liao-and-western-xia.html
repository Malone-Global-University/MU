Part VI — Song China and the Multi-State East Asian World
35

Liao and Western Xia

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the year 1005, envoys from two rival courts met at Chanyuan on the Yellow River to end a war that neither side could win outright. One embassy represented the emperor of the Song dynasty, ruler of the wealthiest and most populous agrarian state in East Asia. The other represented the emperor of the Liao dynasty, a Khitan ruler whose court moved seasonally across the grasslands and forests of the northeast and who governed, from a network of shifting and fixed capitals, a population that included pastoral herders, farmers, city dwellers, and forest peoples speaking several different languages. Neither ruler addressed the other as a subordinate. Neither treaty text described the settlement as an act of submission by one side to the other. Instead, the two courts agreed to fixed annual payments, a defined border, reciprocal diplomatic protocol, and mutual security guarantees — an arrangement that would hold, with only occasional strain, for well over a century, and that would be followed by the expansion of regulated trade along the new frontier.

This chapter asks students to set aside an assumption that shapes much popular writing about this period: the assumption that the Song dynasty was simply "China" during the tenth, eleventh, and twelfth centuries, surrounded by nomadic peoples who existed at the edges of a Chinese world, occasionally raiding it, occasionally being absorbed by it, but never themselves constituting a full and independent political center. The archaeological, textual, and art-historical evidence examined in this chapter tells a different and considerably more interesting story. During the period covered by this chapter, East Asia was not organized around a single legitimate imperial center with peripheral neighbors. It was organized as a multi-state system, in which the Song shared the political stage with at least two other durable, sophisticated, literate, bureaucratically organized empires: the Khitan Liao dynasty, which for more than two centuries controlled a vast territory stretching from Manchuria and the eastern steppe into part of the North China Plain, and the Tangut Western Xia dynasty, which controlled the Ordos region and the strategically vital corridors of the northwest for almost two hundred years.

Both Liao and Western Xia were founded by peoples whom earlier generations of historians, following the language of Song-dynasty court documents, often described simply as "barbarians" pressing upon the borders of a civilized Chinese state. This chapter asks students to read past that framing. The Khitan and the Tangut were not simply raiders who eventually copied Chinese institutions until they became indistinguishable from a Chinese dynasty; nor were they simply nomads whose political achievements can be dismissed as derivative or temporary. Both peoples built genuinely original, durable states that combined institutions and practices drawn from the steppe, from settled agricultural societies, from Inner Asian political traditions stretching back to earlier confederations such as the Xiongnu and the Turks, and, selectively, from Chinese administrative models — combining these elements into governing systems suited to their own specific populations, environments, and political needs. Liao rulers developed a system of parallel government, administering pastoral and agricultural populations through separate institutions rather than trying to force a single administrative model onto radically different kinds of subjects. Tangut rulers, meanwhile, commissioned an entirely new writing system, developed a substantial body of Tangut-language religious and administrative literature, and constructed a state ideology that asserted their equality with, rather than subordination to, the Chinese imperial tradition.

The relationship between these states and the Song was not simply one of unbroken hostility. Warfare mattered, and this chapter does not minimize it: Song forces and Liao forces fought major campaigns in the late tenth century, and Song forces and Western Xia forces fought repeatedly across the eleventh century. But warfare gave way, in each case, to negotiated coexistence — annual payments described in different, sometimes deliberately ambiguous, terms by each side; formalized diplomatic protocol; regulated border markets; and, over time, patterns of mutual accommodation that allowed merchants, monks, envoys, and migrants to move across notionally fixed frontiers. Understanding how three rival imperial courts constructed a workable, long-lasting political order without any one of them conquering the others is one of the central historical problems this chapter asks students to examine.

This chapter also introduces a set of source and interpretation problems that recur throughout the study of Inner Asian history. Much of the written evidence historians have long relied upon for Liao and Western Xia was produced by Song officials and later, by the compilers of the official Chinese dynastic histories, whose authors approached Khitan and Tangut rulers from a distinctly Song-centered and often unsympathetic point of view. Only in recent decades has a growing body of archaeological evidence — Khitan-language funerary inscriptions, Liao tomb art, Tangut-language administrative and religious texts recovered from sites such as Khara-Khoto, and Buddhist manuscripts and woodblock prints produced under both dynasties — allowed historians to hear something closer to Khitan and Tangut voices on their own terms, even though much of that evidence remains difficult to read and only partially deciphered. This chapter treats that unevenness in the sources as a central methodological lesson rather than a minor caveat.

Geography anchors the entire chapter. The Liao state controlled the strategically decisive Sixteen Prefectures south of the Great Wall line, a heavily populated agricultural region whose loss the Song never fully accepted and whose recovery Song statesmen debated for generations. The Western Xia state controlled the Gansu, or Hexi, Corridor and the Ordos region, territory that commanded the overland trade routes connecting China to Central Asia. Neither state's location was accidental: both Liao and Western Xia positioned themselves astride the economic and military corridors that connected the agricultural heartlands of North China to the pastoral and commercial worlds of Inner Asia, and both drew much of their wealth and strategic importance from that position.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to describe the origins, institutions, societies, and diplomatic relationships of the Liao and Western Xia states; explain why historians increasingly describe the period as a multi-state system rather than a Song-centered order with peripheral neighbors; and evaluate the strengths and limits of the evidence available for reconstructing Khitan and Tangut history on terms other than those set by Song and later Chinese sources. This chapter also prepares students for Chapter 36, in which a fourth power, the Jurchen Jin dynasty, will destroy Liao and drive the Song court south of the Yangtze — a reminder that the multi-state world examined in this chapter was itself only one stage in a longer, still more complicated history of competing East Asian empires.

Historical Context

Chapter 30 examined the political fragmentation of the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period that followed the collapse of Tang authority, and Chapter 31 examined how the Song dynasty, founded in 960, gradually reunified most of the territory of the former Tang empire south of the Great Wall line under a government that emphasized civil administration and centralized bureaucratic control rather than autonomous regional military rule. That account, while accurate as far as it goes, leaves out a crucial piece of the picture: the Song never controlled, and after an initial period of contest never seriously attempted to reconquer, the entire territorial extent of the earlier Tang empire. To the north, in the region historians call the Sixteen Prefectures, and to the northwest, in the Ordos and Gansu Corridor regions, other states had already established themselves, or would establish themselves within a few decades of the Song founding, and would remain independent, militarily capable, and diplomatically influential for as long as the Song dynasty itself endured.

The Khitan people, from whom the Liao dynasty emerged, had a long history of interaction with Chinese dynasties and with other Inner Asian peoples well before the Song founding. Khitan groups are first clearly attested in Chinese sources of the fourth and fifth centuries CE, inhabiting a region in what is now eastern Inner Mongolia and western Liaoning, an ecological transition zone between the agricultural lands of North China and the open grasslands of the eastern steppe. For several centuries, Khitan groups existed as a loose confederation of tribes, sometimes allied with, sometimes subordinate to, and sometimes at war with neighboring powers, including Tang China, the Turkic and Uyghur steppe empires, and the Bohai (Balhae) kingdom of southern Manchuria and northern Korea. This long history of contact meant that by the time the Khitan built an independent empire in the early tenth century, Khitan elites already possessed considerable familiarity with Chinese administrative concepts, Inner Asian steppe political traditions, and the religious and cultural practices of neighboring Buddhist kingdoms such as Bohai — a foundation of accumulated political knowledge that shaped how the new Liao state would be built.

The Tangut people, from whom the Western Xia dynasty emerged, had a comparably long and complex history rooted in the northwestern frontier zone. Most historians connect the Tangut to Qiang-related populations of the Tibetan plateau's eastern margins who migrated northeastward over several centuries, eventually settling in the Ordos region and adjacent areas along the northwestern frontier of Tang China. Tangut leaders served, at various points, as regional military governors within the Tang and post-Tang political order, receiving imperial titles and the prestigious surname Li from Tang and later Song emperors in recognition of military service — a pattern of incorporation into the wider Chinese-centered political and honorific system that later Western Xia rulers would deliberately and dramatically reject when they proclaimed full imperial status in the eleventh century.

Students should understand that the very concept of a single, continuously "Chinese" political space controlling all of the territory associated with earlier unified dynasties such as the Han and Tang is itself a retrospective simplification. The Tang empire at its height incorporated, allied with, and exercised varying degrees of influence over an enormous range of peoples and polities across Inner Asia; when Tang central authority weakened and collapsed, as examined in Chapters 29 and 30, the region did not simply revert to a single successor state. It fragmented into multiple political centers, several of which — including the polities that would become Liao and Western Xia — proved considerably more durable and more capable of independent state-building than many later, Song-centered historical narratives have acknowledged.

This chapter's chronological scope runs roughly from the founding of the Liao dynasty in 907 (or, by some reckonings, its formal imperial proclamation in 916 under Yelü Abaoji) through the Western Xia dynasty's continued independent existence into the early thirteenth century, with particular attention to the period from the Song founding in 960 through the early twelfth century, when the multi-state order examined in this chapter reached its mature and most stable form. Chapter 36 will examine how this order was shattered by the rise of a fourth power, the Jurchen Jin dynasty, which destroyed Liao in the 1120s and drove the Song court south of the Yangtze River, ending the Northern Song period and inaugurating the Southern Song and Jin division of China examined in that chapter.

Section 35.1

The Multi-State Political World

Before turning to the specific histories of the Khitan Liao and Tangut Western Xia states, this chapter must establish an interpretive framework that differs from the one implicit in much older historical writing about this period. That older framework treats the Song dynasty as the legitimate continuation of Chinese imperial civilization, and treats Liao and Western Xia as peripheral, essentially foreign powers whose relations with the Song can be understood primarily as a story of Chinese defense against, and periodic humiliation by, non-Chinese "barbarian" states. This chapter asks students to set that framework aside in favor of one better supported by the evidence: East Asia during the tenth through early twelfth centuries was a multi-state system, in which several imperial courts, each claiming universal legitimacy in its own terms, coexisted, competed, warred, and negotiated with one another over an extended period, without any one court exercising, or seriously expecting to exercise, uncontested authority over the others.

A map of this period makes the point immediately. To the north of the Song, occupying Manchuria, part of the eastern steppe, and the Sixteen Prefectures south of the traditional Great Wall line, lay the Liao empire, controlling territory roughly comparable in extent to the Song's own. To the northwest, occupying the Ordos region and the Gansu Corridor, lay the Western Xia empire, controlling a smaller but strategically indispensable territory that commanded the overland routes connecting China to Central Asia. The Song itself controlled the densely populated agricultural heartland of the former Tang empire south of these two states, an area larger in population and generally wealthier in aggregate economic output than either Liao or Western Xia, but not, in any meaningful territorial or military sense, the unchallenged center of an otherwise unified Chinese political space.

Terminology matters a great deal in approaching this period, and students should use several key terms with particular care. The word "China," used loosely, can obscure rather than clarify the historical reality of this period: if "China" is taken to mean the territory later associated with the modern nation-state, then substantial portions of that territory, including regions in the northeast, the north, and the northwest, lay outside Song control for the entire period covered by this chapter, governed instead by Liao and Western Xia. If "China" is instead taken to mean a shared cultural and administrative tradition descended from earlier unified dynasties such as the Han and Tang, then Liao and Western Xia arguably participated in, adapted, and contributed to that tradition as much as the Song did, even as each state also maintained institutions, languages, and identities distinct from, and in some respects opposed to, exclusive Chinese cultural claims.

The word "tribute," similarly, requires careful handling. Song court documents and, later, the official Chinese dynastic histories compiled under subsequent dynasties often describe payments made by the Song to Liao and Western Xia using vocabulary historically associated with tribute relationships in which a subordinate state acknowledges a superior one. Yet the actual diplomatic protocol established at Chanyuan in 1005, examined in detail in Section 35.5, explicitly recognized the Liao and Song emperors as diplomatic equals, addressing one another as brothers rather than as sovereign and subordinate, even as each court's internal records sometimes described the relationship in terms more flattering to its own claims of superiority. Historians today generally treat the payments involved in these settlements as closer to a form of subsidized peace, or negotiated diplomatic tribute in a more neutral technical sense, than as evidence of Song subordination to Liao or Western Xia authority, while also recognizing that the payments provided real and sometimes considerable revenue to the receiving states.

The word "frontier" also deserves scrutiny. This chapter treats the borders between Song, Liao, and Western Xia not as fixed lines separating fundamentally different civilizations, but as negotiated, garrisoned, and, in ordinary times, surprisingly permeable zones across which merchants, monks, envoys, migrants, and, in some periods, refugees moved with reasonable regularity, subject to formal controls that were often only partially enforced in practice. Sections 35.5 and 35.8 will examine these frontier zones directly, showing that the lived experience of the border differed considerably from the way court documents on any side described it.

Finally, students should understand that claims to universal imperial legitimacy were not confined to the Song court. Liao rulers styled themselves emperors (huangdi) using the same title claimed by Chinese sovereigns since the Qin dynasty, while also maintaining distinctively Khitan political and ritual traditions rooted in steppe confederation practices. Western Xia rulers, upon proclaiming their empire in 1038, likewise claimed full imperial status, rejecting the subordinate regional-governor titles their predecessors had accepted from Tang and Song emperors. Section 35.9 will return to this question of parallel imperial legitimacy directly, but students should keep it in view from the outset of the chapter: this period's political history cannot be understood as the story of one legitimate empire defending itself against illegitimate outsiders. It is the story of multiple legitimate, if very differently organized, empires negotiating a shared political space.

Historical Vocabulary

Term
Parallel Imperial Legitimacy

Parallel imperial legitimacy refers to a historical condition in which two or more rival courts simultaneously claim full, universal imperial authority — rather than one ruler holding genuine sovereignty while others hold merely delegated or subordinate status — without either court being able to eliminate or permanently subordinate the other. In the period examined in this chapter, the Song, Liao, and, after 1038, Western Xia courts each used the imperial title huangdi, each maintained court rituals and historiographical traditions asserting their own cosmological and moral legitimacy, and each, in practice, was compelled by military and diplomatic circumstance to treat at least one of the others as a genuine equal rather than a subordinate.

This concept matters historically because it resists a common but misleading habit of treating only one court in a multi-sided political relationship as "really" legitimate, with the others cast as pretenders, usurpers, or foreign intrusions. Recognizing parallel imperial legitimacy allows historians to take seriously the fact that Liao and Western Xia rulers, court historians, and subjects genuinely regarded their own dynasties as fully legitimate empires, and that the diplomatic protocols examined in Sections 35.5 and 35.6 — including the reciprocal, non-subordinate terms of the Chanyuan settlement — reflect that reality in ways that later, more Song-centered historical narratives often obscured.

Section 35.2

Khitan Origins and the Rise of Liao

The Khitan people inhabited, for several centuries before the founding of the Liao dynasty, a region spanning parts of what is now eastern Inner Mongolia and western Liaoning province, an ecological transition zone where steppe grassland, forest, and river valleys suitable for limited agriculture met one another. This environment shaped a mixed subsistence economy: Khitan communities practiced pastoral herding of horses, sheep, and cattle, supplemented by hunting, and, particularly in more settled or southern areas, some agriculture, rather than following a purely nomadic pastoral pattern of the kind associated with earlier steppe confederations such as the Xiongnu examined in Chapter 16. Khitan society before unification was organized into multiple tribal groups, loosely linked through kinship, alliance, and shared ritual practice, and periodically dominated by, or allied with, more powerful neighboring states, including Tang China during periods of Tang strength, the Turkic and Uyghur steppe empires, and the Bohai kingdom of southern Manchuria.

The decisive figure in Khitan state formation was Yelü Abaoji, a leader of the Yila tribe who, in the first decades of the tenth century, consolidated authority over the various Khitan tribal groups through a combination of military success, strategic marriage alliance, and, significantly, a departure from earlier Khitan practice in which paramount leadership had rotated periodically among leading tribal groups. Abaoji instead established a hereditary, permanent form of rulership for himself and his line, formally proclaiming himself emperor between 907 and 916 — a title and a claim to permanent, dynastic authority that marked a fundamental transformation from the earlier confederation model of Khitan political organization. Students should recognize this as a specific and consequential political choice, not an inevitable development: other steppe confederations, including some of the Khitan's own neighbors, did not make this same transition to permanent hereditary rule, and Abaoji's success in doing so depended on his ability to overcome resistance from rival Khitan leaders who might otherwise have expected their own turn at paramount leadership under the older rotational system.

Two subsequent developments proved especially important in shaping the kind of state that Liao would become. First, in 926, Liao forces conquered the Bohai kingdom, a sophisticated, Buddhist, administratively developed sedentary state in southern Manchuria and northern Korea that had itself drawn extensively on Tang Chinese administrative and cultural models. The conquest of Bohai gave the young Liao state its first substantial experience governing a large, literate, agriculturally based, non-Khitan population organized according to bureaucratic principles quite different from Khitan tribal political tradition — an experience that provided an important precedent for the fuller system of governing sedentary populations that Liao would later develop for its Chinese subjects.

Second, in 936, the Liao court intervened decisively in a succession struggle among rival warlords in North China, providing military support to Shi Jingtang, who in gratitude ceded the Sixteen Prefectures — a substantial, heavily populated, agriculturally rich region south of the traditional Great Wall frontier, including territory around the strategically important cities later known as Beijing and Datong — to Liao control in 936 to 938, in exchange for recognition of Shi Jingtang's own regime, the Later Jin, as the legitimate ruler of the remaining Chinese territory. This transfer proved enormously consequential for the rest of the period examined in this chapter and the next: the Sixteen Prefectures gave Liao direct control of a large, wealthy, Han Chinese agricultural population and several major fortified cities, transforming Liao from a purely steppe-based confederation into a genuinely composite empire spanning pastoral, forest, and agricultural zones. Song rulers, once the Song dynasty was founded in 960, refused to accept this loss as final and made repeated attempts to recover the Sixteen Prefectures militarily, as examined in Section 35.5, but never succeeded in doing so.

Students should resist a common but misleading way of narrating this history, in which the Khitan begin as pure nomads and gradually "become Chinese" as they absorb more Chinese territory and Chinese administrative practices. The evidence examined in the following sections shows a more complex process: Liao rulers selectively adopted specific Chinese and Bohai administrative institutions for governing specific populations and specific purposes, while simultaneously maintaining, developing, and in some respects intensifying distinctively Khitan political, ritual, linguistic, and cultural institutions for governing the Khitan population and for expressing dynastic identity and legitimacy. The result was not a Khitan state gradually dissolving into a Chinese one, but a genuinely composite empire in which multiple governing traditions operated side by side, a pattern examined directly in Section 35.3.

Section 35.3

Liao Government and Dual Administration

The single most distinctive and historically significant feature of Liao governance was its system of dual, or parallel, administration, formally organized around a Northern Chancellery, responsible chiefly for governing the Khitan population and other pastoral and tribal peoples according to Khitan customary law and steppe-derived administrative practice, and a Southern Chancellery, responsible chiefly for governing the Han Chinese and other sedentary agricultural populations, particularly those of the Sixteen Prefectures, using administrative structures, official titles, and legal principles closely modeled on Tang and Five Dynasties Chinese precedent.

Historical Vocabulary

Term
Dual Administration

Dual, or parallel, administration refers to the Liao dynasty's practice of governing different populations within its empire through different administrative structures, laws, and official hierarchies, rather than imposing a single uniform system of government across the entire territory. Under this system, the Northern Chancellery administered the Khitan population and allied tribal and pastoral peoples through offices, titles, and legal customs rooted in Khitan and broader Inner Asian steppe political tradition, while the Southern Chancellery administered Han Chinese and other settled agricultural populations, especially in the Sixteen Prefectures, through a structure of prefectures, counties, and civil offices closely paralleling contemporary Chinese bureaucratic practice, including, eventually, a limited form of the examination system discussed in Chapters 31 and 34.

This system mattered historically because it allowed a single empire to extract revenue, mobilize labor and military service, and maintain political order across populations with fundamentally different economic bases, social structures, and legal expectations, without forcing pastoral Khitan communities into an agricultural administrative framework unsuited to their way of life, and without imposing unfamiliar steppe-derived obligations on settled Chinese farming communities accustomed to a different kind of governance. Historians debate exactly how rigidly the "dual" system operated in practice, and this question is examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, but the basic principle — that different populations warranted different administrative treatment within a single imperial structure — represents one of Liao's most historically significant institutional innovations, one that later multi-ethnic Inner Asian empires, including the Mongol Yuan dynasty examined in Chapter 39, would draw upon and adapt in their own ways.

At the apex of the Liao state stood the emperor, who held authority over both administrative systems and who, unlike a purely Chinese-style sovereign confined to fixed capital cities, maintained a seasonal pattern of movement between several capitals and a system of mobile encampments known as nabo, following long-established steppe practices of seasonal migration even while presiding over an increasingly bureaucratized empire. The Liao court maintained five capitals distributed across its territory, including a Supreme Capital in the Khitan homeland and a Southern Capital located at the site of modern Beijing, reflecting the geographically dispersed and culturally composite character of the empire as a whole. This pattern of seasonal, mobile rulership combined with fixed administrative centers illustrates precisely the kind of institutional blending this chapter emphasizes: rather than simply adopting a Chinese-style fixed-capital monarchy, Liao rulers maintained steppe-derived mobility as a central feature of imperial governance while also relying on fixed bureaucratic centers to administer settled populations.

Liao law similarly operated on a differentiated basis for much of the dynasty's history, with Khitan customary law generally applied to Khitan subjects and Chinese-derived legal codes generally applied to Han Chinese subjects, though the historical record shows some blurring and adjustment of this principle over time, particularly as legal disputes increasingly crossed ethnic and administrative lines in a genuinely mixed society. Liao also developed, over the course of the dynasty, a limited version of the Chinese civil service examination system for recruiting Han Chinese officials into the Southern Chancellery administration, though this system operated on a considerably smaller scale than the fully developed Song examination system examined in Chapter 31, and high military and political office within the Khitan-dominated Northern Chancellery generally remained reserved for Khitan aristocratic lineages rather than being opened to examination-based recruitment.

Military organization likewise reflected this dual character. Liao's core striking force consisted of Khitan cavalry, organized according to tribal and lineage structures and renowned for its mobility and effectiveness, particularly against the largely infantry-based Song armies examined in Section 35.5. At the same time, Liao maintained garrison forces, fortifications, and, when needed, conscripted or recruited Han Chinese and Bohai-descended troops for specific campaigns and for the defense of settled territory, integrating multiple military traditions within a single overall command structure controlled by the Khitan imperial court and leading aristocratic clans.

Students should take from this section a nuanced understanding of what "dual administration" actually meant in practice. It was not primarily a policy of rigid ethnic segregation for its own sake, nor was it simply a temporary transitional stage on the way to a fully unified, Chinese-style bureaucracy. It was, most persuasively, a pragmatic and evolving governing strategy suited to an empire whose component populations differed dramatically in economic organization, social structure, and historical experience, allowing the Liao state to extract resources, maintain order, and sustain its rule over a genuinely composite empire for more than two centuries — a considerably longer span than many purely Chinese dynasties of comparable size achieved.

Section 35.4

Liao Society, Economy, and Culture

Beyond the institutions of the imperial court, Liao society encompassed a genuinely diverse population whose daily lives, economic activities, and cultural practices varied considerably by region, occupation, and ethnic background. In the Khitan heartland and adjacent pastoral zones, most people continued to practice a mixed economy of herding, hunting, and limited agriculture, organized around extended kinship groups and seasonal patterns of movement that predated the Liao state and continued, with some modification, throughout its existence. In the Sixteen Prefectures and other agricultural territories, by contrast, Han Chinese farming communities continued long-established patterns of settled grain agriculture, market exchange, and village and lineage organization broadly continuous with practices examined in earlier chapters on Tang and Five Dynasties China, now operating under Liao rather than Chinese dynastic administration.

Cities within the Liao empire, particularly the Southern Capital at the site of modern Beijing and other major administrative centers, functioned as important sites of craft production, commerce, and cultural exchange, drawing merchants, artisans, and officials from Khitan, Han Chinese, Bohai-descended, and other backgrounds. Archaeological excavation of Liao tombs, particularly those of elite Khitan and Han Chinese officials, has yielded rich evidence of material culture, including fine ceramics produced in kilns operating under Liao patronage, metalwork, textiles, and elaborate tomb murals depicting hunting scenes, court life, and religious themes that illuminate aspects of Liao elite culture not well documented in surviving textual sources.

Religion played a significant role in Liao society, with Buddhism enjoying substantial imperial patronage throughout the dynasty's history. Liao rulers sponsored the construction of Buddhist temples and pagodas, several of which survive today as important architectural monuments, and supported the production and printing of Buddhist scriptures, including a large-scale project to carve and print a Khitan-sponsored edition of the Buddhist canon. This Buddhist patronage connected Liao to a broader East Asian and Inner Asian religious world that included Song China, the earlier Bohai kingdom, and neighboring Buddhist communities, even as Liao rulers also maintained distinctively Khitan ritual practices, including ancestor veneration and ceremonies connected to the imperial hunt and to seasonal movement between capitals, that had no direct equivalent in Chinese court ritual.

Language and writing present one of the most historically significant and, for a long time, most poorly understood aspects of Liao culture. The Khitan developed two distinct writing systems, generally referred to by modern scholars as the Khitan large script, created under Yelü Abaoji shortly after his imperial proclamation and apparently influenced by Chinese characters, and the Khitan small script, created somewhat later and apparently incorporating a more phonetic approach possibly influenced by contact with Uyghur script traditions. Both scripts were used for inscriptions, particularly funerary inscriptions for elite Khitan individuals, and, to a more limited extent, for administrative purposes, operating alongside continued use of Chinese script for many official documents, particularly within the Southern Chancellery administration. Modern scholars have made significant, though still incomplete, progress in deciphering the Khitan scripts, and this ongoing decipherment work has begun to provide historians with textual evidence produced by and for Khitan elites themselves, rather than filtered entirely through Chinese-language sources — an important corrective to the historically Song-dominated textual record.

Women's lives and status within Liao society varied by social position and ethnic background, and the surviving evidence, while limited, suggests that elite Khitan women in some respects exercised greater political and social visibility than was typical for elite women in contemporary Song China: Khitan empresses and empress dowagers played significant political roles at several points in Liao history, including as regents for young emperors, and Khitan marriage practices and inheritance customs differed in various respects from Chinese Confucian norms regarding female seclusion and property. Archaeological evidence from elite Khitan tombs, including grave goods and funerary portraiture, provides additional evidence of women's social position, though, as with much of the evidence for Liao society generally, this evidence is unevenly distributed and heavily weighted toward elite rather than ordinary experience.

Section 35.5

Song-Liao Warfare and Diplomacy

The Song dynasty, founded in 960 under Zhao Kuangyin, inherited from its Five Dynasties predecessors an unresolved grievance regarding the Sixteen Prefectures, which Song rulers regarded as rightfully part of the Chinese cultural and political sphere and which Song strategists believed were essential to the defense of the North China Plain against further steppe incursion, since the prefectures included mountainous and fortified terrain that had historically served as a defensive buffer. Song emperors Taizu and, particularly, Taizong launched major military campaigns in 979 and again in 986 attempting to recover the Sixteen Prefectures by force. Both campaigns ended in significant Song defeats, demonstrating that Liao's Khitan cavalry forces, combined with the fortified cities and defensive infrastructure of the Sixteen Prefectures themselves, could reliably repel Song attempts at reconquest.

These defeats had lasting consequences for Song strategic thinking and political culture, contributing to a Song military and political posture that emphasized defensive consolidation, diplomatic accommodation, and internal administrative strength over further offensive campaigns against Liao — a posture examined further in Chapter 31's discussion of Song civil government and its relationship to military policy. Liao, for its part, launched periodic raids and, in 1004, a major invasion deep into Song territory under the Liao emperor's personal command, advancing to the vicinity of Chanyuan on the Yellow River, within striking distance of the Song capital at Kaifeng.

Rather than resulting in a decisive military conclusion, the 1004 to 1005 campaign produced a negotiated settlement, the Chanyuan Covenant, examined directly in this chapter's Primary Source Window and Cause and Effect feature. The covenant established several key terms: a fixed border approximating the existing line of control; an annual payment from Song to Liao of silver and silk, described in Song sources using terminology that could be read as either a form of tribute or a form of subsidized peace, depending on interpretation; formal recognition of the Liao and Song emperors as diplomatic equals, addressing each other in official correspondence using kinship terminology implying parity rather than subordination; and the establishment of regulated markets along the border to facilitate legitimate trade, discussed further in Section 35.8.

The Chanyuan settlement proved remarkably durable, inaugurating more than a century of largely peaceful Song-Liao relations, interrupted only occasionally by localized border disputes and diplomatic tensions rather than by renewed major warfare. This durability itself deserves historical attention: it demonstrates that two rival imperial courts, each maintaining its own claims to universal legitimacy and neither willing to formally acknowledge subordination to the other, could nonetheless construct and sustain a stable, mutually beneficial diplomatic order over a long period, provided that both sides found the costs of continued warfare to outweigh the costs of formalized coexistence. Song strategists generally regarded the annual payments as considerably less costly than the expense of sustained military campaigning, while Liao rulers gained a predictable and substantial revenue stream without the risks and costs of continued warfare against a wealthy, populous, and increasingly well-defended Song state.

Section 35.6

Tangut Origins and the Rise of Western Xia

The Tangut people trace much of their ancestry to Qiang-related populations of the eastern margins of the Tibetan plateau, who migrated northeastward over several centuries during the Tang period and afterward, gradually settling in the Ordos region and adjacent areas of the northwestern frontier. This frontier environment, positioned at the intersection of agricultural North China, the Tibetan plateau, the Gansu Corridor's overland trade routes, and the Inner Asian steppe, shaped a Tangut economy and society that combined pastoral herding, oasis and river-valley agriculture, and active participation in long-distance trade, rather than fitting neatly into either a purely nomadic or a purely settled agricultural pattern.

Tangut leaders had long histories of political incorporation into the Tang and post-Tang Chinese-centered political order, serving at various points as regional military governors and receiving imperial recognition, titles, and even the prestigious Tang and Song imperial surname Li as marks of honor for military service, particularly service rendered in suppressing rebellions and defending frontier regions during the difficult final century of Tang rule and the subsequent Five Dynasties period. This pattern of incorporation continued into the early Song period, with Tangut leaders in the Ordos region generally accepting nominal Song overlordship and honorific titles while maintaining considerable practical autonomy over their own territory and followers.

This arrangement began to change decisively under the Tangut leader Li Jiqian in the late tenth century, who resisted Song attempts to draw Tangut territory into more direct administrative control and who began building a more independent and consolidated Tangut polity in the Ordos region, laying essential groundwork for the fuller state-building project his successors would complete. Li Jiqian's successors continued this process of consolidation over the following decades, gradually extending Tangut control westward into the Gansu Corridor, a development of enormous strategic and economic significance, since control of the corridor gave the emerging Tangut state command over the overland trade routes connecting China to Central Asia, examined further in Section 35.8.

The decisive break came under Li Yuanhao, who in 1038 formally proclaimed the founding of an independent empire, adopting full imperial titles and rejecting the subordinate regional-governor status his predecessors had accepted from Tang and Song emperors. This was a dramatic and deliberate political statement: rather than simply continuing to operate as an autonomous but nominally subordinate frontier power within the Chinese-centered political order, Li Yuanhao asserted that Western Xia constituted a fully sovereign empire, entitled to the same imperial dignity claimed by the Song and Liao courts. Song reaction to this proclamation was predictably hostile, precipitating a series of wars in the 1040s that, much like the earlier Song-Liao wars, ultimately produced a negotiated settlement rather than a decisive military resolution, with the Song court agreeing to make annual payments to Western Xia in exchange for at least nominal, face-saving recognition of a modified, less fully equal diplomatic status than Li Yuanhao had originally claimed — an ambiguous compromise that satisfied neither side completely but that, as with the Liao settlement, proved more durable than continued warfare.

Students should note an important comparative point here: while Liao's origins lay substantially in an independent steppe confederation with only partial and gradual prior integration into Chinese political structures, Western Xia's origins lay more directly in a Tang and Song frontier military-governor lineage that had operated for generations within, before ultimately rejecting, the Chinese-centered honorific and political order. This different starting point shaped, in ways examined further in Section 35.9, the specific character of Western Xia's assertion of independent imperial identity, which involved not merely military resistance but also a deliberate, comprehensive program of cultural and institutional state-building examined in the next section.

Section 35.7

Western Xia Government, Language, and Buddhism

Western Xia's founders pursued a strikingly deliberate and comprehensive program of state- and identity-building, one of the most historically significant elements of which was the creation of an entirely new writing system, the Tangut script, developed under Li Yuanhao's sponsorship in the early 1030s, shortly before the formal imperial proclamation of 1038. The Tangut script consisted of several thousand logographic characters, structurally influenced by Chinese characters in their general visual complexity and method of construction from smaller components, but representing an entirely distinct language and employing entirely distinct character forms, such that the script could not be read by literate Chinese speakers despite its superficial visual resemblance to Chinese writing.

The Tangut Script

The Tangut script was a logographic writing system created by order of Li Yuanhao in the early 1030s to render the Tangut language, a Sino-Tibetan language related to, but quite distinct from, Chinese. The script's creation should be understood as a deliberate act of political and cultural state-building rather than a purely practical or linguistic development: by commissioning an entirely new, distinctively Tangut writing system rather than continuing to rely solely on Chinese script for administrative and religious purposes, Li Yuanhao's court asserted a cultural identity and political legitimacy independent of Chinese textual and administrative tradition, comparable in political function, though not in specific form, to the Khitan scripts developed under Liao and, in a later period, the Mongol and Manchu scripts developed under subsequent Inner Asian dynasties.

Once created, the Tangut script was used extensively for government documents, legal codes, historical chronicles, and, especially, for an enormous body of translated and original Buddhist religious literature, printed using both woodblock and, at some sites, early movable-type techniques. Following the Mongol destruction of Western Xia in 1227, examined further in Chapter 38, knowledge of the Tangut script and language gradually disappeared, and the script remained largely undeciphered until the early twentieth century, when the discovery of a substantial cache of Tangut-language manuscripts and printed texts at the abandoned frontier city of Khara-Khoto provided modern scholars with the material needed to begin systematic decipherment, a process that remains incomplete but that has already transformed historians' ability to study Western Xia on its own linguistic and cultural terms rather than exclusively through Chinese-language sources.

Beyond the script itself, Western Xia rulers established a government structure that, like Liao's, drew selectively on Chinese administrative models — including a system of central ministries, provincial administration, and, eventually, a limited examination system for recruiting officials — while also maintaining distinctively Tangut institutions, including a system of monastic and religious administration, tribal-military organization for mobilizing Tangut fighting forces, and court ritual practices distinct from Chinese precedent. Western Xia law, similarly, combined elements adapted from Chinese legal tradition with distinctively Tangut provisions, and the survival of Tangut-language legal codes among the Khara-Khoto manuscripts has allowed modern historians to study Western Xia law in considerably more direct detail than is possible for many other aspects of Tangut government.

Buddhism occupied an especially central place in Western Xia religious and cultural life, arguably even more prominently than in contemporary Liao or Song court culture. Western Xia rulers sponsored extensive programs of Buddhist textual translation, rendering scriptures from Chinese and, in some cases, directly from Tibetan sources into the Tangut language, and supported large-scale woodblock printing projects to produce and distribute Tangut-language Buddhist texts. This intensive religious and textual production reflected Western Xia's position at the intersection of Chinese Buddhist, Tibetan Buddhist, and Central Asian Buddhist traditions, drawing on and synthesizing elements from multiple regional Buddhist cultures in a way that distinguished Western Xia Buddhism from the religious culture of either Song China or Tibet considered separately, and that would later influence Buddhist practice under the Mongol Yuan dynasty, whose rulers drew on Tangut Buddhist expertise and translated texts following their conquest of Western Xia.

Military organization in Western Xia relied heavily on a system of mobilized tribal levies supplemented by more specialized standing forces, and Tangut armies proved capable of sustained, effective resistance against both Song and, later, Liao forces despite Western Xia's considerably smaller population and territorial base than either of its larger neighbors — a testament to effective military organization, favorable defensive terrain in the Gansu Corridor and Ordos region, and the strategic importance of the territory Western Xia controlled, which gave it leverage in negotiations disproportionate to its raw population or territorial size.

Taken together, the evidence examined in this section supports a central interpretive point this chapter has emphasized throughout: Western Xia's extensive borrowing of Chinese administrative and Buddhist textual models did not represent a gradual absorption into Chinese civilization or a loss of independent Tangut identity. On the contrary, the same court that adapted Chinese-style ministries and legal principles also commissioned an entirely new script, sponsored a massive program of Tangut-language religious literature, and maintained distinctively Tangut military and social institutions — a pattern of selective borrowing in service of assertive, independent state and cultural identity that closely parallels, while also differing in important particulars from, the pattern examined for Liao in Sections 35.3 and 35.4.

Section 35.8

Trade and Frontier Networks

Diplomatic settlements such as the Chanyuan Covenant addressed relations between imperial courts, but the borders between Song, Liao, and Western Xia were also lived spaces, crossed regularly by merchants, herders, monks, envoys, and migrants whose experiences differed considerably from the formal categories used in court diplomacy. This section shifts attention from the interstate level examined in Sections 35.5 and 35.6 to the frontier itself, treated here as a zone of ongoing interaction rather than simply a defended line separating three sealed political and cultural worlds.

The Chanyuan Covenant and the later Song-Western Xia settlements each established officially regulated border markets, at which merchants from both sides could exchange goods under government supervision and, typically, taxation. These markets handled a range of commodities central to the economic relationship between agricultural and pastoral economies: Song merchants exported tea, silk, grain, and manufactured goods including ceramics and metalwork, while Liao and Western Xia merchants exported horses, livestock, furs, and other pastoral products, along with goods obtained through their own trade connections further into Inner Asia and, in Western Xia's case, along the Gansu Corridor's Central Asian trade routes. Horses deserve particular emphasis: Song China's agricultural economy could not easily sustain the large-scale horse breeding that steppe and frontier economies supported, making Song forces chronically dependent on horse imports from Liao, Western Xia, and other frontier sources for cavalry mounts, a strategic vulnerability that shaped Song military policy throughout the period examined in this chapter and the next.

Beyond officially sanctioned markets, considerable unofficial and technically illegal trade also crossed the Song-Liao and Song-Western Xia frontiers, particularly in commodities such as salt, where state monopolies on one or both sides of the border created strong incentives for smuggling, and in various goods subject to export restrictions, including, at various points, metals with potential military applications. Border officials on all sides faced the practical challenge of enforcing trade regulations across long, only partially garrisoned frontiers, and the historical record suggests that enforcement was often inconsistent, shaped as much by local economic relationships and the practical interests of border communities as by the formal policies articulated at distant imperial courts.

Religious travel likewise crossed these frontiers with some regularity. Buddhist monks moved between Song, Liao, and Western Xia territory, carrying texts, relics, and religious knowledge across political boundaries that mattered less to religious communities organized around shared doctrinal and institutional networks than to the secular administrations that nominally controlled the territory those networks spanned. Diplomatic envoys, too, traveled regularly between the three courts, carrying not only official communications but also, inevitably, informal information, gifts, and cultural influences that moved along with the formal business of diplomacy.

Migration across these frontiers, while less consistently documented than trade or diplomacy, also occurred in both directions and under varying circumstances: some Han Chinese subjects of the Sixteen Prefectures who found themselves under Liao rule maintained family and economic ties with Song territory to the south; some individuals fled across frontiers to escape taxation, conscription, or political persecution in their home states; and captives taken during periods of warfare, particularly the Song-Liao and Song-Western Xia wars examined in Sections 35.5 and 35.6, were in some cases repatriated under the terms of subsequent peace settlements, while others remained permanently within the territory of their captors, gradually becoming integrated into new communities.

This evidence should lead students to a more nuanced understanding of what "frontier" meant in this period than a simple military line on a map can convey. The Song-Liao and Song-Western Xia frontiers were simultaneously heavily garrisoned military boundaries, subject to real strategic concern and periodic conflict, and ordinary economic and social spaces, crossed daily by merchants, monks, envoys, and migrants pursuing entirely mundane purposes. Both realities were true simultaneously, and historians who focus exclusively on either the military-diplomatic dimension or the economic-social dimension of these frontiers risk missing half of the historical picture.

Section 35.9

Political Identity in the Song-Liao-Xia World

This chapter's concluding analytical section returns directly to the interpretive framework introduced in Section 35.1, asking students to synthesize the evidence examined throughout the chapter around a central question: how should historians understand the competing claims to imperial legitimacy advanced by the Song, Liao, and Western Xia courts, and what are the risks of retrospectively privileging one court's claims over the others?

Song court literati, particularly historians, statesmen, and officials associated with the Confucian scholarly tradition examined in Chapters 31 and 34, generally articulated a vision of political legitimacy rooted in claims of cultural and moral superiority: the Song, in this view, represented the authentic continuation of a civilizational tradition descended from the sage-kings, the Zhou dynasty, and the great unified dynasties of Han and Tang, a tradition centered on Confucian ethics, classical learning, ritual propriety, and a particular vision of proper government, from which the Khitan and Tangut, described in Song sources using terminology historically applied to non-Chinese "barbarian" peoples, were by definition excluded regardless of their political or military achievements. This vision provided a coherent and, for Song audiences, persuasive account of why the Song court deserved to be regarded as the legitimate center of civilization even when it could not match Liao or Western Xia militarily and even when it made regular payments to both states under the diplomatic settlements examined in Sections 35.5 and 35.6.

Liao and Western Xia rulers, however, did not accept this framing, and the evidence examined throughout this chapter shows that both courts constructed their own, competing claims to legitimate imperial status. Liao emperors used the Chinese imperial title huangdi, commissioned Chinese-style dynastic histories and administrative documents, and, through the Southern Chancellery, governed a substantial Chinese population using recognizably Chinese administrative methods — while simultaneously maintaining a specifically Khitan dynastic ideology rooted in claims of divine sanction, steppe political tradition, and, at various points, genealogical claims connecting Khitan rulership to earlier prestigious steppe and even, in some formulations, Chinese antecedents. Western Xia rulers, as Section 35.7 examined, went even further in constructing an independent identity, commissioning an entirely new script and an extensive body of distinctively Tangut religious and administrative literature specifically in order to assert cultural and political independence from Chinese civilizational claims, even while adapting numerous specific Chinese administrative and legal practices for practical governing purposes.

The formal diplomatic protocol established at Chanyuan in 1005, in which the Song and Liao emperors addressed one another using reciprocal kinship terminology implying parity, provides particularly clear evidence that the Song court itself, whatever its officials' private views about Khitan cultural inferiority, was compelled by practical political and military circumstances to accept a form of diplomatic equality with Liao that directly contradicted the exclusive civilizational claims articulated in Song scholarly and historical writing. This tension between practical diplomatic equality and continued rhetorical claims of exclusive cultural superiority represents an important and historically revealing contradiction within Song political culture, one that later Chinese dynastic historians, writing after the fall of both Liao and Western Xia, would generally resolve in favor of Song-centered narratives that minimized or dismissed the independent legitimacy of Khitan and Tangut rule — a historiographical pattern students should recognize and evaluate critically rather than simply reproduce.

Students should draw from this section a broader methodological lesson applicable well beyond this specific chapter: when historical sources overwhelmingly originate from one party to a multi-sided political relationship, as is substantially, though not entirely, true for this period, historians must actively seek out alternative forms of evidence — archaeological remains, non-Chinese-language inscriptions and manuscripts, art historical evidence, and comparative material from surviving Khitan and Tangut textual traditions — and must read even the dominant textual tradition critically, attentive to its own political interests and blind spots, rather than accepting its framing of legitimacy, civilization, and historical significance as simply accurate description.

Section 35.10

Chapter Review and Liao and Western Xia Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the Khitan Liao and Tangut Western Xia dynasties as major, independent participants in a multi-state East Asian political order that also included the Song dynasty, challenging any framework that would treat the Song as the sole legitimate "Chinese" state of this period with Liao and Western Xia as peripheral or foreign intrusions upon it.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. Liao and Western Xia were not failed or derivative versions of Chinese dynasties, nor were they simply nomadic raiders gradually absorbed into Chinese civilization; both were genuinely original states that combined institutions and practices drawn from Inner Asian steppe political tradition, from earlier sedentary states such as Bohai, and, selectively, from Chinese administrative and legal models, adapting these elements to the specific populations, environments, and political goals of their own composite empires. Liao's system of dual, or parallel, administration allowed a single state to govern pastoral Khitan and sedentary Han Chinese populations through different institutions suited to each, an innovation later drawn upon by subsequent Inner Asian empires. Western Xia's creation of an entirely new script and its extensive program of Tangut-language religious and administrative literature represented a deliberate, comprehensive assertion of independent cultural and political identity, pursued even as Western Xia's government simultaneously adapted numerous specific Chinese institutional models for practical purposes. Song-Liao and Song-Western Xia warfare, while real and consequential, ultimately gave way in each case to negotiated diplomatic settlements — the Chanyuan Covenant of 1005 and the subsequent Song-Western Xia peace of the 1040s — that established durable, if not always comfortable, patterns of coexistence involving fixed borders, formalized diplomatic protocol, annual payments, and regulated trade. Frontier zones between these states functioned simultaneously as defended military boundaries and as lived spaces of ongoing economic and social interaction, crossed regularly by merchants, monks, envoys, and migrants. And political legitimacy in this period was genuinely contested among multiple imperial courts, each constructing its own claims to universal authority, rather than residing exclusively or naturally in the Song court alone.

Historical Debate

What Was Liao's Dual Administration For?

The question: Was Liao's system of separate Northern and Southern Chancelleries best understood as a policy of rigid ethnic separation designed to preserve Khitan cultural and political dominance, as a pragmatic and flexible response to governing very different kinds of populations, or as a more fluid, evolving system that changed considerably over the dynasty's two centuries?

Interpretation A — Dual administration as structured ethnic and functional separation: Some historians emphasize that the Northern and Southern Chancelleries maintained genuinely distinct legal codes, official hierarchies, and, for the most part, distinct personnel, with high military and political office in the Northern Chancellery generally reserved for Khitan aristocratic lineages. On this view, the system functioned to preserve Khitan political dominance and cultural distinctiveness even as the empire incorporated a large, and in some regions numerically dominant, Han Chinese population.
Interpretation B — Dual administration as pragmatic, evolving governance: Other historians emphasize that the boundary between the two administrative systems was never absolute, that some Han Chinese officials achieved significant influence within the broader Liao government, that legal and administrative practice showed considerable local variation and change over time, and that the overall system should be understood primarily as a practical solution to the genuine challenge of governing pastoral and agricultural populations with fundamentally different economic bases, rather than as an expression of rigid ideological commitment to ethnic separation for its own sake.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — Liao administrative titles, legal provisions, the composition of officeholders over time, and archaeological evidence from Liao tombs and inscriptions — but weigh the degree of rigidity versus flexibility in the system differently, and historians increasingly note that the system likely became more flexible and more integrated in practice as the dynasty matured, even if its formal structure remained officially dual throughout.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes broader understanding of how conquest empires balance the preservation of a ruling group's distinct identity and privileges against the practical necessity of governing, and eventually integrating, a much larger subject population — a question relevant well beyond Liao to later Inner Asian empires including the Mongol Yuan and Manchu Qing dynasties.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 35.3 and 35.4, evaluate which interpretation is better supported, or explain why a combined explanation, acknowledging both structured separation and considerable practical flexibility, may be more accurate than either interpretation alone.

From the perspective of a Han Chinese farming household in the Sixteen Prefectures under Liao rule: Daily agricultural life, taxation, and local administration likely continued in forms broadly recognizable from earlier Chinese dynastic rule under the Southern Chancellery's administration, even as ultimate political authority, and the possibility of military conscription in service of Khitan campaigns, now rested with a Khitan imperial court rather than a Chinese one.
From the perspective of a Khitan pastoral household in the Liao heartland: Seasonal herding and hunting likely continued much as before Liao state formation, but participation in the empire's cavalry forces, tribute and labor obligations to the Northern Chancellery administration, and the prestige associated with Khitan aristocratic status within an increasingly wealthy and far-flung empire represented significant, if unevenly distributed, changes to community life.
From the perspective of a Song border merchant: Regulated frontier markets established after the Chanyuan Covenant offered legitimate opportunities to trade tea, silk, and manufactured goods for horses and other pastoral products, though official restrictions on certain goods, shifting diplomatic tensions, and the risks of smuggling penalties shaped the practical business of cross-border trade.
From the perspective of a Song court official debating frontier policy: The loss of the Sixteen Prefectures and the annual payments made to Liao and Western Xia likely appeared, to some officials, as a painful and continuing compromise of proper Chinese sovereignty and cultural precedence, while other officials likely regarded the arrangement as a prudent and cost-effective alternative to continued, unaffordable warfare against militarily capable neighbors.
From the perspective of a Tangut Buddhist monk involved in scriptural translation: Participation in the large-scale project of translating Buddhist texts into the newly created Tangut script likely combined genuine religious devotion with an awareness of the project's larger political significance in establishing an independent Tangut cultural and religious identity distinct from both Chinese and Tibetan Buddhist traditions.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Tang Frontier Politics to a Multi-State Order

What Changed

  • Frontier peoples who had previously served as regional military governors or tribal leaders within a Chinese-centered political order, such as the Tangut, transformed themselves into fully independent, imperially titled states rejecting subordinate status
  • Steppe confederations that had previously rotated leadership among tribal groups, such as the Khitan, adopted permanent hereditary imperial rule
  • Political legitimacy became genuinely contested among multiple simultaneous imperial claimants rather than residing, even nominally, in a single unified dynastic center
  • Diplomatic relationships between Chinese and non-Chinese states shifted from a framework of nominal Chinese superiority toward formalized, reciprocal equality in specific negotiated instances such as the Chanyuan Covenant

What Stayed the Same

  • Chinese administrative concepts, legal principles, and the Chinese written language continued to be selectively adapted by non-Chinese states, as they had been by earlier frontier peoples in contact with Tang China
  • The Gansu Corridor and the Sixteen Prefectures remained strategically vital corridors and territories, as they had been in earlier periods examined in Chapters 16 and 27
  • Buddhism continued to serve as a shared, though variously adapted, religious and cultural tradition connecting Chinese, Khitan, and Tangut courts
  • Frontier trade in horses, textiles, and other goods continued to connect agricultural and pastoral economies, as it had for centuries before this period

Who Experienced Change Most Directly

Tangut leaders and their followers experienced the most dramatic transformation, moving within a few generations from accepting subordinate Tang and Song military-governor titles to rejecting that status outright and constructing an independent script, government, and religious literature. Khitan aristocratic lineages likewise experienced significant change, shifting from a rotational tribal leadership structure to a permanent hereditary imperial court governing a vast, ethnically diverse empire.

Who Experienced Continuity

Han Chinese farming households in the Sixteen Prefectures and ordinary Khitan pastoral households likely experienced more continuity than change in daily economic and family life, even as the larger political order above them was transformed; merchants and monks moving along established frontier and trade routes likewise continued long-standing patterns of exchange that predated, and persisted through, the political changes examined in this chapter.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine transformation in political structure and the underlying continuities in administrative borrowing, strategic geography, and economic exchange helps students understand the Song-Liao-Xia order as a significant but not wholly unprecedented development, building on long-standing patterns of frontier interaction even as it produced a genuinely new multi-state political configuration.

Cause and Effect

The Chanyuan Settlement of 1005

Event or transformation: Following Liao's major invasion of Song territory in 1004, Song and Liao negotiators concluded the Chanyuan settlement of 1005, fixing the border, establishing annual Song payments to Liao, and formally recognizing the two emperors as diplomatic equals — replacing more than four decades of intermittent warfare with a durable diplomatic order.

Military causes

Song campaigns of 979 and 986 had already demonstrated the limits of Song military capacity against Liao's cavalry forces, while Liao's 1004 invasion, though advancing deep into Song territory, faced logistical strain and determined Song defense at fortified points, leaving neither side capable of a decisive, war-ending victory.

Fiscal and political causes

Continued warfare imposed heavy fiscal and administrative burdens on both states; Song court officials increasingly favored a negotiated settlement over the uncertain costs of further campaigning, while the Liao court had strong incentives to convert military pressure into a predictable, ongoing revenue stream.

Diplomatic causes

Both courts had functioning traditions of formal diplomatic negotiation and precedent for interstate agreements, providing a workable framework and vocabulary, including reciprocal kinship terminology, through which a settlement could be reached and formalized.

Effects

The settlement produced over a century of largely stable Song-Liao relations, regulated frontier trade, and a durable template of fixed payments and formal diplomatic parity that Song negotiators would draw upon again in the 1040s settlement with Western Xia, establishing a broader pattern of negotiated coexistence across the multi-state order examined in this chapter.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains why the Chanyuan settlement succeeded where earlier military campaigns had failed to resolve Song-Liao tensions. Most historians favor explanations combining military stalemate, fiscal pragmatism, and the availability of workable diplomatic precedent, rather than treating any one factor as sufficient on its own.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Border Market Town

Evidence from Song administrative records and archaeological remains allows historians to offer a cautious reconstruction of daily life in a regulated border market town of the kind established after the Chanyuan Covenant and the subsequent Song-Western Xia settlement. Such towns likely combined a permanent local population engaged in agriculture, craft production, and innkeeping with a fluctuating population of merchants, government inspectors, tax collectors, and occasional travelers, including monks and diplomatic couriers passing through en route to or from the neighboring court.

Market days likely brought considerable activity, with Song merchants offering tea, silk, grain, and manufactured goods for exchange against horses, sheep, furs, and other pastoral products brought by Liao or Western Xia traders, all conducted under the supervision of border officials responsible for collecting duties and enforcing restrictions on prohibited goods such as certain metals and, at various points, salt subject to state monopoly. Local households in these towns likely depended significantly on this cross-border commerce for their livelihoods, giving them a practical stake in continued peaceful relations that may have differed considerably from the more abstract concerns about sovereignty, precedence, and cultural superiority debated at the distant imperial courts.

Students should recognize that most people living in these frontier zones, whatever their formal political allegiance, likely experienced the Song-Liao-Xia order primarily through the practical realities of trade, taxation, and the ever-present possibility of renewed conflict, rather than through the elevated diplomatic and civilizational rhetoric that dominates the surviving court records from all three states.

Regional and Frontier Context

The Sixteen Prefectures and the Northwest Corridor

The Sixteen Prefectures and the Gansu Corridor, the two territorial linchpins of Liao and Western Xia power respectively, illustrate a broader theme relevant across this textbook: strategically located frontier regions often exercised influence on the wider course of Chinese and East Asian history disproportionate to their population or size, precisely because control of such regions conferred military, economic, or logistical advantages that no rival power could easily match. The Sixteen Prefectures combined defensible mountainous terrain with substantial agricultural wealth, making them valuable both as a buffer against invasion from the north and as a source of revenue and manpower — which is precisely why the Song court never accepted their loss as final, even though it never succeeded in recovering them by force. The Gansu Corridor, meanwhile, commanded the overland trade routes linking China to Central Asia, examined in earlier chapters in connection with the Han and Tang Silk Roads, giving Western Xia a degree of strategic leverage and commercial revenue that its relatively modest population would not otherwise have supported.

Students should resist describing either region simply as a "gap" in Song territory or a symbol of Song weakness. Both regions functioned as genuinely contested, strategically vital spaces whose status was actively negotiated, fought over, and diplomatically managed by all parties involved, and whose non-Song rulers built durable, sophisticated states precisely by controlling them.

China and the Wider World

Eurasia Before the Mongols

The multi-state order examined in this chapter connected East Asia to a wider Eurasian world of competing steppe and sedentary states in the centuries before the Mongol conquests examined in Chapter 38. Liao's relationship with Bohai, its position relative to Uyghur and other Central Asian polities, and its own steppe political traditions connected it to a long lineage of Inner Asian confederations stretching back to the Xiongnu and Turks examined in earlier chapters. Western Xia's control of the Gansu Corridor placed it directly astride the overland trade networks linking China to Central Asia, the Islamic world, and, indirectly, the Mediterranean, a position that made Western Xia an important, if often overlooked, participant in the broader Eurasian commercial and religious exchanges examined in Chapters 16 and 27. The extensive Buddhist textual and artistic exchange connecting Western Xia, Tibet, Song China, and Central Asian Buddhist communities further illustrates that this period's most historically significant developments were not confined to any single state's internal history but emerged from ongoing interaction across a wider Inner and East Asian world — a pattern that would intensify dramatically, and violently, with the rise of the Mongol empire examined later in this textbook.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Khitan Liao and Tangut Western Xia dynasties as major, independent states that shared the East Asian political stage with the Song dynasty during the tenth through early twelfth centuries, challenging the common but misleading assumption that the Song represented the sole legitimate "Chinese" power of this period while Liao and Western Xia constituted peripheral or foreign interruptions of an otherwise unified Chinese political history.

Students have learned that the Khitan people, before founding the Liao dynasty, possessed a long history of interaction with Chinese, Bohai, and other Inner Asian states, and that Yelü Abaoji's unification of the Khitan tribes and adoption of permanent hereditary imperial rule in the early tenth century represented a deliberate and consequential political transformation rather than an inevitable development. The conquest of Bohai in 926 and the acquisition of the Sixteen Prefectures in 936 to 938 transformed Liao from a steppe confederation into a genuinely composite empire spanning pastoral, forest, and agricultural zones, a transformation that Liao's distinctive system of dual, or parallel, administration was specifically designed to manage.

The chapter examined this dual administration in detail, explaining how the Northern and Southern Chancelleries allowed Liao to govern Khitan pastoral populations and Han Chinese agricultural populations through different institutions suited to each, while noting that historians continue to debate how rigid or flexible this system actually was in practice. The chapter also examined Liao society, economy, religion, and material culture beyond the imperial court, emphasizing the importance of Buddhism, the significance of the Khitan large and small scripts, and the relatively prominent political role of elite Khitan women.

Turning to Song-Liao relations, the chapter traced the failure of Song military campaigns to recover the Sixteen Prefectures and the subsequent negotiation of the Chanyuan Covenant in 1005, arguing that this settlement's fixed payments, defined border, and formal diplomatic parity between the two emperors inaugurated a durable, largely peaceful century of coexistence that decisively contradicts any simple narrative of continuous Song-Liao hostility.

The chapter then turned to Western Xia, tracing Tangut origins among Qiang-related populations of the northwestern frontier, the gradual consolidation of Tangut power under Li Jiqian and his successors, and Li Yuanhao's decisive 1038 proclamation of full imperial status, which rejected the subordinate regional-governor titles his predecessors had accepted from Tang and Song emperors. The chapter examined Western Xia's remarkable program of deliberate state- and identity-building, centered on the creation of the Tangut script and an extensive body of Tangut-language Buddhist and administrative literature, arguing that this program represented an assertive construction of independent cultural and political identity rather than a simple by-product of Chinese cultural influence.

The chapter's examination of trade and frontier networks demonstrated that the borders between Song, Liao, and Western Xia functioned simultaneously as defended military boundaries and as lived, permeable spaces crossed regularly by merchants, monks, envoys, and migrants, with horses, tea, silk, and other goods moving across regulated and unregulated channels alike in ways that connected agricultural and pastoral economies on both sides of every frontier examined in this chapter.

Finally, the chapter's synthesis in Section 35.9 argued that political legitimacy in this period was genuinely and simultaneously claimed by multiple imperial courts, each constructing its own account of universal authority, and that historians must read the heavily Song-centered surviving textual tradition critically, supplementing it wherever possible with archaeological and non-Chinese-language evidence, rather than accepting Song claims to exclusive civilizational legitimacy as an accurate description of this period's actual political reality.

This chapter's account of a stable, if periodically contested, three-way multi-state order sets up a crucial contrast with Chapter 36, which will examine how the rise of a fourth power, the Jurchen Jin dynasty, shattered this order within little more than a generation, destroying Liao, driving the Song court from North China entirely, and creating an entirely new and, in important respects, less stable political configuration across the region.

Two distinct writing systems developed for the Khitan language, used mainly for inscriptions and limited administrative purposes.

The 1005 diplomatic settlement establishing fixed Song-Liao borders, annual payments, formal diplomatic parity, and regulated trade.

Fixed quantities of silver and silk (or other goods) paid under diplomatic settlements such as Chanyuan, interpreted variously by historians as tribute or subsidized peace.
Tangut
A people descended substantially from Qiang-related populations of the eastern Tibetan plateau margins, who founded Western Xia.
Li Yuanhao
The Tangut ruler who proclaimed the independent Western Xia empire in 1038 and sponsored the creation of the Tangut script.

The Tangut-ruled empire controlling the Ordos region and Gansu Corridor from 1038 until its destruction by the Mongols in 1227.

The area enclosed by the great northern bend of the Yellow River, a core Tangut settlement area.

A Western Xia frontier city whose ruins yielded a major cache of surviving Tangut-language manuscripts.

The historical condition in which multiple courts simultaneously claimed universal imperial authority without one being subordinate to another.

A framework describing how neighboring, formally independent states influence and compete with one another's development.

The Chinese imperial title ("emperor") claimed by Song, Liao, and, after 1038, Western Xia rulers alike.

The scholarly disagreement over whether non-Han dynasties such as Liao and Western Xia were gradually absorbed into Chinese culture or maintained distinct, independent identities while selectively adapting Chinese institutions.
Li Jiqian
The late tenth-century Tangut leader who began consolidating independent Tangut power in the Ordos region.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a period in Chinese and East Asian history that is too often flattened into a simple story of Song China defending itself against nomadic outsiders. Understanding the Liao and Western Xia dynasties on their own terms — as durable, innovative, genuinely legitimate empires in their own historical context, rather than as peripheral disturbances to a Song-centered narrative — matters because it corrects a persistent distortion in how this period has often been taught and remembered, one rooted substantially in the perspective of the very Song-produced sources on which so much of the surviving historical record depends.

The institutions examined in this chapter — Liao's dual administration, Western Xia's deliberately constructed script and religious literature, and the diplomatic machinery that produced the Chanyuan Covenant and the subsequent Song-Western Xia peace — did not vanish without consequence once these particular states came to an end. Liao's approach to governing a composite empire of pastoral and sedentary populations anticipated, and in some respects directly influenced, the administrative strategies later Inner Asian empires, including the Mongol Yuan and Manchu Qing dynasties examined in later chapters, would develop for their own vastly larger and more diverse territories. The pattern of formalized, negotiated coexistence between rival imperial courts established at Chanyuan likewise offered a template, imperfect and eventually superseded, for how large states with incompatible claims to legitimacy might nonetheless construct a workable shared order.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill: reading a dominant historical narrative critically, attending carefully to whose interests and perspectives that narrative serves, and actively seeking out alternative forms of evidence — archaeological, art historical, and drawn from surviving Khitan- and Tangut-language sources — when the textual record is heavily weighted toward one party in a multi-sided historical relationship. This skill will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook, particularly in Chapter 39's examination of the Mongol Yuan empire and Chapter 48's examination of Qing frontier policy, both of which raise comparable questions about whose perspective should shape the historical narrative.

As students move forward into Chapter 36's examination of the Jurchen Jin conquest of Liao and the subsequent division of China between the Jin and the Southern Song, they should carry with them the central lesson of this chapter: the political map of East Asia during this period was never simply a single Chinese center surrounded by peripheral outsiders. It was a genuinely multipolar world of competing, innovative, and durable states, each constructing its own vision of legitimate empire, whose interactions — diplomatic, commercial, military, and cultural — shaped the subsequent course of Chinese and East Asian history as profoundly as any single dynasty's internal development could have done on its own.
