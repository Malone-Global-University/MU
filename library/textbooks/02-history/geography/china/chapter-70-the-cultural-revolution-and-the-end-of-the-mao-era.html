Part XI — Maoist China

The Cultural Revolution and the End of the Mao Era

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

On the morning of August 18, 1966, roughly a million people filled Tiananmen Square to see Mao Zedong. Thirteen days earlier, across the city, a school administrator had been beaten to death by her own students. Both events belong to the same history.

At the rally, Mao appeared in a plain green military-style uniform. A teenage student from a Beijing girls' school fastened a red armband marked "Red Guard" onto his sleeve, and the crowd, mostly secondary-school and university students, responded with a roar. Mao had just publicly signaled, in a short sequence of speeches, poems, and documents, that the young were to be the shock troops of a new movement. It was called the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution. Mao's supporters presented it as a way to prevent the Communist revolution from decaying into a privileged bureaucracy. Within weeks, it would become a campaign in which schoolchildren denounced teachers, crowds ransacked homes and temples, and officials who had spent decades in the revolution were paraded in dunce caps.

The school was the Girls' Middle School attached to Beijing Normal University. Its vice-principal, Bian Zhongyun, was beaten by students on August 5, 1966, left in a courtyard cart, and died. She is among the best-documented early victims of the movement, and the student who later pinned the armband on Mao attended the same school. Decades later, that student apologized in public for the school's violence. The juxtaposition matters because the Cultural Revolution cannot be told only as a story of Mao's political maneuvering, or only as a story of ordinary people's suffering. It was both, and it unfolded through the interaction of a leader's goals, a Party leadership in conflict, an army, mass organizations, local officials, and millions of individuals making choices under pressure.

This chapter studies the decade from the movement's launch in 1966 to Mao's death on September 9, 1976; the Chinese Communist Party's later 1981 historical resolution instead dated the movement's end to October 1976, after the arrest of the Gang of Four. It asks why Mao launched the movement, how a campaign directed against "those in authority taking the capitalist road" became a nationwide upheaval, and how its character changed from stage to stage. The student mobilization of 1966, the worker-led power seizures and armed factional fighting of 1967–1968, the military-run consolidation after 1968, the crisis around Lin Biao in 1971, and the leadership contests of 1973–1976 were distinct phases with distinct logics. Treating them as ten identical years obscures more than it explains.

What changed was immense. Party committees, ministries, universities, and local governments were overthrown or hollowed out and then rebuilt under army supervision. Millions of people were denounced, detained, beaten, or driven to suicide; many were killed. Roughly seventeen million urban youths were eventually sent to live in villages and frontier farms. Education, scientific work, publishing, and the performing arts were reorganized around political criteria. What continued was equally important: the state still ran factories, railways, and rural communes; harvests grew; barefoot doctors expanded village health care; nuclear and space programs advanced; and China's diplomacy made an extraordinary turn, from confrontation with both superpowers to a strategic opening to the United States.

Those affected included Mao's closest colleagues, students on every side of the movement's divisions, factory workers who joined rebel organizations, teachers and writers who were targeted, soldiers who were ordered to "support the Left," villagers who watched their landlord-labeled neighbors tormented, ethnic minority communities subjected to campaigns of suspicion, and the young people who left home for the countryside. Different groups experienced the same events very differently, and the same person might be a persecutor in one year and a victim in the next.

Historians work with unusually contested evidence. Official documents were issued in the middle of factional struggle. Red Guard newspapers were partisan. Memoirs written decades later shape and are shaped by memory and by the political climate in which they were composed. Local gazetteers and Party investigations conducted after 1978 offer detailed but incomplete counts of the dead. Foreign observers saw only a small part of the country. This chapter therefore distinguishes, wherever it can, between established events, estimates, official judgments, and open scholarly debate. The Chinese Communist Party's own 1981 assessment, discussed in §70.44, is treated as a historical source in its own right.

The chapter connects to larger themes of the textbook: the changing relationship between Party, state, army, and society that began in Chapters 61–62; the revolutionary mobilizations and campaigns of Chapters 66–68; the ideological conflicts of Chapter 69; and the post-Mao transition of Chapter 71, which begins with the arrest of Mao's closest radical colleagues in October 1976. It also connects China to world history, through Cold War rivalries, the Vietnam War, global youth radicalism, and the diplomatic realignment of the early 1970s that reshaped the international order.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain Mao Zedong's political and ideological objectives in launching the Cultural Revolution, and distinguish them from the outcomes the movement produced.
  2. Trace the sequence of events from the Wu Han controversy of 1965 to the Sixteen Points of August 1966.
  3. Describe the organization, social composition, and internal divisions of the Red Guard movement.
  4. Distinguish early Red Guard violence, armed factional conflict, official purges, and military-run campaigns as different forms of political violence.
  5. Analyze the January Storm in Shanghai, the Wuhan Incident, and the rise of revolutionary committees as turning points in the breakdown and reconstruction of authority.
  6. Evaluate the role of the People's Liberation Army, including both its stabilizing and coercive functions.
  7. Assess the evidence for, and competing interpretations of, the Lin Biao Incident of September 1971.
  8. Compare the experiences of Red Guards, persecuted officials, intellectuals, workers, ethnic minorities, rural residents, and sent-down youth.
  9. Interpret primary sources from the period, including the May 16 Circular, the Sixteen Points, UN Resolution 2758, and the Shanghai Communiqué.
  10. Explain how China's foreign policy changed between the 1969 border clashes with the Soviet Union and the 1972 opening to the United States.
  11. Connect the leadership struggles of 1973–1976 to the death of Mao and the succession crisis, while recognizing that economic, scientific, and public-health activity continued.
  12. Contextualize later official and scholarly assessments of the Cultural Revolution as interpretations rather than neutral records.

  2. How did a dispute about a historical play become a nationwide political upheaval?
  3. Why did so many young people join the Red Guards, and why did they divide into hostile organizations?
  4. What distinguishes the violence of 1966 from the violence of 1967–1968 and from the repression of 1968–1971?
  5. Was the People's Liberation Army a force for restoring order, a participant in repression, or both?
  6. How did ordinary people, in cities and villages, experience the movement differently from leaders in Beijing?
  7. How did China's diplomacy change dramatically while its domestic politics remained turbulent?
  8. What can historians establish about the Lin Biao Incident, and what remains uncertain?
  9. Which parts of Chinese society continued to develop during the decade, and which were damaged or disrupted?
  10. How do later official judgments, memoirs, and scholarly reconstructions shape what we know about the period?

Historical Context

By 1965, the People's Republic had recovered from the catastrophe of the Great Leap Forward and its famine (Chapter 68), but the recovery left unresolved disagreements (Chapter 69). Economic readjustment under Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, and others had restored grain output, reopened rural markets in some places, and rebuilt industrial planning. Mao had accepted much of this, yet he increasingly saw it as drifting from socialist principles. His anxieties were sharpened by the Sino-Soviet split: Nikita Khrushchev's 1956 denunciation of Stalin and, after 1964, the Soviet leader's removal convinced Mao that a Communist party could produce a privileged bureaucratic stratum, which he called revisionism.

The society in which the movement erupted was organized through institutions that shaped how it unfolded. Urban residents belonged to work units (danwei) that supplied employment, housing, and rations; rural residents belonged to people's communes, production brigades, and teams; and a household registration system limited movement between city and countryside (Chapter 67). Since land reform, families had been assigned class labels, such as landlord, rich peasant, worker, poor peasant, or "bad element," which were inherited and affected schooling and career prospects. These labels created a ready-made list of potential targets and a source of resentment among those who held "good" and "bad" backgrounds alike.

The Party itself was a hierarchy of committees from the Central Committee down to village branches, supervised from Beijing by the Secretariat under Deng Xiaoping and the Politburo Standing Committee. Beside it stood the army. The People's Liberation Army (PLA) had been reshaped since 1959 by Lin Biao, who replaced the purged Peng Dehuai as defense minister, emphasizing political study of Mao's writings. The PLA's General Political Department compiled the pocket-sized Quotations from Chairman Mao Zedong in 1964, a text that would become the movement's emblem. Mao enjoyed enormous personal prestige as the founder of the state, and he increasingly used it to bypass institutions he distrusted.

Internationally, China was in an uncomfortable position in 1965–1966. It had broken with Moscow, confronted the United States over Vietnam and Taiwan, and had weak ties with much of the non-aligned world after a series of setbacks in Africa and Asia (Chapter 69). Many Chinese leaders feared simultaneous threats from the north and south. This sense of siege influenced the Third Front program that moved defense industries into the interior (§70.32), and later contributed to the strategic reassessments that produced the opening to Washington.

It is also important to recognize what "China" meant in this period. The People's Republic governed the mainland, including the Tibet and Xinjiang regions and the Inner Mongolian Autonomous Region; the Republic of China government on Taiwan (Chapter 65) held the United Nations seat until 1971; Hong Kong and Macao remained under British and Portuguese administration. Questions of territory, ethnicity, and sovereignty therefore ran through both the domestic and foreign history of the decade. This textbook follows the convention of calling the 1966–1976 movement the "Cultural Revolution" while recognizing that the term was Mao's own, and that the 1981 official verdict rejected its description as a revolution in any positive sense.

Division I — Origins and Launch of the Cultural Revolution, 1965–1966

Division purpose: to explain how conflicts within the leadership became a nationwide campaign that challenged established Party institutions.

70.1 Mao Zedong and the Origins of the Cultural Revolution

The Cultural Revolution did not have a single cause, and historians separate several strands. The first was ideological. Mao feared that China's socialist state could follow the Soviet path: a ruling party whose officials enjoyed privileges, identified with their own institutions, and drifted from the poor. He called the tendency "revisionism" and saw its signs in the economic readjustment after the Great Leap Forward, in the persistence of bourgeois and traditional influences in education and the arts, and in officials who, in his view, treated the revolution as finished. The Socialist Education Movement of 1962–1966 (Chapter 69) had already named "persons in authority taking the capitalist road" as the main enemy within the Party.

The second strand was political. After the Great Leap famine, Mao's authority was questioned in private by senior colleagues, and he withdrew from daily administration. Liu Shaoqi, as head of state, and Deng Xiaoping, as head of the Secretariat, ran the Party's machinery and, in Mao's eyes, increasingly acted without consulting him. Mao also worried about succession and about whether the revolution would survive him. Scholars disagree about weighting: some emphasize genuine ideological conviction, others the defense of Mao's personal power, and most recognize that for Mao the two were intertwined. A student should avoid reading either motive back as the sole explanation, and should distinguish Mao's stated aims from the outcomes, which differed sharply from what he said he wanted.

Other leaders carried their own agendas. Lin Biao sought to demonstrate the army's loyalty and expand his own standing. Jiang Qing, who had been politically sidelined for years, saw an opportunity in cultural policy. Provincial leaders and ministry officials responded to signals from Beijing with a mixture of loyalty, calculation, and fear. The movement was thus shaped from the beginning by multiple actors interpreting Mao's signals, not just by a single plan being executed.

The leadership struggle did not take place outside institutional history. During the early 1960s the Central Committee, the Secretariat, state ministries and regional Party organizations had regained some room to administer recovery. The Socialist Education Movement then made the identity of supposedly unreliable officials a central political issue. Mao interpreted the ability of a bureaucratic apparatus to obstruct his priorities as evidence of a political danger within the socialist state. In this setting a campaign against revisionism could also operate as a campaign against the officials who controlled everyday administration. The tension between revolutionary renewal and the destruction of stable institutions shaped the movement from its earliest months.

70.2 The Wu Han Controversy and the Political Struggle of 1965

The first public step was an attack on a play. Hai Rui Dismissed from Office was written by Wu Han, a historian of the Ming dynasty and deputy mayor of Beijing, between 1959 and 1961. It retold the story of a sixteenth-century official who criticized an emperor and was removed. Mao had urged officials to emulate Hai Rui's candor in 1959, but after he dismissed Defense Minister Peng Dehuai at the Lushan conference that year, critics could read the play as an allegory about Peng's treatment. Mao later made that connection explicitly.

Jiang Qing, working with Zhang Chunqiao and Yao Wenyuan in Shanghai, arranged for Yao to write a long critique, published in Shanghai's Wenhui Bao on November 10, 1965. Its significance lay in its method: political attack presented as literary and historical criticism, launched from outside Beijing. The Beijing authorities, led by Mayor Peng Zhen, were slow to reprint it, and when the People's Daily did so on November 30, it framed the matter as an academic discussion. In Mao's view, this was an attempt to shield Wu Han and to control the debate. The historical lesson is that in the PRC, cultural criticism could be a form of political intervention, and that disputes about who controlled the press and the interpretation of history were disputes about power.

Wu Han's drama had a life before the controversy. Historical writing and theater in the early People's Republic offered intellectuals some scope to discuss upright officials, abuses of power and the tension between moral principle and political command. Such themes were not inherently attacks on Mao or defenses of Peng Dehuai. The later transformation of the play into a political test depended on decisions by Mao's allies and opponents as well as on the atmosphere of suspicion. For students, the relevant evidence includes the play itself, the 1965 critical essay, official responses in Beijing, and later recollections. Each was produced for a different audience and cannot be used as if it revealed the playwright's private intent with certainty.

70.3 The February Outline and the Purge of Senior Officials

A "Group of Five" for cultural revolution, headed by Peng Zhen, had been established in 1964. In February 1966, it produced a report, the February Outline, which urged that the Wu Han dispute be handled as an academic matter under Party guidance. It was approved by senior leaders, including Liu Shaoqi, and circulated to Party organizations. The leadership that issued it did not yet know that Mao intended something far larger.

Meanwhile, the purge had already begun in the military and the security apparatus. Luo Ruiqing, the army's chief of staff, was removed in December 1965 after clashing with Lin Biao. By spring 1966, Mao had repudiated the February Outline; Peng Zhen, Lu Dingyi (head of propaganda), and Yang Shangkun (a senior Party secretary) were removed in May, and the Beijing Party committee was reorganized in June. This episode is significant because it dismantled much of the Party's own coordinating machinery in the capital. Officials who thought they were protecting orderly procedure learned that procedure itself was now suspect, and that the line between loyalty and betrayal was defined by Mao's reading of events.

The February Outline is particularly instructive as a document about jurisdiction. It attempted to place cultural criticism under the regular Party apparatus and to prevent a dispute about literature from becoming an uncontrolled attack on political figures. Its repudiation established a precedent by which following a formally approved Party procedure could itself be condemned as political disloyalty. The removals of senior officials also deprived subordinate administrators of clear guidance about which directives remained authoritative. As disagreements spread from Beijing outward, local cadres confronted the danger that carrying out yesterday's instructions would make them targets under tomorrow's policy.

70.4 The May 16 Circular and the Cultural Revolution Group

At an enlarged Politburo meeting in May 1966, the leadership adopted a circular, now known by its date, May 16, 1966. It repudiated the February Outline, dissolved the Group of Five, and announced a new body that would answer directly to the Politburo Standing Committee. The new body became the Central Cultural Revolution Group (CCRG). It was initially headed by Chen Boda, with Jiang Qing as a deputy head, Kang Sheng as an adviser, and members drawn from radical writers and propagandists such as Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, Wang Li, Guan Feng, and Qi Benyu. The CCRG soon took on tasks far beyond culture, receiving petitions, directing the press, and calling mass organizations to account; effectively, it became a parallel center of authority, with its legitimacy resting on Mao's backing.

The circular also contained the movement's theory. It warned that representatives of the bourgeoisie had infiltrated the Party, government, army, and cultural circles; that some of them would eventually seize power; and that some were sleeping beside the leadership, in a phrase aimed at senior colleagues. This was a crucial move. It made the enemy not a class defeated in 1949 but a hidden group within the ruling party, and it meant that no official's record could guarantee safety. The circular was initially circulated internally to cadres and was published only in 1967.

The circular's immediate circulation also reveals a distinction between those who controlled political language and those expected to implement it. Cadres receiving an internal instruction had to decide how it altered previous guidance on criticism, education and security. The central leadership did not issue a detailed administrative manual explaining which accusations could be tested or how accused individuals could defend themselves. Instead, the document's authority rested on warnings about concealed political enemies. This combination of an extremely consequential diagnosis with few enforceable procedures gave radicals and anxious administrators wide latitude in interpreting Mao's objectives. The resulting uncertainty helps account for the movement's rapid diffusion beyond the original disputes in Beijing.

Central Committee Circular of May 16, 1966

Analysis: The document shows how the movement was defined from above in language that treated hidden enemies in the leadership as the central danger. It is a statement of Mao's perspective rather than a neutral description of the Party's condition. What it cannot show is how many officials actually held the views attributed to them. Its limits include the fact that it was drafted in secrecy and deployed against specific individuals already marked for removal; the voices of those it accused are absent.

70.5 Universities, Work Teams, and the First Mass Criticism Campaigns

The campaign reached the educational system in late May. On May 25, 1966, Nie Yuanzi, a philosophy lecturer at Peking University, and six colleagues posted a big-character poster (dazibao) attacking the university's leadership. Mao, who had been shown the text, ordered it broadcast nationally on June 1, and the People's Daily issued an editorial calling on readers to sweep away "ghosts and monsters." Classes were suspended in many schools, and the university entrance examination was postponed, which began the long disruption of formal education.

Liu Shaoqi and other leaders responded in the way Party practice suggested: they sent work teams, groups of officials, to schools and universities to guide the movement and prevent disorder. But the work teams placed themselves between students and the campaign's targets, and in some places they labeled student activists as rightists. A confrontation developed at Tsinghua University, where Kuai Dafu opposed the work team. The question of who had the right to direct criticism, Party officials or the students themselves, became the first major fault line. Mao, returning to Beijing in July, concluded that the work teams had suppressed the movement.

Student involvement did not begin as an unrestricted license to rebel. Different schools experienced competing instructions from work teams, local Party committees, revolutionary activists and the central leadership. Disputes over a person's class background and whether a student had attacked a legitimate authority could become decisive in determining who was labeled a revolutionary or a counterrevolutionary. Some children of senior officials initially claimed a privileged right to lead the movement; other students challenged that entitlement. School records, contemporary posters and subsequent testimony show that the conflict over organization preceded the later, more visible street violence. The suspension of teaching also removed familiar routines of examination, attendance and academic assessment, giving political activity an unusually large place in young people's lives.

70.6 Mao's Return to the Forefront and the Sixteen Points

Mao's return was staged. In mid-July 1966, he appeared in Wuhan and swam in the Yangtze River, a widely publicized demonstration of vigor at the age of seventy-two. Back in Beijing, he withdrew the work teams and convened the Eleventh Plenum of the Eighth Central Committee in early August. On August 5, he wrote a short big-character poster, "Bombard the Headquarters," which was read as an attack on Liu Shaoqi's leadership. The plenum rearranged the leadership ranking, putting Lin Biao second and demoting Liu to eighth place. On August 8, it adopted the "Decision Concerning the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution," known as the Sixteen Points.

The Sixteen Points defined the movement as a new stage of the socialist revolution, identified the primary target as persons in authority within the Party taking the capitalist road, and told Party committees to trust the masses and let them educate themselves. They called for new organizations, such as cultural revolution committees elected on the model of the Paris Commune, to be set up in schools and workplaces. They also contained restraints: they advised using argument rather than force and set limited protections for scientists and technical experts. The contradiction is central to the history of the next two years. Officials were told to avoid suppressing the masses, and on August 22 the security forces were instructed not to intervene against student activity; yet there was no mechanism to enforce the restraints once crowds acted.

The decision's procedural language created a second problem. Encouraging mass organizations to challenge officials was not equivalent to defining which institution would adjudicate conflicting accusations. Schools and universities already faced rival claims about who represented the correct line, and the suspension of work teams removed an important, if contested, mechanism of supervision. The Sixteen Points expressed preferences for persuasion and protection of scientific work, but the system lacked practical safeguards for people whose status was disputed. For classroom analysis, compare what the resolution authorized, the conduct it nominally prohibited and the choices later made by local authorities. These three levels of evidence should not be conflated.

Primary Source Window

The Sixteen Points, August 8, 1966

Creator: Central Committee, Eighth Party Congress, Eleventh Plenum · Date: August 8, 1966 · Audience: the Party and, soon, the public · Type: Party decision · Edition: English translation in Peking Review (1966); modern scholarly collections also reproduce it. Students should identify which edition they use.

Context: The decision followed Mao's attack on his own leadership colleagues and supplied the first comprehensive framework for popular participation.

Paraphrase: The Decision calls on the Party to trust and rely on the masses, names Party leaders taking the capitalist road as the main target, encourages mass organizations to run their own movement, and urges that struggle be waged by argument rather than coercion.

Analysis: The text is a framework document whose language of restraint coexisted with a call to attack. It tells us how the leadership wanted the movement to be understood, not what happened in schools and neighborhoods. Its limits are those of any official text: it does not record the violence, the interpretation of "capitalist road" by local activists, or how people on the receiving end understood its terms. Compare it with the May 16 Circular: the earlier document defined enemies; the later one empowered the public to find them.

Division II — Red Guards, Mass Mobilization, and Persecution, 1966–1967

Division purpose: to show how an officially encouraged revolutionary movement transformed social relationships and enabled widespread political persecution.

70.7 The Origins and Organization of the Red Guards

The first Red Guard groups appeared in late May 1966 among students at a middle school affiliated with Tsinghua University in Beijing, and the name spread rapidly. Red Guard groups were formed by schoolchildren and university students, often with a base in a single school or department. They were not a unified organization. They adopted their own names, newspapers, and leaders, claimed to be the true defenders of Mao, and in many cases accused one another of being false revolutionaries.

Family background shaped early membership. Students from families labeled "red" (workers, poor and lower-middle peasants, revolutionary soldiers, cadres, and martyrs) were the first to be accepted, while those from "black" families (landlords, rich peasants, counterrevolutionaries, bad elements, and rightists) were excluded or attacked. An influential couplet asserting that revolutionary parents produced revolutionary children, and others reactionary offspring, expressed this "bloodline" thinking, and some activists enforced it violently. Critics, including students from less favored backgrounds, argued that political conduct, not family origin, should define a revolutionary. By late 1966, the rift between "bloodline" groups and "rebel" groups reshaped the movement. The children of senior cadres often belonged to the first; those with less secure backgrounds, or whose parents were already targeted, were often among the second.

Historians note that motives varied. Some students were sincere believers; some sought status or protection; some followed friends; some joined to avoid being targeted. Scholarship by Andrew Walder and others shows that factional alignments often arose from local circumstances, such as how a school's authorities had treated particular students, more than from any consistent ideological position.

Social composition mattered, but no single class formula explained the organizations. Young people from families of revolutionary cadres, workers, teachers and people classified as politically suspect could all join or support groups under changing circumstances. Many students regarded Mao's endorsement as proof that action against school authorities was both permitted and morally required. Others feared that remaining inactive would be read as evidence of disloyalty. Rival factions used competing political genealogies and quotations to claim central backing. These rivalries demonstrate why researchers must reconstruct the history of particular campuses rather than equate a faction's name with its later political behavior.

70.8 Mass Rallies, Revolutionary Travel, and the Spread of the Movement

Between August and November 1966, Mao reviewed Red Guards at eight mass rallies in Beijing; official figures reported about eleven million participants in total. Many arrived by train from distant provinces. In September, the leadership authorized students to travel freely to "exchange revolutionary experience" (chuanlian), with free rail transport and lodging. Young people walked, rode, and filled stations, visiting Beijing, Mao's birthplace in Shaoshan, and Jinggangshan, a Communist base from the 1920s. They carried the movement to every province, bringing methods learned in the capital: criticism meetings, house searches, and attacks on local officials.

Travel disrupted transportation and administration, and it also spread information and conflict. Students who investigated their home-region officials turned local rivalries into national contests. The government began urging students to return to their schools in early 1967, but by then the Red Guards had changed the political landscape. Their contact with Mao, however formal, gave them an exaggerated sense of authority and a claim to legitimacy that could be used against nearly any local official.

Free rail travel and accommodation arranged for revolutionary exchanges allowed young activists to cross distances rarely accessible to their families. Visits to Beijing reinforced Mao's personal prestige while also connecting groups that had previously been organized around individual schools. Travelers circulated posters, slogans, accusations and rumors, creating a nationwide political language faster than the central bureaucracy could supervise it. The movement of students also strained transportation, food supplies and the administration of cities receiving visitors. This revolutionary networking helped spread mobilization, but it did not produce a stable national chain of command: the activists who returned home frequently disagreed about which Beijing slogans should take priority.

70.9 The Cult of Mao Zedong and the Politics of Revolutionary Loyalty

Public life in these years was organized around ritualized expressions of loyalty to Mao. Citizens carried or displayed the pocket-sized Quotations from Chairman Mao Zedong, printed in many hundreds of millions of copies; wore Mao badges; participated in morning requests for instruction and evening reports; and sang songs praising him. Portraits hung in homes, factories, and public buildings. The "loyalty dance" and similar displays spread in many areas, and errors in handling Mao's image could be treated as political crimes.

The cult served multiple purposes. It gave Mao's authority a direct line to the public, bypassing the Party bureaucracy. It supplied a shared vocabulary that rival factions used against one another, since each declared itself the truest follower. And it gave Lin Biao and the army a means of demonstrating allegiance. Historians stress that the relationship between charisma and institutions was unstable: the more intense the personal cult, the weaker the formal procedures of Party government.

Mao's quotations acquired authority not simply as political reading but as portable evidence in disputes between competing groups. A declaration of fidelity could be made with the same words by factions attacking one another. Mass meetings, portraits, loyalty rituals and the symbolic treatment of books turned political loyalty into public performance, often subject to monitoring by peers. Yet expressions of admiration had multiple meanings. They could be sincere, tactical, compulsory or some mixture of these. Memoirs and interviews require care because participants reconstructed their feelings after the movement's discrediting, and because it could be dangerous to express private doubts during the campaign itself.

The relationship between these rituals and coercion was particularly visible in workplaces. Employees might be required to display Mao quotations, attend collective readings or demonstrate an approved attitude at meetings. Refusal could provoke scrutiny even when a person made no explicit political objection. Nevertheless, the repetition of a ritual does not prove uniform belief. Researchers therefore need to distinguish the performance required by organizations, the language preserved in official records and feelings recalled by participants years later. This separation allows a more precise account of Mao's personal cult without treating the population as a single undifferentiated audience.

70.10 Attacks on the Four Olds

In August 1966, Red Guards announced a campaign against the "Four Olds": old ideas, old culture, old customs, and old habits. In Beijing and other cities, groups removed shop signs, changed street names, cut people's hair or clothing deemed bourgeois, and raided homes in search of gold, books, antiques, and foreign items. Temples, churches, mosques, and monasteries were closed, vandalized, or destroyed; religious figures and books were attacked. Graves and cultural sites were damaged, including the temple and family cemetery of Confucius at Qufu in Shandong, attacked in November 1966 by a group from Beijing Normal University.

Experiences differed by region. In Beijing and Shanghai, house searches targeted urban professionals and families with historical associations. In Tibet, Red Guards, including Tibetan and Han activists, attacked monasteries and religious objects, and only a small proportion of Tibet's monasteries survived intact. Local officials sometimes protected sites: Premier Zhou Enlai, for example, ordered protection for the Forbidden City and certain other major monuments, and some museums were closed or guarded. The destruction was not only about objects but about communities. People lost family records, ritual practices, and religious life, and the effects on cultural memory persisted long after the campaign ended.

The campaign's name encouraged a sweeping view of inherited culture, but local practices differed. Temples might be attacked as religious institutions, homes searched for allegedly feudal objects, and ancestral records or family collections destroyed as evidence of backwardness. Some residents surrendered possessions in advance to avoid punishment, while others hid books, artworks or religious objects. The people who suffered were not limited to wealthy families: religious practitioners, artisans, custodians of local heritage and ordinary households all faced potential suspicion. Destruction was also selective and inconsistent, since authorities sometimes preserved objects thought to have historical or diplomatic value. What survives in museums today is therefore not a complete guide to what was lost.

Historical Vocabulary
Red Guards, Capitalist Roaders, and the Four Olds

Red Guards (hongweibing) were mass student organizations that claimed to defend Mao Zedong Thought. The term covered rival groups that frequently fought one another. Capitalist roaders (zouzi pai, "those taking the capitalist road") was Mao's label for officials in authority who allegedly promoted capitalism or revisionism. The label was ideological and political: it did not reflect any systematic policy program and could be applied to anyone out of favor. The Four Olds (si jiu) were the targets of the August 1966 campaign: old ideas, culture, customs, and habits.

Students should note that these terms were used by participants in changing ways. A group could be called "Red Guard" and "conservative" simultaneously; a person could be accused of "taking the capitalist road" for actions that had previously been Party policy.

70.11 Teachers, Intellectuals, Officials, and the Growth of Persecution

The central instrument of persecution was the struggle session (pidou). A victim, labeled a reactionary academic authority, a landlord or rightist, a "traitor," or a capitalist roader, was brought before a crowd, made to stand bent forward in the "airplane" position, forced to wear a placard or tall paper hat, and subjected to verbal and physical abuse, often accompanied by demands for confession. Victims included school teachers and principals, university professors, writers and artists, doctors, clergy, former Nationalist officials and their families, and large numbers of Party officials at every level. In Beijing during August and September 1966, scholars cite thousands of deaths in the "Red August" violence, including in suburban counties where people labeled as class enemies were killed in groups; precise totals are uncertain and vary by source.

The novelist Lao She drowned in a Beijing lake in August 1966 after being beaten; the translator Fu Lei and his wife died by suicide the same month; the historian Wu Han died in custody in 1969; the vice-principal Bian Zhongyun was killed on August 5. Suicide was common among the persecuted, and many who survived were permanently injured. Teachers were especially vulnerable because schools were the movement's launching point and because students had direct grievances against authority figures. Yet some students defended teachers, and some teachers survived because individuals took risks to protect them.

Students should distinguish carefully among categories. Perpetrators included students and, increasingly, adults: work-unit colleagues, neighbors, and local officials who joined or directed the attacks. Victims included people targeted for political reasons and people targeted because of inherited class labels, with little relation to their individual conduct. Participation was not uniform. Some people acted out of conviction, some out of fear, and some out of opportunism, and some resisted passively or actively. Scholarly estimates of the total number of people killed during the entire decade vary widely depending on the source and the definition used. The official figure given at the 1980–1981 trials of the "Lin Biao and Jiang Qing cliques" included 34,800 deaths among 729,511 persecuted people in the cases attributed to the defendants; these figures do not cover all casualties of the Cultural Revolution, while studies using local gazetteers, such as that of Andrew Walder and Yang Su, extrapolate an estimated 750,000 to 1.5 million deaths in rural China from a sample of county annals, with a similar estimated number permanently injured. These figures are not directly comparable, and all are subject to revision.

Violence developed through repeated encounters in which accusations could escalate without ordinary legal protections. A public denunciation might be followed by detention, demands for confession, investigation of relatives or further accusations generated by coerced testimony. Fear spread beyond the person initially accused, because colleagues were pressured to demonstrate political reliability through participation. Scholarly accounts must distinguish deaths caused by direct assault, suicide under persecution, deaths in custody and later killings during official campaigns. Even when documents contain apparently precise victim totals, their scope can be restricted to particular institutions, provinces, years or categories of accusation. Comparisons require common definitions before the numbers can be interpreted.

70.12 The Downfall of Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping

The campaign against the Party leadership reached the top in 1966–1967. Liu Shaoqi made self-criticisms in autumn 1966 and was increasingly attacked in Red Guard newspapers and CCRG-directed publications. In April 1967, his wife Wang Guangmei was subjected to a struggle session at Tsinghua University, and Liu himself was denounced as the leading capitalist roader. In October 1968, the Twelfth Plenum expelled him from the Party and removed all posts, labeling him a traitor and scab. He died in November 1969 in Kaifeng, Henan, while held in isolation and without adequate medical care.

Deng Xiaoping was criticized as the second-ranking capitalist roader. He lost his posts in 1966–1967, was held under supervision in Beijing, and was transferred in late 1969 to Jiangxi province, where he worked in a tractor repair factory and lived with his family. Unlike Liu, he survived, in part because Mao remained unwilling to treat him as an irredeemable enemy. Many other senior leaders were persecuted: Marshal He Long died in 1969 after harsh treatment, and former defense minister Peng Dehuai, who had been dismissed in 1959, was brought back for interrogation and died in custody in 1974. These cases show that the movement's top-level purges turned on Mao's shifting judgments, and that outcomes varied from rehabilitation to death.

Liu Shaoqi's fate shows the destruction of safeguards at the highest level of the state. His offices and revolutionary record did not protect him after Mao's political campaign defined him as the chief representative of a capitalist road. Investigations drew on forced confessions, selective accounts of his earlier career and the conversion of political disagreement into accusations of betrayal. His isolation and denial of appropriate medical care formed part of the persecution leading to his death. Deng's removal followed a different trajectory: he survived, spent years away from national decision-making and ultimately returned to office. Placing their histories alongside one another helps students see both the danger faced by senior officials and the variability of individual outcomes.

70.13 Families, Neighborhoods, and Everyday Life in the First Revolutionary Wave

For ordinary people, the first wave of the movement entered through the family, the school, and the workplace. Children were pressed to denounce parents. Neighbors reported neighbors. People discarded books, photographs, and heirlooms in fear of searches. Some families split along political lines, and others developed private codes to protect one another. Class labels followed children into school, where those from "black" families were often excluded from activities. Many people withdrew into silence as a strategy of survival.

A household could have very different public and private histories. In meetings, family members might recite approved denunciations, while at home they worried about a detained relative or tried to preserve letters and photographs. Students with disfavored class backgrounds faced stigma that affected friendships, schooling and prospects for advancement. Some people used personal networks, relatives in other places or the protection of sympathetic colleagues to avoid the harshest consequences; others had no such resources. Local archives and oral testimony are valuable precisely because they record relationships that national proclamations omitted. At the same time, personal memories may not reveal whether apparently voluntary participation was motivated by fear, ambition or conviction.

Social pressures extended to the recording of family histories. Employment records, school dossiers and neighborhood knowledge could turn a relative's long-forgotten political association into an accusation against a child or spouse. Families sometimes destroyed documents that otherwise might have been treasured as evidence of personal achievement. Where one family member belonged to a mass organization and another was under suspicion, ordinary disagreements could acquire unexpected political consequences. Remembering this intimate dimension also helps explain why the movement continued to shape relationships after particular organizations dissolved. A family's later account of silence, departure or reconciliation may reveal long-lasting effects that national political chronologies cannot easily measure.

Division III — Power Seizures, Factional Warfare, and Military Intervention, 1967–1968

Division purpose: to explain how revolutionary mobilization destabilized government institutions, why competing factions fought one another, and how military intervention combined institutional reconstruction with further repression.

70.14 The January Storm and the Seizure of Power in Shanghai

In late 1966, the movement moved from schools to factories and city governments. In Shanghai, a worker named Wang Hongwen, a security officer at a cotton mill, helped form the Workers' Revolutionary Rebel General Headquarters. Rebel workers challenged the municipal Party committee, whose leaders had responded by offering concessions, including wage increases and bonuses, in an effort to defuse the unrest, a policy later condemned as "economism." The city's services slowed. In early January 1967, rebel groups took over the city's main newspapers, and on January 6 a mass rally denounced the mayor and Party first secretary. With support from Zhang Chunqiao and Yao Wenyuan, who had returned from Beijing, the rebels seized control of the city's administration. Mao praised the "January Storm" as a model.

On February 5, activists proclaimed the Shanghai People's Commune, invoking the 1871 Paris Commune and the Sixteen Points' promise of elected organs. Mao, however, doubted that a commune could govern a city of millions or that it fit the Party-led state; it was soon replaced by a "revolutionary committee," a form that combined representatives of rebel organizations, former officials, and the army. The change signaled that Mao favored reconstructing authority under Party and army leadership rather than a fully democratic commune. Similar seizures took place across the country, and Heilongjiang established the first provincial revolutionary committee in January 1967.

The Shanghai case also illustrates the difference between seizure of power and the construction of an alternative government. Worker organizations could mobilize thousands of supporters, close offices and take over newspapers, but they still needed arrangements for maintaining transport, food distribution, factories and public security. The short-lived commune invoked an ideal of revolutionary participation that did not fit comfortably with a nationally directed Party-state. Mao's preference for the revolutionary-committee formula therefore marked an institutional choice: political authority was to be reconstructed through a coalition sanctioned by higher leaders rather than transferred indefinitely to autonomous mass assemblies. Other provinces copied the language of power seizure while producing different combinations of military and civilian rule.

70.15 Worker Rebels, Conservative Organizations, and Rival Revolutionary Factions

The division in schools reappeared in workplaces. In many factories, one organization, typically composed of workers with good records or Party members who supported local leaders, defended the existing management; others, often composed of temporary and contract workers, younger workers, and those with grievances about pay, promotion, or discipline, attacked it. The first group was labeled "conservative," the second "rebel," but the labels were applied by outsiders and could be reversed. Each insisted on its loyalty to Mao.

Local cadres sometimes organized or encouraged "conservative" groups to protect themselves, which gave rebels evidence for accusations. The 1967 "February Countercurrent," in which several veteran marshals and vice-premiers criticized the CCRG at Politburo meetings, was condemned by Mao, and the participants were denounced. In January 1967, the army was ordered to "support the Left," a phrase that left commanders to decide who was Left. The result was inconsistent: some units backed rebels, others conservatives, and a few attempted neutrality. The instability of faction identities means students should not assume that "rebel" meant "radical" or that "conservative" meant "pro-Party" in every locality.

Factory grievances were not reducible to elite maneuvering. Temporary workers could resent unequal wages or access to benefits; permanent employees might fear losing their security; managers might seek to preserve production and personnel records. Revolutionary slogans offered ways to express these conflicting interests, but the subsequent alignment of a faction depended on local patronage and outside intervention. When orders from Beijing changed, the same group might be described as a defender of the revolutionary line one month and an obstacle to it the next. Historians who rely on factional pamphlets must therefore compare rival claims with enterprise files, local chronologies and the timing of military involvement.

70.16 Armed Factional Conflict and the Wuhan Incident

By spring and summer 1967, conflict in many cities turned violent. Factions seized weapons from army depots or received them from sympathetic units, fought with rifles and, in some places, artillery, and blockaded rivals. Wuhan, a major industrial city on the Yangtze, was a flashpoint. The local military commander, Chen Zaidao, backed an organization called the Million Heroes against rebel groups supported by the CCRG. In July, two central emissaries, Xie Fuzhi and Wang Li, traveled to Wuhan. On July 20, Wang Li was seized and beaten by soldiers and conservative activists, and Xie Fuzhi was confined. Zhou Enlai flew to the city, and the central leadership responded by removing Chen Zaidao, dispatching troops, and rehabilitating rebels. The Wuhan Incident showed that a regional commander could defy Beijing's emissaries, and that the central leadership's preferred outcome depended on balancing the army against mass organizations.

After Wuhan, rebel groups elsewhere attacked army units and took arms. Jiang Qing declared in September that the masses should defend themselves with arms, a statement quickly withdrawn. By then, the CCRG itself was divided; Wang Li and Guan Feng were purged in August, and Qi Benyu in January 1968. In 1967–1968, order broke down in multiple regions. Mao responded by approving harder measures against both radical groups and armed factions, ordering the PLA to stop supplying weapons and to take control.

The Wuhan crisis exposed the political ambiguity of the instruction to support the Left. Military units were responsible for public order but lacked a reliable standard for deciding which competing organization represented the revolutionary masses. A commander might regard a group as disciplined and loyal while central radicals treated it as conservative. The arrival of central emissaries did not settle the dispute because local troops and activists contested their authority. After the confrontation, factions across China drew different conclusions: some took Beijing's intervention as permission to challenge military command, while others saw the danger of uncontrolled rebellion. The incident was one step in an escalating cycle, not a single cause of nationwide violence.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Cultural Revolution Become Violent?

The question: The Sixteen Points urged argument rather than force, yet violence was widespread by August 1966 and intensified after 1967. Historians disagree about how to explain it.

Interpretation A — Violence arose from the movement's design and from leadership signals: Scholars emphasizing this view point to the identification of hidden enemies within society, the instruction to police not to intervene, the political cultivation of class hatred, and Mao's and the CCRG's endorsement of attacks. On this reading, state encouragement and the absence of protection for targets made violence predictable.
Interpretation B — Violence arose from local conflicts, factional dynamics, and institutional collapse: Others, including Andrew Walder, stress that much of the killing was organized locally, that factions emerged from specific institutional grievances, and that the largest numbers of deaths occurred in 1967–1968 and especially during the military-run campaigns that followed, rather than in the initial student phase. They argue that central directives were often ambiguous and that local actors, including army units and local officials, made decisions with their own interests in mind.

Evidence: Interpretation A draws on central documents, speeches, and early Red Guard testimony; Interpretation B draws on local gazetteers, county-level investigations, and quantitative analysis of death counts by time and place. The two bodies of evidence do not necessarily contradict each other: central signals could create conditions in which local dynamics produced violence.

Why it matters: The answer affects how responsibility is assigned among Mao, central leaders, local officials, military commanders, mass organizations, and individuals, and it affects what lessons are drawn about political mobilization and institutional safeguards.

Student task: What kinds of evidence would help determine whether violence in a particular county followed central instructions or local conflicts? How do the different time periods (1966, 1967–1968, 1968–1971) fit each interpretation?

70.17 Regional Violence and Persecution Beyond Beijing

Some of the movement's most lethal episodes occurred far from the capital. In Guangxi, a region in southern China with a large Zhuang population, rival factions fought in 1967–1968, and after the army intervened in 1968, local authorities organized killings of people labeled supporters of the losing faction or class enemies. A later investigation confirmed 89,810 deaths and recorded almost 20,000 additional missing persons, suggesting that the overall toll may have exceeded 100,000; subsequent research has documented cases of cannibalism in certain counties. In Inner Mongolia, a campaign from 1967 to 1969 to "dig out" members of an alleged "New Inner Mongolian People's Revolutionary Party" led to mass detentions and torture; hundreds of thousands were persecuted, and deaths numbered in the many thousands (figures from official investigations and scholarly estimates vary widely). Ulanhu, the region's long-time leader, was purged.

In Hunan's Dao County in 1967, local militia organizations killed several thousand people labeled as class enemies. In Yunnan, in 1975, the army suppressed a Hui community at Shadian after a dispute over religious freedom, killing well over a thousand people. In Tibet, conflict between rival factions and the military in places such as Nyemo county in 1969 led to executions. Persecution in minority areas often combined class labels with suspicion of "local nationalism" and foreign ties. Students should note regional differences: some areas experienced limited violence, others mass killing, depending on local factional alignments, army posture, and political leaders.

The evidentiary problem is especially acute in the most violent regions. In Guangxi, later investigations collected testimony and compiled statistics that were not generally available to outsiders during the movement. Published local histories often recorded fewer deaths than internal inquiry materials. The numbers also reflect different accounting choices: confirmed killings, missing persons, deaths associated with armed fighting, and victims of organized purges may be counted separately. Violence in Inner Mongolia followed a different political logic, centered on the allegation of a clandestine separatist party. Investigators used confessions and networks of supposed association to widen the circle of suspects. Comparing these cases requires attention to institutions and accusations rather than treating all regional killings as spontaneous Red Guard clashes.

70.18 The People's Liberation Army and Revolutionary Committees

In January 1967, the army was ordered to support the Left, and soldiers took control of newspapers, radio stations, factories, and local governments. In some places, units protected officials and prevented fighting; in others, they backed one faction and suppressed another. From 1967 to 1968, revolutionary committees were set up in provinces, cities, and counties under a formula called the "three-in-one combination": representatives of the army, of cadres, and of mass organizations. In practice, military representatives usually held the decisive positions. By September 5, 1968, revolutionary committees existed in every provincial-level unit, with Tibet and Xinjiang the last.

The PLA's role was double. It restored railways, kept supplies moving, and ended the most open armed conflicts, but it also carried out arrests, supervised campaigns of investigation, and sometimes directed massacres. Its units were not unified, and many commanders sided with locally favored groups. The army also acquired a large role in the Party and state, which later became a political problem for Mao himself.

Army intervention was not uniform across time or place. Local commanders had to secure roads and industrial facilities while interpreting directives issued by a divided central leadership. Some units protected particular mass organizations or armed them; other commands attempted to disarm factions; still others became targets of attacks. Revolutionary committees could keep factories functioning and restore government offices, but they frequently placed commanders above civilians and gave selected mass representatives limited influence. The restoration of administrative capacity therefore altered the form of conflict without necessarily protecting the population from arbitrary detention and punishment. This dual role must be central to any evaluation of the PLA during the movement.

The new revolutionary committees were expected to combine mass representation with institutional continuity, but selecting their members was itself a political struggle. Which faction counted as revolutionary? Which former cadres were considered reliable enough to restore? Which military officer could command obedience outside his own unit? Answers often emerged from negotiations backed by force rather than from the elective procedures that the movement's early slogans had suggested. In some places residents welcomed the reopening of public services; elsewhere the replacement of one armed group by another brought new fear. Students should connect these outcomes to the earlier conflict between the ideal of direct mass participation and the practical demand for a governable state.

Cause and Effect

Military Intervention and the Restoration of Authority

Causes

Short-term: administrative paralysis after power seizures; armed factional fighting; the Wuhan Incident; attacks on military facilities. Long-term: the PLA's prestige and national organization; Mao's distrust of the Party bureaucracy; the absence of other institutions able to keep order.

Transformation

The PLA took over civilian administration through the "support the Left" order, revolutionary committees, military control commissions, and propaganda teams in schools and factories.

Effects

Immediate: reduced open warfare; restored transport and production; expanded repression through investigations and campaigns. Long-term: a Party-state with heavy military representation; resentments among civilian cadres; tensions between Mao and the military leadership.

Why it is complex

The army was both a source of order and of coercion, and it was divided internally. Intervention did not follow a single policy and cannot be reduced to one motive.

70.19 The Cleansing of Class Ranks and the Expansion of Political Repression

From mid-1968, the revolutionary committees and military authorities launched the Cleansing of Class Ranks (qingli jieji duiwu), a campaign to identify hidden traitors, spies, and class enemies in workplaces, schools, and communities. It combined investigation of personal histories with interrogation and detention, often in improvised "cowsheds" or study classes, and with public criticism sessions. Teams drawn from the army and from "workers' Mao Zedong Thought propaganda teams" moved into schools and universities. The campaign followed the collapse of factional fighting and replaced mass violence by systematic, organized repression. Walder and Su's quantitative work suggests that a large proportion of all deaths in rural counties occurred in the 1968 period, during these state-organized campaigns.

The campaign reached into every work unit. People who had joined the wrong organization, had overseas connections, or had once served the Nationalist government were investigated. Many victims were not "targets" of the Red Guard phase but ordinary workers and local cadres. The campaign blurred the line between victims and perpetrators, since people who had been active in earlier phases were sometimes among those investigated.

The Cleansing of Class Ranks illustrated the danger of turning allegations into administrative facts. Investigators searched political biographies for allegedly hidden identities, past affiliations or family associations and treated inconsistencies as evidence requiring further confession. Accusations could spread through workplaces and villages as one detention prompted allegations against others. Production and teaching were interrupted when large numbers of staff became subjects of investigation. Later official reviews classified many of the accusations as false or unjustified. In assessing this period students should ask who controlled detention and investigation, whether defendants could challenge evidence, and why repression grew even while national leaders claimed that revolutionary disorder was ending.

A further distinction concerns the chronology of repression. Early attacks in 1966 were commonly associated with student activism and campaigns against disfavored social backgrounds. During 1968–1971, restored or militarized authorities increasingly directed investigations and punishment. The change in perpetrators and institutional settings is supported by large comparative studies of local annals. Its implication is important: the return of an official administrative hierarchy did not necessarily signify the end of mass persecution. For many households it created a more systematic system of interrogation and recordkeeping. Narratives that end the story of violence when street demonstrations diminished consequently miss a substantial portion of the deaths recorded in local histories.

70.20 The Dispersal of the Red Guards and the Turn Toward Rustication

By mid-1968, Mao and the leadership wanted to end student factional fighting. At Tsinghua University, a prolonged armed battle between student groups in the spring and summer led Mao to send workers' propaganda teams into the campus. On July 28, 1968, Mao met with leading Red Guard leaders, including Nie Yuanzi and Kuai Dafu, and told them the movement had to end; many were later detained or sent away. Propaganda teams were installed in universities and schools across the country.

The solution for the millions of young people who had completed or interrupted school between 1966 and 1968, often called the "old three classes" of middle-school graduates, was relocation. In December 1968, the People's Daily published a directive from Mao saying that educated youth should go to the countryside to be re-educated by poor and lower-middle peasants. Rustication relieved urban unemployment and school congestion and removed a mobilized, politicized population from cities, though its stated rationale was ideological. Over the following years, the movement grew, as discussed in §70.27.

The December 1968 call to send educated youth to the countryside drew on earlier resettlement schemes but transformed their scale and political meaning. It addressed both the problem of militant urban student organizations and the official ambition to remake privileged youth through manual labor. Parents often had little influence over postings, and destinations could be distant from home. Rural communities were expected to house and supervise newcomers whose practical farming skills varied considerably. For the urban young, rustication could mean hardship, new friendships, work experience and restricted educational prospects at once. The policy endured beyond the formal period covered by this chapter, so figures must distinguish participants sent before 1976 from totals reported for the entire program.

The transition from organized political activism to compulsory rural resettlement was not equally sudden for every student. Some young people had already lost years of schooling; others entered the movement only after its early mass rallies had passed. District authorities decided who was eligible to leave and where they would go, while families confronted the costs of separation and the uncertainty of return. Those sent to frontier farms also entered regions with distinct ethnic and linguistic communities, adding an important geographic dimension to rustication. This aspect of the policy belongs alongside its political function of dispersing organizations that Beijing regarded as increasingly difficult to control.

Division IV — Revolutionary Consolidation, Lin Biao, and the Crisis of Succession, 1969–1971

Division purpose: to explain why the restoration of order did not resolve the political problems created by the Cultural Revolution.

70.21 The Ninth Party Congress and the Reconstruction of Political Authority

The Ninth National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party met in Beijing from April 1 to 24, 1969, the first congress since 1956. It was held after the revolutionary committees had been set up, and it was designed to declare that the "great victory" of the movement had been won and to restore a formal Party structure. Lin Biao delivered the political report, which praised the movement and Mao's leadership. The new Party constitution described Lin as Mao's close comrade-in-arms and successor, an unusual step, since Party constitutions do not normally name a successor. The Central Committee elected at the congress included a large proportion of military figures, roughly two-fifths of its members, and many of the delegates were drawn from revolutionary committees; veteran cadres purged in earlier years were largely absent. The Politburo Standing Committee consisted of Mao, Lin Biao, Chen Boda, Zhou Enlai, and Kang Sheng.

A congress can restore offices without resolving the underlying problem of consent and accountability. The Ninth Congress assembled delegates under conditions shaped by the recent political purges and the expanding influence of military representatives. It presented the Cultural Revolution as a victory, but many experienced administrators remained outside the leadership and civilian Party structures required rebuilding. The decision to name Lin Biao in the Party constitution was extraordinary because a supposedly durable legal-organizational document became tied to the status of one individual. Its rapid reversal after 1971 offers a clear example of how personal succession politics could destabilize official claims about institutional permanence.

The reconstitution of Party authority after the Ninth Congress was also incomplete. The congress restored a central structure but could not automatically rebuild experienced local committees, resolve factional claims or settle the army's proper place in civilian administration. Even the category of rehabilitated cadre remained politically uncertain. A leader who had been denounced might be restored for technical competence without recovering independent authority or protection from later campaigns. The Party constitution's endorsement of Lin Biao thus stood in contrast to a broader problem: highly personal decisions about loyalty and succession coexisted with a political system claiming formal organizational discipline.

70.22 The Military-Influenced Party-State and Continuing Repression

After the Ninth Congress, the Party apparatus was rebuilt, with Party committees reestablished at provincial and lower levels in 1970–1971, but military officers continued to dominate many provincial leadership posts. Military commanders served as heads of revolutionary committees and as first secretaries of provincial Party committees. This arrangement placed enormous administrative power in the hands of the army, while leaving the civilian Party structure unevenly restored.

The end of factional fighting did not mean the end of coercion. Campaigns against "counterrevolutionaries," the "One Strike, Three Antis" campaign of 1970 against crime and "corruption," and continued investigations of alleged conspiracies produced new arrests and executions. The 1968–1971 period saw large numbers of political prisoners, labor reform sentences, and executions of people accused of organizing "counterrevolutionary" groups. The state had restored a measure of order, but it did so through methods that reinforced fear.

The military-influenced government was never a single centralized command operating identically everywhere. Different regions had different ratios of army representatives, surviving cadres and former rebel leaders. Some commanders attempted to reopen schools and factories promptly; others treated the suppression of political enemies as the precondition for any return to work. Purges removed skilled administrators and required the hasty training or reassignment of replacements. Reconstituted Party committees also had to decide what to do with officials accused by now-discredited factions. The same political machinery that supplied administrative stability could compel people to repeat confessions or accept judgments made during years of factional struggle.

Military governance raised difficult questions of responsibility for policies that had not been issued by the army alone. When commanders supervised committees or investigation teams, civilians subjected to punishment might have little access to a separate authority. Party institutions could be represented on the committees while being unable to overrule a commanding officer. At the same time, some local military officials argued that production and public security required a reduction in political confrontation. Sources from different provinces therefore record sharply contrasting behavior. The analytical objective is not to declare the PLA uniformly protective or uniformly destructive, but to identify its powers and actions in specific places at specific times.

70.23 Governance, Administrative Discipline, and the Limits of Stabilization

The leadership sought to restore economic and administrative functioning. Railways resumed normal schedules, factories returned to production, and planning bodies were reconstituted, though in a disorganized fashion. Industrial output, which had fallen in 1967–1968 by official figures, rose strongly in 1969–1971. Workers and managers were sent to "May Seventh cadre schools," rural labor camps where officials did farm work and political study, a practice that kept thousands of experienced administrators away from their posts for years.

Stabilization had limits. Civilian officials resented military supervisors, and military officers lacked administrative experience. Many experienced cadres remained under investigation, and morale in ministries and enterprises was low. The Party and state were hybrid structures, in which authority was shared among military commanders, revived Party committees, former rebels who had become officials, and rehabilitated cadres. This overlapping authority produced conflicts that would contribute to the leadership crisis of 1970–1971.

Production was one area in which the limitations of revolutionary administration were immediately visible. Railways and factories required technical schedules, maintenance, accounting, trained staff and uninterrupted supplies. Political committees could mobilize workers but could not always replace engineers or experienced managers. Officials therefore reintroduced conventional procedures while describing them in revolutionary language. Restoring transport or an enterprise's payroll did not imply that its staff could openly question political directives. It is important to separate the return of routine administration from the restoration of legal protection or professional independence. These were different processes, and the second advanced much more unevenly than the first.

Administrative normality could itself become contested. Production managers needed accurate accounts and stable personnel, but requiring workers to comply with safety rules or schedules could be denounced as reliance on bourgeois expertise. Officials sometimes couched necessary technical decisions in approved political vocabulary, or brought representatives of revolutionary organizations into decisions better handled by trained specialists. The cost was not always visible in dramatic newspaper stories: delayed repairs, staff transfers, lost documentation and uncertain authority could accumulate gradually. Research into particular enterprises can demonstrate these effects more reliably than an aggregate claim that all economic institutions either collapsed or recovered.

70.24 Lin Biao's Political Ascendancy

Lin Biao had been a successful field commander in the civil war and was among the ten marshals appointed in 1955. Often in poor health, he had been absent from public life for long periods. He became defense minister in 1959 and, in the early 1960s, turned the army into what he and Mao called a "great school of Mao Zedong Thought," sponsoring study campaigns, the Quotations, and the model-soldier movement. He backed Mao's attack on civilian leaders in 1966 and, as Mao's deputy, became the most prominent public supporter of the cult.

After the Ninth Congress, Lin's circle included senior commanders, such as the chief of the general staff, Huang Yongsheng; the air force commander, Wu Faxian; the navy's political commissar, Li Zuopeng; and the logistics chief, Qiu Huizuo; his wife, Ye Qun, and his son, Lin Liguo, an air force officer, also held influence. Successors designated by a leader face a structural problem: the leader may come to see the designated successor as an impatient rival. The historian's task is to explain Lin's rise without presuming from the end of the story that his downfall was inevitable.

Lin's rise depended both on his military position and on the political symbolism of the army. Quotations from Mao, military political study and exemplary soldiers helped present the PLA as a reliable revolutionary institution when civilian Party organizations were under attack. Yet the armed forces contained their own hierarchies, rival networks and disagreements about the scope of revolutionary intervention. The growth of military appointments increased the importance of personal relationships among commanders. Lin was not simply a passive symbol raised by Mao, but his ability to act independently was constrained by Mao's authority and by other senior figures who feared excessive military power. These tensions made the succession arrangement inherently unstable.

70.25 Mao, Lin Biao, and the Leadership Disputes of 1970–1971

Tensions emerged over several issues. One was the restoration of the post of state chairman, abolished after Liu Shaoqi's fall; Lin's supporters favored a restoration with Mao in the post, while Mao, who said he did not want it, interpreted the proposal as a maneuver. Another was the praise of Mao as a "genius," advocated by Chen Boda, which Mao saw as a way for Lin's circle to position themselves. A third was the army's political role and civilian-military balance.

At the Second Plenum of the Ninth Central Committee, held at Lushan in late August and early September 1970, Chen Boda and others pressed these points, and Mao responded with a short critique, "My Opinions," which attacked Chen Boda. Chen was removed and later purged. Mao then began a campaign to "criticize Chen and rectify the style of work" and took steps to reduce the power of Lin's military allies, including reorganizing the Beijing Military Region leadership and replacing senior figures. Lin himself was not named but understood the implications.

Disagreement over the restoration of a state chairmanship became one element in the confrontation, though historians differ on how to interpret individual statements and motives. At the 1970 Lushan plenum, Chen Boda advanced arguments connected to Mao's position and Lin's interests that drew Mao's criticism. The subsequent attack on Chen weakened Lin's allies and exposed the fragility of the apparent unity of the Ninth Congress leadership. Changes in personnel and the location of armed units must be read alongside speeches and reports: surviving records reveal growing distrust, but not a transparent transcript of every private conversation. For that reason, later official narratives cannot automatically be treated as an unbroken factual account of a single planned coup.

70.26 The Lin Biao Incident of September 1971

Established events can be stated with confidence. On September 13, 1971, a Trident aircraft belonging to the Chinese air force crashed near Öndörkhaan in the Mongolian People's Republic. All nine people aboard died. The Chinese authorities later identified them as Lin Biao, his wife Ye Qun, his son Lin Liguo, and associates. The Mongolian authorities investigated the site. In China, a period of confusion followed, with air traffic grounded and senior leaders summoned. The Chinese public learned of the affair only gradually, after internal circulars in the following months and years; Lin was officially named as a traitor.

The official account, circulated in internal documents, states that Lin Liguo and associates drew up a plan, code-named "Project 571," for an armed coup, including the possible assassination of Mao during Mao's inspection tour of southern China in the late summer of 1971; that Mao returned to Beijing early and the plot failed; and that Lin then attempted to flee to the Soviet Union and died when the plane ran out of fuel or lost control. Following the incident, Huang Yongsheng, Wu Faxian, Li Zuopeng, Qiu Huizuo, and others were arrested and later put on trial in 1980–1981.

The evidence for the last days of Lin Biao is a mixture of official investigations, recollections, reconstructed flight movements, accounts from Mongolia and documents attributed to Lin's son and associates. These materials agree on some essential points but leave uncertainties about Lin's knowledge of the alleged plan, the timing of decisions, and why the aircraft departed when it did. The phrase Project 571 belongs to the official account of a conspiracy, but students should ask what can be independently established about its authorship and operational significance. The political aftermath is clearer: people who had been told to regard Lin as Mao's constitutionally designated successor had to accept the assertion that he had betrayed the revolution. That reversal damaged the credibility of political education and opened space for further changes in elite alignments.

The aftermath placed exceptional demands on official propaganda. Portraits, textbooks and political rituals had elevated Lin to a position of unquestioned loyalty, yet the public now received an account presenting that same leader as a traitor. Explanations unfolded in stages and not every local audience encountered the same information at the same time. These changes affected confidence in political instruction and influenced later campaigns that attempted to reinterpret the recent past. Students should compare the language of the 1969 Ninth Congress with documents issued after 1971, asking how each describes the legitimacy of the political order. This is an exercise in source criticism, not an invitation to assume that every later claim must be false.

Historical Debate

The Lin Biao Incident and Its Sources

The question: How much of the official account of a coup plot and attempted flight can be independently verified?

Interpretation A — The official account is essentially accurate: Scholars who accept its main outline note the existence of the "Project 571" document, the testimony of surviving associates at the 1980–1981 trials, and the physical fact of a flight to Mongolia. They argue that the documents and the circumstances of the flight are consistent with an attempt to escape after a failed plot.
Interpretation B — The account contains uncertain or politically shaped elements: Other scholars argue that key evidence is unverified: the origin and authenticity of some documents, the extent of Lin Biao's own involvement as opposed to that of his son and others, and the possibility that the plan was less developed than the official version claimed. They note that the Chinese government controlled all investigation and that the participants who could have testified died in the crash or were tried under political conditions.

Evidence: Sources include official internal documents, the trial record, the Mongolian examination of the wreckage, memoirs by officials and participants, and later scholarship. The aircraft crash itself is well established; the details of motives, planning, and Lin's own role are not independently verified.

Why it matters: The incident discredited the Cultural Revolution's leaders and the idea of a designated successor, and the way it is interpreted affects how historians assess Mao's control, the army's role, and the integrity of official history.

Student task: Identify which claims in the official account rest on physical evidence, which on documents of uncertain provenance, and which on testimony given in custody. What additional sources, if released, would be most informative?

The consequences were large. Mao, who was in poor health, suffered a decline in his own political standing and personal well-being. The movement's rhetoric of loyalty was undermined by the discovery that its chosen successor was branded a traitor, and many people privately drew skeptical conclusions about the leadership. Zhou Enlai took charge of daily affairs, and the purge of Lin's military allies reduced the army's political weight. The incident opened a period in which leaders of different views, including those who had been purged, could be rehabilitated.

70.27 Sent-Down Youth and the Experience of Rural Reeducation

The December 1968 directive launched the largest organized youth migration in the history of the People's Republic. Scholars commonly estimate that about seventeen million urban young people were sent to rural areas between the late 1960s and about 1980. The peak came in the early 1970s, and the total should not be treated as having occurred before Mao's death, since the program continued until the end of the decade. Some went to production teams in villages near their home cities; others went to state farms, the Production and Construction Corps in Xinjiang and Heilongjiang, and the border regions of Inner Mongolia and Yunnan, where they cleared land, built roads, and served as a population presence in strategic frontier areas.

Experiences varied widely. Some youths came to value their rural experience, formed friendships, and gained practical knowledge; others suffered from hunger, illness, isolation, hard labor, and sexual abuse. Relationships with villagers could be cooperative or strained, since the newcomers were additional mouths to feed and their work points sometimes cut into villagers' shares. Participation was officially "voluntary," but in many cases pressure from work units, schools, and neighborhood committees made refusal difficult. Families with "bad" backgrounds had limited ability to resist; in some places, families with influence arranged exemptions, which created resentment. Return was blocked by household registration rules, and a small number of youths received places in schools, factories, or the army through personal connections. In 1973, a letter from a Fujian father complaining about the treatment of his son and other sent-down youths led Mao to respond sympathetically, and the leadership issued some reforms. After 1976, mass returns began, and in 1978–1979 sent-down youths in Yunnan staged protests demanding permission to go home (Chapter 71).

Experiences of rustication varied by destination and social position. State farms could offer more organized housing and supplies but imposed rigid discipline; remote production teams might require newcomers to adapt to unfamiliar farming methods and languages. Families with connections sometimes arranged more favorable postings or an eventual return, while others had little leverage. For local villagers, urban newcomers could bring literacy or new contacts but also consume scarce food and housing. Gender shaped exposure to hardship and vulnerability. These distinctions explain why oral histories of sent-down youth range from accounts of meaningful solidarity and personal independence to accounts of lost education, illness and exploitation. A composite scene can suggest these pressures, but documentary case studies are needed to establish particular experiences.

Young people's eventual paths varied widely. Some adapted to rural labor and built close relationships with their host communities; others experienced illness, isolation or a prolonged separation from their families. Opportunities to return might depend on recruitment to an enterprise, military service, selection for higher study, family influence or later changes in state policy. Women could face vulnerabilities and burdens that male recollections underrepresent. The social history of rustication thus requires gender-aware evidence and a distinction between individual memoirs and nationally representative outcomes. The fact that later prominent figures had themselves been sent down does not establish that their experiences were typical of the larger group.

70.28 Schools, Universities, and the Reconstruction of Education

Formal education was heavily disrupted. Universities closed in 1966 and most did not reopen until 1970–1972; the national university entrance examination was suspended and did not return until 1977. Primary and secondary schools reopened gradually from 1967–1968, with curricula reshaped around political study, "learning from workers, peasants, and soldiers," and shortened schooling. Many teachers had been persecuted; textbooks were revised; and exams were criticized as bourgeois.

From 1970, universities admitted "worker-peasant-soldier students" by recommendation from work units and communes, rather than by examination. The aim was to open higher education to groups previously underrepresented and to reduce the advantage of urban professional families. The system did expand access for some rural and working-class young people, and it also created opportunities for favoritism and use of personal connections. Roughly nine hundred thousand students entered higher education through this route, though the figure is approximate and sources differ. In 1973, an attempt to restore entrance examinations was reversed after a student named Zhang Tiesheng, who submitted a largely blank paper with a political letter, was praised by radical leaders. Meanwhile, rural primary and middle school enrollment expanded in many areas, and literacy campaigns reached more villagers, a contribution often noted by historians alongside the damage to advanced learning.

The long interruption to ordinary schooling affected more than examination results. Teachers were publicly criticized or reassigned, libraries were inaccessible, and cohorts of students passed through secondary school with uneven access to mathematics, foreign languages and laboratory work. When universities began enrolling worker-peasant-soldier students, political recommendations and work experience took on greater importance than the previous competitive admission system. Some entrants gained opportunities that had been inaccessible under older social hierarchies; at the same time, disrupted preparation and politicized selection created lasting educational gaps. The pattern changed over time and differed between institutions. The movement did not eliminate all technical instruction, but it made stable professional standards harder to sustain.

The disruption of higher education also had a cumulative effect on academic disciplines. When experienced professors were removed and younger students received shortened or politically selected training, the ability of departments to teach advanced subjects could decline long after formal classes resumed. Conversely, some institutions developed programs combining laboratory work with factory practice, and technical students sometimes gained useful practical experience. How should the historian assess these divergent outcomes? By comparing admission criteria, curricula, examination records and later professional trajectories, not by relying exclusively on either the official promise of worker-peasant education or the retrospective language of a lost generation.

70.29 Literature, Theater, Film, and Revolutionary Culture

Jiang Qing was the most influential figure in cultural policy. A 1966 summary of a forum on literature and art in the armed forces, prepared under her direction and revised with Mao, declared that since 1949 cultural work had been dominated by a "black line" opposed to Mao's thought. It became the basis for sweeping restrictions. Most pre-1966 films, plays, novels, and music were banned or criticized, and writers, directors, and performers were persecuted. Publishing was reduced to a narrow list of titles, dominated by Mao's works and political texts.

In their place, the authorities promoted a small group of "model works" (yangbanxi), usually counted as eight: the Peking operas Taking Tiger Mountain by Strategy, The Red Lantern, Shajiabang, On the Docks, and Raid on the White Tiger Regiment, the ballets The Red Detachment of Women and The White-Haired Girl, and a symphonic version of Shajiabang. They were performed, filmed, broadcast, and taught widely, and they made use of Western orchestral and ballet techniques alongside Chinese forms. Scholars debate their artistic value; many were technically accomplished, and they reached enormous audiences, but they were also a vehicle for restricted political messages and for Jiang Qing's authority. Underground literature circulated privately: handwritten manuscripts, copies of banned novels, and poetry later associated with the "Misty" poets, evidence that cultural life continued outside official channels.

Cultural policy involved both prohibition and production. The attack on allegedly bourgeois work limited artistic repertoires, but the state still sponsored new performances, posters, songs, films and publications intended to express revolutionary values. The prominence of model operas should be understood within this narrower approved cultural space rather than as proof that artistic production ceased. Performers and technicians continued to work, often adapting established techniques to new content. Audiences could appreciate a performance while remaining skeptical of its politics, although evidence for private reactions is difficult to recover. Students should compare official cultural directives with actual playbills, recordings, memoirs and production records to distinguish ideological aspiration from artistic practice.

70.30 Scientists, Intellectuals, and the Contradictions of Technical Development

Scientists and engineers faced persecution as "bourgeois academic authorities." Research institutes were disrupted, journals suspended, and international contacts severed. Yet strategic programs received protection, often through the intervention of Zhou Enlai and military authorities. China detonated its first thermonuclear device in June 1967, launched its first satellite, Dongfanghong 1, in April 1970, and launched its first nuclear-powered submarine in 1970. In public health, a research program begun in 1967 (Project 523) led to the isolation of the antimalarial compound artemisinin in the early 1970s by a team including Tu Youyou, later a Nobel laureate. In agriculture, Yuan Longping's work on hybrid rice reached a breakthrough in 1973, and the strains were distributed widely in the following years.

These achievements did not erase the damage. Many scientists lost years of work; some died; students lost training; and universities and journals were closed. Historians therefore describe a contradiction: sustained progress in selected, state-protected projects alongside a general decline in open scholarly activity and training.

Scientific work was exposed to contradictory demands. Some researchers were denounced because of overseas education, foreign contacts or specialist authority; laboratories lost staff and higher education pipelines weakened. At the same time, strategic sectors such as nuclear weapons, missiles and space technology retained political priority and some administrative protection. These programs depended on technical expertise accumulated before 1966 and on continuing work by engineers who navigated ideological scrutiny. National achievements cannot be used to dismiss the damage suffered by individuals and institutions, just as the persecution of scientists does not mean that every research project stopped. A balanced account compares different disciplines, the varying security status of facilities and the timing of political disruption.

China's space program makes a useful case study. The launch of its first satellite in 1970 required coordinated work by engineers, factories, military administrators and research institutions despite a turbulent political setting. The achievement cannot be separated from earlier investments and from the privileged priority given to strategic technology. It also does not demonstrate that scientists in less protected disciplines worked without interference. Research institutions in the same decade could experience purges, curtailed publications and changing training requirements. Keeping the chronology and institutional location of different projects visible allows students to evaluate claims about scientific progress without erasing the movement's disruptive effects.

70.31 Rural Communities, Public Health, Women, and Social Change

Rural public-health programs expanded markedly in the late 1960s and 1970s. Following a 1965 directive from Mao urging that medical work emphasize the countryside, communes trained village health workers, known as "barefoot doctors," who combined farming with basic care, vaccinations, and sanitation work, and cooperative medical schemes pooled funds to subsidize treatment. By the mid-1970s, there were well over a million barefoot doctors. Life expectancy rose over the decade by international estimates, and the program later attracted international attention as a model for primary health care. Historians caution that quality varied greatly by region and that data are imperfect.

Women were encouraged to participate in labor and politics; slogans said women hold up half the sky, and "Iron Girls" brigades were promoted as models. Women entered local leadership and industrial jobs in larger numbers, but they were under-represented at the top of the Party, were paid fewer work points than men in many rural teams, and continued to carry most household labor. Rural life was shaped by the commune system, in which collective labor and state procurement governed income. Model villages like Dazhai in Shanxi were promoted as examples, though later investigations found that claims about Dazhai's achievements were exaggerated. The gap between egalitarian claims and everyday conditions is a theme in evidence from both official reports and personal accounts.

Village life did not mirror the experience of metropolitan schools. Rural communities continued to plant, harvest, pay collective obligations and manage household needs while receiving political directives through communes and brigades. Campaigns sometimes reopened older local disputes and class-based accusations, but conditions varied by region and local leadership. Barefoot-doctor programs trained village-level health workers with a mix of practical instruction and referral arrangements; their reach and effectiveness differed greatly across provinces. Women could enter new forms of collective work and public participation while still bearing domestic responsibilities and encountering discrimination. These developments require attention to local evidence rather than treating either revolutionary slogans or later nostalgia as complete descriptions of rural experience.

Public health campaigns also had limits that differed by locality. Village health workers could provide basic preventive advice, treatment and referrals, but shortages of medicines, trained personnel and equipment restricted what they could do. Changes in childhood survival, infectious disease or access to care cannot be attributed automatically to a single campaign without checking statistical methods and preexisting trends. For women, the rhetoric of emancipation could support participation in work and public meetings while continuing to impose exhausting dual responsibilities. Everyday experience is best reconstructed through village studies and comparisons across regions, not through promotional images of model communities alone.

70.32 Industry, Agriculture, the Economy, and Uneven Development

The economy neither collapsed continuously nor grew smoothly. By official figures, industrial output contracted in 1967 and 1968 due to power seizures, transport disruption, and factional fighting, and recovered from 1969. Grain output rose through most of the decade, reaching roughly 285 million tonnes by 1975–1976 by official statistics, thanks to expanded irrigation, chemical fertilizer, mechanization, and new seed varieties. Rural industries, run by communes and brigades, expanded and later provided a base for the township and village enterprises of the 1980s (Chapter 72).

The Third Front program, begun in 1964, built defense-related industries in the mountainous interior of Sichuan, Guizhou, Hubei, and other provinces. It included the Panzhihua steel complex and the Chengdu–Kunming railway, completed in 1970, and the Second Automobile Plant in Hubei. These projects were costly and sometimes poorly sited, but they changed the economic geography of the interior. Other infrastructure included the Nanjing Yangtze River Bridge, designed and built by Chinese engineers and opened in 1968. After the opening to the West, China imported entire industrial plants in 1972–1973, in the program known as the "43 Plan." The picture is one of uneven development, with continued investment and technical progress, but also waste, planning disorder, and constrained consumption.

The economic record is particularly vulnerable to generalization. Industrial growth in certain years may reflect new state investment or the completion of projects begun before 1966; declines in other years may arise from transport disruption, factional fighting, shortages or poor management. The Third Front expanded strategically located capacity inland but often imposed high costs and logistical disadvantages. Agricultural performance depended on weather, inputs, collective organization and regional conditions that cannot all be attributed to the Cultural Revolution. For students, the correct comparison is between documented sectoral series and the counterfactual claims that the economy either flourished uniformly or lost a decade of all development. The existence of construction and innovation does not erase the movement's human and institutional costs.

Third Front construction illustrates the trade-off between economic efficiency and perceived security. Factories placed far inland could be more difficult for an adversary to attack, but transport costs, dispersed sites and the expense of building housing and infrastructure could reduce immediate productivity. Some projects created lasting industrial capabilities for poorer interior regions; others required substantial later reorganization. National output statistics also obscure how political campaigns affected individual plants differently. A careful evaluation distinguishes capital formation, actual production, productive efficiency, workers' conditions and the opportunity cost of diverting investment from other uses.

Historical Evidence

Assessing Scientific, Industrial, and Agricultural Performance

Historians use official statistical yearbooks, enterprise and ministry records, scientific publications, and reports by foreign observers. Each has limits. Official statistics from the period were compiled under political pressure, and some series were interrupted; post-Mao revisions corrected some figures; local gazetteers provide detailed regional data but vary in quality; and foreign visitors saw only selected sites. The 1981 Party resolution acknowledged that the economy and science continued to develop despite severe losses. Students should therefore treat aggregate claims of either "ten lost years" or "steady growth" as simplifications and look for evidence disaggregated by sector, region, and year.

Division VI — Foreign Policy and China's Changing International Position, 1969–1975

Division purpose: to explain how China pursued diplomatic and strategic change despite the political turmoil of the Cultural Revolution. China was not isolated for the whole decade; it moved from confrontation with both superpowers to a strategic opening to the United States.

70.33 The Sino-Soviet Border Clashes of 1969

By the late 1960s, the Sino-Soviet relationship had deteriorated into open hostility (Chapter 69). Both sides had massed forces along the border. Disputes over the line, which dated from nineteenth-century treaties between the Qing and Russian empires, centered on islands in the Ussuri River. On March 2, 1969, fighting broke out at Zhenbao Island (called Damansky by the Soviets), killing dozens of soldiers on both sides, and a larger clash occurred on March 15. Further incidents followed in August in Xinjiang. Each side accused the other of provoking the fighting, and historians continue to examine Chinese and Soviet archival material to establish who initiated the first clash and why.

The clashes alarmed the Chinese leadership. Soviet officials hinted at possible strikes on Chinese nuclear facilities, and Beijing responded with civil defense measures, including digging tunnels and dispersing industry, and ordered troops to a high state of readiness. Zhou Enlai met Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin at Beijing airport in September 1969, which eased the immediate crisis and led to border negotiations. At Mao's request, four senior marshals (Chen Yi, Ye Jianying, Xu Xiangqian, and Nie Rongzhen) studied the international situation and concluded that the Soviet Union posed the main threat and that the United States could be a counterweight. These findings influenced the later opening to Washington.

The March 1969 fighting was especially consequential because leaders in both states feared that a frontier confrontation might expand into a larger war. Disputed interpretations of past Qing-Russian treaties helped frame the immediate territorial disagreement, but the danger also reflected the rapid deterioration of the socialist alliance and the militarization of the border. Soviet and Chinese official accounts assigned responsibility differently. The later concentration of troops and discussion of possible escalation raised China's sense of strategic vulnerability. That vulnerability helps explain why senior Chinese leaders began considering a diplomatic opening to the United States, even while public rhetoric continued to portray Washington as an imperialist opponent. Security concerns and revolutionary ideology were operating simultaneously rather than replacing one another.

70.34 China, Vietnam, and Revolutionary Diplomacy

China supported North Vietnam in its war against the United States and South Vietnam, with weapons, supplies, and engineering and anti-aircraft units; Chinese sources report large numbers of personnel serving in North Vietnam between 1965 and 1970. This support reflected both revolutionary solidarity and strategic concern about a hostile power on China's southern border. Beijing also backed revolutionary movements and governments elsewhere in Asia, supported the Khmer Rouge in Cambodia, provided aid to Albania, and financed the Tanzania–Zambia (TAZARA) railway, built between 1970 and 1975 with Chinese loans and labor.

During the early Cultural Revolution, radical activists disrupted Chinese diplomacy: Red Guards attacked foreign embassies, including the British mission in Beijing in 1967, and many ambassadors were recalled. By 1969–1970, professional diplomacy resumed, and China built relations with newly independent states. The tension between revolutionary ideology and national security appears here: China criticized both superpowers but increasingly acted on strategic calculation.

China's relationship with North Vietnam was shaped by shared opposition to American military intervention, geographic proximity and increasingly difficult relations with the Soviet Union. Beijing supplied material support, technical assistance and personnel in various support capacities, while Hanoi attempted to preserve freedom of action among its larger Communist partners. The Vietnamese leadership did not simply follow Chinese instructions; its diplomatic and military priorities could differ substantially from Beijing's. China also maintained relations with revolutionary movements elsewhere, but it had to balance ideological solidarity against the possibility of deeper military confrontation. The opening to the United States therefore created a strategic tension for a government that continued to present itself as an opponent of American intervention in Asia.

The early 1970s sharpened the contradiction between revolutionary diplomacy and state interests. Beijing continued to speak in support of anti-imperialist movements, but its leaders also had to consider the possibility of war with the Soviet Union and the importance of avoiding encirclement. Hanoi's relationship with both Beijing and Moscow complicated Chinese diplomacy, especially when negotiations involving Washington appeared to conflict with public solidarity. China sought links with governments and movements across the developing world, not all of which shared its ideology. The chapter should therefore present foreign policy as a set of practical relationships conditioned by ideology, security and bargaining rather than a single international revolutionary campaign.

Another question is how the Cultural Revolution affected China's conduct toward neighboring revolutionary governments. Changes in the leadership of Chinese diplomatic and propaganda organs sometimes disrupted ordinary contacts, yet strategically important relationships continued. Vietnamese leaders had to evaluate Chinese aid in light of their own war aims and their access to Soviet support. Chinese commentary about revolution likewise did not always translate into a corresponding material commitment abroad. The historian should compare propaganda, diplomatic negotiations, military aid records and decisions by recipient governments before describing Beijing as controlling or directing another movement. These records also illuminate why the diplomatic opening to Washington could coexist with continued assistance to Hanoi.

70.35 The People's Republic and the United Nations in 1971

Since 1949, the UN seat for China had been held by the Republic of China government on Taiwan, though the PRC controlled the mainland. Successive annual votes had blocked the PRC's entry. By 1971, support had grown, partly due to the entry of newly independent states, and partly due to shifts in the position of countries such as Canada and Italy that recognized the PRC. On October 25, 1971, the General Assembly adopted Resolution 2758 by a vote of 76 to 35, with 17 abstentions, deciding to restore the rights of the PRC and to recognize its representatives as the only legitimate representatives of China to the United Nations. The text also provided for the removal of the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek from the seat.

The representation dispute at the United Nations had lasted for two decades after 1949. The Republic of China government continued to occupy China's seat, including the permanent seat on the Security Council, while many governments gradually recognized Beijing. The General Assembly's 1971 vote replaced the representatives of the ROC with those of the PRC. It did not conduct a referendum among people living in Taiwan or settle every legal and political controversy concerning that territory. An accurate classroom discussion should examine the resolution's words, its voting history and the difference between diplomatic recognition, UN representation and territorial sovereignty. China's expanded international institutional position then affected trade, diplomacy and the ability to participate in global negotiations.

UN representation also affected the ability of the PRC to speak in global forums and participate in Security Council decisions. But the institutional change did not erase the separate political institutions governing Taiwan, nor did the 1971 resolution specify a program for their future. Contemporary diplomatic records show that states often distinguished their positions on representation from broader claims about cross-Strait sovereignty. Understanding this distinction is essential for students because later political arguments sometimes cite the resolution for propositions not expressly stated in its text. The primary source window should direct attention to what the General Assembly actually resolved and to questions it left untouched.

The 1971 decision was both a diplomatic breakthrough for Beijing and a political shock for Taipei, whose representatives had long defended the ROC's claim to represent China. Debates in the General Assembly involved competing proposals about expulsion, continued representation and the status of a permanent Security Council seat. The final text should be read in the context of that vote rather than retrospectively supplying answers to questions it did not address. For students, the important methodological distinction is between the wording of a legal-diplomatic act, the positions taken by individual member states and interpretations subsequently advanced by governments.

70.36 Ping-Pong Diplomacy and the Opening of Sino-American Contacts

Both Beijing and Washington had strategic reasons to improve relations. China wanted to counter the Soviet threat and end its isolation; the United States, under President Richard Nixon and National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger, sought a way out of Vietnam and leverage against Moscow. Signals were exchanged during 1969–1971 through intermediaries, including Pakistan and Romania, and through small steps like easing trade restrictions.

The public turning point came in April 1971 at the World Table Tennis Championships in Nagoya, Japan, when an American player, Glenn Cowan, boarded the Chinese team's bus, and Chinese officials, with Mao's approval, invited the American team to visit China. The visit in April 1971 included a meeting with Zhou Enlai. In July 1971, Kissinger traveled secretly to Beijing from Pakistan and met Zhou; on July 15, Nixon announced he would visit China. The episode shows how personal encounters and carefully timed gestures can support strategic decisions already under consideration.

The table-tennis exchanges were public signals of a deeper strategic reconsideration. The American and Chinese governments used intermediaries, especially Pakistan, to explore contacts while avoiding commitments that might provoke domestic opposition or alarm allies. During this process the Taiwan issue remained sensitive for both sides, as did China's support for North Vietnam. Kissinger's secret journey provided a private diplomatic channel in which both governments could test the limits of possible accommodation. The public sporting visit made the rapprochement intelligible to audiences that had been taught to regard the other state as an enemy. These different layers of diplomacy should be reconstructed separately: public symbolism, confidential negotiation and the geopolitical interests motivating both.

The opening also required domestic political management. Chinese citizens had received years of strongly negative descriptions of the United States, while American officials had defined Communist China as a threat. The sudden possibility of a presidential visit therefore needed to be presented as consistent with each government's prior principles. Beijing emphasized strategic independence and the danger from Soviet power; Washington emphasized the benefits of communication and a more stable international balance. When students compare public announcements with diplomatic memoranda, they can see how an important change in state policy may be introduced through symbols that reveal only part of the underlying negotiations.

70.37 Nixon's Visit, the Shanghai Communiqué, and Diplomatic Realignment

President Nixon visited China from February 21 to 28, 1972, meeting Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai and visiting Beijing, Hangzhou, and Shanghai. On February 27, in Shanghai, the two sides issued a joint communiqué. Unusually, it set out each side's differing views alongside shared positions. The United States acknowledged that Chinese on both sides of the Taiwan Strait maintain there is one China and that Taiwan is part of China, and stated that it did not challenge that position; the Chinese side stated that Taiwan was the crucial question obstructing normalization. The two sides agreed to expand contacts and opposed hegemony in the Asia-Pacific region.

The communiqué's format was central to the diplomacy. Instead of pretending that the two governments had reached complete agreement, it recorded separate positions while identifying areas for practical cooperation. Beijing emphasized sovereignty and Taiwan; Washington expressed its own understanding of the views held across the Strait without resolving the island's future by the communiqué alone. The joint opposition to hegemony served as an indirect message in the context of the Sino-Soviet confrontation. Normalization with Japan also required diplomacy over wartime memory, economic interests and relations with Taipei. Students should evaluate these outcomes as a changing international alignment, not as evidence that the Cultural Revolution had ended domestically.

Division VII — The Final Political Struggles and the End of Mao's Era, 1973–1976

Division purpose: to explain how political disagreements persisted into the final years of Mao's life and why his death created both a crisis of succession and an opportunity for political change.

70.38 The Tenth Party Congress and the Reconfiguration of Leadership

After Lin Biao's death, Zhou Enlai oversaw government affairs and, with Mao's approval, began to rehabilitate purged officials and criticize extreme leftist practices. Deng Xiaoping, who had written to Mao seeking to return to work, came back to Beijing in February 1973 and became a vice-premier in April. The Tenth Party Congress, held in August 1973, formally expelled Lin Biao. Zhou delivered the main report. The congress elected Wang Hongwen, the Shanghai worker leader, as a Party vice-chairman, while Zhang Chunqiao joined the Politburo Standing Committee, Jiang Qing and Yao Wenyuan remained on the Politburo, and Deng was elected to the Central Committee.

The resulting leadership was a balance among veteran administrators, military figures, and radicals. Mao himself reshuffled regional military commanders in December 1973, reducing the concentration of power that the army had enjoyed. Students should note that the political environment of the early 1970s was neither a simple return to pre-1966 norms nor a continuation of 1967: it involved the rehabilitation of some victims, the continuation of ideological campaigns, and conflict over the movement's legacy.

The Tenth Congress inherited the institutional ambiguities of the Ninth. After Lin Biao's death, Mao could no longer rely on the earlier succession arrangement, and veteran officials, regional military leaders and the Shanghai radicals competed for influence. Wang Hongwen's rise was presented as evidence that workers could enter the highest leadership, yet his appointment also depended on Mao's support. Deng's reappearance signaled a limited restoration of experienced administration. The coexistence of these figures did not produce a coherent cabinet program: questions of political campaigns, economic management and succession remained open. That helps explain why apparently procedural changes in Party ranking or military command appointments were interpreted as signals of support or distrust.

Leadership renewal occurred under conditions unlike those of an ordinary party congress. The legitimacy of individual officials still depended heavily on the official verdicts of previous campaigns, and the promotions of younger radicals did not settle how expertise or seniority should figure in future appointments. Some veteran cadres returned to government work while others remained confined to lesser positions. The army retained influence, though Mao's transfers of regional commanders signaled unease about entrenched military networks. The political balance of 1973 should therefore be understood as provisional, with competing groups each trying to translate Mao's approval into lasting institutional advantage.

Differences between official titles and effective influence were important in 1973. An appointment to a high Party office did not necessarily provide a secure administrative base, and personal access to Mao could outweigh seniority in formal institutions. The prominence of Shanghai radicals sometimes obscured their dependence on alliances within the central leadership. Conversely, restored veterans could influence ministries and provincial administration despite continuing political vulnerability. Leadership conflict therefore operated through propaganda, appointments, military relationships and government decisions at the same time. This institutional complexity should be retained in students' explanations of the period.

70.39 The Campaign to Criticize Lin Biao and Confucius

In 1973–1974, the leadership launched the "Criticize Lin, Criticize Confucius" campaign (pi Lin pi Kong). Publicly, it linked Lin Biao to the Confucian tradition and attacked Confucius as a defender of hierarchical, reactionary order, while praising Legalist figures from the Qin dynasty as supporters of centralized reform. It involved study sessions, press articles, and reinterpretations of Chinese history (Chapters 11–13). Historians think the campaign was also an indirect political weapon. Radical leaders, especially Jiang Qing, used allusions to criticize "modern Confucians" and officials who restored old policies, apparently including Zhou Enlai, though Mao did not allow an open attack on the premier.

Mao criticized the radical leaders at times. According to Party accounts, in 1974 and 1975 he warned Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen against forming a faction, a warning from which the label "Gang of Four" later emerged. The campaign illustrates how the interpretation of the past could serve present political purposes, a theme introduced in Chapter 1.

The campaign's historical references were selective. Confucius and the Legalists became symbolic categories through which writers could praise or condemn modern policies without naming the living people being discussed. The contrast between an allegedly conservative tradition and revolutionary legalist figures oversimplified ancient Chinese history, but its political usefulness lay in allowing allusions that different audiences could interpret differently. The status of Zhou Enlai during this campaign should be described with caution: scholars agree that radical polemicists tried to undermine veteran leaders, but the precise intention of every article and Mao's response to particular attacks remain matters for documentary interpretation. Cultural history, official media and leadership politics were inseparable during these campaigns.

Campaign language also offered a way to contest the legitimacy of technical expertise and established hierarchies. A criticism of Confucius could appear to be a lesson about ancient philosophy while implying that particular officials had revived reactionary values. Some audiences recognized the political allusion immediately; others encountered it through compulsory study. The campaign shows the danger of treating propaganda as transparent evidence of leaders' intentions, because public messages could be deliberately ambiguous. Comparing different newspapers, internal instructions and memoirs helps identify both shared slogans and disputes over their meaning.

70.40 Zhou Enlai, Deng Xiaoping, and the Four Modernizations

Zhou Enlai was diagnosed with cancer in 1972 and was hospitalized from 1974. With his health failing, he and Mao agreed that Deng should take on major responsibilities. At the Fourth National People's Congress in January 1975, Zhou delivered a Government Work Report that restated the goal of the "Four Modernizations" (agriculture, industry, national defense, and science and technology) and set a two-stage plan: to build an independent and relatively comprehensive industrial and economic system by 1980 and to bring about the comprehensive modernization of the country before the end of the century. The Congress adopted a new state constitution, the 1975 Constitution, which reflected Cultural Revolution principles and eliminated the post of state chairman. Deng became first vice-premier, Party vice-chairman, and chief of the general staff; Zhang Chunqiao took charge of army political work.

The Four Modernizations were not an invention of the post-1976 reform era. Their appearance in Zhou's 1975 report demonstrated that industrial development, agricultural productivity, defense and scientific expertise retained high priority even amid revolutionary politics. But an officially stated goal is not the same as a workable plan or a political consensus on how to implement it. The government still confronted inefficiency, transport bottlenecks, uneven technical training and uncertainty over the rights of experienced administrators to make decisions. The 1975 Constitution embodied features of the Cultural Revolution political order, revealing the tension between modernizing ambitions and the institutional framework under which those ambitions were pursued.

The Fourth National People's Congress created another institutional benchmark by formalizing arrangements altered during the Cultural Revolution. Its shortened Constitution reflected the language of revolutionary political authority and the reduced separation between Party and state. That document stood beside a government report that emphasized professional and technological modernization. The combination is analytically important: officials could agree that China needed more productive agriculture, reliable industry and scientific expertise while disagreeing sharply about the political institutions necessary to achieve those goals. The tension would remain visible during Deng's 1975 rectification and the struggles of 1976.

Zhou's report was delivered to an institution operating after years of irregular national political life. The Congress demonstrated the return of formal government proceedings but did not restore the full constitutional arrangements of 1954. Its political composition and public discourse reflected the surviving authority of the Cultural Revolution. Students should consider why a program emphasizing scientific and industrial progress could be articulated within an institutional framework that continued to impose political constraints on administrators and specialists. This contrast became increasingly visible in the subsequent struggles over Deng's policies.

70.41 Deng Xiaoping's 1975 Rectification and the Renewal of Political Conflict

In 1975, Deng carried out a program of "rectification" to restore administrative effectiveness. He focused on railways, where disruption was holding up supplies, on steel and other industries, and on discipline in factories, as well as on science and education, using documents that called for economic development and for the revival of research institutions. Production in several sectors recovered. The program also reinstated purged officials and restricted factional activity, which threatened radical leaders' positions.

Mao initially supported the effort, but by late 1975 he became concerned that Deng's policies amounted to a reversal of the Cultural Revolution's verdicts. Following complaints by officials at Tsinghua University and by his nephew Mao Yuanxin, Mao in November 1975 authorized a campaign to "criticize Deng and counter the Right deviationist wind to reverse correct verdicts." The sequence illustrates how disagreements about economic management became questions of political loyalty.

Rectification in 1975 was practical and political at once. Railway schedules and industrial targets demanded coordination that years of factional interventions had weakened. Deng's efforts to restore managerial responsibility and technical standards therefore promised improvements in output, but they could also be interpreted as downgrading revolutionary committees and those who gained power through mass campaigns. Mao's initial tolerance did not remove the ideological dispute. When criticism turned toward Deng, the issue was framed as whether rectification amounted to reversing the Cultural Revolution itself. A careful account should distinguish measurable improvements in particular sectors from the broader claims made by Deng's supporters and opponents.

A historian should resist evaluating the 1975 campaign entirely through its later reputation. Deng and his collaborators acted within a state still formally committed to the Cultural Revolution, and their projects did not constitute the full reform program introduced after 1978. Some efforts represented restoration of normal administrative practice; others challenged the political position of officials empowered by earlier campaigns. The available evidence includes speeches, draft documents, ministry reports, and later memoirs, each recording different priorities. Separating immediate administrative aims from retrospectively assigned political meanings makes the struggle more intelligible.

Historical Debate

Revolution, Political Authority, and the Limits of Institutional Reform

The question: Why did the 1975 effort to restore order and development fail, and what does it show about the possibilities for reform under Mao?

Interpretation A — Reform was blocked by Mao's ideological commitments: Scholars emphasizing this view argue that Mao would never accept a program that appeared to negate the Cultural Revolution, so any rectification was necessarily provisional.
Interpretation B — Reform was blocked by factional politics and information: Others stress that radical leaders and Mao's nephew shaped the information reaching an ailing Mao, and that Mao also sought balance between factions rather than the defeat of one.

Evidence: Deng's 1975 documents, records of Politburo meetings reported in later publications, Mao's statements, and memoirs of participants. Each has limits: later accounts were influenced by the post-1976 victory of Deng's line.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians understand continuity between the 1975 program and the reforms after 1978.

Student task: What evidence would distinguish between Mao's principled opposition and the influence of his advisers? How might the outcome of 1976–1978 affect the later recording of these events?

70.42 Zhou Enlai's Death and the April Fifth Tiananmen Incident

Zhou Enlai died on January 8, 1976. His long service and role in limiting damage made him widely respected, and many people mourned privately even as the authorities restricted public displays. Deng delivered the eulogy at the funeral, his last major public role before being removed. In February, Hua Guofeng, a former Hunan official and minister of public security, was named acting premier, not Deng.

During the Qingming festival, the traditional time of remembering the dead, crowds brought wreaths, poems, and banners to the Monument to the People's Heroes in Tiananmen Square. Many of the poems indirectly criticized the radical leaders. Authorities removed the wreaths, and on April 5, crowds clashed with police and militia; arrests followed. The leadership, with Mao's concurrence, labeled the incident counterrevolutionary. On April 7, the Politburo named Hua Guofeng premier and first vice-chairman of the Party and dismissed Deng from all posts, though he kept Party membership. The incident showed that a considerable number of citizens were willing to express views that diverged from the official line, a point later officials would reinterpret after 1978 (Chapter 71).

The mourning demonstrations were not a simple organized campaign for a particular succession candidate. Participants included workers, students and ordinary residents who interpreted Zhou's service and the official restrictions on mourning in different ways. Poems and wreaths could express personal grief, patriotism, frustration with Jiang Qing's allies or support for more orderly government. Security authorities treated the gathering as a political challenge, and the decision to remove offerings helped turn commemoration into confrontation. Subsequent rehabilitations of the incident changed its official meaning. Comparing contemporary notices, police accounts, poems and later recollections shows how a mass public event acquired competing political interpretations over time.

The sequence of official responses matters. Zhou's death prompted public mourning; the removal of offerings generated anger; the April 5 confrontation was then classified by the authorities as a political offense. Each stage involved different participants and decisions. Later accounts that label the entire gathering either a spontaneous democratic protest or a coordinated factional plot simplify the available evidence. The historical task is to describe who participated, what they said in poems and banners, how security forces responded and how subsequent governments revised the official verdict. This case illustrates the construction and reconstruction of political memory.

70.43 Mao Zedong's Final Months, Death, and the End of an Era

In 1976, Mao was physically frail, with failing health and difficulty speaking. Succession was unresolved. Hua Guofeng's elevation reflected Mao's search for a candidate acceptable to both veterans and radicals; one report has Mao telling Hua that with him in charge he was at ease, but the exact wording and circumstances come from later accounts and are treated with caution. On July 6, Zhu De, the veteran commander and former head of the Party's army, died. On July 28, a destructive earthquake struck Tangshan, Hebei. Official Chinese compilations report approximately 242,000 deaths (one published figure is 242,419), while other studies have proposed higher estimates; recorded magnitudes also differ between seismological scales and catalogs. The government declined international aid, and the disaster, in a traditional view of omens, heightened public unease.

Mao died shortly after midnight on September 9, 1976, at the age of eighty-two. The announcement came later that day, and a mass memorial was held in Tiananmen Square on September 18. Millions of Chinese publicly mourned, with feelings ranging from deep grief to anxiety to private relief. The leadership was divided: Hua Guofeng, veteran leaders such as Ye Jianying, and the radical group around Jiang Qing each held different power bases. This chapter ends with Mao's death; the arrest of Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen in October 1976 and the subsequent political settlement are the subject of Chapter 71.

The deaths of senior leaders and the Tangshan disaster intensified uncertainty, but it is misleading to treat the earthquake as a supernatural explanation for political change. Officials and citizens sometimes interpreted the timing through older cultural associations between disaster and political legitimacy; such interpretations are themselves historical evidence about public belief, not evidence of causation. The immediate problem after Mao's death was institutional: no broadly accepted succession mechanism guaranteed which group would control the Party, army and central government. Hua's offices gave him an advantage but did not settle conflicts among veteran leaders and the Shanghai radicals. For this reason the chapter closes with unresolved succession rather than narrating the October arrests, whose causes and consequences require the next chapter.

Division VIII — Chapter Review and Cultural Revolution Checkpoint

70.44 Chapter Review and Cultural Revolution Checkpoint

The governing question of the chapter is this: how did the Cultural Revolution transform political authority, social relationships, education, cultural life, and China's international position, and why did its consequences extend far beyond its formal conclusion? A good answer distinguishes the stages. In 1966, Mao and the CCRG mobilized students against Party institutions, and early Red Guard violence targeted teachers, intellectuals, officials, and people with disfavored class labels. In 1967–1968, power seizures, worker rebellion, armed factional fighting, and army intervention produced both the movement's most chaotic phase and some of its deadliest regional violence. From 1968 to 1971, revolutionary committees, the Cleansing of Class Ranks, the Ninth Congress, and the rise and fall of Lin Biao defined a military-influenced consolidation that carried its own repression. From 1972 to 1976, rehabilitation, diplomatic realignment, ideological campaigns, Deng's rectification, the Tiananmen mourning, and Mao's death defined the final phase, in which leadership conflict centered on the legacy of the movement itself.

A second requirement is to distinguish kinds of evidence: contemporary documents, factional publications, internal Party records, local gazetteers, memoirs, oral histories, foreign reports, and later official judgments. Each answers different questions and carries different risks, and students should say which kind of source supports a claim.

Historical Interpretation

The Party's 1981 Historical Resolution

Source: "Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party Since the Founding of the People's Republic of China," adopted by the Sixth Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee, June 1981. It is available in English translations, including Beijing editions; students should note which they use.

What it says (paraphrase): The Resolution describes the Cultural Revolution as a catastrophe that brought the most severe setback and losses to the Party, state, and people since 1949. It assigns primary responsibility for the initiation of the movement to Mao, attributes the main misuse of the movement to the Lin Biao and Jiang Qing groups, and also acknowledges that Mao's contributions overall were primary and his errors secondary. It states that the economy and science continued to develop in some respects despite the losses.

How to read it: The Resolution was a retrospective assessment written by a leadership that included many survivors of the period, in a political environment that sought to limit the discrediting of Mao and the Party itself. It is therefore a vital source for post-Mao Party history, but not a neutral record. Its distinction between Mao's responsibility and the "cliques" has been questioned by scholars who emphasize broader participation and institutional failures, and defended by others as a politically necessary compromise.

Questions for students:

  1. Who wrote the Resolution, and in what circumstances?
  2. What does it affirm and what does it avoid?
  3. How do its claims compare with scholarship based on local records?

Regional and Frontier Context

The Cultural Revolution varied by region. Beijing and Shanghai, as political and industrial centers, experienced early mass mobilization and leadership contests. Wuhan, Chongqing, and other Yangtze cities saw intense factional fighting and military confrontation. In Guangxi and Hunan, violence was organized locally and sometimes carried out by militia and officials. Inner Mongolia, Tibet, Xinjiang, and Yunnan experienced campaigns shaped by suspicion of "local nationalism," religious practice, and border security. In Tibet, Han and Tibetan Red Guards attacked monasteries and religious objects, and conflict among factions continued. In Xinjiang and the northeast, the Production and Construction Corps and state farms received large numbers of sent-down youth, changing local demographics. Historians therefore avoid generalizing from Beijing to the whole country, and they use provincial and county records to map differences in timing, scale, and organization.

China and the Wider World

The Cultural Revolution coincided with global upheavals: the Vietnam War, student protests in Europe and North America, decolonization, and the Cold War. Some radical groups abroad admired Maoist ideas, and the Quotations circulated widely in translation. China's own diplomacy underwent a larger shift. The Sino-Soviet border clashes pushed Beijing toward a strategic opening to Washington, which also served the American goal of leverage against Moscow. The PRC's admission to the UN in 1971, its normalization with Japan in 1972, and the arrival of foreign trade, including imported industrial plants, altered China's position. These were active Chinese choices, taken by leaders such as Mao and Zhou, not simply responses to outside pressure. The decade thus combined domestic political turmoil with a reorientation of external relations whose effects extended through the reform era.

Chapter Summary

Between 1966 and 1976, China experienced a political and social upheaval launched by Mao Zedong that transformed the relationship among the Party, the state, the army, and society. Mao's aims combined genuine concern about revisionism and bureaucratic privilege with the defense of his own authority and legacy. The movement's origins in the Wu Han controversy, the purge of senior officials, the May 16 Circular, and the Sixteen Points show how a dispute about culture became a nationwide campaign against Party institutions.

The early mass phase, driven by Red Guards, brought violence against teachers, intellectuals, officials, and people with disfavored class labels, together with attacks on religious and cultural sites. The movement's cult of Mao supplied both a rallying point and a weapon for rival groups. Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping, once among the Party's most senior leaders, were purged, and Liu died in custody.

In 1967–1968, the movement moved into factories and local governments. The January Storm, the Shanghai People's Commune, the Wuhan Incident, and widespread armed conflict demonstrated how mobilization could undermine the institutions that mobilization was meant to renew. Regional campaigns in Guangxi, Inner Mongolia, and elsewhere produced some of the deadliest violence. Scholars debate how much to attribute to central signals and how much to local factional dynamics, but they agree that the violence differed in form and scale over time.

The army's intervention restored administrative functioning and ended open warfare, but it also directed campaigns of repression, notably the Cleansing of Class Ranks. The revolutionary committees, the Ninth Party Congress, and the elevation of Lin Biao created a military-influenced Party-state. The rustication of roughly seventeen million urban youths removed a politicized generation from the cities and reshaped families and education.

The Lin Biao Incident of 1971 exposed the fragility of the arrangement. Historians can establish the crash and its consequences but must treat the details of the alleged coup as incompletely verified. The rupture opened space for the rehabilitation of some officials, including Deng Xiaoping, and for the contest between administrators and radicals that shaped 1973–1976.

Society and culture changed unevenly. Education was disrupted and then restructured; culture was narrowed around model works; scientists were persecuted even as strategic programs advanced; rural health care expanded; industry and agriculture contracted in 1967–1968 and then grew. The decade should be neither reduced to complete paralysis nor described as ordinary development.

In foreign policy, China moved from confrontation with both superpowers to a strategic opening to the United States, gained the UN seat, and normalized relations with Japan, while remaining tied to revolutionary solidarity with Vietnam and the Third World. The diplomacy reflected both security concerns and leaders' calculations.

The final years were marked by competing programs: Zhou Enlai's and Deng Xiaoping's emphasis on modernization and administrative order, and the radicals' defense of the movement's principles. Zhou's death, the Tiananmen mourning, Deng's removal, the Tangshan earthquake, and Mao's death in September 1976 left a succession crisis unresolved. Chapter 71 examines how the leadership arrested the radical leaders, rehabilitated the persecuted, and began a new political direction.

Chapter Reflection

The Cultural Revolution matters because it shows, at very high human cost, how a political movement can turn against the institutions that created it. It reshaped the Party, the army, schools, families, and the experience of an entire generation. Understanding it requires attention to Mao's decisions, but also to the roles of students, workers, soldiers, local officials, and ordinary people.

The decade shaped what followed. The leaders who emerged in the late 1970s had been purged or persecuted, and their sense of the costs of mass mobilization influenced the reform era. The educational gaps, the memories of persecution, and the unresolved questions of accountability remain part of Chinese public life, and the Party's official interpretation continues to be debated by historians.

The chapter also reveals broader themes: the tension between revolution and institutions, the diversity of regional experiences, the difficulty of historical evidence for recent and politically sensitive events, and the interaction between domestic politics and international strategy. China's diplomatic opening, in the midst of upheaval, shows that a decade can contain contradictory developments.

Chapter 71 takes up the story at Mao's death, with the arrest of the radical leaders, the rehabilitation of the persecuted, and the political rise of Deng Xiaoping. Students should carry forward the habits of the chapter: distinguishing phases, weighing sources, and resisting simple explanations of a complex and painful history.
