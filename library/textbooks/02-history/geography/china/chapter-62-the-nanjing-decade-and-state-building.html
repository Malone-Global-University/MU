The Nanjing Decade and State-Building

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Between 1928 and 1937 the Nationalist government in Nanjing built ministries, banks, highways, legal codes, and an army, and still found that its authority thinned rapidly as it moved away from the Yangtze delta, from the cities, and from the main rail lines. The decade is best understood as a serious and partly successful attempt at state-building carried out under severe limits.

Before dawn on 12 December 1936, soldiers of the Northeast Army surrounded the Huaqing hot-spring resort at the foot of Mount Li, near the city of Xi'an in Shaanxi province. Inside was Jiang Jieshi (Chiang Kai-shek), chairman of the Military Affairs Commission and the most powerful figure in the Nationalist government. He had come to Xi'an to press his commanders to renew a campaign against the Communist forces that had recently reached northern Shaanxi. Awakened by gunfire, he fled up the hillside in his nightclothes and was found by troops hiding among the rocks. His captors were not foreign enemies or Communist guerrillas. They were two senior Nationalist-aligned commanders, Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng, who believed that fighting fellow Chinese while Japan expanded across northern China was the wrong national priority. The episode, known as the Xi'an Incident, is the dramatic endpoint of this chapter, and it reveals both the achievements and the limits of the government Chiang headed.

This chapter studies the years from 1928, when the Nationalist Party (Guomindang, GMD) completed the military phase of the Northern Expedition and established a government at Nanjing, to 1937, when full-scale war with Japan began. Historians often call this period the "Nanjing Decade." The label is convenient but should be used with care. The Nanjing government never governed all of China directly. Manchuria was detached by Japan in 1931 and 1932; large regions were governed by provincial military leaders who accepted Nanjing's authority only conditionally; Communist-led governments controlled rural districts in several provinces; and foreign powers retained concessions, treaty-port privileges, and military garrisons. "The Nanjing Decade" is therefore a name for a particular regime and its efforts, not a description of a unified country.

What changed during these years was substantial. The Nationalists created a party-state in which the GMD claimed to guide the government during a period of "political tutelage." They established five branches of government on a model drawn from Sun Yat-sen's thought, reorganized finance, regained tariff autonomy, built a central bank, introduced a substantial body of new national legal codes, expanded highways and aviation, and in 1935 replaced silver with a managed paper currency. They also began a campaign of moral and civic discipline known as the New Life Movement. Meanwhile the Chinese Communist Party, after being driven from the cities in 1927, rebuilt itself in the countryside, established a rival government in Jiangxi, was dislodged by Nationalist military campaigns, and survived the Long March to a new base in the northwest.

What continued was equally important. China remained overwhelmingly agricultural, with most people living in villages where the reach of the central government was weak and where landholding, tenancy, debt, taxation, and local power determined everyday life. Regional military leaders retained armies and revenues. The county, not the national capital, was the point at which most people encountered the state, and county government remained underfunded and dependent on local elites. Foreign privileges, including extraterritoriality and foreign-controlled concessions in Shanghai and elsewhere, were narrowed but not abolished. The Great Depression, mediated through the world price of silver, struck the Chinese economy in the early and mid-1930s with serious effects on farmers, merchants, and banks.

Many kinds of people were affected. Peasants faced new taxes, military levies, and in some regions the disruptions of campaigning armies. Industrial workers in Shanghai, Tianjin, and Wuhan lived under a state that had destroyed independent left-wing unions in 1927 but promoted its own labor institutions. Civil servants, engineers, bankers, lawyers, and professors built careers in a growing national bureaucracy and modern professions. Women gained new legal rights on paper while facing a government that celebrated domestic virtue. Students and writers experienced both expanding education and political censorship. Non-Han peoples on the frontiers of Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, and the southwest negotiated with a government that claimed to represent a unified Chinese nation while lacking the power to control them. Communists, and many who were merely suspected of sympathizing with them, experienced harsh repression and, in the base areas, a new and also coercive form of rule.

Historians draw on a rich and contested body of evidence for this period: government gazettes and statistics, Maritime Customs reports, rural surveys by scholars such as J. Lossing Buck and R. H. Tawney, local gazetteers, newspapers and magazines, photographs, diaries and memoirs, captured Communist documents, party records, diplomatic archives, and the personal diaries of Chiang Kai-shek, opened to researchers at the Hoover Institution in the 2000s. These sources were produced by participants with strong political commitments. Nationalist publications emphasized order and progress; Communist records emphasized class struggle and revolutionary success; foreign observers varied from admiring to condescending. Careful comparison is essential.

The Nanjing Decade also connects China to global history. The Nationalists drew on German military advisers, American and British financial expertise, League of Nations technical missions, and Soviet-trained organizational models. China's economy was exposed to the world silver market and the Great Depression. Japan's expansion in Manchuria and North China, and the global crisis of the 1930s, shaped nearly every decision the Nanjing government made. To study this decade is to see a state attempting to modernize while under pressure from rivals within, challengers on the left, and an aggressive empire across the sea.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how and why the Nationalist government was established at Nanjing in 1927–1928 and what "reunification" did and did not mean.
  2. Describe the institutions of the Nationalist party-state, including political tutelage, the Five Yuan system, and the relationship between party and government.
  3. Analyze the administrative structure of Nationalist government from the capital to the province and county, and identify its practical limits.
  4. Evaluate Nationalist economic policy, including tariff autonomy, taxation, banking, the 1935 currency reform, and state-led industrial planning.
  5. Assess the extent and uneven geography of infrastructure development in roads, railroads, aviation, and communications.
  6. Interpret the rural crisis of the 1930s, including tenancy, taxation, debt, flood and famine, the world silver market, and rural reconstruction efforts.
  7. Explain the role of provincial military leaders and frontier regions in limiting and shaping Nationalist authority.
  8. Trace the development of Communist rural base areas, the Jiangxi Soviet, and the social and political methods used there.
  9. Analyze the encirclement campaigns and the Long March, distinguishing military, political, and mythic dimensions of these events.
  10. Interpret the Xi'an Incident as a consequence of Japanese pressure, Nationalist military strategy, and shifting politics among Chinese commanders.
  11. Compare competing historical interpretations of the Nanjing Decade and evaluate them against evidence.
  12. Contextualize the decade within global economic and international developments of the 1930s.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what sense did the Nationalist government "reunify" China in 1928, and in what senses did it not?
  2. How did the Nationalist party-state differ from imperial government, from the Beiyang governments, and from the Soviet model that had influenced the GMD in the 1920s?
  3. Why was it so difficult for the central government to extend its authority to counties and villages?
  4. What were the strengths and weaknesses of Nationalist economic policy, and how did the world economy shape its outcomes?
  5. Why did infrastructure development concentrate in some regions rather than others, and who benefited?
  6. How did the rural crisis of the 1930s affect different groups of rural people, and why did Nationalists and Communists propose different responses?
  7. Why did the Communist movement survive the Nationalist campaigns, and what did survival cost?
  8. How should historians weigh the achievements of the Nanjing Decade against its failures without reading the outcome of 1949 backward into the 1930s?

Historical Context

The Nanjing Decade grew out of the political crises that followed the 1911 Revolution. The early Republic (Chapter 58) had produced a parliament, political parties, and a presidency, but no stable arrangement for civilian control of armies or for raising national revenue. After Yuan Shikai's death in 1916, authority fragmented among regional military governments and rival cliques that fought over the Beijing government (Chapter 59). In this fragmented landscape, intellectual and cultural movements of the New Culture and May Fourth era (Chapter 60) generated widespread demands for national strength, social reform, and an end to foreign privilege. Chapter 61 traced how the Guomindang, reorganized with Soviet assistance, and the young Chinese Communist Party cooperated in the First United Front, built the Whampoa Military Academy, mobilized workers and peasants, and launched the Northern Expedition in 1926. It also traced the violent rupture of 1927, when Chiang Kai-shek and allied Nationalists turned on their Communist partners in Shanghai and elsewhere. The government at Nanjing was born from that rupture.

The new government inherited a country of approximately four to five hundred million people, most of them farmers. Population and agricultural productivity were concentrated in the Yangtze delta, the Sichuan Basin, the North China Plain, and the southeast coast, but these regions were linked by transport networks that remained weak. Rivers and canals carried much of the bulk freight; railroads were fewer than the country's size would suggest; and many roads were unpaved tracks impassable in wet weather. Modern industry, finance, and foreign trade clustered in treaty ports, especially Shanghai, which had a population of several million and housed the headquarters of the major banks, the largest cotton mills, and the foreign-administered International Settlement and French Concession. Outside such centers, the economy rested on household farming, handicraft production, and local markets.

The meaning of "China" in 1928 did not match the modern boundaries of the People's Republic. Taiwan had been a Japanese colony since 1895; Hong Kong and Macao were governed by Britain and Portugal; the Mongolian People's Republic, proclaimed in 1924 under Soviet influence, was de facto independent although the Republic of China did not recognize this; Tibet had governed itself since 1913 under the Dalai Lama's government, with the Republic claiming sovereignty but exercising no administration; Xinjiang was governed by provincial authorities who negotiated with both Nanjing and the Soviet Union; and Manchuria, controlled by the military leader Zhang Zuolin and then his son Zhang Xueliang, would be seized by Japan beginning in September 1931. Nationalist ideology insisted that all of these lands belonged to a single Chinese nation, a claim rooted partly in the territorial inheritance of the Qing empire. Students should recognize this as a political position of the Nationalist state, which was in practice unable to enforce it.

Finally, the Nanjing government operated within a global setting that was both enabling and constraining. The treaty system imposed on the Qing in the nineteenth century (Chapters 52 and 55) still limited Chinese control over tariffs, jurisdiction over foreigners, and some territory. The Nationalists' campaign for "revising the unequal treaties" gained concrete results during these years, but the Japanese military's ambitions and the depression of the 1930s shifted the strategic environment. In the background lay the Soviet Union, which had abandoned its alliance with the GMD and now sheltered the Chinese Communist leadership; Germany, which supplied military advisers; Britain and the United States, which held major commercial interests; and Japan, whose army increasingly pursued its own policy on the Asian mainland.

Section 62.1

Establishment of the Nanjing Government

Nanjing had a long history as a capital of southern states, including the Eastern Wu, the Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties, the early Ming, and the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom (Chapters 19, 21, 40, and 53). Sun Yat-sen had briefly made it the seat of the Provisional Government of 1912 and was commemorated there. The choice of Nanjing in 1927 had practical and symbolic reasons. It lay in the Yangtze delta, the most prosperous region of the country and the base of the financiers and merchants of nearby Shanghai, whose financial support Chiang Kai-shek sought after the April 1927 purge. It allowed the new government to distance itself from Beijing and the northern warlord tradition. And it connected the regime symbolically to Sun Yat-sen, whose burial place was planned for Purple Mountain on Nanjing's eastern edge.

The Nanjing government was proclaimed on 18 April 1927, six days after the Shanghai purge, in competition with the Wuhan government that included left-wing Nationalists and, for some weeks, Communists. The two Nationalist governments were reconciled in the autumn after the Wuhan leaders broke with the Communists. Chiang briefly resigned in August 1927, partly because of military reverses and partly because of pressure from rival factions, and was out of office until early 1928. During that interval, Nationalist leaders including Wang Jingwei, Hu Hanmin, and regional commanders such as Li Zongren and Feng Yuxiang maneuvered for influence. Chiang's return in January 1928 was supported by Song Ziwen's financial arrangements and by his marriage in December 1927 to Song Meiling, which tied him to a powerful network of Shanghai and Christian-educated elites.

The military phase of the Northern Expedition resumed in April 1928, now organized into four army groups led by Chiang, Feng Yuxiang, Yan Xishan, and Li Zongren. This list itself shows the character of the victory: the armies that took the north belonged to coalition partners whose loyalty was conditional. The campaign was complicated by the Jinan Incident of May 1928, when Chinese and Japanese troops clashed in Shandong, leading to Japanese occupation of parts of Jinan and the killing of Chinese officials. Chiang chose to bypass Jinan rather than fight Japan, a decision that exemplified a pattern he followed throughout the decade: avoiding large confrontations with Japan while pursuing internal consolidation. The northern warlord Zhang Zuolin withdrew from Beijing in early June and was killed on 4 June when a bomb destroyed his train near Shenyang; Japanese Kwantung Army officers were responsible, though this was concealed for years. Yan Xishan's forces entered Beijing, which the Nationalists renamed Beiping ("Northern Peace") to mark that the national capital had moved south.

The final step of nominal unification came in Manchuria. Zhang Xueliang, who had succeeded his father, faced Japanese pressure to remain autonomous. On 29 December 1928 he announced his allegiance to the Nanjing government by raising its flag, an act often called the "change of banners." Nationalist leaders celebrated the completion of the Northern Expedition and Sun Yat-sen's remains were ceremonially transferred to the Purple Mountain mausoleum in June 1929. Yet "unification" rested on bargains. Zhang Xueliang retained his army and administration in the northeast; Yan Xishan remained dominant in Shanxi; Feng Yuxiang held parts of the northwest and Henan; and the Guangxi clique led by Li Zongren, Bai Chongxi, and Huang Shaohong controlled Guangxi and parts of central China. Nanjing's direct administration was strongest in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and parts of Anhui, Jiangxi, and Hubei. In much of the country, Nanjing governed less through appointment of its own agents than through negotiated alliances.

Foreign recognition and treaty revision came in stages, and it is important to distinguish which government achieved what. The returns of the British concessions at Hankou and Jiujiang occurred in early 1927 under the Wuhan Nationalist government, in the wake of mass demonstrations and a negotiated settlement, and therefore belong to the revolutionary phase described in Chapter 61 rather than to the Nanjing regime. After 1928, the Nanjing government secured the more durable diplomatic gains. During 1928–1930 the United States and then other powers agreed to restore Chinese tariff autonomy, and in 1930 Britain agreed to return the leased territory of Weihaiwei in Shandong. Nanjing also announced that it intended to end extraterritoriality, but the powers resisted, and the privilege was not abolished until the Second World War (Chapter 64). These were real, if limited, changes to the semicolonial order, and they gave the government a measure of nationalist legitimacy, but they left the great Shanghai concessions and foreign naval and military privileges largely intact.

Section 62.2

Chiang Kai-shek, Party Factions, and the Sources of Power

Jiang Jieshi (Chiang Kai-shek, 1887–1975) was born into a salt-merchant family in Fenghua, Zhejiang. He trained at military schools in Baoding and Japan, joined the revolutionary movement around Sun Yat-sen, and became commandant of the Whampoa Military Academy in 1924 (Chapter 61). His rise after Sun's death in 1925 depended on command of officers who had trained at Whampoa, on his role in the Northern Expedition, and on the decisions of 1926–1927 that removed rivals and Communist partners. By late 1928 he held the chairmanship of the National Government and of the Military Affairs Commission, and he was commander of the central armies, though these offices were only part of what gave him influence.

Chiang's power rested on several resources. He commanded the most reliable body of troops, the "central army" built around Whampoa graduates. He had access to Shanghai finance through Song Ziwen and Kong Xiangxi and through his December 1927 marriage to Song Meiling, whose family connected him to Christian-educated and commercial elites; he was baptized as a Methodist in 1930, a decision that helped his standing with American Protestants and mission networks though his personal religiosity is debated. His private diaries, now at the Hoover Institution, show a man who thought in terms of moral self-discipline, admired the nineteenth-century official Zeng Guofan (Chapter 53), and distrusted both Communists and regional rivals. He was a shrewd manager of factions, shifting offices among followers, rewarding loyalty, and dividing opponents with money and appointments. He resigned more than once, in 1927 and 1931, partly as a tactic that exposed how difficult it was for others to govern without him.

The label "dictator" fits the Nanjing regime only loosely. Chiang's authority was personal and often decisive in military and financial matters, and a cult of leadership developed around him in the mid-1930s, especially at the celebration of his fiftieth birthday in October 1936. But he repeatedly had to bargain with party rivals, regional commanders, bankers, and foreign powers, and he lacked a disciplined mass party to carry out policy in the countryside. The Nanjing regime is better understood as a coalition of factions held together by Chiang's military and political skills, and by shared fear of the Communists and Japan, than as a single, centrally directed machine.

Factions within the GMD mattered as much as institutions. The "Whampoa clique" consisted of officers who had trained at the academy and were personally loyal to Chiang. The "CC Clique," led by the brothers Chen Guofu and Chen Lifu, dominated party organization and, in some periods, education and intelligence. A group of technocrats and bankers, including Song Ziwen and Kong Xiangxi, controlled finance. The "Political Study Group" included conservative bureaucrats and professionals. In 1932 a secret organization of young officers, the Society of Vigorous Practice, often called the "Blue Shirts" because of comparisons to European fascist movements, promoted loyalty to Chiang and sought to enforce discipline. Historians debate how far this group resembled European fascism and how much influence it had; the evidence suggests that it was an elite network inspired by some European models, but that Chiang used it selectively and kept it under his control.

Section 62.3

Political Tutelage, the Five Yuan, and the Party-State

The Nationalist political system was built on ideas drawn from Sun Yat-sen's later writings, especially his Fundamentals of National Reconstruction (1924) and his lectures on the Three Principles of the People: nationalism, democracy (or "people's rights"), and people's livelihood. Sun had argued that China should pass through three stages: a period of military government to defeat the warlords, a period of political tutelage during which the party would train the people in the exercise of self-government, and a final period of constitutional government. In October 1928 the GMD formally declared that China had entered the second stage. The Organic Law of the National Government and the Outline of Political Tutelage specified that the party, through its National Congress and Central Executive Committee, would exercise sovereignty on behalf of the people until constitutional government could be introduced.

Historical Vocabulary
Political Tutelage (xunzheng, 训政) and the Party-State

Political tutelage was the Nationalist doctrine that the people needed a period of instruction under the guidance of a revolutionary party before they could exercise full democratic rights. In practice it meant that the Guomindang claimed a monopoly on legitimate political leadership, and that government organs were formally subordinate to party committees. Historians call such a system a party-state, a government in which a ruling party's organization overlaps with and directs the state.

The term should not be read as meaning that the Nationalist party-state was as thoroughly organized as the later Communist one. The GMD had weak local branches, factions that quarreled continually, and a membership that was strongest among officials, military officers, and urban professionals. Its leaders often justified tutelage in the name of Sun Yat-sen's plan, while critics at the time, including liberal intellectuals, argued that it had become a pretext for postponing representative government.

The government was organized into five branches, called Yuan, following Sun's scheme for combining Western separation of powers with older Chinese institutions: the Executive Yuan, which functioned as a cabinet and administered ministries; the Legislative Yuan, which drafted laws; the Judicial Yuan, which supervised courts; the Examination Yuan, which was to select and certify officials, drawing consciously on the imperial examination tradition (Chapter 41); and the Control Yuan, which was to audit and impeach officials, echoing the old censorate. Above the Yuan stood the National Government Council, headed by a chairman, and above that, in theory, the party's Central Executive Committee and its Central Political Council. The Military Affairs Commission, headed by Chiang Kai-shek, controlled the army and became in practice the most important single institution.

This formal structure concealed real conflict, and the sequence of the 1931 crisis matters. The Legislative Yuan's first president, Hu Hanmin, was a doctrinal conservative who insisted on the supremacy of party over government and objected to Chiang's push for a provisional constitution that would strengthen the presidency. In late February 1931 Chiang had Hu detained, and Hu's allies, joined by Wang Jingwei's supporters and southern military leaders, formed a rival government in Guangzhou in May. Chiang nevertheless proceeded: the Provisional Constitution of the Period of Political Tutelage took effect on 1 June 1931. The split was still unresolved when Japan seized Mukden in September 1931, and the national emergency made reconciliation urgent. Chiang resigned the chairmanship of the National Government in December 1931, Lin Sen became formal head of state, and Wang Jingwei headed the Executive Yuan from early 1932 while Chiang concentrated on military affairs. This arrangement gave Chiang the army and, through allies, much of finance, while Wang handled civil administration and diplomacy. After an assassination attempt on Wang in November 1935, Wang withdrew, and Chiang assumed a wider civil role.

Primary Source Window

Sun Yat-sen, Fundamentals of National Reconstruction (Jianguo dagang), 1924

Creator: Sun Yat-sen, leader of the Guomindang · Date: 1924 · Type: Political program written for the reorganized GMD; later adopted as a foundation of Nationalist government

Context: Sun wrote the outline while reorganizing his party in Guangzhou with Soviet advice. After his death in 1925, the document and his lectures became a near-scriptural authority for competing GMD factions. In 1928 the party cited it to justify the period of tutelage.

Paraphrase: Sun described national reconstruction as proceeding through three periods. In the first, military force would defeat the obstacles to unification. In the second, the party would assist local governments in preparing citizens for self-rule, county by county. In the third, a constitution would be adopted and power transferred to elected bodies.

Analysis: The text assigns a large role to the county as the basic unit of self-government, a point later Nationalists often neglected in practice. It also treats the timing of each stage as determined by the party rather than by the people, which allowed leaders to postpone constitutional rule indefinitely. The source shows what the party said it intended, not what it achieved, and it does not discuss how factional conflict or military power would be managed.

  3. What can historians learn from it about Nationalist ideology?
  4. What can they not learn from it alone about how the Nanjing government actually worked?
Section 62.4

Regional Militarists and the Central Plains War

The coalition that had completed the Northern Expedition was an alliance of armies rather than a single force. Feng Yuxiang, Yan Xishan, Li Zongren, and Zhang Xueliang each had their own troops, officers, arsenals, and tax revenues, and each saw the new government as a framework that could protect or threaten his autonomy. Chiang's attempt in 1929 to reduce the armies from well over a million men to a much smaller number threatened precisely these resources. What followed is best understood as a series of struggles over the terms of unification.

Military power remained fragmented. In 1929, Chiang called a conference to reduce and reorganize the armies, which regional commanders saw as a threat to their own forces. The result was a series of wars. In 1929 Chiang defeated the Guangxi clique's challenge in central China and faced a revolt by Feng Yuxiang. In 1930 Feng, Yan Xishan, and Li Zongren, with Wang Jingwei's Reorganizationist faction, formed an anti-Chiang coalition and fought the Central Plains War, among the largest conflicts of the Republic, with heavy casualties estimated by some at a quarter of a million. The decisive moment came in September 1930 when Zhang Xueliang, whose Northeast Army was courted by both sides, entered the fighting on Chiang's side. The war strengthened Chiang's central forces and led to Zhang's greater role in North China, but it also drained resources and weakened the regime just before the Manchurian crisis.

Later challenges continued. In 1931 the Guangzhou government formed in protest at Hu Hanmin's detention; in 1933–1934 the Fujian Rebellion, led by officers of the Nineteenth Route Army who had fought the Japanese at Shanghai in 1932, proclaimed a People's Government in Fuzhou, seeking an alternative to both Chiang and the Communists; Nanjing suppressed it. In 1936 the Liangguang Incident, a revolt by Guangdong and Guangxi leaders, collapsed when Chen Jitang's air force and generals defected, allowing Nanjing to extend control over Guangdong. Each episode shows that "unification" required repeated political and military bargaining.

Nanjing's victories in these wars owed as much to money, defections, and diplomacy as to battlefield superiority. Chiang and his financial advisers spent heavily to detach commanders from rival coalitions, and Zhang Xueliang's decision in 1930 was decisive. The wars strengthened the central forces and Chiang's personal position, but they also exhausted resources, deepened mutual suspicion, and diverted attention just as Japan prepared its move in Manchuria. They also showed how Nationalist strategy worked: rather than annihilating rivals, it often reabsorbed them into the system with reduced autonomy, a method that kept the regime together while leaving regional power only partly broken.

Regional and Frontier Context

Nanjing and the Frontiers

Northeast. After Japan's occupation of Manchuria in 1931–1932, the Qing's last emperor Puyi was installed as head of the puppet state of Manchukuo, which Japan sponsored with Manchu, Mongol, Han, Korean, and Japanese participation. Nanjing refused recognition but could do little militarily; the League of Nations' Lytton Commission criticized Japan, and Japan left the League in 1933. Inner Mongolia. Mongol princes, led by Prince De (Demchugdongrub), pursued autonomy in the 1930s, negotiating with Nanjing and with Japanese-backed forces; their choices reflected local aims as well as outside pressure. Xinjiang. Provincial rulers governed with limited reference to Nanjing; after 1933 Sheng Shicai established a regime that relied on Soviet support. Tibet and Qinghai–Gansu. Lhasa remained autonomous in practice, while the Ma family consolidated Muslim-led military governments in the northwest. Southwest. Yunnan, Guizhou, and Sichuan, home to Yi, Miao, Tibetan, and other peoples as well as Han migrants, remained under provincial militarists until the central armies' entry in 1935. In each case, local rulers, communities, and foreign powers made their own calculations, and the Nationalist claim to sovereignty stood alongside very different realities.

Division II — Building the State
Section 62.5

Central and Provincial Administration

State-building means the construction of the institutions, personnel, revenue systems, and capacities through which a government can govern its territory. The Nationalists approached it through several channels: ministries and technical agencies in Nanjing, a provincial administration under governors appointed by the party, a reorganized county system, a modern army, a police and intelligence apparatus, and educational and propaganda institutions meant to shape the loyalties of citizens. The success of each channel varied, and the variations help explain the unevenness of the regime.

At the center, Nanjing grew into a new capital. Foreign and Chinese planners, including the American architect Henry Murphy, produced a Capital Plan in 1929 envisioning ministries, boulevards, and a monumental Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum designed by the architect Lu Yanzhi. Ministries of Finance, Railways, Industry, Education, Communications, and others recruited people trained in China's universities and abroad: economists, engineers, lawyers, and statisticians. The Academia Sinica, founded in 1928 under the educator Cai Yuanpei, brought together research institutes in history, philology, geology, and the sciences, and the excavation of Anyang (Chapter 7) was carried out under its auspices. The Examination Yuan began holding civil service examinations in the early 1930s, though only a small number of the country's officials were recruited through them. Many positions were still filled through patronage, kinship, and factional ties.

Provincial government was more difficult. Governors were nominally appointed by Nanjing, but in many provinces the governor was the military leader, and local armies dominated provincial finance. In Shanxi, Yan Xishan maintained an independent administrative model; in Guangdong, Chen Jitang headed a regime that was almost autonomous until 1936; in Yunnan, Long Yun managed a mountainous province with its own revenue; in Sichuan, rival commanders divided the basin; and in the northwest, the Ma family controlled Qinghai and parts of Gansu. Chiang's strategy was to build central authority gradually: defeating the most dangerous rivals in war, using financial inducements and promotions to separate others from them, extending railways and telegraph lines, and posting central officers and troops into provinces under cover of campaigns against Communists. The pursuit of the Red Army through Guizhou, Yunnan, and Sichuan in 1934–1935, discussed in Section 62.21, gave Nanjing an opportunity to reach regions it had previously barely influenced.

Personnel were a persistent problem. Nanjing's ministries employed thousands of clerks and officials, many recruited through patronage and factional ties. Salaries for lower officials were modest, which encouraged side income and dependence on superiors. The Examination Yuan held its first higher civil-service examinations in the early 1930s, but candidates entered a system in which political connections often mattered more than test results. Tension also existed between the party apparatus and the government: party committees claimed authority over officials, while ministers resisted party interference. The government's principal fiscal and political support came from the Yangtze delta provinces of Jiangsu and Zhejiang, which were also where its administration worked best.

Section 62.6

Law and Legal Administration

The legal system was another area of institutional building. Beginning in the late 1920s the Nationalists promulgated a set of codes, often called the Six Codes, including a Civil Code, Criminal Code, and procedural laws, drawing on Japanese, German, and Swiss models and on the reforms of the late Qing and early Republic (Chapter 56). The Civil Code, enacted in stages in 1929 and 1931, recognized the principle that marriage required the consent of the parties and gave daughters inheritance rights in principle. These provisions were significant on paper, but enforcement was uneven, many disputes were settled by lineage and local mediation, and courts existed in few counties. The legal system also served political purposes. Emergency criminal laws and special tribunals were used against Communists and critics, and publication and press laws restricted dissent.

The Judicial Yuan, under figures such as Wang Chonghui, supervised a court system of district courts, high courts, and a Supreme Court. This structure existed mainly in cities and larger county seats. In most counties there was no separate modern court, and the magistrate, assisted by clerks, continued to hear civil and criminal cases as in imperial times. Lawyers' associations and legal journals grew in Shanghai, Nanjing, and other cities, and judges and lawyers educated in Chinese and foreign law schools formed a new profession. But the legal system remained thin, expensive, and far from the experience of most villagers, who relied on lineage elders, local notables, and informal mediation to settle disputes.

Law was also tied to sovereignty. The Nanjing government announced that it intended to end foreign extraterritorial jurisdiction, and in the late 1920s and 1930 it took over the Mixed Court in Shanghai's International Settlement, replacing it with Chinese-staffed courts operating under agreements with foreign authorities. These steps reduced, but did not remove, foreign legal privilege. Negotiations with Britain, the United States, and others continued through the 1930s but were slowed by Japanese aggression and by the powers' doubts about Chinese courts. For Nationalist officials, legal codification was a means to demonstrate that China met international standards and to justify the end of the treaty system.

Political justice operated on different principles. Emergency criminal laws of 1931 and special tribunals dealt with Communists and others deemed to endanger the Republic, and military courts imposed severe penalties, including execution. Many suspects were held without ordinary trial, and detention and torture were reported by contemporaries. At the same time, courts in major cities sometimes handled commercial and civil disputes with relative independence, and the arrest and trial of the "Seven Gentlemen" of the National Salvation Association in 1936–1937 became a public test of the regime's tolerance for patriotic dissent. Historians therefore treat Nationalist law as both a real institutional achievement in civil and commercial regulation and an instrument of political control.

Section 62.7

Education, Ideology, and the Public Sphere

The Nationalists also sought to build loyalty through schools, the press, and mass ritual. The Ministry of Education required instruction in the Three Principles of the People, flag ceremonies, and commemorations of Sun Yat-sen. The government promoted a standardized national language based on the Beijing dialect, expanded primary schooling, and supported universities, though most Chinese adults remained illiterate and school access was uneven by region and gender. Broadcasting began with the Central Broadcasting Station in Nanjing in 1928. The New Life Movement, launched by Chiang and Song Meiling in 1934, urged citizens to observe cleanliness, order, frugality, and punctuality and to revive virtues associated with Confucian tradition, presented as the basis of a disciplined modern citizenry. It had some local effects, such as public-hygiene campaigns, but critics at the time and historians since have noted a gap between its moral rhetoric and the conditions of poverty and insecurity that shaped everyday behavior.

Higher education and scientific institutions expanded within an ideological framework. Universities such as National Central University in Nanjing, Peking and Tsinghua in Beiping, and mission and private universities in Shanghai, Tianjin, and elsewhere trained engineers, physicians, economists, and teachers, and Chinese scholars published in the sciences, history, archaeology, and the social sciences. The Nationalists sought to make these institutions serve national goals and required courses in party doctrine, which students and faculty often resented. Cai Yuanpei, the leading liberal educator, defended academic freedom while serving the government. Women's education grew, with women admitted to many universities, and legal bans on foot-binding and other customs were promulgated, though enforcement varied widely and many rural women remained illiterate and subject to older family constraints.

The regime's relationship with civil society was ambivalent. It tolerated, and often relied on, chambers of commerce, banking associations, and professional groups, especially in Shanghai, but it also asserted control. The government dissolved or restructured independent unions in favor of party-supervised labor organizations, used censorship against publications, and arrested or killed critics. In February 1931, several young left-wing writers were executed in Shanghai. In 1933 Yang Xingfo, a prominent liberal secretary-general of the Academia Sinica who had criticized political repression, was assassinated by agents believed to be linked to Nationalist security services. Writers such as Lu Xun used satire and the League of Left-Wing Writers (1930) to resist censorship. The Nanjing regime's repression of the left coexisted with active promotion of science, engineering, and urban modernization, and students should avoid reducing it to one or the other.

Historical Evidence

Reading the Sources for the Nanjing Decade

Official publications and statistics. Government gazettes, ministry reports, and yearbooks provide data on budgets, trade, schools, and roads, but figures were often compiled by local officials with incentives to inflate achievements or conceal shortfalls. Maritime Customs records are among the best statistical series, because the Chinese Maritime Customs Service was run with relatively consistent methods, although they cover mainly foreign trade and treaty-port activity.

Surveys and social science. Studies such as J. Lossing Buck's Land Utilization in China (1937), R. H. Tawney's Land and Labour in China (1932), and the work of Chinese sociologists and the Academia Sinica sampled villages and farms; they are invaluable but not randomly representative. Newspapers and magazines reveal urban public opinion and political debate, subject to censorship and commercial bias. Party and private papers, including Chiang Kai-shek's diaries, show motives and private calculations but are selective and reflect the author's self-image. Captured and later published Communist documents illuminate base-area policies but were produced in wartime conditions and later edited for political purposes. Historians compare these sources against one another and against physical evidence such as railway lines, buildings, and photographs.

Section 62.8

Military Reform and the Nationalist Army

The Nationalist army that emerged from the Northern Expedition was large but heterogeneous. Estimates of its total strength after 1928 range well above a million men, divided among central forces, provincial armies, and allied units. Only a minority, perhaps a few hundred thousand, belonged to the central army that Chiang commanded directly. Attempts to disband or reduce regional units failed in 1929, and the Central Plains War showed the limits of Nanjing's control. Chiang therefore concentrated on building an elite core, equipped and trained to a higher standard, around which other forces could be arranged.

German military advisers played the central role in this effort. Beginning in 1928, a mission headed successively by Max Bauer (who died in 1929), Hermann Kriebel, Georg Wetzell, and, from 1934, Hans von Seeckt, followed by Alexander von Falkenhausen in 1935, advised on staff organization, training, doctrine, and armaments, as William C. Kirby's research has shown. Their influence contributed to the reorganization of a small number of divisions, on the order of eight by 1937, into better-trained and better-equipped "adjusted divisions," the creation of officer-training programs including the Central Military Academy in Nanjing and the Lushan training corps of 1933, and plans for fortified defensive lines on the lower Yangtze. Other foreign assistance came from Italian and American advisers for the air force. A military service law was enacted in 1933 and implemented gradually in the mid-1930s. Domestic arsenals at Hanyang, Nanjing, and Gongxian expanded, and in 1936 Sino-German barter agreements exchanged tungsten and other raw materials for industrial and military equipment.

The limits were severe. Command remained factional: regional armies obeyed Chiang conditionally, and officers cultivated personal followings. Corruption, including padded payrolls and diverted supplies, reduced the effective strength of many units. Conscription was often carried out by press-ganging or by local officials who exempted the wealthy, and soldiers from poor families experienced harsh discipline, inadequate pay and medical care, and long separation from home. Logistics, communications, and heavy equipment were weak compared with those of Japan. Military spending absorbed a very large share of central expenditure, often cited as more than forty percent, which limited investment in other state functions. The army that fought Japan in 1937 was therefore a mixture of improved elite units and much weaker forces, a fact that conditioned both strategy and outcomes in Chapter 64.

China and the Wider World

Foreign Advisers, Global Markets, and Chinese Choices

The Nanjing government was an active participant in global exchange. It invited the League of Nations to conduct technical cooperation in public health, flood control, and economic planning; its representatives lobbied the League after the Manchurian crisis. It employed German military advisers, including Max Bauer, Hans von Seeckt, and Alexander von Falkenhausen, who helped train a core of divisions and influenced fortification and strategy. Sino-German trade agreements in the mid-1930s exchanged Chinese raw materials such as tungsten and antimony for German industrial and military equipment. American and British experts advised on currency and finance. Pan American Airways held a stake in the China National Aviation Corporation, and the German firm Lufthansa participated in the Eurasia Aviation Corporation.

These relationships were not simple dependence. Chinese officials chose which advisers to hire, pursued multiple partners to avoid reliance on one power, and used foreign expertise to build Chinese institutions. Yet they also operated within the limits set by foreign interests, including Japan's, and by the global price of silver, which no Chinese government could control.

Division III — Economic State-Building
Section 62.9

Revenue, Tariff Autonomy, and Fiscal Foundations

The Nationalists inherited a fiscal system in which the central government had little control of the largest revenue source, the land tax, which was collected by provincial and local authorities. In 1928 the National Economic Conference and National Financial Conference, guided by Finance Minister Song Ziwen, set out to build central revenue and credit. Land taxes were formally assigned to the provinces, while the national government relied on customs duties, the salt tax, and a group of consolidated taxes on items such as tobacco, flour, cotton yarn, and matches. Customs revenue became the foundation of national finance, supporting debt service on loans and indemnities and allowing the government to float domestic bonds.

The restoration of tariff autonomy was therefore an economic as well as nationalist achievement. Under the nineteenth-century treaties, China's import duties had been held at about five percent. In 1928–1930, after negotiations with the United States, Britain, Japan, and others, the Nationalists secured agreement to a new tariff regime, taking effect from 1929 and revised upward in following years, with rates for many goods eventually well above the old limit. The government also abolished likin, the internal transit duties levied on goods moving between regions, in 1931, a reform with long antecedents (Chapter 54). The abolition removed a major obstacle to interregional commerce but also deprived provincial authorities of revenue, which they replaced with other levies. Revenue from tariffs, salt, and consolidated taxes rose substantially, but military spending and debt service absorbed a large share of it, leaving limited resources for social programs and rural development.

The central budget in these years depended heavily on indirect taxes, which tended to be more easily collected in treaty ports and large cities than in the countryside. Customs duties, followed by the salt tax and the consolidated taxes, together provided the large majority of central revenue, with customs the largest single source. Expenditure told a parallel story: military costs and the service of foreign and domestic debt consumed most of the budget, leaving relatively little for education, health, or rural development. The land tax, the single largest tax on the population, remained in provincial hands and fell most heavily on peasants, who also paid miscellaneous surcharges. This fiscal pattern helps explain why the regime was relatively strong in cities and in finance and relatively weak in rural administration.

Section 62.10

Banking, Silver, and the Currency Reform

Banking reform was a central project. The Central Bank of China opened in Shanghai in November 1928, and the Bank of China and Bank of Communications were reorganized with greater government participation. The Farmers' Bank, established in the 1930s, extended credit toward agriculture. Shanghai financiers, including members of the Chinese Bankers' Association, were crucial partners and sometimes reluctant ones; in the early years, the government raised money through domestic bonds sold to the banks and sometimes through pressure and, in the case of some capitalists, forcible solicitation. Historians such as Parks Coble have shown that the relationship between the regime and the Shanghai business elite combined cooperation, coercion, and bargaining. The government also worked with underworld figures such as the Green Gang leader Du Yuesheng, who held formal positions in civic and charitable organizations while operating in opium, gambling, and labor control. This reliance blurred the line between state and criminal power in the port city.

The world economy then shaped outcomes in ways that policy-makers could not control. China was on a silver standard, so its currency value depended on global silver prices. Falling silver prices in 1929–1931 made Chinese exports cheaper and cushioned the country from the first shocks of the depression. After Britain left the gold standard in 1931 and especially after the United States passed the Silver Purchase Act in 1934, the international price of silver rose. Silver flowed out of China, domestic money supply contracted, prices fell, and credit tightened. Farmers whose crop prices dropped but whose taxes and debts remained fixed were squeezed, exporters of silk and tea suffered, and banks in Shanghai faced runs and failures in 1934–1935. The regime responded in November 1935 with a currency reform.

Two further steps in the mid-1930s deepened state control over finance. In 1933 the government abolished the old silver tael as a unit of account in favor of the silver dollar, simplifying commercial and fiscal accounting. In March 1935, amid the silver crisis and bank runs in Shanghai, the government used new bond issues to take control of the Bank of China and Bank of Communications, replacing much of their management and bringing the principal commercial banks closer to official direction. These measures were criticized by some bankers as an expropriation of private institutions and defended by officials as a response to emergency. Together with fabi, they set the stage for the later growth of state-controlled finance, sometimes called "bureaucratic capital" by critics.

Historical Vocabulary
Fabi (法币, "legal tender")

Fabi was the managed paper currency introduced in November 1935. The government ended silver's role as the monetary standard: it prohibited private circulation of silver as money, required holders to exchange it for notes issued by designated government banks (the Central Bank, Bank of China, Bank of Communications, and later the Farmers' Bank), and took control of the silver reserves, which could be sold abroad to build foreign-exchange holdings.

The reform was supported by foreign advice and finance but was not a formal peg to any foreign currency. The British economic adviser Frederick Leith-Ross visited China in 1935–1936 and counseled reform, and in 1936 the United States agreed to purchase Chinese silver, providing dollar resources for stabilizing the new notes. The Chinese government publicly described fabi as an independent currency not tied to any foreign monetary unit; in practice its external value was managed through foreign-exchange operations and moved closely with sterling and the dollar, which is different from a documented formal link. Many older coins and local monetary practices persisted for years after 1935.

Fabi ended the silver standard, stabilized money supply, and gave the government unprecedented control over currency. It also gave Nanjing a tool to finance expenditure by issuing notes, a power that would have destructive consequences during the war and civil war, when inflation became severe (Chapters 64 and 65). Students should therefore see fabi as both a successful modernizing measure in 1935 and a source of later vulnerability.

Section 62.11

Industry, Labor, and Economic Planning

The government also pursued industrial planning. In 1932 it created a planning body that was reorganized in 1935 as the National Resources Commission, led by the geologist Weng Wenhao and the engineer Qian Changzhao, to survey mineral resources and develop state-owned heavy industry, with a focus on strategic needs: iron and steel, coal, electrical equipment, machinery, and chemicals. The commission was staffed by trained engineers and emphasized technical competence, and it became an important institutional legacy. Its practical output before 1937 was limited, but it planned plants in the interior, particularly in Hunan and Sichuan, as insurance against a Japanese attack on the coast. Most industry remained in private hands, especially textile mills in Shanghai, Wuxi, and Tianjin, flour mills, match factories, and small workshops. Many were owned by Chinese entrepreneurs; others by foreign firms, especially British and Japanese. Some economic historians, notably Thomas Rawski, argue that modern industry and transport grew at significant rates through the early 1930s; others stress that the modern sector remained small relative to the whole economy, and that the depression and Japanese competition hurt Chinese textile firms.

Chinese industry in the 1930s was concentrated in light manufacturing: cotton spinning and weaving, silk reeling, flour milling, cigarette and match making, and food processing, much of it in Shanghai, Wuxi, Tianjin, Qingdao, and Guangzhou. Foreign-owned factories, especially Japanese and British cotton mills, competed with Chinese firms. The National Products Movement of the early 1930s urged consumers to buy Chinese goods, reflecting both economic and nationalist motives. Factory conditions, including long hours, child labor, and dangerous workplaces, were harsh, and the Factory Law promulgated in 1929 and implemented in the early 1930s set standards that were often evaded. Labor activism continued despite party-controlled unions and police repression, and the regime sought to channel it through official organizations while using informal power-brokers, including Green Gang leaders, to control workers in Shanghai.

Another feature of the economy was the continued importance of the opium trade. The government publicly pledged to suppress opium, announced multi-year plans in the mid-1930s, and executed some dealers. But opium taxes and monopolies provided revenue to provincial and military authorities, and local commanders and officials protected the trade. This ambivalence exemplified the gap between modernizing policy and revenue pressure.

Section 62.12

Roads, Railroads, Aviation, and Cities

Transportation and communications were essential both to economic integration and to political control. For Nationalist leaders, roads and railways were means of moving troops against rivals and Communists, collecting taxes, delivering goods to markets, and giving citizens a sense of belonging to a national community. For merchants and farmers they meant access to markets; for peasants who lived along new routes, they sometimes meant forced labor and land requisition. The decade saw substantial construction, but its geography was uneven.

Highway building was the most visible effort, and its baseline should be stated carefully. By one scholarly estimate cited by William C. Kirby, China already had more than 20,000 miles (roughly 32,000 kilometers) of motor roads by 1928, though most were simple unpaved tracks laid out by provincial and military authorities, passable in dry weather and often unusable in the rains. The decade's achievement was therefore less the creation of roads from nothing than the extension, linking, and improvement of networks. Official figures by 1937 reported more than a hundred thousand kilometers of highways, but statistics mixed improved, graded, all-weather roads with rudimentary routes, and quality varied sharply by province. Expansion was greatest in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Hunan, Jiangxi, and the Yangtze provinces, partly to support military campaigns against the Communists. Roads were built by provincial governments, military authorities, and local communities using conscripted labor, and the motor bus and truck became common in parts of the lower Yangtze. Highways reconfigured trade where they connected towns to railheads and ports, while elsewhere they were too sparse or too poorly maintained to alter existing patterns.

Railroad development was more modest. Many lines in North China and Manchuria had been built in the late Qing and early Republic with foreign loans (Chapter 55), and some were under foreign management or influence, especially in Manchuria. During the Nanjing Decade, the Ministry of Railways, under officials such as Sun Fo and later Zhang Jia'ao, repaired lines damaged by wars, improved management, and completed key links. The Guangzhou–Hankou line, long interrupted by a gap in Hunan and Guangdong, was completed in 1936, creating a continuous north–south trunk route linking Beiping, Hankou, and Guangzhou. A line from Hangzhou toward Nanchang and Zhuzhou was extended, and the Qiantang River Bridge at Hangzhou, designed by the engineer Mao Yisheng, opened in September 1937, just as war with Japan began. No bridge yet spanned the lower Yangtze; trains between Nanjing and Pukou were ferried across the river. The railway network remained thin relative to the size and population of the country, and a large share of lines lay in regions that were under Japanese influence or occupied after 1931.

Aviation and telecommunications developed quickly from a small base. The China National Aviation Corporation, founded in 1929, flew passenger and mail routes along the Yangtze and coast, and the Eurasia Aviation Corporation linked Chinese cities to Central Asia and Europe. The Nationalists also formed an air force with foreign assistance, and Song Meiling oversaw air-force administration. Telegraph and long-distance telephone networks extended along main routes, radio stations proliferated in cities, and newspaper distribution grew with railways and roads. Postal service was among the most reliable national institutions. These networks tended to connect large cities and strengthen the dominance of the coast and lower Yangtze over the interior.

Cities themselves were reshaped. Nanjing's boulevards and ministries, Shanghai's expanding Chinese-governed district of Jiangwan and its civic center, Guangzhou's municipal programs, and Beiping's preservation as a cultural city all reflected differing urban visions. Municipal governments built roads, water systems, parks, and public-health offices. These changes gave the cities a modern appearance and generated employment, but also deepened the gap between city and countryside, a theme explored in Chapter 63.

Division IV — Rural China
Section 62.13

The Agrarian Crisis of the Early 1930s

Roughly three of every four Chinese lived in the countryside, and the rural economy determined the fate of the state. Rural conditions varied enormously. In the lower Yangtze and Pearl River deltas, farms were small, intensively cultivated, and tied to commercial crops such as silk, cotton, and tea. In the wheat and millet regions of the north, families farmed dry land with low yields and high exposure to drought. In Sichuan and the southwest, landlords, military authorities, and local strongmen dominated agrarian life. Surveys by J. Lossing Buck and others suggested that a substantial minority of farmland was tenanted, with tenancy more common in the south than in the north, and that many owner-cultivators held plots too small to sustain a household without by-employments such as weaving, hauling, or seasonal labor. Rents commonly consumed half or more of a tenant's main crop. Debt to landlords, moneylenders, and merchants was widespread, with interest rates that could be punishing.

Several pressures converged in the early 1930s. Taxes rose, both official land taxes and surcharges imposed by provincial and local governments and armies, which sometimes collected taxes many years in advance. The depression and the silver outflow lowered farm prices; rural silk production collapsed as the Japanese silk industry and then synthetic fibers undercut Chinese exports. Natural disasters, including the floods of 1931 and the northwest famine of 1928–1930, destroyed crops and killed large populations. Campaigns against Communists and wars among military leaders devastated some areas and sent refugees toward cities. These conditions did not affect everyone equally: landlords with stored grain and capital could buy land cheaply, while tenants and small owners lost it. Many historians see in these years an acceleration of rural indebtedness and land concentration, although the degree varied by region and remains debated.

Water control and disaster relief were also infrastructure. The Yellow River and Yangtze floods of 1931 and 1935 and the Yellow River flood of 1933 caused severe loss of life; estimates for the 1931 flood vary widely, from several hundred thousand to several million deaths, including from subsequent famine and disease. The government established a National Flood Relief Commission and, with League of Nations and American assistance, undertook some dike repairs and emergency relief, but resources were insufficient. Droughts and famines in the northwest in the early 1930s also killed large numbers. Environmental hazard, weak infrastructure, and the strain on rural households all converged.

Rural hardship also generated movements of people. Famine and banditry in Shandong, Hebei, and the northwest, together with the pull of wage work in Manchuria, had sent millions of migrants northeast in the 1920s; after Japan's takeover in 1931 this stream diminished, and many rural people instead moved to nearby towns and treaty ports. Campaigning armies, whether Nationalist, regional, or Communist, requisitioned grain, labor, and animals, and some areas suffered repeated devastation. The northwest famine of 1928–1930, which affected Shaanxi, Gansu, and neighboring provinces, killed very large numbers of people, with estimates running into the millions, and its causes combined drought, military exactions, poor transport, and inadequate relief. These crises mean that the rural economy cannot be understood through averages alone: conditions differed sharply between a silk-producing delta village, a Sichuan basin hamlet under a militarist's taxes, and a drought-stricken loess county.

Section 62.14

County Government, Baojia, and Local Power

The county was the pivot of state-building because it was the lowest level of regular government. Late imperial counties had been governed by a single magistrate, assisted by private secretaries, clerks, and runners, and dependent on local gentry for tax collection and public works (Chapter 41). The Nationalists passed laws in 1928–1930 to reorganize county government, divide counties into districts, townships, and villages, and eventually to train county officials. In practice, county governments were poorly funded and often subordinated to local power-holders. Many county magistrates changed frequently, and low salaries encouraged corruption and dependence on landlords and local militia leaders. Taxes were frequently collected by intermediaries who retained a share. Because the central government depended on provincial and local officials to collect land taxes, rural revenue stayed largely outside Nanjing's direct reach.

Local tax collection shows how the system worked. Magistrates and their clerks relied on village heads and rural gentry, who knew landholdings and could mobilize neighbors, but who also kept a portion of what they collected or shifted burdens to poorer households. The Nationalist government issued regulations in the late 1920s and early 1930s for local self-government, creating township and village bodies, and in the early 1930s experimental counties, such as Jiangning near Nanjing and Lanxi in Zhejiang, tested administrative reforms with trained officials and expanded services. These experiments produced useful lessons but covered a small number of places and were hard to replicate without trained staff and funding.

Local militia, known as minjian tuan or similar names, and rural strongmen complicated state authority. In 1927 the Nationalist revolution had denounced "local tyrants and evil gentry," but after the break with the Communists the regime relied on landlord and gentry cooperation. Militia leaders could be allies against banditry and Communists, but they could also dominate villages, tax residents arbitrarily, and resist outside interference. The practical result was a form of layered authority: the state claimed sovereignty, county offices administered, and local power-holders mediated or controlled what happened in villages. Understanding this layering is essential for interpreting both Nationalist rural policy and the appeal of Communist alternatives.

Historical Vocabulary
Baojia (保甲)

Baojia was a system of mutual responsibility in which households were grouped into units of ten (jia) and a hundred (bao), whose members were collectively accountable for crime and tax delinquency. It had imperial antecedents (Chapters 31 and 41) and was revived by the Nationalists in the early 1930s, first in provinces such as Henan, Hubei, and Anhui where Communists were active, and later more widely.

The Nationalists used baojia for registration, policing, taxation, and militia training, and in the anti-Communist campaigns to isolate guerrillas by monitoring movement and supplies. Advocates saw it as a means of building local order; critics, then and later, saw collective punishment and dependence on local power-holders, since baojia headmen were often drawn from established village elites.

Section 62.15

Land Policy and Rural Reconstruction

The Nationalists had inherited from Sun Yat-sen the slogan of "land to the tiller" and the principle of equalizing land rights, and in 1927 the Zhejiang provincial government under GMD influence ordered a twenty-five percent reduction in rents. The measure met landlord resistance and was weakly enforced. The Land Law of 1930 set limits on rents, in principle capping them at roughly thirty-seven and one-half percent of the main crop, and gave tenants certain protections, but the law was not widely implemented until limited trials in the mid-1930s. The regime's social base included landlords, urban capitalists, military officers, and officials with landed ties, and its leaders feared that attacking landlord power would split the coalition and strengthen the Communists, who made land redistribution a principal weapon. The Nationalists' preferred approach was therefore technical and administrative: agricultural experiment stations, improved seeds, cooperatives and rural credit, and local government reform.

Outside government, a rural reconstruction movement of educators and reformers sought to improve village life through literacy, public health, cooperatives, and agricultural extension. James Yen (Yan Yangchu) led the Mass Education Movement's experimental county at Dingxian in Hebei, combining adult literacy classes, health clinics, and agricultural improvements. Liang Shuming, a philosopher influenced by Confucian thought, established a rural institute at Zouping in Shandong, aimed at rebuilding community through moral education, village self-government, and cooperatives. Educators such as Tao Xingzhi created experimental village schools. These projects were small relative to the problem and sometimes depended on cooperation from local military or provincial authorities. They also differed in philosophy, with some emphasizing technical improvement and others communal reconstruction. Critics, including Communists and some social scientists, argued that without altering land relations they would not solve the crisis; supporters answered that radical redistribution risked violence and that capacity-building was the practical path.

Division V — The Communist Challenge
Section 62.16

After 1927: Communists Turn to the Countryside

After the 1927 purges, the Communist Party lost most of its urban organizations and tens of thousands of members. The party's leaders, guided by the Comintern, organized armed uprisings in the autumn and winter of 1927: the Nanchang Uprising in August, the Autumn Harvest Uprising in Hunan and Jiangxi led by Mao Zedong in September, and the Guangzhou Uprising in December. All failed in the short term. Survivors withdrew to remote mountainous areas on provincial borders where Nationalist control was weak. Mao Zedong led remnants to the Jinggang Mountains on the Hunan–Jiangxi border in late 1927; Zhu De's forces joined him in 1928, forming the Fourth Red Army. Other base areas arose in the Hubei–Henan–Anhui border region, in western Hunan and Hubei under He Long, and in Fujian and Guangdong.

The party's strategy was contested. Between 1928 and 1930, leaders including Li Lisan, then dominating the central leadership in Shanghai, urged attacks on major cities in the belief that a revolutionary tide was rising. Red Army assaults on Changsha in 1930 failed, and the Comintern criticized the line. A group of Soviet-trained returnees, including Wang Ming and Bo Gu, then took leadership, emphasizing orthodoxy and Soviet guidance. Meanwhile, in rural Jiangxi and western Fujian, Mao, Zhu De, and local cadres built a base on land revolution and guerrilla war. Harsh internal struggles marked the period. In late 1930 the Futian Incident and the campaign against the alleged "AB League" involved the arrest and execution of large numbers of suspected opponents inside the Red Army and local party, a dark episode that historians have documented from Communist and Nationalist sources. In 1931, the arrest and defection of a security chief, Gu Shunzhang, devastated the Shanghai party organization, and by 1933 the Central Committee relocated to Jiangxi.

While building the base in Jiangxi, Mao Zedong also conducted local investigations, including a study of Xunwu county in 1930, which analyzed the county's commerce, classes, and landholding in detail. This kind of empirical survey, together with his 1927 Hunan report (Chapter 61), shaped his conviction that revolutionary strategy should rest on detailed knowledge of rural conditions rather than on abstract doctrine. It also illustrates a methodological point for students: Communist documents of this era are sources both about rural society and about how a political movement chose to describe it.

Section 62.17

The Chinese Soviet Republic and Revolutionary Administration

On 7 November 1931, the First All-China Congress of Soviets proclaimed the Chinese Soviet Republic at Ruijin in southern Jiangxi, with Mao Zedong as chairman of the central government. The republic claimed authority over scattered soviet areas; its core territory in Jiangxi and western Fujian included several million people at its peak, though numbers are estimates. It issued a constitution, a Land Law, labor regulations, and marriage regulations; created courts, a bank, a postal service, and a currency; ran newspapers and schools; and mobilized villagers through peasant associations, women's organizations, and the Red Army. Its land policy, which evolved over time and was applied with varying severity, confiscated holdings of landlords and, in some phases, "rich peasants," and redistributed them to poorer households. Classification of households by class status was difficult and sometimes arbitrary, and campaigns to investigate land could generate local conflict.

The Soviet government extracted resources through taxes, grain levies, bonds, and recruitment for the Red Army, which expanded during the early 1930s. It also depended on mobilization through persuasion, local self-interest, social pressure, and coercion. Its leaders targeted landlords and those labeled counter-revolutionaries, and used executions and confiscations. At the same time, the soviet offered some poor villagers land, literacy classes, and organizational roles unavailable to them before, and women found new legal protections in the marriage regulations, including free choice of partner and divorce rights. The republic was weakly integrated: its economy, based on a mountainous region exporting tungsten, timber, and tea, was strained by Nationalist blockade, and its administrative reach beyond the main valleys was limited.

The republic's government included a Central Executive Committee elected by the Congress of Soviets, a Council of People's Commissars, and commissariats for finance, land, military affairs, education, and other functions, with provincial, county, district, and township soviets below. Elections used a class-based franchise that excluded landlords, rich peasants, and others labeled exploiters or counter-revolutionaries. A security organ, the State Political Security Bureau, modeled on Soviet institutions, investigated and punished suspected enemies, and revolutionary tribunals used summary procedures. Party committees supervised the soviets at every level, so that formal state institutions and party authority overlapped in a manner similar to the Nationalist party-state, though with a far stronger emphasis on class categories and mass campaigns.

Economic administration was inventive and improvised. The government issued its own paper currency and silver coins, established a national bank, promoted cooperatives for consumption and marketing, organized the export of tungsten, timber, and other products to Nationalist-held regions, and imported salt, cloth, and kerosene through trade networks that the blockade tried to cut off. Its capacity was limited by geography, the small surplus of mountainous farmland, and the demands of war. The comparison with Nationalist administration is instructive: the Nationalists were stronger in finance, technical agencies, and urban institutions; the Communists in the soviet area were stronger in direct village-level organization and mobilization within a small territory.

Section 62.18

Land Revolution, Class, and Gender in the Soviet Areas

Land policy was the heart of the revolution. The Land Law of 1931 provided for confiscation of the property of landlords and redistribution among poorer households, with details that changed as the leadership debated how to treat "rich peasants" and middle peasants. Villagers were classified by class label, landlord, rich peasant, middle peasant, poor peasant, or hired laborer, a procedure that could be accurate or arbitrary, and that made class status a determinant of rights and obligations. In 1933 a Land Investigation Movement sought to find landlords and rich peasants who had escaped classification; it intensified class conflict and, according to later Communist assessments, produced excesses that alienated middle peasants and local supporters. Mao advocated more cautious application in some respects, a controversy that fed into later disputes within the party.

Marriage reform was another distinctive policy. The Provisional Marriage Regulations of 1931, later revised, proclaimed freedom of marriage and divorce, prohibited child betrothal and the sale of brides, and gave women new legal claims in family disputes. Women's organizations promoted literacy, labor in the fields, and support for the Red Army. These measures gave some women new leverage, but many villagers, especially men and older relatives, resisted, and cadres sometimes limited divorce to protect soldiers' families. Women's labor became essential as men joined the army or served as porters. The soviet's gender policy thus combined an egalitarian legal program with practical constraints and local resistance, and its record illustrates how revolutionary law and village practice interacted.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a Soviet County

For villagers in the Jiangxi Soviet, the new government arrived as a combination of opportunities and demands. Poor households might receive land, but also faced requisitions for the army and for transporting supplies, while families sent sons to the Red Army, sometimes under pressure from local cadres. Women's marriage rights expanded, but many male peasants resisted divorce claims, and reports from the period show tension between women's organizations and village men. Literacy classes, newspapers, and theatrical performances spread party messages. Markets were disrupted by the blockade: salt, cloth, and kerosene became scarce, and local cadres promoted cooperatives and home production. Former landlords and "rich peasants" lost property and political rights, and some were killed. Residents of adjacent Nationalist-held counties faced baojia registration, taxes, and troop movements of their own. Surviving evidence, including Communist reports, Nationalist interrogations, and later memoirs and oral histories, reflects the viewpoints of the producers and must be read critically.

Section 62.19

The Red Army and Competing Forms of State Capacity

The Red Army developed from the troops that mutinied at Nanchang in 1927 and from peasant volunteers and local guerrillas. Zhu De and Mao Zedong combined these forces in the Jinggang Mountains and later in Jiangxi. The Gutian Conference of December 1929 affirmed that the party must control the army through political commissars and political work, and rules of discipline governed treatment of civilians. The army's doctrine emphasized mobile warfare, luring enemy columns into friendly territory, and relying on local support for intelligence and supplies. In 1933–1934 the leadership launched campaigns to expand the army by large numbers; some recruits volunteered, but quotas, social pressure, and sometimes coercion also played roles, and desertion was a persistent problem. By 1934 the Red Army in the central soviet is often estimated at around eighty to one hundred thousand, though figures from Communist and Nationalist sources are uncertain.

The Nationalist and Communist states can usefully be compared as competing forms of state capacity. The Nationalists commanded a larger territory, a modern financial system, foreign advisers, and technical institutions, but depended on landlords, militarists, and local intermediaries in the countryside. The Communists governed a small and poor base but combined party organization, class mobilization, and military discipline in ways that allowed intensive extraction of labor and grain. Each system was constrained by its social base: the Nationalists by dependence on groups that resisted rural reform, the Communists by the limited resources of their territory and the resistance generated by coercion. Neither outcome was foreordained, and the fifth campaign would test both.

Division VI — Crisis and Survival
Section 62.20

The Encirclement Campaigns

Nationalists called the campaigns against the base areas "bandit suppression" or "extermination" campaigns; historians commonly call them encirclement campaigns, after the Communist term. After the Central Plains War, Chiang launched the first campaign in December 1930 with roughly a hundred thousand troops. The Red Army, using mobile warfare and local knowledge, lured Nationalist columns into the soviet and defeated them, capturing a divisional commander. The second campaign in spring 1931, with a larger force under He Yingqin, failed in similar fashion. The third, in summer 1931, was commanded personally by Chiang with perhaps three hundred thousand troops; it also failed to destroy the base, and the Japanese seizure of Manchuria in September 1931 forced Nanjing to halt.

The fourth campaign (1932–1933) had different results in different places, and its outcome should be stated carefully. In the Hubei–Henan–Anhui (Eyuwan) base area, Nationalist offensives in 1932 forced Zhang Guotao's Fourth Front Army to abandon the base and move west into northern Sichuan, where it established a new base. In the central soviet in Jiangxi and Fujian, however, the Nationalist advance in early 1933 was defeated: Red Army forces under Zhu De and Zhou Enlai destroyed or crippled several advancing Nationalist units in March 1933. The fourth campaign therefore failed to destroy the Jiangxi base, even though it drove Communist forces from one important region. This was the background to the more systematic fifth campaign, which achieved in Jiangxi what the first four had not.

The fifth campaign, from October 1933 to October 1934, differed from the previous efforts in method and scale. Chiang had stabilized his position, mobilized several hundred thousand troops, and adopted a methodical strategy supported by German advisers including Hans von Seeckt: advancing slowly, building chains of blockhouses and roads, tightening an economic blockade on salt and other goods, organizing baojia in recovered areas, and training officers at Lushan. The strategy aimed to deny the Red Army the room to maneuver. The Communist leadership, dominated by Bo Gu and the Comintern adviser Otto Braun (Li De), and with Mao sidelined since 1932, adopted positional defense and fought set-piece battles at fortified positions. When Nationalist forces took Guangchang in April 1934, the Red Army's casualties and shortages mounted. The Fujian Rebellion had given the Communists an opportunity for alliance, but their leaders hesitated, and the revolt collapsed. By autumn the leadership decided to break out.

The costs to civilians in the contested counties were heavy but poorly documented. Blockhouse lines were built with forced labor, villages were depopulated or burned in some districts, and Nationalists applied baojia registration and reprisals against suspected supporters of the Communists. Inside the soviet, grain levies, labor demands, and recruitment strained households as the blockade tightened. Historians working from Communist, Nationalist, and local sources have found it difficult to establish reliable mortality figures, and the experience varied sharply by locality.

Section 62.21

The Long March

In October 1934, around eighty-six thousand people, including soldiers, party officials, and support personnel, left Ruijin to join Communist forces in Hunan and Sichuan; small numbers of local forces remained behind, and many were killed in subsequent repression. The march was initially cumbersome, with heavy equipment and a rigid route. After crossing several Nationalist blockade lines, the column suffered catastrophic losses at the Xiang River in late November and early December 1934 and shrank to some thirty thousand. In January 1935, at Zunyi in Guizhou, party leaders held an enlarged meeting that criticized the earlier military leadership; Mao Zedong gained influence in military decisions, though his consolidation of leadership took years. The army adopted flexible maneuver, crossed the Wu and Chishui Rivers, entered Yunnan and Sichuan, and in May 1935 passed the Dadu River at Luding Bridge; accounts of the details of the crossing differ and later accounts have been dramatized. Crossing snow-covered mountains in western Sichuan, First Front Army units met the Fourth Front Army under Zhang Guotao in mid-1935, but a dispute over strategy and leadership led to a split. Zhang's forces suffered heavy losses during their westward and then northward marches; the Second Front Army under He Long and Ren Bishi also marched from the Hunan–Hubei–Guizhou border. The surviving elements of the three armies gathered in Gansu and Shaanxi by late 1936.

Mao's First Front Army reached northern Shaanxi in October 1935, where it joined local Communist forces who had established a base in the region. Estimates of survivors from the original column are commonly given as roughly eight to ten thousand, though numbers depend on counting methods and on later reinforcement. The official figure of about 25,000 li (some 12,500 kilometers) is symbolic; recent estimates vary, and the experiences of different columns differed greatly. The party made Yan'an its capital in early 1937.

The Long March became a foundational story of Chinese communism. It has also been studied critically. Historians distinguish between its military reality, including defeat and retreat, its political meaning, as the occasion for Mao's rise and for the party's rebuilding in the northwest, and its later mythic role in propaganda. Accounts by participants and the journalist Edgar Snow, whose Red Star Over China (1937) drew on interviews at Bao'an in 1936, shaped international perceptions. For Nationalists, the pursuit of the Red Army allowed the central government to enter provinces it had not controlled. Chiang visited Chongqing, Guiyang, and Kunming in 1935, extending influence over Sichuan, Guizhou, and Yunnan and prefiguring the decision to make Chongqing a wartime capital.

Section 62.22

Japanese Pressure, National Salvation, and Nationalist Strategy

The Japanese threat shaped the final years of the decade. After the Mukden Incident of September 1931, Japan occupied Manchuria and in 1932 attacked Shanghai, where the Nineteenth Route Army resisted for more than a month before a truce was arranged. The Tanggu Truce of May 1933 created a demilitarized zone in northern Hebei, and in 1935 Japanese pressure produced the He–Umezu agreement removing Nationalist forces from Hebei and the creation of autonomous regimes in East Hebei and Chahar. Chiang's policy, often summarized as "first internal pacification, then resistance to external enemies," held that a divided China could not defeat Japan and that the Communists and regional rivals had to be subdued first. He also used time to build the army, the economy, and German-trained units. The policy was defensible strategically and deeply unpopular. Student demonstrations in Beiping in December 1935 (the December Ninth Movement), the National Salvation Association in Shanghai, which led to the arrest of seven prominent leaders in November 1936, and newspapers across the country demanded resistance. In November 1936 provincial forces under Fu Zuoyi defeated Japanese-sponsored Mongol and Chinese forces in Suiyuan, raising morale.

Zhang Xueliang's situation made him receptive. His Northeast Army, driven from Manchuria in 1931, had been assigned to fight Communists in the northwest, where it suffered setbacks. Its officers and soldiers, many from the northeast and longing to return home, were affected by anti-Japanese appeals. The Communist Party, in a shift encouraged by the Comintern's popular front line in 1935, had called for a united front against Japan and sent emissaries to Northeast and Shaanxi commanders. Zhou Enlai met Zhang in April 1936, and local truces took hold. Chiang, determined to complete the anti-Communist campaign, flew to Xi'an in early December 1936 to press Zhang and Yang Hucheng.

Nationalist diplomacy with Japan combined resistance in specific crises, negotiation, and avoidance of a general war. After the Tanggu Truce, the 1935 He–Umezu understandings, and Japanese-supported autonomy movements in North China, Tokyo promoted proposals associated with Foreign Minister Hirota Koki in October 1935 that would have required China to suppress anti-Japanese activity and cooperate against communism. Chiang rejected formal acceptance while seeking to buy time; his speeches in late 1935 signaled that concessions had limits. Wang Jingwei, who had favored negotiation, withdrew after the November 1935 assassination attempt.

Communist strategy was shifting as well. After the Seventh Congress of the Comintern in 1935 endorsed popular-front tactics against fascism, the Chinese party issued appeals for a united anti-Japanese front, later extending these appeals to the Nationalists. Whether this change was initiated primarily by Moscow or by Chinese circumstances and leaders is discussed by historians, who often find elements of both. For Chinese citizens in 1935–1936 the practical result was that anti-Japanese patriotism increasingly shaped urban politics, and the government's priority of fighting Communists first appeared to many as a failure of national leadership.

The negotiations that followed were fraught. Cause and Effect

The Xi'an Incident

Long-term causes

Regional military autonomy; the Nationalist priority of defeating the Communists before confronting Japan; Japan's expansion after 1931; widespread patriotic sentiment among students and urban groups; the displacement of the Northeast Army from its homeland.

Short-term causes

The Communist arrival in the northwest in 1935; the Comintern's popular-front turn; Zhou Enlai's contacts with Zhang Xueliang; the 1936 arrests of the Shanghai leaders; Chiang's decision to press a new offensive and his visit to Xi'an.

Immediate effects

Chiang's release without a formal written agreement; suspension of the northwestern campaign; talks between the GMD and CCP; Zhang's detention; greater public support for Chiang as a national leader.

Long-term effects

Formation of the Second United Front after the outbreak of war in July 1937; survival and legitimization of the Communist base at Yan'an; transformation of Chiang's political position within a war of national resistance (Chapter 64).

Section 62.24

Chapter Review and Nanjing Decade Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the ambitions and limits of Nationalist state-building. The government built a party-state with national institutions, secured financial and tariff reforms, expanded infrastructure and professional bureaucracies, and enjoyed some diplomatic gains. Yet its reach in the countryside was limited, its dependence on military leaders and local elites persisted, and its repression of critics narrowed its legitimacy. The Communist movement, expelled from cities, built a rural government that was coercive but responsive to some peasants' grievances, was nearly destroyed by the fifth campaign, and survived through the Long March. The Xi'an Incident showed that national resistance to Japan had become the dominant political question.

Continuity and Change Over Time

State and Society from the Late Qing to the Nanjing Decade

What Changed

  • A party-state, a central bank, a national paper currency, and a set of modern legal codes were layered onto the institutions of the late Qing and early Republic; the 1935 reform ended silver's role as the monetary standard, but older coinage and local monetary practices persisted
  • Tariff autonomy and the abolition of likin altered the fiscal relationship between the state, the treaty system, and commerce
  • Highways, aviation, radio, and technical agencies connected cities more tightly than before
  • The Communist Party built an alternative rural government based on class mobilization

What Stayed the Same

  • The county remained the base of government, dependent on local elites and underfunded
  • Most people were farmers whose lives were shaped by land, rent, debt, weather, and local power
  • Regional military leaders retained armies and revenues, as in the 1910s and 1920s
  • Foreign privileges persisted in modified form; institutions such as baojia and the examination idea were revived in new forms

Who experienced change most directly: Urban professionals, bankers, officials, and residents of coastal and Yangtze cities. Who experienced continuity: Most villagers, especially in the interior. Why it matters: The unevenness of change helps explain why different groups reacted differently to both Nationalist and Communist programs.

Assessing the Decade

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Assess the Nanjing Decade?

The question: Was the Nanjing Decade a period of significant, if incomplete, state-building and economic development, or was it a period in which the Nationalist revolution stalled and failed to address the structural problems that later contributed to its defeat?

Interpretation A — A state-building decade with real achievements: Scholars emphasizing this view point to the tariff and financial reforms, new legal codes, modern research institutions, highway and aviation expansion, and growth of the modern sector. Some economic historians, notably Thomas Rawski, estimate that the modern sector, though small, grew rapidly before the depression and that the broader economy was less stagnant than earlier accounts assumed. They argue that the regime's difficulties arose largely from external pressures, including Japanese aggression, the global depression, and the burden of fighting Communist insurgency, and that historians should not judge the decade solely by the later outcome.
Interpretation B — An "abortive revolution" constrained by social and political limits: Other historians, notably Lloyd Eastman in The Abortive Revolution (1974), argued that the Nationalists failed to carry out social revolution, relied on landlord and merchant allies, subordinated reform to military needs and factional politics, and cultivated authoritarian methods that alienated intellectuals and peasants. On this view, the regime never reached the villages with policies that would address tenancy, debt, and taxation, and its weaknesses became fatal under the strain of war.

A note on the two positions: The interpretations do not directly contradict one another on every point, because they often address different questions. Work on economic growth, such as Rawski's, concerns output, investment, and transport, while Eastman's argument concerns political and social limitations of the regime. A historian can accept evidence of economic expansion in some sectors and still find political and rural weaknesses.

Evidence: Defenders of A cite trade and industrial statistics, institutional histories, and the continuing development of technical agencies; defenders of B cite rural surveys, tax records, descriptions of local government, and accounts of repression and corruption. Some recent scholarship, including work on German military cooperation and on institutional capacity, emphasizes that state capacity grew in some sectors such as finance, the military, and technical bureaucracies while remaining thin in rural administration. The two interpretations are not completely incompatible.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how students understand the outcome of the Chinese Civil War (Chapter 65). If the Nanjing regime was almost doomed by its social base, 1949 appears as the culmination of long-term weaknesses. If it was building real capacity, then the Japanese war and wartime inflation appear as decisive shocks. Historians caution against both inevitability and mere contingency.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the period from 1928 to 1937 as an era of state-building under constraint. The Nationalist government at Nanjing emerged from the Northern Expedition and the 1927 rupture with the Communists, and it inherited a country divided among regional military leaders, shaped by the treaty system, and dominated by rural poverty. Its legitimacy rested on Sun Yat-sen's authority and on nationalism, but its power rested on a coalition of generals, bankers, officials, and party factions.

The government's institutions reflected both revolutionary ideology and practical compromise. Political tutelage justified party supremacy; the Five Yuan system borrowed from Sun's theory; the Military Affairs Commission and personal networks around Chiang Kai-shek provided the real core of power. Factional conflict, including the Hu Hanmin crisis, showed that the party-state was never monolithic.

In administration, the Nationalists built ministries and technical agencies, expanded education, and began to professionalize officials. Their reach declined sharply below the provincial level. County government, financed and staffed inadequately, remained dependent on local elites, and the revived baojia system extended control but also reinforced local power structures.

Economic policy achieved real gains. Tariff autonomy, the end of likin, banking reform, and the fabi currency reform of 1935 strengthened central finance, while the National Resources Commission laid foundations for state-led industrial planning. But the Great Depression, transmitted through silver, and the high costs of military campaigns constrained results. The modern sector grew from a small base while most of the population remained in agriculture.

Infrastructure development was substantial but unevenly distributed. Highways, railways, aviation, and communications linked the Yangtze delta and major cities more closely, and some projects, such as the Guangzhou–Hankou railway, were milestones. Floods and famines demonstrated the limits of state capacity and the vulnerability of rural populations.

The rural crisis was central. Tenancy, debt, taxation, falling prices, and disaster put heavy pressure on farm households. Nationalist policy prioritized technical and administrative measures over redistribution, while rural reconstruction projects and Communist land revolution offered contrasting responses. Regional militarists and frontier rulers continued to limit Nanjing's authority, and the central government extended its reach through bargaining, war, and, after 1934, the pursuit of the Red Army.

The Communist movement survived by moving to the countryside, building the Jiangxi Soviet, and adapting. Its government combined land redistribution, mobilization of women and peasants, and coercive methods, and it suffered from internal purges and strategic errors. The fifth encirclement campaign forced it out of Jiangxi; the Long March preserved a fraction of its forces and brought Mao Zedong to prominence, and the party established a new base in the northwest.

The Xi'an Incident brought together the strands of the decade: Japanese expansion, regional military politics, patriotic mobilization, Communist strategy, and Chiang's priorities. It ended the primary focus on anti-Communist war and prepared the Second United Front. The decade as a whole illustrates how a regime can build institutions and still face structural limits, and why historians continue to debate whether it was a failed revolution or a partial success cut short by war.

Students should carry from this chapter a way of thinking about state capacity: states are built unevenly, through institutions, revenue, personnel, and local bargains. The chapter also prepares for Chapters 63 to 65, which turn to social and cultural life in Republican China, the war with Japan, and the civil war that followed.

Party-state
A system in which a ruling party's organization overlaps with and directs government; the GMD claimed this role during tutelage.

Sun Yat-sen's second stage of reconstruction, in which the party guided the people toward self-government; used to justify party rule after 1928.

Guomindang (GMD)
The Nationalist Party, reorganized in 1924 and ruling at Nanjing after 1927–1928.

Officers trained at the Whampoa Academy and personally loyal to Chiang.

Blue Shirts
Informal name for a secret officers' society of the 1930s promoting loyalty to Chiang; its influence and fascist resemblance are debated.
New Life Movement
A state-sponsored campaign from 1934 promoting hygiene, order, frugality, and moral discipline.

China's regained right to set its own import duties, achieved in stages from 1929.

Internal transit tax on goods, abolished by Nanjing in 1931.

Government bank opened in 1928 to support national finance and credit.

System of mutual household responsibility revived by Nationalists for registration, policing, and taxation.

1930s reform projects aiming to improve village life through education, health, cooperatives, and local self-government.

January 1935 party meeting that criticized earlier military leadership and increased Mao's influence.

Japanese-sponsored state in Manchuria established in 1932.

Treaty privilege exempting foreigners from Chinese courts; negotiations continued but it ended only in 1943.

Chiang's policy of defeating internal rivals before confronting Japan.

December 1936 detention of Chiang by Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng, leading to a shift toward united resistance.

GMD–CCP cooperation against Japan, formalized after July 1937.

Chapter Reflection

The Nanjing Decade matters because it was the moment when a revolutionary movement attempted to become a government. The Nationalists' choices, to rely on military leaders, financiers, and officials, to build institutions from above, and to postpone social reform, shaped what the state could and could not do. Their achievements in finance, law, science, and infrastructure were real and left legacies that persisted in Taiwan and in the People's Republic. Their limits in the countryside and their repression of critics narrowed their support.

The decade also shaped the Communist movement. Driven to the countryside, the party learned to govern territory, mobilize peasants, and survive military defeat, and the Long March made Mao Zedong a central leader. These experiences were formative for the wartime base areas of Chapter 64 and the land reform of Chapter 65, but students should avoid reading 1949 into 1935. In the mid-1930s the Communists were a small, hunted force whose survival was uncertain.

The chapter also shows China embedded in global history: tied to the world silver market, advised by Germans, Americans, British, and League of Nations experts, pressured by Japan, and linked to the Soviet Union and the Comintern. Chinese leaders made active choices within these constraints. Understanding the Nanjing Decade as diverse, contested, regionally varied, and unfinished prepares students for the great ordeal of war that began in 1937, and for the continuing question of how a large and varied society could be governed.
