Part V — Sui, Tang, and the Post-Tang Transition
29

The An Lushan Rebellion and the Transformation of the Tang

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the dark hours before dawn in the sixth month of 756, the emperor of the Tang left his capital through a side gate of the palace. With him went a small escort, a favored consort, a few senior ministers, and some thousands of guardsmen. Behind them lay Chang'an, a city of perhaps a million people, home to Buddhist monasteries, Persian and Sogdian merchants, examination candidates, poets, and ministries that had administered an empire stretching from the northeastern approaches of the Korean peninsula to the Pamirs. Ahead lay the mountain roads to Sichuan. Within days, at a post station called Mawei, the escorting soldiers would refuse to go on until the chief minister was dead, and the court would be forced to yield. Few contemporaries could have believed, a year earlier, that the ruler of one of the wealthiest and most powerful states of its age would travel this road as a fugitive.

This chapter studies the An Lushan Rebellion, the civil war that began in the winter of 755 and was suppressed only in 763, and, more importantly, the transformation of the Tang state that the rebellion set in motion. Students often first meet the rebellion as a dramatic story: a favored frontier general, a scandal-tinged court, an emperor in flight, a doomed consort. Those elements are real and will be told here. But the chapter argues that the deeper significance of 755–763 lies less in the drama of those eight years than in the processes that continued long afterward. The rebellion did not simply destroy the Tang. The dynasty ruled for another century and a half, until 907. What changed was how the Tang state worked: how armies were raised and commanded, how revenue was collected, how authority was shared between the court and the regions, where the empire's people and wealth were concentrated, and how emperors governed among palace officials, eunuchs, commanders, and ministers.

The chapter therefore asks students to hold together two ways of seeing the same events. One is crisis: an eight-year war that killed and displaced very large numbers of people, destroyed cities and farmland across the north, and shook confidence in the dynasty. The other is transformation: the gradual replacement of the institutional arrangements described in Chapters 25 and 26, with their household registration, equal-field landholding, conscript militias, and centrally managed grain, cloth, and labor taxes, by a different set of arrangements built on regional military commands, taxation of property and commerce, salt revenue, professional armies, and a far more negotiated relationship between capital and province. A student who remembers only the crisis will misjudge the Tang's remarkable survival. A student who remembers only the transformation will underestimate the human catastrophe.

What continued through this period is as important as what changed. The Tang court remained the center of a recognized imperial order. The examination system and the civil bureaucracy continued to operate, and Chang'an and Luoyang were rebuilt and reoccupied. Commerce, literature, Buddhist and Daoist institutions, and the religious life of ordinary people carried on, and in some respects flourished. Some of the greatest poets of the age, such as Du Fu and, later, Bai Juyi and Han Yu, wrote in the shadow of the war and its aftermath. The rebellion is thus not the end of the Tang described in Chapter 28. It is the pivot between the high Tang and the different, more regionally fragmented, more commercially financed Tang of the late eighth and ninth centuries.

Those affected were numerous and their experiences differed sharply. Farming families in the Yellow River plain faced armies, requisitioning, and famine, and many fled south. Registered taxpayers vanished from the government's books, sometimes because they had died, and often because they had moved, hidden, or fallen outside an administration that could no longer see them. Frontier soldiers, civilian officials, palace women, eunuchs, Uyghur cavalry, Sogdian merchants, Buddhist monks, and Tibetan armies all played roles. So did local militias and city defenders whose names are mostly lost, and commanders who tried to hold the north together and others who chose to rebel or to bargain.

The evidence for these events is rich but must be read with care. It includes the official Tang histories and later compilations, the anthologized poetry of eyewitnesses, epitaphs and inscriptions, and administrative documents preserved at Dunhuang and Turfan. The statistical evidence, especially the famously dramatic fall in registered households between the 750s and 760s, is easily misunderstood, and this chapter uses it to teach how historians interpret numbers that appear to be simple counts. All of the sources were produced by people with purposes of their own. Many of the histories were written after the fact by officials with strong views about who was to blame.

Finally, the rebellion belongs to more than Chinese history. It unfolded amid the rise of the Tibetan Empire, the Uyghur Khaganate, and the Abbasid Caliphate, and it altered the connections between the Tang and Central Asia described in Chapter 27. Within the empire it reshaped the balance between north and south, a shift that would carry forward into the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms of Chapter 30 and the commercial economy of the Song in Chapter 32. Understanding it helps students see Chinese history as a story of institutional change, not of simple dynastic rise and fall.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the structural conditions that made a large-scale military rebellion possible in the mid-eighth century from the specific causes that produced the rebellion of 755.
  2. Explain the jiedushi (military commissioner) system and why concentrating large frontier forces under individual commanders created structural risk for the central government.
  3. Describe An Lushan's career and the multiethnic frontier military world in which he rose, without treating his ethnic background as an explanation for his rebellion.
  4. Trace the course of the rebellion from the outbreak at Fanyang in 755 through the loss of Luoyang and Chang'an, the flight of the court, the Yan regime's internal struggles, and the suppression of the revolt in 763.
  5. Analyze the demographic and economic effects of the war, including the critical interpretation of Tang registration statistics.
  6. Evaluate why the Tang court accommodated rather than abolished powerful regional military governors after 763, and assess the varied degrees of regional autonomy that resulted.
  7. Explain the fiscal transition from household-based taxation to the Two-Tax Law of 780, and the growing importance of salt revenue and commercial taxation.
  8. Assess how eunuchs, palace armies, factions, and regional commanders changed the political environment in which later Tang emperors ruled, without treating the period as one of continuous imperial powerlessness.
  9. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether 755 was a sudden rupture or an acceleration of existing trends.
  10. Contextualize the rebellion within Central Asian and Inner Asian history, including relations with the Uyghurs and Tibetans.
  11. Use poetry, official histories, epitaphs, and administrative documents as evidence while recognizing the limits and purposes of each.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a state at the height of its wealth and prestige suffer a rebellion that nearly destroyed it, and how can historians explain this without making it seem inevitable?
  2. How did the way the Tang organized its frontier defense create both military success and political risk?
  3. Why did the court, after suppressing the rebellion, so often confirm the power of regional commanders instead of dismantling it?
  4. How should historians interpret the dramatic fall in registered households after 755, and what does it tell us about the limits of official statistics?
  5. What changed in how the Tang state raised revenue, and why did this change matter more in the long run than the fighting itself?
  6. How did the balance between the north and the Yangtze region shift, and what were the consequences?
  7. Why did the Tang survive until 907 despite the catastrophe of 755–763, and what does that survival reveal about its legitimacy and adaptability?
  8. Is it more accurate to describe 755 as a rupture or as an acceleration of earlier changes?
  9. How did ordinary people, from farmers to soldiers to city dwellers, experience the war and its aftermath?

Historical Context

By the reign of the emperor Xuanzong (r. 712–756), the Tang state had passed through more than a century of institutional development. Chapter 25 traced the founding of the dynasty in 618 and its expansion under Taizong and Empress Wu, and Chapter 26 examined the government and society that sustained it. The central government worked through the Three Departments and Six Ministries, staffed increasingly by officials recruited through examinations and through hereditary and recommendation channels. Local administration ran through prefectures and counties. The state's income rested on the household registration system and the linked arrangements of equal-field landholding, in which adult males were assigned land and owed grain, cloth, and labor service. The fubing militia, drawn from registered households, had supplied the army of the early dynasty.

These arrangements had never worked as neatly as the legal codes suggested. By the early eighth century, population growth and the accumulation of private and monastic estates were making equal allotment difficult in many regions, and registers were falling behind reality as households moved, hid, or fell into dependence on larger landholders. The state responded pragmatically, adding new officials, new levies, and new ad hoc institutions to handle problems the original framework could not. Scholars debate how far these strains had already weakened the fiscal system before 755; the debate is presented later in this chapter. It is enough here to say that the system the Tang inherited from its early emperors was already in adaptation.

The most consequential adaptation was in military organization. The dynasty's frontier policy, examined in Chapters 25 and 27, had extended Tang power across the Hexi Corridor and into the Tarim Basin, and confronted the Tibetan Empire in the west and southwest, the Turks and later the Uyghurs on the steppe, and the Khitan and Xi peoples in the northeast. Defending such long and varied frontiers with rotating conscripts from the interior proved impractical. The court increasingly relied on long-serving, recruited soldiers stationed permanently in large frontier commands, each under a jiedushi, a military commissioner with authority over troops, supplies, and often local civil administration. By the 740s there were about ten such commands, holding a very large share of the empire's soldiers, according to Tang sources roughly half a million men. Most were on the northern and western frontiers, while the interior contained relatively few troops.

Geography shaped this arrangement. The northeastern frontier, the region of the modern Beijing–Tianjin area and the Liaoxi corridor, lay along the meeting zone of the North China Plain, the Mongolian steppe, and the Manchurian forests. It was a region of mixed populations, including Han Chinese farming families, Khitan, Xi, Turkic, Sogdian, and Koreans-descended communities, and had been fought over for generations. Its commanders led troops who spoke several languages and often had personal and patron-client ties to their officers. This was the world into which An Lushan rose (Section 29.2). The capitals in the Wei River valley (Chang'an) and the Luo River valley (Luoyang), by contrast, were tied to the grain-producing plains and the Grand Canal system that had been developed under the Sui (Chapter 24) to bring southern grain northward.

Students should also be careful about the meaning of "China" here. The Tang was an empire, not a nation-state. It ruled a multiethnic and multilingual population, its army and court included people of Turkic, Sogdian, Korean, Khitan, and other backgrounds, and its ruling house itself had ties to the northwestern aristocracy shaped by the Northern Dynasties (Chapters 22 and 23). Cultural life in Chang'an drew on Central Asian music, dress, religion, and trade, as Chapter 27 discussed. Modern national categories, and the modern idea of a "foreign general betraying his country," fit poorly the political world of 755. The rebellion was a struggle within an imperial order among people who moved within the same institutions, not a national war against outsiders.

Finally, students should recall that the Tang was not the only power in its world. The Tibetan Empire had become a serious rival on the western frontier, contesting the Tarim Basin and the routes of the Silk Roads. The Uyghur Khaganate, established in 744 on the steppe after the fall of the Second Turkic Khaganate, would become the Tang's most important military partner and, in time, creditor. Beyond them the Abbasid Caliphate had recently replaced the Umayyads (750). These powers were active participants in the events of this chapter. The Tang court's decisions in 755–763 were made in a world of competing states, all with their own interests.

Section 29.1

Conditions Before the Rebellion

Historians who study rebellions must avoid a trap: once an event has happened, every earlier development can be made to look like a step toward it. The task of this section is to reconstruct the conditions of the early and mid-eighth century, the things that made a rebellion of this scale possible, while keeping clear that possibility is not inevitability. Many empires have contained powerful frontier commanders without suffering civil wars. The conditions described here shaped the rebellion, but the rebellion itself grew from decisions made by specific people at specific moments, the subject of Sections 29.2 and 29.3.

Frontier expansion and the new armies

The first condition was the scale and permanence of the Tang frontier commitments. From the seventh century the dynasty had extended military garrisons into the Tarim Basin, campaigned against Turkic khaganates, fought Tibetan armies on the plateau margins, and confronted the Khitan and Xi in the northeast. These commitments required troops who could stay on the frontier for years, learn its terrain, and command local resources. The early Tang militia system, the fubing, had been designed for a different situation. Its soldiers were farming households who served in rotation, supplied their own equipment, and trained seasonally. The system worked well for the campaigns of the early seventh century and for garrison duty near the capitals, but it was poorly suited to long deployments far from home. By the 730s and 740s many fubing units were under-strength, and the court had turned to recruited, long-service soldiers paid and supplied by the state or by frontier commands. The formal fubing quotas were effectively abandoned by the late 740s.

The consequences were significant. A recruited soldier's loyalty was tied less to a household and a local community and more to his commander and the command's pay. A frontier army was now a self-contained institution with its own supply system, officers, and tradition of service. And because the interior of the empire retained few troops, the balance of armed force was concentrated on the periphery. Tang sources describe a distribution in which frontier commands in the mid-740s held a large majority of the empire's soldiers, while the region around the capitals was lightly garrisoned. This is sometimes summarized as a strong "outer" and weak "inner" military structure. It made military sense against external enemies but created a vulnerability if a frontier army turned inward.

The concentration of command

The second condition was the concentration of authority in single commanders. The jiedushi had begun as commissioners assigned to coordinate frontier defense, but by the 740s they had come to combine command of troops with control over supplies, local revenues, and often the civil offices of their regions. Earlier Tang policy had tried to keep military and civil authority separate and to rotate commanders. The frontier system eroded these safeguards. Commanders held office for long periods, built personal followings, and managed enormous resources. The traditional histories, notably the Old Tang History and the New Tang History, also report that the chief minister Li Linfu encouraged the appointment of frontier generals from non-Chinese backgrounds and long tenures, partly to prevent rival civil officials from gaining prestige through military success. Historians treat this account cautiously, as it comes from sources written later and eager to assign responsibility, but it fits the broader pattern of the reign.

The court in the Tianbao era

The third condition lay at court. Xuanzong's early reign (the Kaiyuan era, 713–741) is remembered as a period of energetic government, fiscal reform, and cultural brilliance. His later years, the Tianbao era (742–756), saw increasing withdrawal from routine administration. Chief ministers, first Li Linfu (in office until his death in 752) and then Yang Guozhong, dominated policymaking. Yang Guozhong was a relative of Xuanzong's favorite consort, Yang Guifei, and rose quickly to hold many offices. He was widely resented by other officials and came into open rivalry with An Lushan. Court factionalism, personal rivalries, and the emperor's reliance on a small circle of trusted advisers are characteristic of the late reign. They meant that disputes with a powerful frontier commander were handled through personal maneuvering rather than through stable institutional channels.

Two cautions apply. First, Yang Guifei herself was not a political agent in the way later literature suggests. Later poems and stories, including Bai Juyi's "Song of Everlasting Sorrow" (806), made her the emotional center of the disaster, and this literary tradition should not be confused with the historical record, in which her importance lies in her family's rise and in her death at Mawei. Second, the emperor's age and reduced attention should not be turned into a simple moral explanation of "decadence." Historians find it more useful to ask how the structure of the court, including a small circle of ministers, personal patronage, and limited institutional checks on military commanders, shaped decision-making at a critical moment.

Fiscal and social strain

The fourth condition was a gradually developing fiscal and social strain. The frontier armies were expensive. Their upkeep depended on grain, cloth, and money transferred from the interior, and on local military farms. The court had begun to supplement the old household taxes with new levies, special officials, and money-raising measures. Meanwhile the registers on which the tax system depended were increasingly inaccurate. Chapter 26 described how households evaded registration or moved without authorization, and the state responded by campaigns to find "escaped households." These pressures did not cause the rebellion, and the Tang state of 754 was in most respects wealthy and functioning. But they meant the state had less flexibility than its prosperity suggested.

Historical Vocabulary
Jiedushi (節度使) — military commissioner or military governor

The term originally referred to an official given a "tally" (jie) of authority to coordinate frontier defense. First appearing in the early eighth century, the office grew until, by the 740s, about ten jiedushi commanded major frontier armies, from Anxi and Beiting in the far west to Fanyang and Pinglu in the northeast. Although the English translations "military governor" and "warlord" are common, neither is quite right. A jiedushi was a court-appointed official, and many were loyal Tang commanders. But the office combined military command with control over supplies and, increasingly, civil administration. After 755 the title spread to interior regions, and the jiedushi's territory (the fanzhen, or regional command) became the basic unit of the later Tang political landscape. Students should notice that the same office could be an instrument of imperial defense or a base for regional autonomy depending on the commander, the region, and the court's strength.

Taken together, these conditions describe a state that was militarily strong on its edges, politically concentrated at its center, and institutionally stretched in between. None of them made rebellion certain. What they did was create a situation in which a commander with enough troops, enough grievance, and enough opportunity could challenge the court, and in which the court would have few immediate means of stopping him. That is the situation in which An Lushan acted.

Section 29.2

An Lushan and the Frontier Armies

An Lushan (c. 703–757) is one of the most vividly, and most unfairly, remembered figures in Chinese history. The traditional accounts portray him as a corpulent, cunning barbarian who flattered his way into the emperor's favor while plotting treason. These portrayals were written after the disaster, by historians who needed to explain it, and they should be read as part of the rebellion's afterlife rather than as neutral biography. What can be reconstructed with more confidence is the career of a capable military officer who rose through the institutions of the frontier.

Origins and background

According to the histories, An Lushan was born in the northeast to a Sogdian father and a Turkic mother, and his original name was probably a Sogdian one, Rokhshan ("light"), rendered into Chinese as Lushan. After his father's early death he was raised in a Turkic milieu and later took the surname An, associated with Sogdians from Bukhara, through a stepfather's family. He spoke several languages, and by his youth he had entered the world of frontier trade and interpreting that connected Sogdian merchants, Turkic and Khitan groups, and Tang garrisons. This was a culturally mixed world, and An Lushan's background was in no way unusual for the Tang northeast. Sogdian merchant communities, Turkic soldiers, and Khitan and Xi allies were parts of the ordinary landscape of the frontier and the capitals alike, as Chapter 27 described.

It is essential to treat his ethnic background carefully. His Sogdian and Turkic ancestry helps explain the networks through which he moved, but it does not explain why he rebelled. Many people of similar background served the Tang faithfully, including generals who fought against him, such as the Turkic-descended Geshu Han and the Khitan-descended Li Guangbi. Meanwhile, the rebels' army included large numbers of Han Chinese soldiers from Hebei. Later writers sometimes used language about "barbarians" (hu) to explain the rebellion, and this rhetoric had lasting effects on how the rebellion was remembered and on attitudes toward Central Asians in the later Tang. But it was a post-rebellion interpretation, not a cause.

The rise of a frontier commander

An Lushan entered Tang service in Youzhou (near modern Beijing) under the commander Zhang Shougui, and distinguished himself in campaigns against the Khitan and Xi. His career then advanced quickly. In 742 he became jiedushi of Pinglu, a command in the northeast; in 744 he took over the much larger command of Fanyang, and in 751 he was also given Hedong, a large region in what is now Shanxi. Holding all three commands, he controlled a very large proportion of the northern frontier's troops, roughly 160,000 to 180,000 men by common estimates, along with the horses, revenues, and administrative apparatus of a broad northern region.

His rise depended not only on military success but on court connections. He cultivated the emperor Xuanzong and the consort Yang Guifei, reportedly presenting himself in a manner that flattered their sense of their own generosity. He maintained agents in the capital and, according to the sources, kept a network of officers loyal to him personally, giving them titles, gifts, and promotions. He also built an internal army culture in the northeast: Sogdian, Turkic, Khitan, Xi, and Han officers served under him, and he recruited elite units of captured or surrendered frontier soldiers whose careers depended on him. In these features he was typical of the powerful jiedushi of his time, though his combination of three commands made him exceptional.

Rivalry and the court

The relationship between An Lushan and the chief minister Yang Guozhong deteriorated after 752. Yang Guozhong, who had risen through his kinship with Yang Guifei and his fiscal skills, saw An Lushan as a rival for the emperor's favor and warned repeatedly that he would rebel. Whether Yang Guozhong sincerely feared a rebellion, or was using the accusation to weaken a competitor, or both, is uncertain. The sources, which were composed after events had vindicated the warning, are not reliable on this point. What is clear is that the court's handling of the situation increased suspicion on both sides. Reported summonses to the capital, investigations of An Lushan's associates, and efforts to divide his commands made him feel threatened. Xuanzong, who continued to trust An Lushan personally, dismissed several warnings. Historians therefore see the outbreak of the rebellion as the product of a structural situation (enormous concentrated military power) combined with a political breakdown between two ambitious men and a distracted court.

The jiedushi system as structural risk

The key analytical point is the one made in the Vocabulary box above: the jiedushi system was an effective response to real frontier problems that carried an institutional risk. If an army answers primarily to its commander for pay, supplies, and promotion, and if that army is large enough and near enough to threaten the interior, the court's ability to control it depends on the commander's loyalty and on the court's own coercive resources. In 755 the court had few of the latter. The interior garrisons were small; the capital guards were thin; and the principal armies capable of opposing the rebellion were under other frontier commanders who were geographically far away or already committed. This is the deepest sense in which the earlier Tang military system, evolved to defend the frontier, produced a vulnerability at the center.

Section 29.3

The Rebellion Begins

In the eleventh lunar month of the fourteenth year of the Tianbao era, in December 755, An Lushan launched his rebellion from Fanyang. According to the histories, he announced that he had received a secret edict ordering him to march on the capital and remove Yang Guozhong, a common pretext in Chinese rebellions, because it framed the movement as loyalty to the emperor against a "treacherous minister." The rebel army, reportedly numbering around 150,000 and drawn from several ethnic backgrounds, including many Han soldiers, crossed the frozen Yellow River and moved rapidly south. The Tang court, whose interior defenses were thin, was slow to grasp the scale of the threat. Many local officials and garrison commanders in the North China Plain either fled or surrendered, though others resisted, as the next section shows.

The fall of Luoyang and the Yan regime

Luoyang, the eastern capital, fell within weeks. The Tang commanders Feng Changqing and Gao Xianzhi, hastily assigned to defend it, were overwhelmed, and the city was taken. In early 756 An Lushan proclaimed himself emperor of a new dynasty, the Great Yan (Da Yan), and established his court there. This proclamation matters: it signals that the rebellion was not only a military campaign but an attempt to create an alternative imperial order, with titles, officials, and claims to legitimacy. It also produced a division within the Tang official class, as some officials in the occupied regions accepted appointments under the Yan while others refused, went into hiding, or fled. The problem of officials who served the rebels, and how to treat them after the war, would trouble Tang politics for years.

Meanwhile Tang resistance began to organize in Hebei and elsewhere. In the plains of Hebei, the local official Yan Zhenqing, later celebrated as a calligrapher, and other prefectural officials raised militias and resisted the Yan advance. The generals Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi, leading Tang forces from Shuofang and Hedong, defeated rebel forces in the spring of 756 and cut the lines connecting Fanyang with the main rebel army. For a time it appeared that the rebellion might be contained in the Luoyang region, and that the Tang might successfully strangle it by threatening its rear.

The Tong Pass decision

The critical moment came at Tong Pass, the fortified gateway between the Central Plain and the Wei River valley, some 100 kilometers east of Chang'an. The Tang commander there, Geshu Han, an experienced general of Turkic descent, held a strong defensive position and advised patience: the rebels had overextended themselves, their rear was threatened, and time favored the Tang. Yang Guozhong and, reportedly, Xuanzong, however, feared that Geshu Han might himself become a threat and pressed him to attack. In the summer of 756 the Tang army left the pass and was defeated in the field, and Tong Pass fell. The pass's fall left the road to Chang'an open.

Historians have long debated this decision. The traditional accounts blame Yang Guozhong and the court's impatience, and it is likely that political mistrust of Geshu Han played a role. But the court also faced genuine pressure: the rebels' occupation of Luoyang meant that grain shipments and the empire's central routes were at risk, and waiting entailed costs. The lesson for students is not that one bad decision "caused" the disaster, but that the court's inability to trust its own commanders, a result of the structural conditions described in Section 29.1, made sound strategy difficult.

The flight of the court and Mawei

With the pass lost, the court left Chang'an around July 756 with an escort of palace guards, choosing the route to Sichuan, a region with a strong tradition of separate administration and defensible mountain approaches. At Mawei, a post station west of the capital, the accompanying troops, exhausted, hungry, and resentful, killed Yang Guozhong and demanded the death of Yang Guifei, whose family was blamed for the crisis. Xuanzong reluctantly agreed, and she was strangled.

Mawei should be understood as a moment of breakdown within the imperial entourage: the emperor's escort, on whom his life depended, dictated terms to him. The episode shows how fragile imperial authority was when it lost its institutional framework. It does not show that Yang Guifei caused the rebellion, which had begun eight months earlier and had its origins in military and political structures, not in her person. Its literary afterlife, including poems, plays, and later stories, gave it a romantic meaning that has little to do with what the participants were experiencing.

A second consequence followed quickly. The crown prince, Li Heng, separated from his father's party and went north. In August 756, at Lingwu in the northwest, he was proclaimed emperor, later known as Suzong. Xuanzong, in Sichuan, was informed and accepted the fait accompli, becoming a retired emperor (taishang huang). This transfer of imperial authority, made under pressure, gave the Tang a functioning government in the field and a focus for the war effort. It also had long-term effects: Suzong depended on military commanders and, notably, on eunuchs in his inner circle, precedents that would shape later politics (Section 29.8). The dynasty survived the flight of 756 because it could re-form around a new emperor while the old emperor lived, an institutional resilience worth noticing.

Section 29.4

War and Destruction

The rebellion lasted from 755 to 763, and its later phases were in some ways more destructive than its opening. The war was not a single campaign but a series of overlapping conflicts across the North China Plain, the Yellow River valley, Shanxi, and the western approaches, involving armies, militias, defecting officials, foreign allies, and civilian populations caught between them.

Recovery of the capitals

In 757 the Tang gathered its forces. Suzong's government moved to Fengxiang, west of Chang'an, and appealed to the Uyghur Khaganate for cavalry. The Uyghurs, under their qaghan Bayanchur (Moyanchur, r. 747–759), agreed to send a contingent, in exchange for silk, marriage ties, and, reportedly, permission to plunder the recaptured cities. In late 757 Tang and allied forces retook Chang'an; Luoyang was recovered soon afterward. An earlier blow to the rebellion had come in early 757, when An Lushan, by then reportedly blind and ill and increasingly erratic, was murdered by his own son An Qingxu, an act that showed how personal and unstable the Yan regime's leadership had become. His son took command, but the Yan regime's authority passed steadily to able commanders such as Shi Siming.

At the same time, Tang resistance elsewhere showed how much of the war was fought by local defenders. The most celebrated example is the defense of Suiyang (in present-day Henan) in 756–757 under Zhang Xun and Xu Yuan, who held the city against a much larger rebel force for roughly ten months, blocking a route toward the rich lands of the Huai and Yangtze. The city ultimately fell after famine reduced the defenders, and the accounts of its siege, including reports of extreme measures to survive, became a moral exemplum in later Tang writing. Historians see Suiyang's importance also in strategic terms: the delay protected the southeast, whose revenues would soon sustain the court.

A war that did not end

The recovery of the capitals did not end the war. The Yan forces retreated to Hebei, and Shi Siming, after briefly submitting to the Tang, rebelled again. In 759 a large Tang force, sometimes called the army of the nine circuits, besieged An Qingxu at Ye. It was defeated, in part because of divided command and a sudden storm that scattered the armies, and the failure showed the price of the court's reluctance to put a single commander in charge, a reluctance rooted in the same fears that had produced Tong Pass. Shi Siming killed An Qingxu, took over the Yan, and retook Luoyang, only to be killed by his own son Shi Chaoyi in 761. The Yan regime was now visibly disintegrating from within.

The Tang recovered Luoyang decisively in 762, again with Uyghur assistance under Bögü Qaghan, and the accounts describe severe looting and violence by the allied troops. In 763 Shi Chaoyi, pursued by Tang forces and abandoned by his own commanders, died, and the rebellion formally ended. Several of the Yan commanders who had defected to the Tang were, as Section 29.6 shows, allowed to keep their commands, a decision with major consequences.

The experience of war

Behind these campaigns lay an enormous human cost. Cities such as Luoyang and Chang'an changed hands several times, and each occupation brought requisitioning, plunder, and executions. Armies, both rebel and Tang, lived off the land in regions already disrupted. Farming households in the North China Plain lost their crops, their animals, and often their menfolk to conscription by either side. Famine followed in several districts, and the sources describe refugees moving south in large numbers, reaching the Huai, Yangtze, and Sichuan regions. Monasteries, which often held land and stored grain, were both refuges and targets. Officials who had accepted Yan appointments faced the risk of punishment when the Tang recovered a district, while those who had resisted the Yan sometimes faced retaliation from occupiers.

Poets who lived through the war left some of the most vivid evidence. Du Fu, who was trapped in occupied Chang'an in 756–757 before escaping to the Tang court, and who later traveled through the war-torn Central Plain, wrote about conscription, hunger, and the devastated landscape. His poems are examined in this chapter as evidence for wartime disruption (see the Primary Source Window), while Chapter 28 treated him as a poet. Such poems are not reports, and Du Fu was an official with his own moral purposes, but they show how the war was felt by ordinary people caught up in it.

Nor was the northern war the only threat. As Tang garrisons in the northwest were withdrawn to fight the rebellion, the Tibetan Empire moved into the vacuum. Over the following decades Tibetan forces took control of much of the Longyou and Hexi regions, cutting the direct route from the interior to the Tarim Basin. In late 763 a Tibetan army briefly occupied Chang'an itself, for about two weeks, forcing Emperor Daizong to flee and showing how exposed the capital remained even after the rebellion was over. The Tang did not "leave" Central Asia because of any single battle. Talas (751), sometimes cited, did not end the Tang presence. What happened was a gradual loss of contact as garrisons in the far west, cut off and unsupported, held out, in some cases for decades, before being absorbed by Tibetan or local powers.

Section 29.5

Demographic and Economic Effects

No aspect of the rebellion is more frequently cited, or more frequently misunderstood, than its demographic effects. The Tang conducted periodic registrations of households, and the surviving figures show a dramatic decline. The registration of 754, the last before the rebellion, recorded roughly 9 million households and about 52 to 53 million people. Returns from the early 760s, after the war, are commonly cited at under 3 million households and roughly 17 million people, with some figures lower still. Read naively, this looks like the death of two-thirds of the population. Historians do not read it that way, and students should learn why.

Historical Evidence

Registers, Households, and the Problem of Counting People

Evidence: Tang household and population registers (as preserved in compilations such as the Tongdian, the Tang huiyao, and the dynastic histories) · Type: Administrative statistics compiled for taxation and labor service

What the numbers are: Tang registers counted households and persons for the state's fiscal and service purposes: the people who owed grain, cloth, and labor, and who could be assigned land. They were not modern censuses aiming to count everyone who lived in the empire. The figures that survive were transmitted in later compilations, sometimes with variation between sources.

Why a fall in registered numbers is not a death toll: A decline in registered households can reflect several different things at once. (1) Mortality: real deaths from combat, massacre, famine, and disease, which were severe in the north. (2) Migration: refugees who moved south or into the mountains and were no longer on their home registers. (3) Administrative breakdown: officials in war zones, or in regions under Yan or later regional control, could not compile or transmit registers. Areas outside effective court control simply did not report. (4) Evasion and concealment: households hid from tax collectors, sought protection from powerful families or monasteries, or moved to places where officials did not know them. (5) Changed reporting: as the state moved away from a household-based tax system (Section 29.7), it had less reason to maintain the old registers at all.

What historians conclude: The war caused real and severe mortality, and the north suffered heavily. But most historians hold that a large share of the documentary decline reflects lost registration, not lost lives. Estimates of actual population loss are uncertain and are debated, and no single figure commands agreement. The register decline is best treated as evidence of the collapse of the state's ability to see and tax its population, which is itself a major historical fact.

  1. Why is a register of taxable households different from a modern census?
  2. List three reasons other than death that could explain a household's disappearance from the registers.
  3. What does the fall in registered households reveal about the Tang state even if actual deaths were lower?

Displacement and the movement south

Whatever the exact mortality, movement was on a large scale. Refugees from the Central Plain and Hebei moved into the Huai and lower Yangtze regions (Jiangnan), the middle Yangtze, and Sichuan. Some fled during the war and returned; many did not. Migrations of this kind had precedents: the fall of Western Jin (Chapter 20) had driven northern elites and farmers south, and that movement had helped develop the south under the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties (Chapter 21). The rebellion of 755 initiated another wave, one that historians often connect with the longer-term southward shift in China's population and economic weight examined further in Chapters 30, 32, and 37. Migration did not mean the north was empty. Large parts of the northwest and the Guanzhong area remained populated, and the northeast, under the Hebei commanders, continued as a distinct and often prosperous region. But the demographic balance moved.

Economic effects in the north

In the war zones the economic damage was substantial. Fields went untilled, irrigation works fell into disrepair, and animals were seized by armies. The rebuilding of Luoyang and Chang'an was slow, and Luoyang in particular remained damaged for a long period. The great granaries and canal networks that fed the capital, notably the Grand Canal and its connection with the Bian River, were disrupted, and grain shipments from the southeast became more dependent on a few routes that military or bandit activity could block. The court's reliance on transport officials to keep grain moving grew accordingly. In these respects the war reversed some of the economic integration achieved under the Sui and early Tang.

The rising importance of the south

The south's fiscal importance grew. The Yangtze and Huai regions, largely spared from direct fighting, had rich agricultural land, expanding rice cultivation, active commerce, and ports and canal cities such as Yangzhou. From the 760s the court depended heavily on revenues from this region: grain, cloth, money, and, above all, salt from the coastal works near the mouth of the Yangtze and Huai. The ninth-century writer Han Yu asserted that a very large majority of the state's revenue came from the southeastern circuits, an exaggeration perhaps but reflecting a real change. Students should not overdraw the picture: the north remained politically dominant and militarily central, and the south's growth built on developments that had been under way since the Southern Dynasties. But the rebellion made the southeast far more critical to the survival of the Tang, and this shaped the later pattern of Chinese economic geography.

The economic effects also included changes in commerce. As the state turned to commercial taxation and salt revenue, it engaged more directly with merchants and markets, and cities, especially on the canal and river network, gained prominence. Markets, money, and merchants play a larger role in the later Tang than in the early Tang, a trend that would continue into the Song commercial revolution (Chapter 32). Some scholars associate the wider changes of this period with what is sometimes called the Tang-Song transition, a subject taken up in the Historical Debate.

Section 29.6

Military Governors and Regional Power

The end of the rebellion in 763 did not restore the Tang military system of 754. The court faced a problem that has recurred in many post-civil-war states: how to end a war with forces that remain in the field. The Tang's own armies were led by commanders who had acquired large followings and expected rewards. The rebel commanders who surrendered still controlled troops and territories. The Tibetans and other external powers pressed on the frontiers. Above all, the court lacked the resources to dismantle or replace the existing military structures by force.

Accommodation of former rebels

The court's decision, often criticized by later historians, was to confirm the surrendered rebel commanders in their positions, appointing them as jiedushi over the territories they controlled. In Hebei, Tian Chengsi, Li Baochen, Xue Song, and Li Huaixian (a former Yan officer who then submitted) took over commands centered on Weibo, Chengde, Lulong, and neighboring regions. This settlement ended the fighting and returned nominal allegiance to the Tang. It also meant that a wide region in the northeast continued under commanders who appointed their own subordinate officials, maintained their own armies, retained most local revenues, and, increasingly, passed office to sons or chosen successors. Contemporary Tang officials and later historians were well aware of this. But the alternative was renewed war, and the court's choice reflected its weakness as much as its judgment.

The spread of the jiedushi system

At the same time, the jiedushi system, previously concentrated on the frontiers, spread throughout the empire. The court created or expanded commands in the interior to defend against rebels, bandits, and Tibetan raids, and to keep armies loyal by giving commanders local revenues. By the later eighth century, dozens of such commands, often called fanzhen ("border garrisons" or "regional commands"), covered much of the empire. Many were held by loyal commanders and by civilian officials who acquired military titles; only some approached the autonomy of Hebei. The pattern was thus varied, and students should resist any picture of a uniformly fractured empire.

A spectrum of autonomy

Historians usually describe the post-rebellion regions along a spectrum. At one end lay the Hebei circuits and, for periods, a few others in the northeast and center, where military governors controlled appointments, revenues, and succession, and in some cases did not send taxes to the capital or accept court-appointed officials. Even here, however, the commanders generally acknowledged Tang sovereignty in form: they used Tang titles, sought imperial confirmation of their successions, and did not proclaim rival dynasties. At the other end were regions such as the Yangtze and Huai circuits, which remained closely tied to the court through revenue transfers and appointment of officials, and which supplied the resources on which the central government depended. In between lay a range of circuits where the court's influence rose and fell with the personality of the commander, the strength of the neighboring powers, and the court's fortunes.

Relations between the center and the regions were, in effect, continuously negotiated. The court could refuse to confirm a successor, dispatch armies, offer titles or marriage alliances, play neighboring commanders against each other, or use the promise of tax remission. Commanders could respond with military threats, gifts, or requests for confirmation. The result was a system that political scientists might call negotiated and uneven sovereignty within a surviving empire: authority was neither wholly centralized nor wholly dispersed.

Why regional commanders were hard to control

Several features explain why the post-rebellion jiedushi were difficult to dislodge. First, their armies were professional, long-service forces whose soldiers depended on the commander for pay and often for family support; a commander who was removed risked mutiny. Second, revenue collected within the circuit was used to sustain those troops, so central attempts to divert resources could provoke resistance. Third, the commanders' successions were often decided by the soldiers themselves, who at times killed a commander and installed a successor, and the court usually ratified the outcome rather than risk a war. Fourth, the court's own forces were limited. Finally, the wider strategic environment, with Tibetans, Uyghurs, and later other powers on the frontiers, meant the court repeatedly needed the regional armies for defense.

The court was not without initiatives. In the late eighth and early ninth centuries it attempted several times to reassert control: Emperor Dezong (r. 779–805) tried to end hereditary succession in some circuits, provoking a revolt of commanders in 781–784 that briefly drove him from Chang'an and ended in compromise; Emperor Xianzong (r. 805–820) launched a series of successful campaigns against rebellious circuits in the 810s, reasserting authority over several regions by around 819. The results were real but temporary. In Hebei especially, court authority did not last, and hereditary or soldier-installed commanders had resumed control by the early 820s. Students should therefore avoid both extremes: the court neither surrendered wholly to regional power nor restored the system of the seventh century.

Section 29.7

Changes in Taxation and Finance

Chapter 26 deliberately left the major transformation of Tang finance for this chapter. Its account of early and high Tang taxation centered on the household and the individual adult male: the zu (grain tax), yong (labor service, or a cloth equivalent), and diao (a levy of local products such as silk and hemp), linked to the equal-field system that assigned land to households. This system depended on accurate registers, on the state's ability to allot land, and on a population that stayed where it was registered. By the mid-eighth century each of these conditions was weakening. The war dramatically accelerated this erosion and made the old system increasingly unworkable across much of the empire.

Why the old system became unworkable

After 755 the state could not maintain the registers it needed. Population movement scattered households; regions under rebel or regional control ceased reporting; and the rapid growth of military expenses required a form of revenue that could be raised quickly and flexibly. The old system's logic, in which the state assigned land and then taxed those it had assigned, made little sense where much land had been abandoned, resettled, or absorbed into large estates and where the state had lost track of who lived where. Ad hoc measures multiplied: emergency levies, sales of official titles and monastic ordination certificates, forced loans, and charges imposed by regional commanders. Officials in the field extracted revenues through whatever means they could. Taxation became increasingly uneven and, in the eyes of many, arbitrary.

Salt and commercial revenue

One of the most successful responses was the development of state revenue from salt. In 758 the fiscal official Diwu Qi established a system under which the state controlled salt production and sale, and in the 760s Liu Yan, appointed to supervise finance, reorganized and expanded it. Under Liu Yan, the state purchased salt from producers at set prices and resold it to licensed merchants, who distributed it throughout the empire. Because everyone needed salt, this generated large and relatively reliable revenue. By the later eighth century, salt provided a very large share of central government income, by some accounts around half. Liu Yan also improved the transport of grain along the Grand Canal and the Bian River, restoring a vital supply line. Salt's importance illustrates an important shift: the state was moving from taxing registered households toward taxing a commodity and the commercial networks that carried it. This required cooperation with merchants and strengthened the role of markets and towns, developments discussed further in Chapters 32 and 37.

Historical Vocabulary
Two-Tax Law (兩稅法, liangshui fa)

Introduced in 780 under Emperor Dezong on the proposal of the chief minister Yang Yan, the Two-Tax Law replaced the older grain, labor, and cloth taxes with a levy collected twice a year, in summer and autumn, and assessed on the property and land that households actually held in the place where they resided, rather than on registered adult males allotted land under the equal-field system. Each circuit was assigned a quota, based on its earlier revenues, which it was responsible for meeting; a large portion was retained locally for regional needs, and the remainder was forwarded to the court. Much of the tax could be assessed in money, so collection increasingly involved cash and markets. The law is sometimes described as the formal end of the equal-field and household-tax regime, but students should be careful: those arrangements had eroded well before 780, and the law recognized as much as it ended. The Two-Tax Law mattered because it accepted that people had moved and land had changed hands, and taxed reality as it stood.

The Two-Tax Law in practice

The Two-Tax Law's design was practical. By taxing residents, whether long-settled or newly arrived, it brought migrants and formerly unregistered households back into the tax base. By assessing property rather than persons, it shifted more of the burden to those with more land and wealth, at least in principle. By fixing regional quotas, it gave the court predictable revenue and gave local officials a target. And by allowing payment in money, it aligned taxation with a more commercial economy.

The law also had costs and limits. Quotas were often set by the revenues of a difficult moment and could become burdensome when prices moved. The reliance on money could hurt farmers when the value of copper cash rose relative to grain, a problem noted by ninth-century critics. Regional authorities kept a large share of revenue, strengthening their ability to sustain armies and pursue their own interests. And the enforcement of the new system varied: in Hebei and other autonomous circuits, the court's authority to collect its share was limited. Yang Yan himself, after a period of influence, fell from power and was exiled and killed in 781, a reminder that fiscal reform in the Tang was intensely political.

The essential point, therefore, is that the state did not simply collect less. It changed what it taxed (property, salt, commerce rather than persons and allotted land), when it collected (twice a year in fixed seasons), and how it raised revenue (through quotas, monopolies, and money). This transformation is one of the most consequential outcomes of the rebellion, and historians who study the Tang-Song transition often see the Two-Tax Law as an early stage in a longer development that would lead to the Song state's heavy reliance on commercial revenues (Chapters 31 and 32).

Finally, students should recall that these changes were not imposed wholesale in 763. Equal-field allotment did not vanish everywhere at once; in some regions, and in certain forms, land assignment and the older taxes persisted for years, as the Dunhuang and Turfan documents show for the far northwest. The fiscal transformation was gradual, uneven, and shaped by regional conditions, much like the military transformation.

Section 29.8

Court Politics and Imperial Weakness

The post-rebellion Tang court operated within a changed political environment. The word "weakness" is often used to describe it, and the word is not wrong: emperors after 763 had fewer resources, less direct military power, and more limited control over regions than Taizong or Xuanzong had commanded. But the word can mislead. Later Tang emperors were not uniformly powerless, some were vigorous and effective, and the court remained the source of legitimacy and the arbiter of many disputes. The more useful description is that the emperor ruled within a more constrained and complicated system, and that the mechanisms through which he exercised power changed.

Eunuchs and palace armies

The most conspicuous change was the rise of palace eunuchs as political actors. Eunuchs had long served in the imperial household, but during and after the rebellion emperors came to rely on them for information, communication, and, increasingly, military command. Suzong's inner circle included powerful eunuchs such as Li Fuguo. In 763, when Daizong fled from the Tibetans, a eunuch commander, Yu Chao'en, brought in the Shence Army, a frontier unit originally created in the 750s and subsequently brought to the capital region. In the 780s, after the crisis of 783–784, Emperor Dezong, having found the regular commanders unreliable, placed the Shence Army and other palace forces under eunuch officers. This gave emperors a force loyal to the palace, one insulated from the regional commanders and the civil bureaucracy. It also gave eunuch commanders enormous influence, including, in later decades, involvement in the selection and removal of emperors. Several emperors of the early ninth century were installed or died in circumstances involving eunuch power; Xianzong, who was himself the victor over rebellious circuits, died in 820 in circumstances widely attributed to palace eunuchs.

The rise of eunuch power reflected a structural logic. An emperor who distrusted both regional commanders and the civil bureaucracy sought agents dependent solely on him, and eunuchs, lacking families and career paths outside the palace, seemed suitable. But the arrangement created a new center of power that could and did act independently of the emperor. Students should approach the eunuchs neither as a simple villainous force nor as mere instruments. They were participants in a political system whose structure made them powerful, and they included capable administrators as well as ruthless ones. Chinese historians, writing from the perspective of the civil bureaucracy, generally portrayed them harshly.

Factions and officials

Civil officials, for their part, fought over policy and patronage. The ninth century saw sharp factional rivalries, most famously the conflict between the so-called Niu and Li factions, associated with Niu Sengru and Li Deyu, over questions including examination graduates and policy toward regional commanders and the Tibetans. The reform movement of 805 under Wang Shuwen and Wang Pi, with participants such as Liu Zongyuan and Liu Yuxi, tried to curb eunuch power and reform finance but collapsed within months. In 835 the Sweet Dew Incident, an attempt by Emperor Wenzong and his officials to eliminate the leading eunuchs, failed disastrously, with many officials killed, and eunuch dominance deepened. Such episodes illustrate the difficulty of reform in a court where the emperor's own guard was under eunuch command.

Reconsolidation and adaptation

Yet the story is not simply one of decline. Emperors and ministers found ways to work within the new system. Dezong learned harsh lessons from the crisis of 783–784, when a mutiny of troops from the northwest, the revolt of the commander Zhu Ci, briefly drove him from Chang'an, and afterward emphasized fiscal caution and palace forces. Xianzong's campaigns of 810–819 restored court authority over several circuits, and the early ninth century saw a genuine, if temporary, revival, often called the "Yuanhe restoration" after the emperor's reign era. The court also handled foreign policy with skill: it maintained relations with the Uyghurs, negotiated a treaty with the Tibetan Empire in 821–822, and adapted to changing frontier conditions. Emperor Wuzong (r. 840–846) presided over a major restriction of Buddhist monasteries in 845, partly for fiscal and political reasons, showing the state's continued capacity for large-scale action. The later Tang, then, was a state constrained but not inert.

Section 29.9

The Long Transformation of Tang Authority

This section steps back to ask what actually changed between the high Tang of Chapters 25 to 28 and the state of the ninth century. The temptation is to compress the answer into a formula: rebellion in 755, decline thereafter, collapse in 907. Historians have rejected this formula because it fits neither the evidence nor the way contemporaries experienced the period. The transformation lasted generations, and it included adaptation, recovery, and renewed central initiatives as well as weakening.

What survived

A great deal continued. The Tang court remained the accepted source of imperial legitimacy; even the most autonomous commanders sought its titles and confirmation. The central bureaucracy, with its ministries and its examination system, continued to recruit officials, and the examination culture arguably grew in importance in the ninth century, as literati built careers through the system and wrote about it. Chang'an, though damaged, remained a great city. Commerce expanded, and trade along the Grand Canal and the Yangtze thrived. Buddhist monasteries remained wealthy and influential until the restrictions of the 840s, and Chan Buddhism (Chapter 28) developed in the regions, a case of religious creativity flourishing in an age of political decentralization. Literature entered another creative period, with Han Yu and Liu Zongyuan in prose, Bai Juyi and Yuan Zhen in poetry, and the emergence of new forms. Popular religion, family life, and the rhythms of agriculture continued in most of the empire much as before.

What changed

At the same time, structural features of the state did change. Military organization: regional commands and recruited armies replaced the militia system; the court relied on palace armies and on its ability to negotiate with commanders. Fiscal organization: the Two-Tax Law, the salt monopoly, and commercial taxation replaced the household-based system. Regional authority: much of the northeast, and at times other regions, operated with substantial autonomy under hereditary or soldier-selected commanders. Demographic geography: the south gained in population and revenue relative to the north. Frontiers: the Tang lost direct control of the far northwest to the Tibetan Empire and later local powers, and its relations with the steppe were renegotiated through the Uyghurs. Palace politics: eunuchs and palace armies became central actors. And cultural attitudes: some historians argue that a more inward-looking, less cosmopolitan tone developed after the rebellion, with suspicion of Central Asian ("hu") influence expressed in some writing, though the evidence is mixed and cosmopolitan practices continued in cities.

Why the Tang survived

The dynasty's survival for 150 years after the rebellion is itself something to explain. Several factors contributed. First, the legitimacy of the Tang house was deeply established. Second, the regional commanders, however autonomous, had few reasons to destroy the court: the empire's revenues, titles, and symbols were useful, and no rival dynasty had an obvious claim. Third, the fiscal reforms and the prosperity of the southeast gave the court a financial base. Fourth, the accommodation of regional power meant that many commanders had a stake in the existing order. Fifth, external threats made a functioning court useful. The rebellion showed that the empire could survive when the center was weak, a resilience that also carried a cost, as the same system left the state vulnerable when new rebellions arose in the late ninth century, the subject of Chapter 30.

The chapter therefore closes not with a dead dynasty but with a transformed but still functioning Tang empire, one whose institutions, geography, and politics differed markedly from those of the high Tang, and whose eventual collapse was the product of processes that continued to unfold, not the direct and delayed result of 755. Chapter 30 will pick up the story from there.

Section 29.10

Chapter Review and An Lushan Rebellion Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the rebellion that began in 755 and the transformation of the Tang state that followed. Students should now be able to separate the structural conditions of the early eighth century (large frontier armies under individual commanders, a weakened militia system, a concentrated court, and strained registers) from the immediate causes of the outbreak (the rivalry between An Lushan and Yang Guozhong and the court's mishandling of a suspicious commander). They should be able to distinguish wartime destruction from longer-term institutional change, explain why post-rebellion military governors became difficult to control, describe the fiscal transition from household taxes to the Two-Tax Law, evaluate the demographic evidence critically, and explain why the Tang survived so long after so severe a crisis.

The core insights are these. The rebellion did not end the Tang; it changed how the Tang worked. Military authority passed increasingly to regional commanders, and the court governed through negotiation as well as command. Revenue came increasingly from property, salt, and commerce, and the Yangtze region became fiscally central. Register statistics document the state's loss of sight more than a literal death toll. Eunuchs and palace armies reshaped court politics. And the process unfolded over generations, with recovery and reconsolidation alongside decline.

Primary Source Window

Du Fu, “The Official at Shihao” (Shihao li), c. 759

Creator: Du Fu (712–770), poet and minor official · Date: 759 · Region: Henan, in the war zone between Luoyang and Chang'an · Type: Poem (five-character old-style verse, wuyan gushi), an eyewitness-literary source

Context: After the Tang defeat at Ye in 759, Du Fu traveled from Luoyang toward Huazhou, passing through villages stripped of men by conscription. The poem belongs to a small group of works recording what he saw on the road. Du Fu's poetry was later celebrated and anthologized, and it is a major source for the experience of the war.

Paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

The narrator stops for the night at a village. That evening an officer arrives to seize men for the army. The old man climbs over the wall and escapes, leaving his wife to answer the door. She tells the officer that her sons have been sent to the front at Ye, that one has written that another has died, and that all that remains in the house is a grandchild at the breast and a daughter-in-law with no proper clothing to wear. She offers to go herself to help cook for the army. By the time the narrator leaves the next morning, only the old man is there to see him off.

Analysis: The poem shows the human machinery of wartime recruitment from the standpoint of an observer: officers forcing men into service after a military defeat, families broken, and elderly women pressed into support work. It is important evidence that the court's need for soldiers and the disruption of the household-based system fell heavily on rural families. Its limits are equally important. It is a literary composition, shaped by conventions of moral protest, and the dialogue is Du Fu's re-creation, not a transcript. It shows the perspective of a sympathetic official and cannot tell us how the state's recruiters saw their work, how common such scenes were, or what the peasants themselves thought of the Tang. Its voice for the woman is filtered through a male official's pen.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say or show about the effects of war on a village?
  2. Who created it, and what might his purpose have been?
  3. What can historians learn from it about conscription, family life, and the wartime state?
  4. What can historians not learn from it alone?
Historical Debate

Was 755 a Rupture or an Acceleration?

The question: Did the An Lushan Rebellion break the Tang state in a way that produced the post-755 order, or did it accelerate changes that were already under way?

Interpretation A — Rupture: Some scholars emphasize the rebellion's shattering effects: the loss of the capitals, the collapse of registers, the spread of jiedushi commands, the permanent autonomy of Hebei, the loss of the northwest, and the change in the relationship between court and region. On this view the rebellion was a genuine watershed, after which the Tang was a different kind of state. Scholars in this tradition also often note the rebellion's roots in the northeastern frontier's distinctive society, an argument developed in different ways by Edwin Pulleyblank and, earlier, Chen Yinke.
Interpretation B — Acceleration: Others point out that the fiscal strain on the equal-field and household system, the rise of the jiedushi, the decline of the fubing, and the growth of commerce were all visible before 755. On this view the rebellion sped up and made irreversible trends already in motion. Denis Twitchett's work on Tang finance is often associated with attention to these longer-term administrative developments, and Charles Peterson's studies of court and province emphasize the continuing adaptability and resilience of the post-rebellion state, in contrast to a narrative of collapse.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same sources: the histories, the registers, the fiscal documents, and the records of commanders and courts. They differ in how much weight to give pre-755 evidence of adaptation and how to interpret the timing of change: the seventeen-year gap before the Two-Tax Law, the survival of the equal-field system in some regions, and the recoveries of the early ninth century are used by both sides.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians see the Tang-Song transition and the meaning of "decline." It also illustrates a broader lesson in historical thinking: a dramatic event can be both an interruption and an acceleration, and the answer depends partly on what is being measured.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, evaluate which interpretation is better supported for (a) military organization and (b) taxation, or explain what additional evidence you would need.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Settlement of the 760s

Note: these are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the evidence, not quotations from named individuals.

The court and its ministers: Facing an unrecovered treasury, foreign armies on the frontier, and undefeated commanders, the court's priority was to end the fighting and keep the dynasty intact. Confirming surrendered rebels was a costly compromise chosen because the alternative was probably worse.
A frontier or Yan-army soldier: For a professional soldier, loyalty ran through his commander, who provided pay, promotion, and protection. Whether he fought for Tang or Yan mattered less than whether his unit and his commander survived, and the confirmation of his commander often meant continuity of his own livelihood.
A northern farming household: The war brought conscription, requisitioning, and famine. Survivors faced the choice between staying on damaged land, sheltering with a powerful family or monastery, and joining the movement south. Their experience was one of loss and dislocation, with little say in how the settlement was decided.
A southern taxpayer or merchant: The southeast was spared the fighting but bore the growing burden of supporting the court and its armies. Merchants dealing in salt and grain found new opportunities under state contracts and monopolies, while farmers faced quotas and the effects of a money-based tax.
A regional military governor: A commander in Hebei or another autonomous circuit saw himself as responsible for his troops and territory. He sought Tang titles for their legitimacy but resisted policies that would remove his sources of power. His interests and those of the court overlapped and diverged.
An Uyghur ally: For the Uyghur leadership, aid to the Tang was a source of silk, prestige, and marriage ties, and a way to secure their position on the steppe. The plunder of Luoyang, allowed by the Tang, and the later horse-silk trade show a relationship of mutual advantage and, for the residents of Luoyang, real suffering.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Tang State Before and After 755

What Changed

  • Militia service gave way to recruited, long-service armies under regional commanders
  • Household-based grain, cloth, and labor taxes gave way to the Two-Tax Law, salt revenue, and commercial taxation
  • The court shared power with regional military governors, some of them hereditary in practice
  • The south gained in population and fiscal weight relative to the north
  • Eunuchs and palace armies became central to court politics
  • Tang lost direct communication with much of the far northwest as Tibetan power expanded through Longyou and Hexi, while some isolated western garrisons continued for decades before falling to Tibetan or local powers

What Stayed the Same

  • The Tang house remained the recognized source of imperial legitimacy
  • The civil bureaucracy and the examination system continued
  • Agriculture remained the base of the economy, and family and village life persisted
  • Buddhist, Daoist, and popular religious life continued, and literature flourished
  • Chang'an and Luoyang remained the political capitals

Who experienced change most directly? Northern farming households, frontier soldiers, and fiscal officials felt the changes most directly. Who experienced continuity? Many scholar-officials, southern villagers, and religious communities. Why it matters: The pattern shows that institutional transformation and cultural continuity can coexist, and that political change does not affect all groups equally.

Cause and Effect

The An Lushan Rebellion

Causes

Long-term: the shift from militia to recruited frontier armies; the growth of jiedushi authority; a thin interior defense; strain on the equal-field and registration system; and a court structure with few institutional checks on military commanders. Short-term: An Lushan's accumulation of three commands; his rivalry with Yang Guozhong; the court's mixed signals of suspicion and favor; and the emperor's reliance on personal trust. Ethnicity and Yang Guifei's favor were not causes.

Event or transformation

A rebellion launched from Fanyang in 755, the fall of Luoyang and the proclamation of the Yan, the loss of Tong Pass and the flight of the court in 756, and a war lasting to 763 with heavy involvement of foreign allies, followed by a longer transformation of the Tang state.

Effects

Immediate: mass mortality, displacement, and destruction in the north. Long-term: regional military power, fiscal transformation, a southward shift of weight, palace-army politics, and loss of direct contact with the northwest.

Historical complexity

The rebellion cannot be reduced to one cause, whether a single ambitious general, a court scandal, an ethnic conflict, or a decadent emperor. Military, fiscal, political, regional, and personal factors interacted, and the consequences unfolded over generations.
Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary People in an Age of War and Restructuring

For farming households in the north, the rebellion meant uncertainty about whose officials would collect taxes, whose soldiers would demand grain, and whether men would be conscripted by one side or the other. Women managed households and fields in the absence of men, endured requisitions, and sometimes, as in Du Fu's poem, were themselves recruited for support labor. Children grew up in refugee communities. Many households left, a difficult decision that meant abandoning ancestral graves and land, and settled in the south, where they became tenants, small owners, or laborers, or joined the households of local magnates or monasteries.

In the cities, Chang'an's residents experienced occupation, recovery, and slow rebuilding. Sogdian and other Central Asian merchants faced suspicion after the rebellion, and some concealed or modified their identities, while others continued to prosper; commerce and religious diversity did not disappear. In the south, the growth of salt and grain trade, canal shipping, and river towns created new employment for boatmen, porters, and merchants. Monasteries provided shelter, storage, and credit, though the state's later restrictions in the 840s would strike at these institutions. For most families, the daily experience of the ninth century was shaped more by the burden of quotas, the reliability of local officials, and the harvest than by court politics, though the two were connected.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regions Experienced the Rebellion Differently

The northeast (Hebei and Fanyang): Origin of the rebellion and, afterward, a semi-autonomous region under military governors with distinct customs, a mixed population, and prosperous agriculture. Interpretations that treat Hebei as culturally distinct are debated, and its autonomy should be seen as political and military as well as cultural.

The Central Plain and Guanzhong: The main war zone. Luoyang and Chang'an suffered occupation and slow recovery, and the surrounding countryside was heavily disrupted.

The northwest and Hexi: Garrisons were withdrawn to fight the rebels, and Tibetan power expanded. The population, including Chinese-speaking farmers, Tibetans, Sogdians, and others, lived under changing rule, as Dunhuang documents from the period of Tibetan control show.

The Yangtze, Huai, and southeast: Largely spared fighting, this region became the court's fiscal mainstay, with salt, grain, and canal commerce. It received refugees and grew in importance.

Sichuan and the southwest: A refuge for the court, and a region with its own military command and a long record of administrative distinctiveness, facing Tibetan and Nanzhao pressure.

These differences show that Tang "China" was not a uniform space. Frontier peoples, including Uyghurs, Tibetans, and the many groups in the northeast, were active participants with their own aims, and events cannot be understood simply from the perspective of the court.

China and the Wider World

The Rebellion in Eurasian Context

The rebellion took place in a Eurasian setting of competing states. The Uyghur Khaganate, newly established on the Mongolian steppe, provided cavalry to the Tang and gained silk, horses trade, and marriage ties; its influence in Chinese affairs grew until the khaganate's fall to the Kyrgyz in 840. The Tibetan Empire used the withdrawal of Tang garrisons to expand across the Hexi Corridor and the Tarim Basin routes, occupied Chang'an briefly in 763, and negotiated with the Tang as an equal, as the bilingual treaty of 821–822 recorded on a pillar in Lhasa attests. The Abbasid Caliphate, whose army had met the Tang at Talas in 751, was a distant but real presence in Central Asian trade. Sogdian merchants continued to link China with Central Asia and Iran, though their position in Chinese cities was affected by the rebellion's aftermath.

These were not one-way interactions. Tang policy toward the Uyghurs, Tibetans, and southwestern kingdoms such as Nanzhao was an active diplomatic and military program, and the Tang's economic transformation, with growing commerce and maritime trade, connected it more closely to Indian Ocean networks through ports like Guangzhou and Yangzhou, a subject Chapters 30 and 37 develop. In this sense the post-rebellion Tang was not a state turning inward but one whose orientation, political and economic, was shifting.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763) and, more importantly, the transformation of the Tang state that it set in motion. Its central argument is that the rebellion did not simply destroy the Tang. The dynasty survived until 907, but the balance among court, military, revenue, and region shifted decisively, so that the post-755 empire differed fundamentally from the high Tang described in Chapters 25 to 28.

The chapter began with the structural conditions of the early eighth century: the expansion of frontier commitments, the decline of the fubing militia and the growth of large recruited frontier armies, the concentration of command in the jiedushi, a court dominated by a small circle of ministers, and a registration and tax system under strain. It stressed the distinction between conditions that made rebellion possible and the specific decisions that produced it.

Students then met An Lushan as a frontier commander in a multiethnic military world. His Sogdian and Turkic background helps explain his networks but not his rebellion, and the chapter emphasized that many people of similar backgrounds fought for the Tang and that the rebel army included many Han soldiers. His accumulation of three commands, his patronage networks, and his rivalry with Yang Guozhong were the operative factors.

The narrative of the rebellion followed its outbreak at Fanyang, the fall of Luoyang, the proclamation of the Yan, the Tong Pass decision, the flight of the court, and the events at Mawei, treated as a breakdown within the imperial entourage rather than as the work of Yang Guifei. The proclamation of Suzong at Lingwu gave the dynasty a new center, and the war continued through the recovery of the capitals with Uyghur help, the defeat at Ye, the disintegration of the Yan from within, and the end of the rebellion in 763, with the Tibetan occupation of Chang'an in the same year showing the empire's exposure.

The chapter's treatment of demographic effects used the fall in registered households to teach source criticism. The registers document the state's loss of the ability to see and tax its population, not simply mortality, and migration, administrative collapse, and evasion all contributed alongside real and severe war deaths. The movement of people to the south and the growing fiscal importance of the Yangtze and Huai regions marked a lasting shift.

The post-war settlement confirmed former rebel commanders as jiedushi in Hebei and spread the jiedushi system across the empire, creating a spectrum of regional autonomy and a system of negotiated and uneven sovereignty within a surviving empire. Commanders were hard to control because their armies depended on them, their revenues supported their troops, their successions were often decided by soldiers, and the court's own forces were limited. Attempts at reassertion, in the 780s and especially under Xianzong, had real but temporary success.

Fiscal change was the other great transformation. The salt monopoly and commercial revenue, and the Two-Tax Law of 780, replaced the household-based system of the early Tang, changing what was taxed, when, and how. This shift, connected to the growing role of money and markets, anticipates the commercial state of the Song.

At court, eunuchs, palace armies, and factions reshaped politics. The later Tang emperors were constrained but not powerless: they found ways to govern, including reconsolidation, diplomacy with the Uyghurs and Tibetans, and large-scale initiatives such as the restriction of Buddhist monasteries in 845.

The chapter rejected the formula of a single rebellion followed by immediate decline and collapse. What continued (legitimacy, bureaucracy, examinations, cities, commerce, religion, and literature) was as important as what changed. The Tang survived for a century and a half because its legitimacy was deep, because regional commanders had reasons to preserve it, and because its finances were reorganized on new foundations.

The debate over rupture versus acceleration showed that historians read the same evidence differently, and the Primary Source Window on Du Fu showed how poetry can illuminate the war's human cost while requiring careful reading. The chapter ends with a transformed but still functioning Tang, setting up Chapter 30's account of what happened when Tang rule finally collapsed and political fragmentation produced the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state can survive a catastrophe by becoming a different kind of state. The Tang after 763 was not the Tang of Taizong or Xuanzong. It governed through negotiation with regional commanders, raised revenue from salt, commerce, and property, drew increasingly on the south, and lived with eunuchs and palace armies at its center. Yet it remained recognizably a Tang court, with an examination system, a bureaucracy, a metropolitan culture, and a claim to imperial legitimacy that few tried to abolish.

The chapter also shows how much of later Chinese history was prepared here. The southward shift of population and revenue foreshadows the economy of the Song; the fiscal turn toward money and commerce anticipates the Song state's heavy reliance on markets; the regional military circuits and their weakening of central authority lead into the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms of Chapter 30; and the Tibetan and Uyghur relationships remind us that the Tang was one power among several in a wider Eurasian system.

Above all, the chapter models a way of thinking. It asks students to resist the story of a single event that explains everything, and to see crisis and transformation together, to treat statistics as evidence with limits, to place individuals in their institutional settings, and to recognize that different groups experienced the same events in different ways. Chinese history, like the history of any large society, is best understood as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, and unfinished. The Tang's long transformation is one of its clearest illustrations.
