The Umayyad Caliphate and the Government of Empire

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the year 662 by the Christian reckoning, a Greek inscription was set up at the hot springs of Hammat Gader, east of the Sea of Galilee, to record that the baths had been restored in the time of Muʿāwiya, whom it styles servant of God and commander of the believers. The text is in Greek, it is dated by a local calendar and by the Arab-Muslim year 42, and it credits the repair to a local official. Nothing in it announces a new world. Yet the inscription already shows the pattern that this chapter examines: a ruler who claims a distinctively Muslim title, an administration that still speaks the language of the Roman East, and a conquered countryside in which public works, taxes, and officials continued to function.

The Umayyad caliphs ruled from 661 to 750 over an empire that, at its greatest extent, reached from the Atlantic approaches of Iberia and Morocco to the Oxus valley and the lower Indus. They governed Greek-, Coptic-, Syriac-, Aramaic-, Middle Persian-, Sogdian-, Berber-, and Arabic-speaking populations, Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, Manichaeans, Buddhists, and a growing Muslim community, in provinces with very different agricultural systems, urban traditions, and relationships to the Byzantine and Sasanian states whose territories Chapter 39 described being absorbed. Chapter 40 ended in 661 with the First Fitna resolved in Muʿāwiya's favor. This chapter asks how his dynasty converted a victorious coalition of conquerors into a government that could collect revenue, pay soldiers, issue orders, and persuade its subjects, and how far it succeeded.

The question is harder than it looks because the Umayyad state is seen largely through the eyes of its successors. The most detailed Arabic narratives, such as al-Ṭabarī's history, were compiled in Abbasid Baghdad, a century and more after the events, from earlier transmitters who often wrote under a dynasty that had overthrown the Umayyads and had reasons to portray them as worldly kings rather than rightful caliphs. Over the past generation, historians have supplemented these narratives with sources produced at the time: papyri, coins, inscriptions, seals, buildings, and non-Muslim chronicles. Scholars such as Andrew Marsham, Marie Legendre, Petra Sijpesteijn, Chase Robinson, and Robert Hoyland have used this material to argue that the Umayyad empire was built in stages, that local administrators carried much of its burden, and that older Roman and Sasanian methods were adapted rather than abolished.

This chapter therefore organizes its narrative around government rather than around a simple succession of caliphs. It begins with Muʿāwiya and the choice of Damascus, follows the Marwanid reconsolidation under ʿAbd al-Malik and his successors, then turns to the machinery of government (the registers, the chancery, the provincial governors, the languages of administration), the armies and frontiers, the fiscal and monetary foundations of the state, and the social and religious arrangements through which rulers and subjects met. It closes by asking how centralized the empire really was and why a state with large resources remained vulnerable.

Three cautions shape the chapter. First, administrative change was uneven. A reform attributed to a caliph by a later author may describe a gradual process, a single province, or a retrospective tidying of memory. Second, the Umayyad ruling class was Arab and Muslim in its core, but the state ran on the expertise of Greek, Coptic, Syriac, and Persian-speaking officials, and it did not operate as an exclusively Arab administration. Third, this chapter treats government, not the transformation of individual and community identities. Conversion, language shift, and the status of clients (mawālī) receive their full analysis in Chapter 42, and the civil wars, Karbalāʾ, and the Abbasid Revolution belong to Chapter 43. They appear here only where an understanding of government requires them.

Placed in the larger arc of Volume III, the Umayyad century is the period in which the conquest movement of Chapters 39 and 40 acquired institutions. The Abbasid state of Chapters 44 and 45 inherited much of what is described here, including fiscal registers, a coinage tied to a religious profession of faith, provincial governors, and an imperial court, even as it rejected the dynasty that built them. Understanding what the Umayyads actually did is therefore the precondition for understanding what the Abbasids claimed to change.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of Abbasid-era narratives, non-Muslim chronicles, papyri, coins, inscriptions, and monuments as evidence for Umayyad government.
  2. Explain why Muʿāwiya governed from Damascus and how the move altered the political geography of the caliphate.
  3. Trace the stages by which ʿAbd al-Malik and his successors rebuilt and extended caliphal authority after the Second Fitna.
  4. Describe the functions of the dīwāns, the chancery, the barīd, and provincial governors, distinguishing securely evidenced institutions from later attributions.
  5. Distinguish administrative Arabicization from the social spread of the Arabic language, and compare its chronology in Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and Khurāsān.
  6. Compare the Syrian jund system with the garrison armies of Iraq and Khurāsān, and explain the military basis of Umayyad power.
  7. Analyze the western, northern, and eastern expansions of the empire and explain why conquest outran institutional incorporation.
  8. Explain the operation of kharāj, jizya, and tribute, and the movement of revenue through stipends, garrisons, and public works.
  9. Assess the coinage reform of 696–697 and later as an instrument of imperial authority and a stage in a longer process.
  10. Interpret the Dome of the Rock and the Great Mosque of Damascus as evidence of imperial patronage and public religious expression.
  11. Evaluate competing arguments about how centralized the Umayyad empire was.
  12. Assess the dynasty's achievements and vulnerabilities without adopting the biases of its supporters or its Abbasid successors.

  2. How did the Umayyads combine inherited Byzantine and Sasanian practices with new forms of Muslim rule?
  3. Why did the aftermath of the Second Fitna encourage a more assertive imperial government under ʿAbd al-Malik?
  4. What evidence allows historians to separate administrative practice from later descriptions of it?
  5. How did armies raised in different provinces shape politics as well as warfare?

Historical Context

The Arab-Islamic conquests of the 630s and 640s, treated in Chapter 39, brought Syria, Palestine, Egypt, Iraq, and much of Iran under the authority of Medina within two decades. The conquerors did not at first replace the administrations they found. In Syria and Egypt, Greek-writing officials and local landowners continued to assess and collect taxes; in Iraq and Iran, the Sasanian system of dihqāns, tax registers, and provincial governors persisted in modified form. The first Muslim rulers settled their troops in garrison cities (amṣār) such as Baṣra, Kūfa, and Fusṭāṭ rather than in the old administrative capitals, paid them stipends from tribute and tax, and left much local government to those who had previously run it.

Chapter 40 described how disputes over the caliphate under ʿUthmān, ʿAlī, and Muʿāwiya turned this system toward civil war, how the assassination of ʿUthmān in 656 split the community, and how ʿAlī's death in 661 and al-Ḥasan's withdrawal left Muʿāwiya, who had governed Syria for roughly two decades, as the accepted ruler. Muʿāwiya's recognition did not settle every question of legitimacy or end every regional grievance. But he inherited armies that were largely in Syria loyal to him, and an empire whose provinces had been conquered faster than they had been systematically governed.

Beyond the caliphate lay two neighbors that had shaped everything about its early government. The Byzantine Empire had lost Syria, Palestine, and Egypt but retained Anatolia, the Aegean, and much of the Mediterranean, and its frontier with the caliphate was a persistent field of raiding and diplomacy. The Sasanian state had disappeared, but its administrative vocabulary, its fiscal registers, and its landowning aristocracy remained in the east. The Umayyads thus governed in the shadow of two imperial models, and part of their achievement was to select, translate, and recombine elements of each. The pre-Islamic world these models came from is described in Chapters 32 and 33.

Division I · §§41.1–41.5

The Establishment of Umayyad Dynastic Rule

This division explains how Muʿāwiya built a workable political coalition after 661 and how his dynasty came to expect hereditary succession, without treating hereditary monarchy as the starting point.

Section 41.1

Reconstructing the Umayyad Empire: Sources, Documents, and Historical Debates

The best-known narrative accounts of the Umayyad period were written after the dynasty fell. Al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) compiled a vast chronicle from earlier transmitters such as Abū Mikhnaf, al-Haytham ibn ʿAdī, and al-Madāʾinī, many of whom worked in Iraq and several of whom wrote under the Abbasids. Al-Balādhurī (d. c. 892) preserved conquest and genealogical traditions, and Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam (d. 871) assembled Egyptian material. Ibn ʿAsākir's twelfth-century history of Damascus contains much local biography. These works are indispensable, but they arrived at their form long after the events, they were shaped by Iraqi and later Abbasid concerns, and they tend to organize the past around moral judgments of individual caliphs.

The most important correctives come from sources produced during the Umayyad period or soon after. Greek, Coptic, and Arabic papyri from Egypt and Palestine preserve tax demands, receipts, and correspondence. Coins carry the names of governors and caliphs and, from the 690s, religious declarations. Inscriptions on buildings, milestones, dams, and baths record patronage and titles. Seals and archaeology illuminate settlements and palaces. Non-Muslim chroniclers, including the Syriac Maronite Chronicle, John bar Penkāyē, the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos, and the later Greek tradition preserved by Theophanes, give outsiders' perspectives, though each wrote with confessional and political aims.

These sources do not always agree. The later literary tradition credits particular caliphs with founding institutions and reforms in neat sequences, while documentary evidence often shows gradual and regional change. This chapter therefore signals, as it goes, whether a statement rests on contemporary documents, on literary narration, or on scholarly reconstruction. It also keeps in mind that Abbasid-era authors often evaluated the Umayyads as kings (mulūk) rather than caliphs, a verdict that serves as a part of the evidence rather than a conclusion to be adopted.

Section 41.2

Muʿāwiya's Accession in 661 and the Restoration of Caliphal Unity

When ʿAlī was killed in 661, his supporters in Iraq acknowledged his son al-Ḥasan, but al-Ḥasan's position was weak, and he withdrew in favor of Muʿāwiya on terms that the sources report in conflicting detail. Later tradition called this the Year of the Community (ʿām al-jamāʿa), a name that tells us how contemporaries and their successors wanted to remember the end of civil war. Muʿāwiya was acknowledged in Jerusalem and then received oaths of allegiance (bayʿa) from the Iraqi garrison cities. The details of those oaths matter less than the practical fact: the caliphate was again under one ruler.

Unity meant balancing three centers. Syria supplied Muʿāwiya's core army and his long administrative experience. Iraq, with its large garrison populations in Kūfa and Baṣra, was wealthy and politically restive, with a legacy of support for ʿAlī and with Khārijite groups that rejected both claimants. The Hijaz, though it had ceased to be the political center, retained religious authority through the sacred places and the Companions of the Prophet who lived there. Muʿāwiya governed these three differently: he relied on Syrian troops and Syrian-born commanders, he installed experienced governors in Iraq, and he courted the leading men of Medina through gifts, appointments, and respectful correspondence.

Restoring unity also meant reducing the risk of renewed civil war by distributing rewards. Muʿāwiya maintained the stipend system, kept most existing governors who proved loyal, and used his reputation for patience and negotiation, which later writers contrasted with the rigid severity of his rivals. These arrangements were a political settlement more than a constitution, and they depended on his personal management of relationships that would be harder for successors to replicate.

Section 41.3

Damascus as the Center of Imperial Government

Muʿāwiya governed from Damascus because he already possessed there a base unmatched by any other. Syria had been a province of the Roman Empire for centuries, with a dense network of cities, roads, and agricultural villages, a Greek- and Aramaic-speaking administrative class, and fiscal registers that the conquerors inherited. Damascus lay at the junction of routes from the Mediterranean ports of Tyre, Beirut, and Acre, from Palestine, from the Euphrates, and from Arabia. Muʿāwiya relocated shipwrights to the coast (the sources mention Acre and Tyre), and Syria's proximity to the Byzantine frontier made it the natural base for the war effort in Anatolia.

The choice transformed the caliphate's political geography. The first caliphs had ruled from Medina, in a region far from the main population centers and sources of revenue. Damascus brought the court into the heart of the conquered Roman East, among populations accustomed to imperial rulers and with a long record of paying for them. It also tied the dynasty to the Syrian military tribes who had been Muʿāwiya's supporters, including Kalb, Ghassān, Judhām, Lakhm, and groups associated with Qays, whose loyalties are explained in §41.17 and §41.33.

The move had costs. It distanced the caliph from the Hijaz, where ideals of rulership drawn from the Prophet's community remained strong, and it located government in a region the Iraqi garrisons regarded as an arrogant rival. Muʿāwiya also maintained residences and estates in the Hijaz and in rural Syria, so that his rule was not purely urban. The Khaḍrāʾ palace in Damascus and other residences are mentioned in later sources, but archaeological evidence for the Sufyānid city is thin, which is one reason this chapter relies on documents rather than on assumptions about palace life.

Section 41.4

Muʿāwiya's Government: Governors, Tribal Alliances, Diplomacy, and Administration

Muʿāwiya's government worked through trusted governors and negotiated relationships. He appointed experienced figures, such as al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba and, most notably, Ziyād ibn Abīhi, to the Iraqi garrisons. Ziyād, whom Muʿāwiya formally acknowledged as a kinsman in a controversial act of genealogical politics, governed Baṣra from about 665 and later Kūfa as well, using strict policing and a reorganization of the garrison populations to keep order. In Egypt, ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ and then his successors remained powerful. In the Hijaz, Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam and other Umayyad relatives served as governors.

His relationships with tribal leaders were central. He gave stipends, offices, and marriages to chiefs, and he was reputed to be skilled at conciliating powerful men without surrendering authority. His marriages to women from the Syrian tribal groups, notably Kalb, reinforced alliances. Provincial officials and commanders mostly belonged to the elite of the Arabian tribal society that formed the conquering armies, but Muʿāwiya also relied on non-Arab and non-Muslim experts. A Christian physician, a Christian fiscal administrator for Syria, and the poet al-Akhṭal, a Christian from the Taghlib group who praised the Umayyads, all appear in the sources around his court, a reminder that Syrian society was Christian in majority.

Later Arabic tradition attributes to Muʿāwiya the creation of the dīwān al-khātam, an office for sealing and copying caliphal orders, and the beginnings of the barīd, the courier service. These attributions are plausible but not securely documented from his own time, and some of them may reflect later attempts to assign institutions to a founder. The securely evidenced picture is more modest: a ruler governing through trusted deputies and chancery practice borrowed from Byzantine and Sasanian models, with correspondence flowing between Damascus and the provinces. A dam inscription from near al-Ṭāʾif dated AH 58 (677–678) styles him amīr al-muʾminīn and shows that he also invested in the Hijaz.

Section 41.5

From Caliphal Selection to Dynastic Succession: The Designation of Yazīd

In the final years of his reign, Muʿāwiya sought acknowledgment of his son Yazīd as heir (walī al-ʿahd). Earlier successions had involved consultation among prominent Companions, an unsettled and contested process. By requesting oaths for a son during his own lifetime, Muʿāwiya moved toward a dynastic principle, though the sources show that he regarded it as something to be negotiated, not simply declared. Delegations from the provinces, including Syrian and Iraqi leaders, came to pledge allegiance, and Muʿāwiya personally visited the Hijaz to press the matter. The Syrian military establishment gave strong support.

The justifications offered combined practical and religious arguments: that Yazīd had proved himself in military and governing roles, that the alternative was another civil war, and that designation by a living ruler was safer than disputed selection after his death. The opposition was substantial. Prominent figures such as al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAlī, ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr, ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Abī Bakr, who had their own claims to leadership or to religious authority, did not accept. Critics objected that the arrangement resembled Byzantine or Sasanian monarchy, a point that later Abbasid writers developed into a charge against the dynasty as a whole.

Muʿāwiya died in 680, and Yazīd's accession followed. The transformation of the caliphate into a dynastic institution was neither complete nor uncontested, because each Umayyad succession still required bayʿa, military support, and claims of religious legitimacy. The challenge from al-Ḥusayn and Ibn al-Zubayr, and the civil wars that followed, belong to Chapter 43.

Division II · §§41.6–41.11

The Marwanid Dynasty and the Consolidation of Imperial Power

This division follows the passage from the Sufyānid to the Marwānid line and the successive reigns in which the empire's institutions were extended, tested, and strained.

Section 41.6

From Sufyānid Rule to the Marwanid Restoration

Yazīd I ruled from 680 to 683. His reign was dominated by the crises treated in Chapter 43, and he died in November 683, leaving a young son, Muʿāwiya II, who died after a short and ill-documented reign. The Umayyad order nearly collapsed. Ibn al-Zubayr, proclaimed in Mecca, gained recognition in much of the empire outside Syria, including Egypt and parts of Iraq, and even some Syrian commanders favored him. The Umayyad position was saved by a gathering of Syrian tribal leaders and a battle at Marj Rāhiṭ in 684, which brought Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, an elder of another branch of the Umayyad clan, to the caliphate.

Marwān reasserted authority in Syria and Egypt in less than a year and died in 685, designating his sons ʿAbd al-Malik and ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz in succession. ʿAbd al-Malik thus inherited a Syria that had been held together mainly by the loyalty of key tribal groups, an Egypt governed by his brother ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, and an Iraq in the hands of rivals, including the Zubayrid governor in Baṣra and a pro-ʿAlid movement around al-Mukhtār in Kūfa. The Byzantine Empire, sensing weakness, extracted tribute and then renewed raiding.

The conditions for state reconstruction were therefore particular. Provincial autonomy had been demonstrated in practice during the civil war, the Syrian army had proved its decisive importance, the fiscal system had been damaged by interruptions and competing governors, and the caliphate's claim to religious legitimacy had been publicly challenged. The responses ʿAbd al-Malik developed addressed each of these issues, though not in a single coordinated plan.

Section 41.7

ʿAbd al-Malik, 685–705: Rebuilding Caliphal Authority

ʿAbd al-Malik spent the first seven years of his reign reestablishing control. He defeated Muṣʿab ibn al-Zubayr, the Zubayrid governor of Iraq, at Maskin in 691, and his general al-Ḥajjāj besieged Mecca and ended Ibn al-Zubayr's rival caliphate in 692. He had meanwhile secured the Byzantine frontier by a truce under which, according to Theophanes, he agreed to pay tribute; when war resumed in 691–692, Justinian II's army was defeated at Sebastopolis after, reportedly, Slavic troops defected. The caliph also confronted Khārijite rebels in Iraq and Iran and had to manage difficult relationships among Syrian commanders. The detailed military course of the Second Fitna belongs to Chapter 43; what matters here is that by 692 a single ruler again controlled Syria, Egypt, the Hijaz, and Iraq.

Reconstruction rested on a particular distribution of power. The caliph placed relatives and trusted men in the main provinces: his brother ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz governed Egypt until 705, his sons and half-brothers held military commands, and al-Ḥajjāj governed Iraq. In the Hijaz and in Syria he relied on the leading Syrian tribal commanders, whose support had secured the Marwānid succession. Syrian troops, by now seasoned in civil war, became the force that disciplined restive Iraq, and the pay and honor they received were the political price. Iraqi revenue, the largest in the empire, was brought back under a governor answerable to Damascus, which was a precondition for paying the army.

Alongside these practical steps came a heightened public assertion of religious legitimacy. The Dome of the Rock (§41.31), the new inscription-only coinage (§41.26), and the introduction of Arabic in the registers (§41.15) all date to the 690s or the years that followed, and poets of the period call the caliph the deputy of God (khalīfat Allāh), whose meaning historians still debate. These measures spoke to several audiences at once: to Muslims who had seen the community split, to Christian subjects and the Byzantine court, and to officials who now handled documents in a new language. The sources add that ʿAbd al-Malik tried to arrange the succession for his sons al-Walīd and Sulaymān over the claims of his brother ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, who died in 704 or 705 before the issue came to a crisis.

These measures are often grouped together as ʿAbd al-Malik's reforms, but the evidence shows that most had earlier experiments behind them and were completed by his successors. What distinguishes his reign is the combination: the restoration of military control, the dependence on strong governors, and the insistence that the caliph's authority should be visible in coins, monuments, offices, and official documents. The Historical Debate in §41.34 asks how far this adds up to a centralized state.

Section 41.8

Al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf and the Government of Iraq and the East

Al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf, of the Thaqīf tribe of al-Ṭāʾif, rose through military service under ʿAbd al-Malik, helped end the Zubayrid caliphate at Mecca, and was appointed governor of Kūfa around 694–695, with authority soon extended to Baṣra and the east. He governed Iraq until his death in 714. His tenure is the clearest example of the relationship between a caliph and a governor: ʿAbd al-Malik gave him wide latitude and steady backing, and he in turn imposed Damascus's will on garrison cities whose settlers had a long tradition of political independence. Later writers preserve his speeches, which are stern and threatening in tone, though their exact wording was shaped by generations of retelling.

His administration worked on several levels. He tightened control of the stipend registers, so that absence from campaigns or disorder could mean removal from the rolls; he used Syrian troops as a disciplinary force; he relied on a corps of police and agents; and he sought to reduce the influence of older Iraqi tribal leaders (ashrāf) who had dominated Kūfa and Baṣra. In fiscal matters he is associated with the transfer of the Iraqi registers from Persian to Arabic (§41.15), with reassessment and the pursuit of tax revenue, and with canal and land projects in the Sawād. He is also credited with the return of peasants who had moved to the garrison cities to their villages, a measure whose consequences for conversion are treated in Chapter 42.

The rebellion of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Ashʿath in 700–701 showed the limits of this system. Ibn al-Ashʿath, a noble of the Kinda, commanded an Iraqi army sent against the ruler of Zābulistan in Sijistān. When al-Ḥajjāj pressed him to advance despite the difficulties of the campaign, the army turned on the governor, drew in discontented Iraqi ashrāf and Qurrāʾ (Qurʾān-reciters), and took Baṣra and Kūfa. Fighting at Dayr al-Jamājim and Maskin ended only when Syrian reinforcements prevailed. The sources report that ʿAbd al-Malik offered concessions, including dismissal of al-Ḥajjāj and equal stipends for Iraqis and Syrians, and that the rebels rejected them; this report should be treated as the later tradition's way of explaining the stakes, not as verified diplomatic record. What is clear is that the revolt gathered several grievances: the burden of distant campaigns, the unequal status of Syrian and Iraqi soldiers, resentment of a governor from outside the Iraqi elite, and religious objections.

In its aftermath al-Ḥajjāj founded Wāsiṭ around 703, a new garrison city between Kūfa and Baṣra, as a base largely for Syrian troops, a physical expression of the relation between the empire's center and its Iraqi periphery. He also directed the commanders of eastern expansion, including Qutayba ibn Muslim in Khurāsān and Muḥammad ibn al-Qāsim in Sind. His reputation in later literature is deeply hostile, shaped by Iraqi memories of repression and by pious tradition's disapproval of his methods. The sources also show a capable administrator who organized revenue and public works. A fair account separates his documented actions from the polemic around them and treats him as the strongest example of how Umayyad authority was exercised through a powerful intermediary, and of how dependent the caliph was on that intermediary's survival.

Section 41.9

Al-Walīd I and Sulaymān: Imperial Expansion and Government, 705–717

Al-Walīd I (r. 705–715) inherited a system that was stable enough to sustain both large-scale conquest and monumental building. His reign saw the conquest of Transoxiana under Qutayba, the campaign in Sind under Muḥammad ibn al-Qāsim, the consolidation of the Maghreb under Mūsā ibn Nuṣayr, and the expedition to Iberia in 711. Maslama ibn ʿAbd al-Malik, the caliph's half-brother, led operations against Byzantium. Building projects included the Great Mosque of Damascus, a rebuilding of the Prophet's mosque in Medina, and work on the Aqṣā mosque in Jerusalem, all treated in §41.31. Later sources credit him with welfare works such as care for the disabled, though details are anecdotal.

Sulaymān (r. 715–717) faced different circumstances. He was brother and designated successor of al-Walīd, but he dismantled much of the network of commanders and officials built around al-Ḥajjāj. Qutayba was killed in Khurāsān after attempting to resist him, and Muḥammad ibn al-Qāsim was recalled from Sind and died in detention. Earlier, while governor of Palestine under his brother (the date is debated, probably before 715), he had founded the city of Ramla as a new provincial center, and building continued there after his accession. As caliph he launched the great siege of Constantinople in 717–718, which failed.

The two reigns differ in political character. Al-Walīd presided over the conclusion of a cycle of conquest and the high point of the state's display of power. Sulaymān reoriented patronage, rewarded those who had been out of favor under al-Ḥajjāj, and presided over the costly attack on Constantinople. The pattern of caliphs rewarding their own networks and sidelining their predecessors' men is a recurrent source of instability, discussed in §41.33.

Section 41.10

ʿUmar II, 717–720: Justice, Taxation, and the Responsibilities of the Caliph

ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz was a grandson of ʿUmar I on his mother's side and a son of the longtime governor of Egypt, and Sulaymān designated him as successor, according to the traditional account, on his deathbed. He withdrew the army from Constantinople in 718 and governed during a brief reign that left a strong impression on later religious memory. Pious tradition made him a model ruler, even styling him the fifth rightly guided caliph in some circles, reflecting a desire to contrast him with other Umayyads.

The sources attribute to him a concern for provincial fairness and for the conditions of converts and taxpayers. Letters to governors reportedly instructed that those who accepted Islam should not be obliged to pay the jizya, that burdensome exactions be curtailed, and that officials treat the population justly. He is also credited with ending the practice of cursing ʿAlī from the pulpit, a reputed Umayyad custom. The difficulty is that most of this material was transmitted by later authors with a strong interest in portraying him as a pious exception, and the policies attributed to him are hard to separate from the general tendency to idealize him. Some measures were reversed by his successors, and fiscal documents do not show a sudden change in practice.

His reign illustrates the limits of historical certainty: a caliph's moral reputation, the administrative consequences of his instructions, and the retrospective use of his memory belong to separate questions. Conversion and its fiscal effects are examined in Chapter 42.

Section 41.11

Yazīd II and Hishām, 720–743: Governing a Mature Empire

Yazīd II (r. 720–724) faced an early revolt by Yazīd ibn al-Muhallab in Baṣra, suppressed by Maslama ibn ʿAbd al-Malik, and in his reign Ifrīqiya saw its governor killed in 721 after provoking his own guard. Hishām (r. 724–743) ruled for nearly two decades, the longest reign after Muʿāwiya's, and was remembered as a careful, even frugal, administrator who nonetheless presided over costly wars. He worked to strengthen fiscal control through land surveys and reassessments, which the sources associate with Egypt, Iraq, and the Jazīra, and he appointed powerful governors such as Khālid al-Qasrī and then Yūsuf ibn ʿUmar in Iraq.

His reign saw demanding military commitments. Arab forces fought the Khazars in the Caucasus, Turgesh Turks in Transoxiana, and Byzantines in Anatolia, and the sources report both successes and notable setbacks, including a defeat at the Battle of the Defile in Sogdia in 731 and the loss at Akroinon to the Byzantine emperor Leo III in 740. In the west, a Frankish force turned back a raid in 732 near Poitiers or Tours, an event whose modern significance has been exaggerated. In 739–741, a major Berber revolt in the Maghreb, connected with Khārijite preaching and grievances over taxation and booty, required Syrian troops to restore order, and it pushed the western provinces further beyond caliphal control.

Hishām invested in agricultural estates and palaces in Syria, such as Ruṣāfa and others, and cultivated a court that mixed Syrian, Arab, and non-Arab officials. After his death in 743, the dynasty's stability evaporated in a rapid succession of caliphs. The structural vulnerabilities this reveals are discussed in §41.35. The detailed story of 744–750 belongs to Chapter 43.

Division III · §§41.12–41.16

The Machinery of Umayyad Government

This division examines the institutions through which caliphal authority was exercised and the documents that show how those institutions worked in practice.

Section 41.12

The Caliph, Court, Dynastic Household, and Political Legitimacy

The Umayyad ruler combined several titles and roles. Khalīfa, the older term, designated a successor or deputy; amīr al-muʾminīn, Commander of the Believers, appears on the Hammat Gader and al-Ṭāʾif inscriptions of Muʿāwiya's reign and became the standard official title. Under ʿAbd al-Malik and his successors, some texts and poets also describe the caliph as khalīfat Allāh, deputy of God, a phrase whose meaning and official status scholars debate. What the sources agree upon is that Umayyad rulers claimed religious as well as political authority, leading prayer on important occasions, directing the pilgrimage, and presenting themselves as protectors of the community.

Court life centered on the caliph's council (majlis), where kinsmen, tribal leaders, governors, secretaries, and poets gathered. Consultation (shūrā) remained a political norm even as succession became dynastic. The household of the caliph was an administrative and political unit: relatives held governorships, marriage tied the dynasty to important tribal groups, and estates in Syria and the Hijaz supported its expenses. Guards, a chamberlain controlling access, and the shurṭa (a police and security force) structured the ruler's presence. Poets such as Jarīr, al-Farazdaq, and al-Akhṭal praised caliphs and are valuable, if partisan, evidence for ideals of rulership.

Contemporary claims should be distinguished from later conceptions. Medieval jurists writing under the Abbasids developed detailed theories of the caliphate and an ideal of the early community against which they measured the Umayyads. The Umayyads themselves presented rulership through coinage, monuments, titles, and public ceremony, and in a world where Byzantine and Sasanian kingship were the obvious comparisons, they adopted and adapted symbols of authority without necessarily describing themselves as kings.

Section 41.13

The Dīwāns: Registers, Revenue Offices, Correspondence, and Communication

The word dīwān referred both to a register and to the office that kept it. The earliest was the army register (dīwān al-jund), originally a list of those entitled to stipends, whose creation the tradition attributes to the caliph ʿUmar I. For each registered fighter it recorded name, tribal group, the amount of ʿaṭāʾ, and often the unit to which he belonged. Tribal heads (ʿurafāʾ) were responsible for the men of their groups, so the register served simultaneously as military roll, payroll, and social ranking. Because inclusion or exclusion carried money and status, the register was a political tool as well as an administrative one.

Fiscal records worked differently. Provincial offices maintained lists of districts, villages, landholdings, and taxpayers, the surveys that fixed what each owed, and accounts showing what was collected and when. The papyri of Egypt show these operations in the 700s: assessments sent down the chain of command from governor to pagarch to village headmen, payment in installments, receipts, and demands for arrears. A central treasury function existed in some form, but the sources do not allow a precise description of how much revenue was held in Damascus and how much was spent in the provinces.

The chancery (dīwān al-rasāʾil) drafted letters and orders in the caliph's or governor's name; the seal office (dīwān al-khātam), which the tradition credits to Muʿāwiya after a forged order was detected, copied and sealed them. Authentication relied on formulas, handwriting, and seals, among them the lead seals attached to documents. The barīd was a network of relay stations and mounted couriers that carried official correspondence between Damascus and the provinces. Later authors describe it as an inheritance from the Sasanians and Byzantines expanded under the Umayyads. The papyri show that official orders, travel permits, and requisitions moved through provincial administrations; they do not give a complete map of the stations or a reliable estimate of travel times.

Secretaries (kuttāb) staffed all these offices. Many came from families that had served Byzantine or Sasanian authorities, and they brought skills in accounting, drafting, and record keeping. The chapter's caution about sources applies directly here: administrative handbooks written under the Abbasids describe a symmetrical set of offices that existed in Baghdad. For the Umayyad period, historians infer institutions from practice and speak of functions (assessment, payment, correspondence, authentication) rather than of a neatly arranged ministry.

Historical Vocabulary
Dīwān and Barīd

Dīwān is a word of Persian origin that came to designate a register, the office that maintained it, and eventually a government department. In the Umayyad period it refers above all to the army stipend rolls and to the fiscal registers of revenue, and, by extension, to the offices of secretaries who drafted official letters.

Barīd denotes the relay-post and courier system. Its Arabic name appears to derive from a Greek or Latin term for a mule or post. The institution's origins in Byzantine and Sasanian practice are widely accepted, but the details of its Umayyad organization are known mostly from later descriptions.

Section 41.14

Governors, Provinces, and the Limits of Central Authority

The caliph exercised authority through governors, called amīr or ʿāmil, supported by subordinate officials who managed finance, justice, and military affairs. Major provinces included Syria, which the caliphs governed directly and which was divided into five military districts (ajnād); Egypt, held by relatives such as ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Malik and by governors such as Qurra ibn Sharīk; Iraq with the east, often combined under a single powerful governor; the Hijaz; Ifrīqiya; and, with varying degrees of independence, Armenia and the Jazīra. Governors commanded troops, supervised revenues, and were expected to remit a surplus, though in practice they also retained substantial discretion.

The provinces differed in structure. Syria was close to the center, held by loyal soldiers and Christian officials. Egypt was governed through a pyramid of local officials, the pagarchs, who supervised districts and collected taxes under the governor's central direction, and papyri show a fairly intensive and bureaucratic fiscal regime. Iraq's garrison populations had a tradition of political independence and were governed by strong governors with Syrian backing. Khurāsān lay far from Damascus, with a mixed population of Arab settlers and Iranian elites and a governor who often had to improvise.

Caliphs intervened by appointing, dismissing, and sometimes killing governors, but they rarely could reorganize local society in detail. Local elites (dihqāns, bishops, pagarchs, tribal chiefs, urban notables) mediated between governors and populations, and they retained their roles because the state needed them. Section 41.15

The Arabicization of Imperial Administration

In the first decades after the conquest, government records were kept in the languages that existing administrations used: Greek in Syria, Palestine, and Egypt (with Coptic in village life), and Middle Persian in Iraq and Iran. Early documents show the first stage of change. A bilingual receipt from Egypt, dated to 643 and recording the delivery of sheep, is written in Greek and Arabic (PERF 558), and Greek and Arabic documents from Nessana in the Negev belong to the later seventh century. Muslim officials therefore inserted Arabic into administration well before they replaced the language of the registers.

The later literary tradition dates systematic Arabicization to the reign of ʿAbd al-Malik and the years that followed. According to these accounts, the Iraqi dīwān passed from Persian to Arabic around 697–700 under al-Ḥajjāj, when the bilingual secretary Ṣāliḥ ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān succeeded Zādān Farrukh after the latter was killed during Ibn al-Ashʿath’s revolt. Al-Balādhurī attributes the translation of the Iraqi fiscal register to Ṣāliḥ, although the precise chronology and episodes of the story depend on later transmitted accounts. The Syrian dīwān passed from Greek to Arabic under ʿAbd al-Malik or al-Walīd, and sources associate the secretary Sarjūn and later Sulaymān ibn Saʿd with the change. The Egyptian dīwān is traditionally dated to about 705–706, under ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Malik, and the Khurāsānī dīwān to Naṣr ibn Sayyār around 742. These dates probably mark real moments when particular offices changed, but they should not be read as a single reform.

Documentary evidence supports a more gradual picture. Greek and Coptic persisted in Egyptian papyri well into the eighth century; the governor Qurra's letters (§41.16) were written in Greek with bilingual protocols; Persian fiscal practice lingered in the east, where coins with Pahlavi legends continued to be struck in Ṭabaristān, and Sogdian and Bactrian remained the languages of local documents. Recent studies suggest that the adoption of Arabic in an office often accompanied changes in personnel, supervision, and political control. Secretaries trained in Arabic became more valuable, and new generations of administrators, including clients, were recruited.

Administrative Arabicization must therefore be distinguished from social Arabization. A government might adopt Arabic registers while the population continued to speak Greek, Coptic, Syriac, Persian, and Sogdian. The social spread of Arabic, and the relation of language to religion and identity, are treated in Chapter 42.

Section 41.16

Records, Documents, Seals, and the Practical Operation of Government

Papyri provide the closest view of Umayyad government in action. From Egypt, the archive of Aphrodito (Kōme Aphroditēs) in Upper Egypt preserves the letters of Governor Qurra ibn Sharīk to the local pagarch Basileios, along with tax assessments, requisitions, and receipts. From the Negev, the Nessana papyri preserve tax demands and receipts from the later seventh century, some of them from the governor's office in Gaza. Other finds include Sogdian documents from Mount Mugh and Bactrian texts from the Hindu Kush region, which show that in the east local legal and administrative languages were used alongside Arabic. Syriac documents from Khirbet Mird and inscribed lead seals attest to authentication and local record keeping.

These documents reveal how central instructions were applied. Governors issued demands; pagarchs and village officials were charged with fulfilling them; local leaders petitioned, negotiated, and sometimes evaded. Qurra's letters show specific operations: complaints about overdue taxes (P.Lond. IV 1380), requests for named numbers of workers for fixed periods (P.Lond. IV 1401), recruitment of laborers for shipbuilding (P.Lond. IV 1391), and orders concerning sailors, craftsmen, and fugitive taxpayers (P.Lond. IV 1464). Taken together they show a government that was administratively sophisticated where established structures existed and dependent on local agents to execute its demands.

The surviving record is skewed toward Egypt because of its dry climate, and caution is necessary when generalizing to Syria, Iraq, or the east. Even so, the papyri correct narratives that focus on caliphs and generals by showing how ordinary taxpayers and officials experienced the state.

Division IV · §§41.17–41.22

Armies, Frontiers, and the Expansion of Umayyad Power

Expansion took different forms in different regions. A recurrent analytical problem in this division is how armies could conquer territory faster than provincial institutions could incorporate it.

Section 41.17

The Syrian Army, the Jund System, and Imperial Military Organization

The Syrian army was the foundation of Umayyad power. Syria was divided into districts called ajnād (singular jund): Dimashq, Ḥimṣ, Filasṭīn, al-Urdunn, and, after a later division of Ḥimṣ's territory, Qinnasrīn. The tradition ascribes the separation of Qinnasrīn to Yazīd I or a later ruler. Each district supported troops, mostly drawn from Arab tribal groups that had settled in the region, and military and fiscal administration were closely linked: soldiers received stipends (ʿaṭāʾ) through the register, and districts supplied provisions. Qinnasrīn and the Jazīra gave the dynasty a northern base facing Byzantium and Armenia, where commanders such as Maslama ibn ʿAbd al-Malik and Marwān ibn Muḥammad later organized frontier armies.

Syrian soldiers differed from the Iraqi garrison armies in that they lived among a long-established civilian population in a province directly governed by the caliph, they were close to the court, and they had a record of loyalty. Commanders came from tribal and Umayyad kin networks, with leaders from groups such as Kalb and, at other times, Qays prominent. The army combined cavalry and infantry and, for naval operations, crews drawn from coastal communities, including local Christians. Frontier forts, supply, and fortification depended on the local population and on the revenue of the districts.

The comparison with Iraq clarifies the contrast. In Baṣra and Kūfa, the troops were settlers organized by tribal groupings, reportedly in fifths and sevenths in the early period and later in quarters under Ziyād, and they were paid by a register under a governor who answered to the caliph. Their numbers were large, their political memory of ʿAlī and of the civil wars was strong, and they were sent on distant campaigns in the east. Al-Ḥajjāj's use of Syrian units to discipline them, and the creation of Wāsiṭ, show that the caliphs themselves understood the difference.

The army made the dynasty, but it also constrained it. Caliphs had to maintain Syrian loyalty with payments and appointments, and in moments of crisis Syrian units, not the caliph, decided outcomes, as at Marj Rāhiṭ and in the later fighting that Chapter 43 narrates. The military basis of Umayyad power was thus also a constraint on policy.

Section 41.18

Iraq, Khurāsān, and the Eastern Military Frontier

Iraq's garrison cities of Baṣra and Kūfa served as bases for the conquest and administration of Iran. According to later accounts, Ziyād ibn Abīhi sent a large contingent of families from the two cities to Khurāsān in about 671 and settled them near Marw, which became the center of Arab power in the east. The figures given in the sources are probably exaggerated, but the settlement of Arab troops and their families in and around Marw is well established. Iraqi commanders and governors, among them al-Muhallab and his sons, played large roles in the eastern wars, and the later sources report tens of thousands of registered fighters in Khurāsān by the 730s and 740s, a figure that cannot be verified.

Settlers in Marw were organized by tribal groups, notably Azd, Tamīm, and Rabīʿa, with a register and stipends like those of Iraq. Governors changed frequently, and the sources describe rivalry between groups when one tribal party displaced another. The governor Asad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Qasrī is reported to have rebuilt Balkh and used it as a base in the mid-730s, and Naṣr ibn Sayyār governed from Marw from 738 to 748, conducting a census or register revision that the tradition associates with the use of Arabic in the register (§41.15).

Governing Khurāsān required relationships with local Iranian elites. Marzbāns, dihqāns, and princes in cities such as Marw, Balkh, and Bukhārā concluded treaties (sulḥ) with Arab commanders, paid tribute, and in some cases retained their local authority. The dihqāns in particular remained important as tax collectors and local landholders. Arab settlers often married local women and acquired land, and over time the distinction between settlers and locals blurred, a process treated in Chapter 42. Fiscal and religious policy interacted: a governor in the 720s, Ashras ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Sulamī, is said to have offered tax relief to Sogdians who accepted Islam and then reneged, which contributed to unrest in Transoxiana.

The challenges were considerable. Khurāsān lay thousands of kilometers from Damascus, communication was slow, governors faced tribal rivalries among settlers, resistant local rulers, and the pressure of Turkic powers beyond the frontier, and the region's strategic importance and its distance made it a place where caliphal authority depended heavily on the governor's judgment. In the 740s it became the setting for the opposition movement examined in Chapter 43.

Section 41.19

Byzantium, Anatolia, the Mediterranean, and the Umayyad Navy

The frontier with Byzantium ran through the Taurus and the Cilician plain, and both sides conducted regular raids. The Arabs organized annual summer raids (ṣawāʾif) and sometimes winter raids into Anatolia, and fortified frontier towns such as Malaṭya and Mopsuestia. The aim was usually plunder, prisoners, and pressure, not permanent conquest. Byzantium reorganized its defenses in Anatolia into military provinces (themes), which helped it hold a territory the Arabs could raid but not durably occupy.

Muslim naval power developed quickly after the conquest of Syria and Egypt. Muʿāwiya, then governor of Syria, led an expedition to Cyprus in 649, and the Arabs defeated a Byzantine fleet at the Battle of the Masts in 655. Later accounts, mostly Byzantine, report assaults on Constantinople between 674 and 678, followed by a peace under which Muʿāwiya reportedly paid tribute or agreed to terms. The chronology and extent of these operations are debated, and historians regard the traditional sequence as compressed or embellished. ʿAbd al-Malik's reign saw further naval building, including shipyards in Egypt and Tunis.

The siege of Constantinople in 717–718, led by Maslama ibn ʿAbd al-Malik, is far better documented. A large land and sea force blockaded the city, but the Byzantine emperor Leo III held out, helped by the city's walls, the use of incendiary weapons (the so-called Greek fire), a severe winter, and, according to some sources, Bulgar attacks on the besiegers. The expedition failed and the army withdrew. It marked the limit of Umayyad ambition against the Byzantine capital, and it should be distinguished from the permanent territorial gains made elsewhere.

Section 41.20

North Africa: Qayrawān, Carthage, and the Western Provinces

Arab forces had raided Byzantine North Africa from Egypt since the 640s, but sustained conquest began after Muʿāwiya's reign. About 670, ʿUqba ibn Nāfiʿ founded Qayrawān in what is now Tunisia, a garrison city that anchored Arab presence in Ifrīqiya. ʿUqba later campaigned deep into the Maghreb and was killed in 683 in fighting against Berber and Byzantine-allied forces, and the Arab position contracted during the Second Fitna.

Under ʿAbd al-Malik, the governor Ḥassān ibn al-Nuʿmān took Carthage in 698, ending effective Byzantine rule in the Carthage region and its hinterland, though not every coastal or interior district passed at once under Arab control. He later reportedly faced resistance led by a Berber leader called al-Kāhina. Mūsā ibn Nuṣayr became governor of Ifrīqiya in the early eighth century; scholarly reconstructions place his appointment anywhere from about 698 to 708, and this chapter does not fix a year. He extended Arab control westward and left his client Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād at Tangier. The conquests combined military pressure with negotiation. Berber (Amazigh) leaders agreed to alliances, provided troops, and sometimes accepted Islam, and their communities in the plains and mountains differed in the degree to which they accepted Arab governance.

Umayyad authority was uneven. Qayrawān and the coastal plains were governed with relatively firm control, while the interior and the far west remained loosely held. Disputes over taxation and the distribution of booty fed resentment, and the Berber revolt of 739–741, linked with Khārijite preaching, showed the fragility of these arrangements, as Section 41.11 noted.

Section 41.21

The Conquest of Iberia and the Formation of al-Andalus

In 711, a force led by Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād crossed the strait to the Iberian Peninsula, traditionally landing near the rock later called Jabal Ṭāriq (Gibraltar). The Visigothic kingdom was politically divided, and the army of King Roderic was defeated in a major battle, probably in 711, near the Wādī Lakka (the Guadalete is a common identification). Mūsā ibn Nuṣayr followed with reinforcements in 712, and the combined forces took cities including Seville, Mérida, and Toledo within a few years. The roles of Ṭāriq and Mūsā in the later accounts are heavily shaped by traditions of rivalry, and Mūsā was recalled to Damascus around 714.

Much of the conquest proceeded by negotiated submission. A treaty reported for the region of Murcia, associated with a local leader called Theodemir (Tudmīr), allowed him to retain authority in return for tribute. Such agreements preserved local leaders and communities, and they allowed a small conquering force to control a large territory. The new province, called al-Andalus, was governed by a governor (wālī) answerable to the governor of Ifrīqiya and ultimately to Damascus. Arab and Berber settlers, many of them Berber soldiers, took land and positions across the peninsula.

North of the Pyrenees the pattern was mixed. Arab forces took Narbonne and parts of Septimania around 719–720, and the city served as a provincial base until the Frankish king Pippin III captured it in 759, so Muslim political control there lasted about four decades. A defeat before Toulouse in 721, the large raid that was turned back near Poitiers or Tours in 732, and a failed Frankish siege of Narbonne in 737 show that attempts to extend rule farther north did not succeed, even though Septimania was held. Al-Andalus's later independent history, beginning after the Umayyad dynasty's fall, lies beyond this chapter. For present purposes, Iberia and Septimania show conquest by small armies exploiting political fragmentation, followed by provincial government that relied on local arrangements and that reached its limits in Gaul.

Section 41.22

Transoxiana and Sind: Expansion at the Eastern Limits of Empire

Beyond the Oxus lay Transoxiana (Mā warāʾ al-nahr), a region of Sogdian and Turkic principalities and rich oasis cities, including Bukhārā and Samarqand. Qutayba ibn Muslim, governor of Khurāsān from 705 to 715, led repeated campaigns that brought Bukhārā, Samarqand, and Khwārazm under Arab authority. He used a combination of force and negotiated settlements, leaving local rulers in place, imposing tribute, and stationing garrisons. The conquests were fragile, and the best Sogdian evidence for how local rulers lived with them comes from slightly later. The archive found at Mount Mugh is associated mainly with Dēwāštīč, ruler of Panjikant, and with the events of 722, when he and other Sogdian leaders fled Arab pressure and were pursued by the governor of Khurāsān. These documents are not records of Qutayba's campaigns of 705–715. They show how a Sogdian prince corresponded, held land, and negotiated in the early 720s, after a generation of Arab tribute demands and garrisons, and they therefore illuminate the instability of the settlement Qutayba had imposed. resistance and Turkic intervention required continuing campaigns, and the setback at the Battle of the Defile in 731 showed the limits.

In the southeast, Muḥammad ibn al-Qāsim led an expedition into Sind around 711–712 under al-Ḥajjāj's direction, taking the port of Daybul and advancing up the Indus valley to Multān. The main surviving narrative, the Persian Chachnāma, was composed centuries later and claims to be based on an older Arabic source, so details should be treated with caution. The Arabs established garrisons and arrangements for tribute with local rulers, but the area did not become a deeply integrated province and Umayyad control weakened in later decades.

Division V · §§41.23–41.27

Taxation, Land, Coinage, and the Imperial Economy

This division asks not only what the Umayyads collected but how revenue moved through government, who controlled it, what it financed, and why fiscal arrangements produced political tension.

Section 41.23

Taxation, Tribute, and the Financial Foundations of Empire

The Umayyad fiscal system rested on taxes the conquerors inherited. In the conquered provinces, the principal levies were a land tax, often called kharāj in Arabic sources, and a poll or capitation tax on non-Muslims, the jizya. The terminology is slippery: early sources sometimes use kharāj for tribute in general and jizya for the poll tax or for tribute, and the meanings narrowed gradually. Much of the revenue was taken in kind or in money, depending on region and period, and assessed according to existing surveys and registers. Muslims owed separate alms and tithes, a distinct category whose administration is less documented.

Practice varied by region. In Egypt, the papyri show detailed assessments of land and persons, collected in installments, with additional levies for supplies for troops and officials and for labor. Population censuses and land surveys are reported for Umayyad Egypt from the late seventh into the early eighth century, with their scope and chronology reconstructed from literary and documentary evidence. The fiscal director Usāma ibn Zayd al-Tanūkhī held office in the 710s, commonly dated to about 714–717. In Syria, the Byzantine fiscal system persisted with adaptations, with Christian officials managing assessment. In Iraq, the Sasanian land survey and tax structure was modified: tax could be assessed on measured land or taken as a share of the crop, and officials, including those under al-Ḥajjāj, carried out reassessments. In Iran and Transoxiana, many districts paid fixed tribute under treaties, which local rulers collected from their own populations, and the sums reported for Samarqand and other cities in the sources are disputed.

Revenue moved upward through governors, who retained what they needed for salaries and local expenses and remitted a surplus to Damascus; the largest surpluses came from Iraq, Egypt, and the Jazīra. Communities often bore collective responsibility, so that the flight of taxpayers shifted burdens onto those who remained, a pattern visible in the Egyptian papyri and in the repeated measures against fugitives. Because the jizya applied to non-Muslims, conversion could reduce revenue, giving governors an incentive to restrict or tax converts, which is why fiscal policy and religion intertwined; Chapter 42 examines this in detail. For government, the key point is that revenue depended on knowing who owed what, which is why the registers of §41.13 mattered so much.

Historical Vocabulary
Kharāj, Jizya, and ʿAṭāʾ

Kharāj denotes in the Umayyad period a land tax or, more broadly, tribute from conquered territory. Its meaning narrowed over time to the land tax in later jurisprudence.

Jizya refers to a tax paid by non-Muslim subjects, usually understood as a poll tax, though early usage was less precise. ʿAṭāʾ is a stipend paid from state revenue to registered soldiers and, in some periods, to others entitled by the register.

Section 41.24

Agricultural Land, Estates, Irrigation, and Provincial Resources

Agriculture remained the main source of wealth. In Egypt, Nile-based agriculture produced grain, flax, and other crops, and the state oversaw the management of irrigation works as it had under Byzantium. Chapter 2 explained how the Nile and the Tigris and Euphrates structured agricultural systems; the Umayyads inherited both. In Iraq, the canal networks of the Sawād, the fertile alluvial plain, needed constant maintenance, and governors undertook land reclamation and canal digging, with al-Ḥajjāj reported to have promoted agricultural development around Wāsiṭ.

Land tenure was diverse. Peasant villages held land and paid taxes collectively or individually; large landowners, such as Egyptian estate holders and Iranian dihqāns, controlled substantial properties and collected revenue; and Umayyad rulers and officials acquired estates, particularly in Syria and Iraq, some by reclaiming land and some by state grants. Revenue rights could be granted to individuals, and the sources mention estates attached to the caliph and his family. Wealthy provincial landowners were useful intermediaries and also potential rivals.

The relationship between landownership and taxation shaped political relationships. Arab soldiers received stipends, not land, in the early period, but over time many acquired holdings, and the question of whether such land paid the same taxes as other land became contentious. These issues were not resolved uniformly, which is another reason to speak of regional fiscal practices.

Section 41.25

The Dīwān, Military Stipends, and the Distribution of Imperial Revenue

A large share of revenue financed soldiers. The stipend system, in which registered troops received annual payments and rations, was the largest regular expense, along with the salaries of governors and officials, the upkeep of fortifications, ships, roads, and the cost of public works and palaces. Provincial governors usually retained part of revenue for local needs and sent a surplus to Damascus, though how much reached the center varied. Caliphs also distributed gifts and pensions to relatives, tribal chiefs, and religious figures.

Continuing expansion strained the system. Each new conquest produced booty and tribute but also required garrisons and supply, and the longer frontiers became, the more resources they consumed. Campaigns in Transoxiana, Anatolia, the Caucasus, and the Maghreb demanded sustained funding while yielding uncertain returns. Unequal distribution of resources produced resentment: Iraqi troops complained of being sent on distant campaigns, Syrian troops expected favored treatment, and provincial populations bore the burden of requisitions and taxes.

Because the register determined who received payment, it became a political instrument. Inclusion on the rolls signified status, and caliphs and governors manipulated it to reward supporters and punish opponents. The fiscal and military systems were therefore inseparable. Financial strain contributed to the pressures discussed in §41.35.

Section 41.26

ʿAbd al-Malik's Coinage Reforms and the Creation of an Imperial Currency

For several decades after the conquests, the caliphate used coins inherited from its predecessors. In the west, Byzantine gold solidi and copper folles continued to circulate, and the Arabs minted imitations with Arabic additions; in the east, silver drachms of the Sasanian type continued to be struck with Arabic inscriptions added and the names of governors. These coins, sometimes called Arab-Byzantine and Arab-Sasanian, show gradual adaptation without a single policy. In the 690s the caliphate struck a gold type with a standing figure usually identified as the caliph, and then abandoned human figures altogether. Silver coins with Arabic religious legends are attested from Iraq during the same decade, some under al-Ḥajjāj, before the empire-wide dirham reform.

In AH 77 (696–697), the caliphate introduced a new gold dinar bearing only inscriptions. The texts proclaimed the unity of God and the mission of Muḥammad, and quoted Qurʾānic verses on the margins. A reformed silver dirham, also inscription-only, followed from AH 79 (698–699), with mints in multiple provinces whose names appear on the coins, including Damascus and, after its foundation, Wāsiṭ. Standardization is visible in weight: the reformed dinar weighed about 4.25 grams, somewhat less than the contemporary Byzantine solidus (roughly 4.38–4.5 grams), while early reformed Umayyad dirhams commonly weighed about 2.8–2.85 grams, with variations among coins and mints. The later theoretical standard of 2.975 grams should not be applied without qualification to all Umayyad silver issues. The multiplication of mints suggests a deliberate effort to create a common imperial currency.

Why was the reform made? Later literary accounts tell stories linking it to an Egyptian papyrus protocol bearing a Christian formula, or to the emperor Justinian II's decision to put the image of Christ on his gold coins in the 690s; these anecdotes show that later authors saw money as a field of religious rivalry, but they are not verified causes. Fiscal and administrative reasons are at least as plausible: a standardized coin simplified payment of stipends and collection of taxes, and a visible identification of the caliph with Islamic belief answered the legitimacy crisis of the Second Fitna. Copper coins continued to be struck locally with different designs, and older coins circulated for years. The reform was therefore not an overnight transformation, but a major step.

The coinage reached populations who never read a chronicle. Soldiers and officials were paid in it, taxpayers handled it, and merchants used it, so its message of unity, belief, and caliphal authority was carried throughout the empire. Its form served as a model for later Islamic coinage under the Abbasids and beyond.

Section 41.27

Markets, Transport, Infrastructure, and Economic Integration

The caliphate unified under one rule an area previously divided among two empires, and that political unity eased some forms of exchange. Roads, ports, and caravan routes inherited from Rome and Persia remained in use, and the state repaired roads, built bridges, and maintained relay stations. Ports such as Alexandria, Acre, Tyre, Baṣra, and Sīrāf connected the provinces with Mediterranean and Indian Ocean trade, themes treated more fully in Chapter 51. Qayrawān became a link between the Mediterranean and trans-Saharan routes.

Government demand shaped the economy. Provisioning armies, supplying garrison cities, constructing palaces and mosques, and building ships required grain, timber, metals, cloth, and labor, drawn from provinces by requisition and purchase. Salaries and stipends injected coin into markets, and garrison cities became commercial centers. Tax payments in cash encouraged peasants to sell crops, and the new coinage provided a common unit. Commercial relationships crossed political boundaries as well, since merchants continued to trade with Byzantium and with India.

Integration remained partial. Regions maintained distinct weights, measures, and production systems, and the movement of goods depended as much on local conditions as on caliphal policy. The empire's economy was a network of regional economies bound together by government demand, taxation, and trade, not a single integrated market.

Division VI · §§41.28–41.32

Imperial Society, Religious Communities, and the Public Representation of Power

A history of government must include those who were governed. This division examines the social groups through which authority operated and the monuments through which it was displayed.

Section 41.28

Arab Military Communities, Tribal Leadership, and Social Hierarchy

At the top of imperial society stood the Arab-Muslim conquest elite: the caliph's kin, tribal leaders (ashrāf), and registered soldiers who lived in garrison cities and Syrian districts. Their privilege was institutionalized in the stipend system, in access to military command, and in prestige. Tribal identities, such as Tamīm, Azd, Rabīʿa, Muḍar, and Kalb, organized military units, though tribal affiliations were flexible and often reconstituted in the garrison cities.

The ashrāf played a key role as intermediaries between governors and troops. They commanded units, negotiated disputes, and received favors, and the caliphs relied on them. Social hierarchy within the Arab community was therefore complex: early Muslims and Companions' descendants enjoyed religious prestige, tribal chiefs held political power, and ordinary soldiers depended on stipends and booty. These distinctions mattered more for government than any simple division between Arab and non-Arab.

Over time the descendants of conquerors acquired land, married locally, and became part of provincial society. How this process changed identities is for Chapter 42. For government, the key point is that the ruling elite was neither a closed caste nor a uniform group, and its internal rivalries are central to §41.33.

Section 41.29

Non-Arab Elites, Secretaries, Clients, and Participation in Government

Local elites remained essential to Umayyad government. In Syria, Greek-speaking Christian families supplied fiscal officials; Sarjūn ibn Manṣūr, who served under Muʿāwiya and ʿAbd al-Malik, belonged to a family that later tradition connects with the theologian John of Damascus. In Egypt, Coptic and Greek-speaking officials, pagarchs, and village headmen ran local administration. In Iraq and Iran, Persian-speaking secretaries and landowning dihqāns operated tax and record systems. In the east, Sogdian and Bactrian rulers served as intermediaries.

Clients (mawālī) of Arab patrons formed an increasingly important group. Some were freed slaves, some were converts attached to a tribe, and some were skilled administrators. They served as secretaries, military commanders, and household officials. The famous secretary ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd, a client who rose under Marwān II, and Sālim Abū l-ʿAlāʾ, who served Hishām, are examples of the intellectual prestige of the secretarial class. The status of mawālī, their grievances, and their role in the opposition movement are developed in Chapter 42 and 43.

The state's dependence on expertise gave non-Arabs real influence, even when formal status was unequal. At the same time, their participation was precarious, since a change of ruler or governor could end a career, and the sources show that elite families used patronage and intermarriage to protect their positions.

Historical Vocabulary
Mawlā (pl. mawālī)

Mawlā is an Arabic word with several meanings related to ties of patronage: it could describe a freed slave attached to a former master, a convert attached to an Arab tribe as client, or a more general dependent. In the Umayyad period the term was most often applied to non-Arab Muslims, and its status implications changed over time. This chapter uses it for individuals in government service; Chapter 42 examines it as a social category.

Section 41.30

Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Other Communities under Umayyad Government

Most subjects of the Umayyad empire were not Muslims. Christians of many denominations, including Chalcedonian (Melkite), Miaphysite (Jacobite and Coptic), and the Church of the East, Jews in Syria, Iraq, and Iran, and Zoroastrians in Iran lived under communal leaders who handled internal matters and dealt with governors. The authorities often recognized bishops, patriarchs, and Jewish and Zoroastrian leaders as intermediaries for taxation and dispute resolution. The Christianization of the region described in Chapter 33 meant that church institutions were already powerful local organizations.

Treatment varied by place and period. In Syria, Christians served in government and at court, and churches remained prominent; in Egypt, Coptic officials administered villages and bishops were recognized; in Iraq, the Church of the East retained its organization, and in Iran, Zoroastrian priests and landowners kept their roles but lost state patronage. Non-Muslims paid the jizya and were subject to restrictions in some periods, such as limits on building new churches, though the extent of enforcement is uncertain. Disputes sometimes led to confiscation of church property or to violence, and Christian and Jewish writers recorded both accommodation and tension.

Government interacted with these communities as institutions as well as individuals. The question of how and when individuals and communities converted, and how Muslim identity itself developed, is the subject of Chapter 42.

Section 41.31

The Dome of the Rock, the Great Mosque of Damascus, and Imperial Religious Architecture

Umayyad rulers used monumental building to communicate authority. The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed in AH 72 (691–692), is the earliest surviving major Islamic monument and one of the most studied. An octagonal building with a gold-covered dome over a rock on the Temple Mount, it is decorated with mosaics and long Arabic inscriptions that include Qur'anic passages and statements about God's unity, Muḥammad, and the status of Jesus. The inscription dating the building originally named ʿAbd al-Malik; the name was later replaced by that of the Abbasid caliph al-Maʾmūn, while the original date was left unchanged, a clue that scholars have used to reconstruct the earlier text.

The motives for its construction are debated. Proposed explanations include a claim to the sacred geography of Jerusalem and the Abrahamic tradition, a response to the grandeur of nearby Christian churches such as the Holy Sepulchre, a means of asserting the caliph's religious authority after the Second Fitna, and a monument tied to Islamic beliefs about the Prophet's night journey. No single interpretation is certain.

Al-Walīd I's building program included the Great Mosque of Damascus, begun around 706 on the site of a precinct that had included a church of St John, with mosaics in a style related to Byzantine decorative art; the Prophet's mosque in Medina, rebuilt with the aid of craftsmen from the Byzantine world; and renovations of the Aqṣā mosque in Jerusalem. Umayyad rulers and princes also built palaces, bathhouses, and agricultural complexes in the Syrian steppe and Palestine, such as Qaṣr ʿAmra, Khirbat al-Mafjar, and Qaṣr al-Ḥayr, which show a court culture drawing on Roman, Sasanian, and local traditions.

Section 41.32

Cities, Households, Labor, and Everyday Life under Umayyad Rule

Most people lived in villages and worked the land. Papyri show peasants paying taxes in coin and kind, supplying labor for state projects, and seeking relief from officials. Villages in Egypt and Palestine, such as Aphrodito and Nessana, recorded receipts, petitions, and private contracts. Urban life centered on markets, workshops, churches, synagogues, and mosques; garrison cities such as Fusṭāṭ and Baṣra combined soldiers, merchants, and craftsmen. Archaeology of towns like Jerash, Pella, and Scythopolis (Bet Shean) shows continued occupation, shifts in building use, and, in some places, damage from earthquakes, including the one of 749.

Households were organized around kinship and economic roles. Women owned and inherited property in many communities, appeared in documents as landholders and litigants, and worked in agriculture, textiles, and trade, although the evidence for their lives is uneven and filtered through male-authored sources. Slavery was widespread: enslaved people worked in households, agriculture, and the military, and some became important figures, including freed slaves who served as commanders and officials. Captives from conquests entered the labor force.

Taxation and military administration touched everyday lives directly. Peasants faced the risk of requisitions, forced labor on construction and shipbuilding, and travel restrictions, as the Aphrodito letters illustrate. Taxpayers sometimes fled their villages to avoid obligations, and governors pursued them. Soldiers' families depended on stipends, and communities near frontiers experienced raids and garrison demands. Because many of the sources were produced by officials, historians must read them for the experience of those they governed.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxpayers, Soldiers, Clerks, and Laborers

A villager in Upper Egypt in the 710s might know the empire through the visit of a tax assessor, a demand for workers for a distant construction project, or the need to obtain a permit to travel. A soldier's family in Marw might live on a stipend and on land acquired locally. A clerk in Fusṭāṭ might switch between Greek and Arabic in the course of a day. A Christian merchant in Damascus might pay the jizya, while worshipping in a church beside the great mosque, and an enslaved laborer might work in a household or on an estate with little record of his or her life.

These people left few direct accounts. Papyri, archaeology, and legal documents allow partial reconstruction, but historians must avoid generalizing from the exceptional survival of Egyptian material. The everyday history of the empire is a mosaic of regional experiences.

Division VII · §§41.33–41.36

Structural Tensions, Imperial Limits, and the Umayyad Legacy

This division asks what the Umayyads built that endured, and why a state with substantial resources remained vulnerable.

Section 41.33

Syrian and Iraqi Interests, Tribal Rivalries, and the Limits of Dynastic Government

The Umayyads governed through coalitions, and those coalitions had interests that could diverge. Syrian soldiers and Iraqi garrison populations competed for stipends, commands, and favor. Iraqi communities resented the political dominance of Damascus and the presence of Syrian troops, while Syrian forces looked on Iraqi turbulence as a threat. Caliphs and governors managed this rivalry by shifting favor, and each shift created new losers.

Sources describe tribal factions called Qays and Yaman, a division that became prominent after Marj Rāhiṭ, where Kalb and their allies supported Marwān against Qays groups. Later writers used these labels to explain many conflicts. Modern historians caution that the factions were not permanent political parties: alliances shifted, groups switched sides, and the labels were often applied retrospectively to rivalries rooted in competition over office and revenue. The fact that tribal language was available to frame disputes still shows how deeply the system relied on tribal networks.

A government resting on regional constituencies had structural weaknesses. Succession disputes could mobilize rival groups, governors with military followings could resist dismissal, and a caliph who displaced the clients of his predecessor created enemies. These tensions were not inevitable causes of collapse, but they limited the dynasty's ability to act as an impartial arbiter.

A provincial governor such as al-Ḥajjāj or Qurra: A governor was both an agent of the caliph and an independent power, responsible for revenue and order while exposed to dismissal when a new caliph preferred other men.
A Syrian soldier in a jund: Service brought stipends and proximity to the court, and the army's loyalty gave its members leverage in politics, but campaigns in distant provinces imposed heavy demands.
An Iraqi garrison settler: Settlers shared in conquest wealth but resented Syrian dominance, strict governors, and expeditions to distant frontiers.
A Coptic or Persian-speaking official: Expertise made officials indispensable, yet their careers depended on patrons, and administrative Arabicization changed the skills required.
A Berber community in the Maghreb: Alliance with Arab commanders brought opportunity, while taxation and unequal sharing of booty bred grievance, as the revolt of 739–741 suggests.
A non-Muslim villager or cleric: Communal leaders mediated with officials, taxes were burdensome, and public monuments proclaimed a new religious order beside older churches and synagogues.
Section 41.34

How Centralized Was the Umayyad Empire? Historical Debate and Institutional Evidence

Older scholarship often described a strongly centralized Umayyad state, particularly after ʿAbd al-Malik, with caliphal control over provincial governors, a unified fiscal system, and standard administrative practice. More recent work, including studies of papyri and of the empire as a whole, such as Andrew Marsham's, has stressed local variation: provincial administrations retained their languages and personnel, governors held considerable discretion, and the effects of reform differed by region and over time. Chase Robinson's work on the Jazīra and Marie Legendre's on administrative practice point in the same direction, toward a government that depended on provincial elites and on negotiation.

The evidence should be sorted by what kind of control it shows. The coinage is the strongest case for central direction: uniform designs and weights across many mints imply a decision at the center. Egyptian papyri show that governors' orders reached village level and that the fiscal regime was intensive. Caliphs appointed, dismissed, and sometimes executed governors, and Hishām's surveys and reassessments suggest an effort to know and tax the provinces. Against this, Syria and Iraq offer little comparable documentation; Khurāsān and Ifrīqiya were far from Damascus, and governors there acted with substantial independence; and regional rebellions, such as al-Ashʿath's in Iraq (§41.8) and the Berber revolt of 739–741 (§41.11), show that authority could not always be enforced.

The distinction between the state's capacity to act and its control of outcomes helps. The Umayyads could do some things well: mint a standard coin, assess and collect revenue where records existed, raise armies, and build monuments. They could not easily dictate local relationships, language of everyday life, or the conduct of distant commanders. Communication was slow, officials relied on local intermediaries, and the largest provinces were governed by men with their own followings. Centralization was therefore real in some respects and limited in others, a middle position that the following debate sets out.

Historical Debate I

Was ʿAbd al-Malik the Creator of a Centralized Islamic State?

The question: Did ʿAbd al-Malik's reign mark a decisive creation of centralized government, or was it a stage in a longer and uneven process?

Interpretation A — A decisive turning point: Supporters emphasize the cluster of coinage reform, the Dome of the Rock, administrative Arabicization, reorganized military control, and strong governors, arguing that these represent a coordinated assertion of imperial authority after the Second Fitna.
Interpretation B — A stage in a gradual process: Others point to earlier experiments in coinage, bilingual administration, and building, to the continued use of Greek, Coptic, and Persian in offices, and to differing regional chronologies, arguing that the reforms were adaptations with deep roots and uneven effects.

Evidence: Both sides use the same coins, papyri, monuments, and literary reports, but differ on how much weight to give later traditions that credit a single caliph and on whether documentary evidence from Egypt can be generalized.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians understand state formation in early Islam. This textbook treats ʿAbd al-Malik's reign as a major stage of consolidation, within a process that was gradual and regionally varied.

Student task: Construct the strongest argument for each interpretation, then decide which evidence would most change your view.

Section 41.35

The Pressures of the Late Umayyad Period, 730s–750

By the 730s, the empire faced several strains at once. Frontier warfare in the Caucasus, Anatolia, Transoxiana, and the Maghreb consumed troops and money and produced setbacks. Fiscal pressures grew as expansion slowed and booty declined, while demands on taxpayers and the question of how converts should be taxed produced grievances. Provincial tensions, including the Berber revolt in the west and unrest among settlers in the east, tested caliphal authority.

Politically, the dynasty's cohesion weakened. Hishām's death in 743 was followed by the brief reign of al-Walīd II, whose killing in 744 set off a sequence of rival caliphs, among them Yazīd III, Ibrāhīm, and then Marwān II, who moved the center of government to Ḥarrān in the Jazīra. Struggles among Umayyad princes and among tribal and regional constituencies exposed the dependence of the dynasty on powerful local forces.

These pressures explain vulnerability without predicting the outcome. The events of 744–750, including the organization of the Abbasid movement in Khurāsān and the dynasty's overthrow, are narrated in Chapter 43.

Cause and Effect

Conquest, Revenue, and State Institutions

Long-term causes

Conquest placed Byzantine and Sasanian provinces under Muslim rule with existing registers, officials, and landowners, and with armies whose pay depended on revenue.

Short-term causes

The Second Fitna exposed provincial autonomy and challenged caliphal legitimacy, prompting ʿAbd al-Malik's consolidation.

Immediate effects

Coinage, Arabic administration, military reorganization, and monumental building strengthened the state's capacities in the early eighth century.

Long-term consequences

New dependencies emerged: on powerful governors, on Syrian and eastern armies, on local elites, and on continuing revenue. These contributed to the dynasty's fragility and shaped the Abbasid inheritance.

Historical complexity: Reform increased some capacities and created others' vulnerabilities, and no single cause explains the later crisis.

Section 41.36

The Legacy of Umayyad State Formation and the Transition to a New Imperial Order

The Umayyads left enduring institutions and practices. Their provincial government through governors, fiscal registers and a tax system adapted from earlier empires, Arabic as an administrative language, a coinage with religious inscriptions, a military system that combined stipendiary armies with garrison cities, and a vocabulary of Islamic rulership expressed in titles and monuments all passed to later states. The Abbasids changed the dynasty, the capital, and the political ideology, but they inherited much of this machinery, as Chapters 44 and 45 show.

The Umayyads were also vulnerable. They depended on regional coalitions, on a small Arab-Muslim ruling group within a diverse population, on continuing revenue, and on a succession system that was never fully settled. Those vulnerabilities, combined with the pressures of the 740s, left the dynasty open to a well-organized opposition. Chapters 42 and 43 take up the social transformations and the political movements that this chapter has described only in outline.

Historical Debate II

Judging the Umayyads

The question: How should historians assess the dynasty's achievements and failures without adopting the prejudices of its supporters or its Abbasid successors?

Approach A — Take the Abbasid-era critique as a guide: The critique of worldly kingship and favoritism toward Arabs identifies real tensions.
Approach B — Read the critique as polemic: Later authors had political and religious reasons to disparage the dynasty, and their judgments should be tested against documents, coins, and monuments.

Why it matters: This textbook treats both the narratives and the documents as evidence, and it assesses institutions and vulnerabilities rather than issuing a moral verdict.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Byzantine and Sasanian Legacies and Umayyad Innovations

What Changed

  • The seat of government moved from Medina to Damascus, and rule became dynastic.
  • Coinage changed from imitation of imperial types to inscription-centered Islamic coins.
  • Arabic gradually replaced Greek, Persian, and Coptic in the central registers.
  • Public monuments proclaimed a Muslim imperial identity.

What Stayed the Same

  • Local officials, landowners, and church leaders continued to administer villages and districts.
  • Land and poll taxes were collected through inherited surveys and registers.
  • Roads, ports, and agricultural systems remained the economic base.
  • The Byzantine frontier remained a zone of raiding and defense.

Why the pattern matters: It cautions against treating the Umayyad state as either a complete break or a mere continuation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Umayyad dynasty governed an empire that stretched from Iberia to the Indus. It began with the problem of evidence, showing that Abbasid-era narratives, non-Muslim chronicles, papyri, coins, inscriptions, and buildings each illuminate different aspects of the period and that later judgments shaped the portrait of the dynasty.

Muʿāwiya, who had governed Syria for two decades, was recognized as caliph in 661 and built a coalition based on Syrian military support, tribal alliances, experienced governors, and the retention of local officials. His choice of Damascus moved the center of government to the Roman East and tied the dynasty to Syria's resources and army. His designation of Yazīd began a dynastic succession that remained contested.

The Second Fitna exposed the fragility of the system, and the Marwānid line under ʿAbd al-Malik reestablished unity and strengthened imperial institutions. His reign and those of al-Walīd I, Sulaymān, ʿUmar II, Yazīd II, and Hishām show different priorities: consolidation, expansion, reform, and administration under pressure. Governors such as al-Ḥajjāj and Qurra ibn Sharīk exercised much of the caliph's authority in the provinces.

The machinery of government combined caliphal courts and households, registers and chancery offices, secretaries and couriers, governors and local elites. Administrative Arabicization proceeded unevenly, and it should be distinguished from the spread of Arabic as a social language. Papyri show how orders were implemented, negotiated, and sometimes evaded.

The army, especially the Syrian jund and the garrison forces of Iraq and Khurāsān, was both the basis of Umayyad power and a constraint on it. Expansion took different forms in North Africa, Iberia, Anatolia, Transoxiana, and Sind, and in each region conquest outran institutional incorporation. The failed siege of Constantinople in 717–718 marked a limit.

The fiscal system rested on land and poll taxes, tribute treaties, and stipend payments, with revenue moving from provinces to soldiers, officials, and public works. The coinage reforms of the 690s and later made money an instrument of imperial authority, though they built on earlier experiments.

Imperial society was hierarchical and diverse. Arab conquest communities held privileges, but non-Arab secretaries, clients, and local elites were essential, and non-Muslim communities retained their institutions under governors and taxes. Monuments such as the Dome of the Rock and the Great Mosque of Damascus communicated religious and political messages to subjects and rivals.

Structural tensions, including Syrian and Iraqi rivalries, shifting tribal alliances, uneven centralization, and fiscal and military strain, left the dynasty vulnerable in the 740s. The question of how centralized the empire was remains debated, and the best answer recognizes both the real capacities of the state and the substantial role of regional power.

The Umayyads' legacy is therefore double. They built institutions, languages of government, and public symbols that endured, and they developed dependencies that limited their stability. Chapters 42 and 43 examine the social transformations and the political opposition that followed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown how an Arabian-led conquest movement became a state. Its institutions were largely adapted from the empires it displaced, its officials often came from the societies it governed, and its distinct contributions lay in its religious language of authority, its coinage, its stipendiary armies, and its public monuments.

The Umayyad empire also illustrates the difference between conquering and governing. Territory could be taken quickly, but revenue, loyalty, and shared institutions took decades to develop, and in some regions they never fully did. The study of that gap helps explain both the achievements and the fragility of early Islamic government.

The chapter also teaches a method. Narratives written by successors, documents produced by officials, coins and monuments created by rulers, and the accounts of outsiders each offer partial perspectives, and responsible history requires reading them together.

Chapter 42 turns from government to society, asking how populations changed religion, language, and identity. Chapter 43 returns to politics to examine the civil wars and the movement that overthrew the dynasty. Together with this chapter, they explain how the Abbasid order of Chapter 44 emerged from the Umayyad past.
