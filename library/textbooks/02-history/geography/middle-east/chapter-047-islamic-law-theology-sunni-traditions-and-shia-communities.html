Chapter Opening

In 833 the caliph al-Maʾmūn, then on campaign near the Byzantine frontier, sent instructions to his governor in Baghdad. Judges and the witnesses who testified in their courts were to be examined on a single doctrinal point: whether the Qurʾan was created or uncreated. The historian al-Ṭabarī, writing about ninety years later, preserved the substance of these letters. They show a caliph claiming the right to define correct belief and to test the officials who administered justice in his name. Within two decades the policy was abandoned. The scholar who became its best-known opponent, Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, held no office and commanded no army. The episode shows that something had developed in Muslim societies that even the most powerful ruler could not simply command: a body of religious scholars with their own sources of authority.

By the middle of the eighth century, Muslim rule stretched from Central Asia to North Africa, and the communities under it were diverse in language, custom, inherited law, and religion. Islam had no church, no council empowered to define doctrine, and no priesthood. After Muḥammad's death in 632, Muslims had disagreed about who should lead them (Chapters 38 and 40). Umayyad caliphs had governed an empire, appointed judges, and offered religious guidance, but they did not produce a systematic body of law or theology (Chapter 41). The Abbasids came to power in 750 claiming kinship with the Prophet and a religious mandate (Chapter 44). They inherited an unresolved question: who had the authority to say what God required of Muslims?

The answer that emerged between roughly 750 and 1050 was neither a single institution nor a single doctrine. Scholars gathered in mosques and teaching circles, collected and criticized reports of what the Prophet had said and done, and argued about how to derive rules from scripture. Judges decided cases, and jurists developed methods for reasoning about law. Theologians debated the justice and unity of God and the freedom of human beings. Different communities also reached different conclusions about leadership. Those who came to be called Sunnis located authority in the Qurʾan, the Prophet's example as transmitted by scholars, and the consensus of the learned. Shiʿi communities held that God had appointed a line of Imams from the Prophet's family as guides. The Shiʿa themselves divided into Zaydi, Ismaili, and Imami (later Twelver) traditions.

This chapter treats these developments as gradual and contested. A common mistake is to project later institutions backward, imagining that four legal schools, six canonical hadith books, Ashʿarī and Māturīdī theology, and Twelver Shiʿism were all fully formed in the eighth century. They were not. Abū Ḥanīfa, Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, and Ibn Ḥanbal were individual scholars whose followers built the institutions later named after them. The collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim acquired their commanding status over generations. The doctrine of a hidden twelfth Imam took shape only after the crisis of 874.

The chapter also faces a problem of evidence. Most of what we know about early Islamic scholarship comes from texts compiled generations after the events they describe, often by members of the traditions they celebrate. Section 47.1 therefore begins with the sources and the debates over how to read them. The chapter then examines religious authority (Division I), law and the formation of legal schools (Division II), hadith, theology, and Sunni traditions (Division III), and Shiʿi communities and the Imamate (Division IV). It closes by comparing the traditions and assessing their long-term significance (Division V). Its central question is how Islamic religious authority developed beyond the political authority of the caliphs, and how competing interpretations of scripture, law, theology, and leadership shaped Muslim communities.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the kinds of evidence available for early Islamic law and theology, and explain why later compilation complicates their use.
  2. Distinguish sharīʿa from fiqh and explain how the Qurʾan and the Sunna functioned as sources of religious authority.
  3. Describe the emergence of the ulama and their changing relationship with caliphs and governors.
  4. Compare the legal methods associated with Abū Ḥanīfa, Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, and Ibn Ḥanbal, and explain how legal schools consolidated gradually.
  5. Explain how hadith were transmitted and evaluated, and how collections such as those of al-Bukhārī and Muslim gained authority.
  6. Analyze the principal arguments of the Muʿtazila and the later development of Ashʿarī and Māturīdī theology.
  7. Assess the miḥna as both a doctrinal controversy and a contest over caliphal authority.
  8. Explain Shiʿi conceptions of the Imamate and distinguish Zaydi, Ismaili, and Imami developments.
  9. Trace the formation of Twelver belief in the Occultation as a historical process rather than an instantaneous event.
  10. Compare Sunni and Shiʿi traditions, identifying both shared foundations and internal diversity.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did religious scholars acquire authority in a community with no church and no formal doctrinal hierarchy?
  2. Why did the Prophet's example, and not only the Qurʾan, become a central source of law?
  3. In what ways did law develop in response to the practical needs of courts and a diverse empire, and in what ways did it develop through scholarly debate?
  4. How should historians weigh the testimony of later Muslim sources against skeptical modern scholarship about the dating of hadith and legal doctrines?
  5. Why did the Abbasid caliphs attempt to impose a doctrine through the miḥna, and why did the attempt fail?
  6. How did different Shiʿi communities answer the question of legitimate leadership, and why did they answer it differently?
  7. What role did political circumstances, including persecution, rebellion, and patronage, play in shaping Sunni and Shiʿi doctrine?
  8. How can historians describe the differences between Sunni and Shiʿi traditions without treating either as uniform or treating their division as inevitable?

Historical Context

Earlier chapters established the setting. The Arab-Islamic conquests (Chapter 39) brought Muslim rulers into contact with populations living under Roman provincial, Sasanian, Jewish, Christian, and customary legal arrangements. Conquest and settlement produced garrison cities such as Kufa, Basra, and Fustat, alongside older centers such as Damascus and the Hijazi towns of Mecca and Medina. The Umayyad period (Chapter 41) saw governors appoint judges and caliphs issue decisions, while pious individuals and groups in each region debated how Muslims ought to live. Conversion and the growing presence of non-Arab Muslims, the mawālī (Chapter 42), meant that religious learning increasingly drew on scholars from Iraq, Iran, and Central Asia.

The Abbasid Revolution and the founding of Baghdad (Chapters 43 and 44) placed the caliphate in Iraq, near the cities where much early legal and theological debate occurred. Chapter 45 examined the Abbasid fiscal and administrative apparatus, in which judges played a part, and Chapter 46 showed how translation and scholarly networks stimulated intellectual life, including the logical and philosophical tools that theologians used. Chapter 47 takes up the religious learning that grew within and alongside that imperial system.

The chapter's chronological focus is c. 750–1050 CE, with earlier foundations and selective later references. It does not repeat the political narrative of Alid rebellions covered in Chapters 43 and 44, nor the household, gender, slavery, and urban social history reserved for Chapter 48. The political histories of the Buyid, Fatimid, and later regional states belong to Chapters 53 and 54 and beyond; the madrasa and the Sunni revival of the eleventh century belong to Chapter 58. Here the task is the formation of religious traditions, not their entire later history.

Section 47.1

Reconstructing Early Islamic Law and Theology: Sources and Historical Problems

The principal sources for early Islamic religious thought fall into several kinds. The Qurʾan is the foundational text, and early manuscripts, along with inscriptions such as those on the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (completed c. 691–692), show that Qurʾanic wording was already in public use in the late seventh century. Legal treatises such as Mālik's Muwaṭṭaʾ and al-Shāfiʿī's Risāla and Umm record the reasoning of jurists. Hadith collections preserve reports about the Prophet and early Muslims. Biographical dictionaries such as Ibn Saʿd's Ṭabaqāt (d. 845) list scholars and their teachers; heresiographies, which catalogue sects and doctrines, and chronicles such as al-Ṭabarī's History (d. 923) describe controversies and events.

Nearly all of these texts, however, reached their present form after the events they describe. Treatises attributed to early figures were often compiled, edited, or expanded by students. Biographical dictionaries and heresiographies were written by members of the traditions they described and sometimes by their opponents. Later authors also arranged the past to show the origins of their own school. A historian must therefore ask who wrote a source, when, for whom, and what later settlement it may be defending.

Documentary evidence offers a partial corrective. Papyri from Egypt, coins, inscriptions, and administrative records show how law and government operated in practice, often independently of the later literary tradition. Modern scholarship since Ignaz Goldziher (1889–1890) and Joseph Schacht (1950) has also argued that much legal and Prophetic material was shaped in the eighth and ninth centuries rather than in the Prophet's lifetime, while other scholars defend earlier dating (see the Historical Debate after §47.10). This chapter adopts a cautious position: it relies on the sources while marking what is traditional, what is reasonably inferred, and what remains uncertain.

Terminology is part of the problem. Words such as "Sunni," "Shiʿi," "Muʿtazilī," and "Ḥanafī" gained sharper meanings over time. In the eighth century a scholar might be a hadith transmitter, a legal opinion-giver, and a theological debater at once, without belonging to a defined school. The chapter therefore uses the later labels carefully and notes when they are retrospective.

Section 47.2

The Qurʾan, Sunna, and the Search for Religious Authority

For Muslims the Qurʾan is God's speech, revealed to Muḥammad (Chapters 36–37), and it stood at the center of religious authority from the beginning. It contains moral guidance, ritual instruction, and a relatively small number of verses addressing legal matters directly, such as inheritance shares, marriage and divorce, and certain contracts and penalties. Its brevity on many subjects meant that Muslims needed ways to interpret and supplement it. Scholars debated Arabic grammar and vocabulary, the circumstances of particular verses, and whether later passages abrogated (naskh) earlier ones. The tradition of commentary (tafsīr) grew alongside law. The text's recitation variants were eventually systematized, and Ibn Mujāhid of Baghdad (d. 936) is associated with the recognition of seven canonical readings.

The concept of sunna was more fluid. In the first Islamic century the word could mean an established practice of a community, such as the "living tradition" of Medina, or the example of respected early authorities, including caliphs. Over the eighth century, a growing number of scholars argued that the most authoritative sunna was that of the Prophet himself, preserved in reports (hadith) traced to his Companions. Al-Shāfiʿī was the most influential advocate of this position (§47.7). The shift mattered because it gave a source of law that was both specific and independent of any one region's custom or ruler's decree.

These arguments about authority had political edges. Umayyad and Abbasid caliphs had issued rulings and asserted religious leadership, and some Abbasid caliphs claimed to be the Prophet's rightful heirs. Scholars who located authority in the Qurʾan and transmitted Prophetic example implicitly limited what rulers could dictate. Shiʿi communities, by contrast, held that the Imams, descended from the Prophet, were divinely guided interpreters, so that the Imams' teaching continued the Prophet's (§47.14). Both positions answered the same question, where does legitimate religious knowledge reside, but they answered it differently.

Section 47.3

Religious Scholars, Teaching Circles, and the Emergence of the Ulama

The ulama (ʿulamāʾ, "those with knowledge") were not a clergy appointed by a central authority. Scholarly standing came from learning, reputation, and the testimony of teachers and peers. Instruction took place in teaching circles (ḥalqas) in mosques and homes, where a teacher dictated, recited, or discussed texts and students attended, often for years. Students traveled between cities to study with respected masters. The biographical dictionaries record chains of teachers and students that functioned as scholarly lineages, and the practice of certifying that a student had heard or read a text under a teacher's supervision developed into the license (ijāza).

Ulama came from varied backgrounds. Many were mawālī, non-Arab converts or their descendants (Chapter 42), and a number came from Iraq, Iran, and Central Asia. Abū Ḥanīfa is said to have been a cloth merchant; al-Bukhārī came from Bukhara. Many scholars supported themselves through trade or crafts, and their independence from the court was a point of pride. Women appear in the sources as transmitters of hadith and teachers, although their visibility increases mainly in later centuries.

The relationship with rulers was ambivalent. Governors and caliphs needed judges (qāḍīs) and legal advisers and rewarded scholars who served. Under the Abbasids, scholars such as Abū Yūsuf rose to high judicial office. At the same time, accounts of pious scholars who refused appointments, or who criticized rulers, became part of scholarly ideals, and several famous figures, including Abū Ḥanīfa and Ibn Ḥanbal, are reported to have suffered for resisting pressure. Chapter 58 will show how rulers later built colleges (madrasas) to sponsor scholars; in the period of this chapter, the ulama's authority was still mostly personal and network-based, and caliphal efforts to control it, as the miḥna shows, could fail.

Section 47.4

The Development of Fiqh and the Administration of Justice

Muslims distinguish between sharīʿa, the divinely grounded path or ideal of guidance, and fiqh, the human effort to understand and articulate it in rules (see the Vocabulary box below). Fiqh was therefore not a fixed code. It was a body of scholarly reasoning and disagreement, and jurists accepted that different qualified scholars could reach different conclusions on the same question. The disagreement literature (ikhtilāf) is itself a major genre.

Law developed in close contact with practice. Judges decided disputes about property, debts, inheritance, marriage, and commerce; they relied on witnesses of recognized reliability and on written documents. A jurist-consultant (muftī) offered opinions on questions put to him. Formularies for contracts and deeds (shurūṭ) show how legal ideas were applied to everyday transactions. Alongside the qāḍī's court, rulers maintained other mechanisms, such as police (shurṭa) and the caliph's own grievance hearings (maẓālim), so that the sharīʿa-based court was one part of a larger system of justice. Non-Muslim communities generally used their own religious authorities for internal affairs, although individuals sometimes used Muslim courts.

Where early Islamic law came from is debated. Scholars have proposed that it drew on inherited practices of the conquered territories, including Roman provincial, Sasanian, Jewish, and customary law, alongside Arabian custom and Qurʾanic norms. Others emphasize the originality of the jurists' scriptural and methodological project. Evidence is difficult to assess because the surviving texts present law as Islamic in origin. A balanced view is that the empire's legal pluralism provided problems and materials, while Muslim scholars created distinctive methods for answering them. The fiscal and administrative work of the state (Chapter 45) also mattered: Abū Yūsuf's Kitāb al-Kharāj (a treatise on taxation associated with Hārūn al-Rashīd's reign) shows the interplay of jurisprudence and governance.

Historical Vocabulary
Sharīʿa and Fiqh

Sharīʿa originally referred to a path, and in Islamic usage it denotes God's guidance for human life as expressed in revelation. Fiqh means "understanding" and refers to the juristic process and its results: the rules scholars derived from scripture and other sources. Modern writers sometimes use "sharia" to mean a fixed code of law. Historically, this is misleading. What existed were competing interpretations, schools, and local legal practices, applied by judges within particular political systems.

Section 47.5

Legal Reasoning: Raʾy, Qiyās, Ijmāʿ, and the Sources of Law

Early jurists in Iraq, Medina, Syria, and elsewhere relied on personal reasoned judgment, called raʾy, when scripture and precedent did not give a clear answer. They also drew on local precedent, and Medinan scholars gave special weight to the continuous practice of the Medinan community (ʿamal ahl al-Madīna). Over time scholars disciplined this process. Reasoning by analogy (qiyās) extended the rule of a scriptural case to a new case sharing the same underlying cause. Some jurists also used methods such as istiḥsān (juristic preference), by which a scholar might depart from a strict analogy for the sake of fairness or practical need.

Consensus (ijmāʿ) became another source, but jurists disagreed about whose agreement counted: the people of Medina, the scholars of a region, or the learned of the whole community. Some argued that consensus protected the community from error, while others noted how rarely it could be demonstrated. The categories of "people of raʾy" (ahl al-raʾy), often associated with Iraq, and "people of hadith" (ahl al-ḥadīth), often associated with the Hijaz, are used in the sources, but they are oversimplifications. Scholars in both groups used reasoning and cited reports, and they differed in degree and in how they ranked sources.

The theory of law, uṣūl al-fiqh, developed as scholars reflected on these disagreements. Al-Shāfiʿī's Risāla is its earliest surviving major statement, and full treatises on legal theory multiplied in the tenth and eleventh centuries. Historians therefore caution against assuming that the early jurists consciously applied a fixed list of "four sources." The list was the result of argument, not its starting point.

Section 47.6

Abū Ḥanīfa and the Development of Iraqi Legal Traditions

Abū Ḥanīfa al-Nuʿmān (d. 767) worked in Kufa, one of the garrison cities founded after the conquests (Chapter 39). Later biographies say he was born around the turn of the eighth century, was of non-Arab, probably Persian, descent, and made his living as a cloth merchant. He taught a circle of students and is remembered for rigorous reasoning, including the use of hypothetical cases. Reports disagree about his dealings with Umayyad and Abbasid authorities: one tradition says he refused judicial office and was imprisoned under al-Manṣūr, but the accounts are not uniform and may reflect the later ideal of the scholar who resists pressure. Texts attributed to him, such as the creed called al-Fiqh al-Akbar, are of uncertain authorship.

Much of what is called Ḥanafī doctrine reached us through two students. Abū Yūsuf (d. 798) became chief judge under Hārūn al-Rashīd and wrote on taxation and law. Muḥammad al-Shaybānī (d. 805) compiled extensive works, including the Aṣl and a major treatise on the law of war and relations with other polities, the Siyar. These scholars show a connection between scholarship and the judiciary of the Abbasid state: legal reasoning was both a scholarly pursuit and a professional qualification for public service.

It would be wrong to say that the "Ḥanafī school" existed as an institution in Abū Ḥanīfa's lifetime. It emerged as followers collected, systematized, and defended their teachers' views over the following generations. Ḥanafī doctrine later became prominent in Iraq and Central Asia, and Chapter 75 will show its importance for the Ottoman state, but those developments lie well beyond the period examined here.

Section 47.7

Mālik ibn Anas, al-Shāfiʿī, and Competing Legal Methods

Mālik ibn Anas (d. 795) taught in Medina, the city of the Prophet's migration (Chapter 37), where he gave great weight to the practice of its inhabitants. His Muwaṭṭaʾ (the "Well-Trodden Path") arranges reports from the Prophet and early authorities, along with Medinan practice and Mālik's own opinions, in the order of legal topics. Later reports say that an Abbasid caliph proposed making the book the official law of the empire and that Mālik declined. The story is traditional and cannot be verified, but it illustrates the idea, important in later scholarly memory, that law belonged to scholars and not to rulers. The Mālikī school later became dominant in North Africa and Islamic Spain, which fall outside this chapter's focus.

Muḥammad ibn Idrīs al-Shāfiʿī (d. 820) was born, according to tradition, in Gaza, studied in Mecca and Medina (including with Mālik), worked in Yemen, spent time in Iraq, and settled in Fustat in Egypt, where he died. His mature views are preserved in a Baghdad phase and an Egyptian phase, leading later scholars to speak of his "old" and "new" doctrines. His major contribution was methodological. He argued that God had commanded obedience to the Prophet, so that authentic Prophetic reports were binding, that the Qurʾan and Sunna should be harmonized, that consensus and analogy were secondary tools, and that unrestricted personal preference in law was unacceptable. His criticism targeted both Iraqi raʾy and Medinan practice, insisting on explicit, transmitted textual evidence.

The Risāla, in its surviving form, appears to be the Egyptian version of a work he first wrote earlier. It is the most important early example of a jurist stating a theory, not merely recording rulings. It did not immediately win everyone over, and other jurists continued to follow their own methods. Over the ninth and tenth centuries, however, its emphasis on Prophetic reports influenced even those who rejected some of its conclusions.

Context: Composed amid disputes among jurists over the weight of local practice, personal judgment, and transmitted reports, the Risāla presents an extended argument, often in the form of dialogue with an imagined objector, about how the Qurʾan, the Prophet's example, consensus, and analogy relate to one another.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): Al-Shāfiʿī argues that the Qurʾan commands believers to obey the Prophet, so the Prophet's example explains, specifies, and supplements the Qurʾan. A report from the Prophet carries binding authority when properly transmitted. Analogy is permitted only for matters on which no text or consensus speaks, and he rejects legal preference that does not rest on identifiable evidence.

Analysis and limitations: The Risāla is valuable as evidence of how a major jurist theorized his sources, but it is advocacy, not a neutral account of what other jurists did. Its surviving text reflects the Egyptian recension transmitted by students, and it is not certain how closely earlier versions matched. Opponents' positions are presented through al-Shāfiʿī's framing.

Section 47.8

Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal and the Formation of the Sunni Legal Schools

Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal (d. 855) lived in Baghdad and studied hadith widely, traveling to scholarly centers in Iraq, the Hijaz, Yemen, and Syria. He was known for his insistence on transmitted reports over speculative reasoning. His Musnad, a very large collection organized by the Companion who narrated each report, was edited and expanded by his son and later transmitters. His fame rests also on his refusal to affirm the createdness of the Qurʾan during the miḥna (§47.12). Later admirers presented him as a defender of the community's tradition against caliphal overreach.

Whether Ibn Ḥanbal should be called the founder of a "school" is doubted. Students such as al-Khallāl (d. 923) gathered and organized his opinions, and in tenth-century Baghdad, groups of followers acquired a public presence, sometimes acting as vigilant defenders of traditionalist belief and morals. Law and creed were closely linked in this community: Ḥanbalī identity combined legal positions with a strict adherence to transmitted texts in theology. The consolidation of a distinct Ḥanbalī legal tradition, with systematic law and a lineage of teachers, mainly occurred after Ibn Ḥanbal's death.

By the late ninth and tenth centuries the Ḥanafī, Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, and Ḥanbalī traditions had become the principal personal schools (madhhabs) of Sunni law, as students gathered around lineages of teachers, wrote commentaries, and identified with the doctrines of a founding master. Other schools also existed. The Ẓāhirī school, associated with Dāwūd al-Ẓāhirī (d. 884), insisted on the literal sense of the texts, and Ibāḍī communities in Oman and North Africa maintained their own legal tradition. Some other Sunni schools eventually waned or disappeared; Ibāḍī legal scholarship, which was not Sunni, continued separately. Scholars debate why four principal Sunni schools endured, and explanations include the need for predictable rules in courts, the loyalty of students to teachers, and the patronage of rulers who appointed judges from particular schools. As jurists increasingly followed established doctrine (taqlīd) rather than pursuing new independent reasoning (ijtihād), some historians have spoken of a "closing of the gate of ijtihād." Recent scholarship disputes that picture, noting continuing legal innovation within the schools. The institution of the madrasa and its effects belong to Chapter 58.

Section 47.9

The Transmission of Hadith and the Evaluation of Reports

A hadith is a report of something the Prophet said, did, or approved, and Muslims also transmitted reports about his Companions and later authorities. Each report typically has two parts: a chain of transmitters (isnād) naming who passed it from whom, and the text itself (matn) (see the Vocabulary box below). Hadith were transmitted orally and in writing in a culture that valued memorization and audition. Scholars traveled long distances (riḥla) to hear reports directly from teachers, and a collector might spend years in Medina, Basra, Kufa, Baghdad, Nishapur, and elsewhere.

The need to assess reports became pressing because their numbers and contradictions grew. Muslim scholars acknowledged that fabricated reports circulated, sometimes serving political, sectarian, or legal arguments. Hadith critics developed a discipline of evaluating transmitters (al-jarḥ wa-l-taʿdīl, "impugning and declaring reliable"), compiling information on their lives, memory, honesty, and whom they had actually met. Figures such as Yaḥyā ibn Maʿīn (d. 847) and Ibn Ḥanbal were prominent critics. Biographical dictionaries of transmitters (ʿilm al-rijāl) made these judgments systematic. Reports were later graded by reliability, for example as sound (ṣaḥīḥ) or weak (ḍaʿīf).

Critics focused mainly on the isnād, although they also rejected texts that contradicted the Qurʾan, established facts, or reason. Modern historians generally treat the traditional method as a serious scholarly effort, but one with limits. Chains can be fabricated or improved, and the tradition's judgments of reliability were shaped by its own commitments. Academic methods of dating hadith, such as comparing variants and tracing common transmitters, ask different questions from those the hadith scholars asked, and the results do not always agree with the traditional verdicts.

Historical Vocabulary
Isnād and Matn

The isnād ("support") is the list of transmitters connecting the compiler to the Prophet or another authority. The matn ("body") is the report's wording. Muslim hadith scholars regarded the isnād as the principal means of verification. Traditional accounts date the demand for chains to the generation after the first civil war (Chapter 40), but the dating is debated, and modern scholars differ about how early chains were used.

Section 47.10

Al-Bukhārī, Muslim, and the Development of Sunni Hadith Collections

Early collections took different forms. Some, such as the Muṣannaf works of ʿAbd al-Razzāq (d. 827) and Ibn Abī Shayba (d. 849), arranged reports by legal topic and included opinions of Companions and later scholars. The Musnad of Ibn Ḥanbal arranged material by transmitting Companion. In the ninth century several scholars produced collections focused mainly on reports they judged authentic from the Prophet.

Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī (d. 870), a native of Bukhara who traveled widely in search of hadith, compiled his Ṣaḥīḥ by selecting reports that met strict criteria from a far larger body of material he had examined. He required, in general, evidence that adjacent transmitters had actually met. Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj (d. 875) of Nishapur compiled his own Ṣaḥīḥ with a somewhat less demanding requirement that they be contemporaries. Other scholars compiled Sunan collections focused on legal topics, including Abū Dāwūd (d. 889), Ibn Māja (d. 887), al-Tirmidhī (d. 892), and al-Nasāʾī (d. 915). Not all contained only reports each author regarded as sound.

Their later status was a gradual achievement. Sunni scholars in succeeding generations commented on, defended, and criticized the collections, and the two Ṣaḥīḥs of al-Bukhārī and Muslim came to be considered the most authoritative. The group of "six books" took recognizable shape over later centuries; there was disagreement, for example, about which work should be the sixth. Shiʿi communities developed their own collections, based on reports from the Imams (§47.15). The authority of hadith collections, in other words, rested on the community of scholars who accepted them, not on any official decree.

Historical Debate

How Early Are Hadith and Islamic Law?

The question: To what extent do hadith and legal doctrines attributed to the Prophet, his Companions, and early jurists reflect the first Islamic century, and to what extent were they produced or reshaped later?

Interpretation A — Much material was projected backward: Following Goldziher and Schacht, some scholars argue that many reports were formulated in the eighth and ninth centuries during legal and theological disputes and then attributed to earlier authorities, with chains of transmission improved or invented. Methods that trace "common links," the transmitters at which many chains converge, try to date the circulation of specific reports.
Interpretation B — Early material is more reliable than skeptics allow: Other scholars, including M. M. Azami and Harald Motzki, argue that early collections such as the Muṣannaf of ʿAbd al-Razzāq preserve material from the late first and early second Islamic centuries, and that wholesale fabrication of chains would have been difficult to coordinate. The Muslim tradition itself holds that hadith criticism distinguished authentic from inauthentic reports.

Evidence and why it matters: Both sides use the same collections and chains but differ about how much deliberate invention and gradual shaping they allow. Many historians now take an intermediate position and evaluate different bodies of material separately. This textbook treats the traditional dates as traditional and notes where independent evidence supports an earlier or later origin.

Section 47.11

Kalām, the Muʿtazila, Divine Justice, and Human Responsibility

Theological argument, kalām ("speech," in the sense of disputation), developed from early debates about political and moral questions: whether a grave sinner remained a believer, whether human beings freely choose their acts, and how to understand God's attributes. Its practitioners also faced Christians, Jews, Manichaeans, and Zoroastrians, whose own traditions had sophisticated arguments, and they drew on logical techniques that became more widely available through translation (Chapter 46). Early figures such as Jahm ibn Ṣafwān (d. 746), whose followers were criticized for denying divine attributes, show that such debates preceded the miḥna.

The Muʿtazila are the best-known early school. Later tradition traces them to Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ (d. c. 748) in Basra. Their thinkers, who included Abū l-Hudhayl al-ʿAllāf, al-Naẓẓām, Bishr ibn al-Muʿtamir, and, as a literary figure sympathetic to their approach, al-Jāḥiẓ, had distinct and sometimes conflicting views and worked in both Basra and Baghdad. Their traditional "five principles" emphasize divine unity (tawḥīd) and justice (ʿadl), the fulfillment of God's promise and threat, the status of the grave sinner as neither believer nor unbeliever, and commanding good and forbidding wrong. They argued that God is just and so does not create evil or compel human wrongdoing, that human beings are responsible for their acts, and that divine unity excludes eternal attributes distinct from God's essence. For many of them the Qurʾan as speech was created.

Historians must use caution. Much knowledge about early Muʿtazilī thought comes from later reports by opponents or from later Muʿtazilī writers such as ʿAbd al-Jabbār (d. 1025). Describing the movement as simply "rationalist" is a distortion: they gave reason an important role in understanding God and ethics, but they also relied on revelation and differed on many questions. The school's ideas later influenced Zaydi and Imami thought (§§47.16, 47.18), even though it declined among Sunnis after the miḥna.

Section 47.12

The Miḥna, the Created Qurʾan, and the Limits of Caliphal Authority, 833–c. 850

Al-Maʾmūn (r. 813–833) came to power after a civil war with his brother al-Amīn (Chapter 45 examines the conflict and its effects on Abbasid government). He sponsored scholarly debate and the translation movement and, in other gestures, made pro-ʿAlid moves: in 817 he named ʿAlī al-Riḍā, the eighth Imam of the Imamis, as his heir, though al-Riḍā died the next year. In 833 he ordered that judges and legal witnesses be examined on the doctrine that the Qurʾan was created. Al-Ṭabarī preserves letters conveying this order. Those who refused to affirm it risked dismissal, and the policy was enforced through the governor of Baghdad and the chief judge, Ibn Abī Duʾād, who was sympathetic to Muʿtazilī views.

Al-Maʾmūn died in 833, but the policy continued under al-Muʿtaṣim (r. 833–842) and al-Wāthiq (r. 842–847). Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal was summoned and, according to the accounts of his supporters, was beaten for his refusal, though he held no political power. Aḥmad ibn Naṣr al-Khuzāʿī, executed in 846 after involvement in a conspiracy, is also remembered as a martyr. Many scholars, by contrast, yielded under pressure or gave guarded responses. Al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861) abandoned the policy, with sources dating its end between about 848 and 851, and favored scholars who stressed adherence to the Prophet's example.

The miḥna has been interpreted as a theological controversy over the nature of the Qurʾan, since the doctrine had implications about God's speech and attributes, and as a political one, as the caliph asserted authority over doctrine and over the judges who served him. Its failure had lasting consequences. This particular attempt to compel agreement about the created Qurʾan failed, and Ibn Ḥanbal and other traditionalists became symbols of scholarly resistance to coercion. Yet the caliphs did not permanently abandon doctrinal intervention: under al-Qādir, religious declarations issued in 1017–1019 and 1029 asserted a Sunni creed, and al-Qāʾim reaffirmed it in 1040–1041. The miḥna therefore altered the relationship between scholars and rulers without establishing a fixed separation of religious and political authority.

Historical Debate

Why Did al-Maʾmūn Impose the Miḥna?

The question: Was the miḥna primarily a doctrinal crusade, a political instrument, or an assertion of the caliph's religious authority?

Interpretation A — Caliphal religious authority: Some historians, including Muhammad Qasim Zaman, argue that the Abbasid caliphs claimed the right to guide the community in religion, as heirs of the Prophet's leadership, and that the miḥna was an attempt to assert that role against the growing independence of the scholars.
Interpretation B — Political and administrative control: Others stress that al-Maʾmūn needed to consolidate support after the civil war, to bind judges and other officials to his authority, and to promote advisers and allies in the elite, so doctrine served as a test of loyalty. Al-Maʾmūn's personal conviction, shaped by Muʿtazilī-leaning courtiers, is also treated as a real factor.

Evidence and why it matters: The letters of al-Maʾmūn support the claim about authority, while the identity of those affected, officials and judges, supports the administrative explanation. The sources do not allow certainty about motives. This textbook treats the episode as combining both elements and emphasizes its consequences.

Section 47.13

The Development of Sunni Theology: Traditionalism, al-Ashʿarī, and al-Māturīdī

After the miḥna, traditionalist scholars, associated with the "people of hadith," set out their beliefs in short creeds (ʿaqīdas) that emphasized the Qurʾan as God's uncreated speech, divine attributes as stated in scripture, and the rejection of speculative theology. Many accepted the attributes "without asking how" (bi-lā kayfa). Others pursued more systematic argument. The Egyptian Ḥanafī al-Ṭaḥāwī (d. 933) wrote a creed that sought common ground among the Sunni legal schools, and Ibn Kullāb (d. c. 855) argued that God has attributes while rejecting the Muʿtazilī position, using kalām methods.

Abū l-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī (d. c. 935) was born in Basra, was trained in Muʿtazilī theology under al-Jubbāʾī (d. 915), and, according to later biographers, broke with the school around 912. He then defended traditional positions with the same argumentative tools, including the position that God's attributes are real and that human acts are created by God while humans "acquire" (kasb) them. His Maqālāt al-Islāmiyyīn catalogues Muslim sects. In Samarkand, Abū Manṣūr al-Māturīdī (d. c. 944), a Ḥanafī, developed a theology that gave human reason and choice somewhat more scope. Each tradition became associated with legal schools: Ashʿarism mostly with Shāfiʿīs and many Mālikīs, Māturīdism with Ḥanafīs, and a traditionalist approach with Ḥanbalīs, though these associations were never perfect.

These theologies were not yet dominant in the tenth century. Their later institutional prominence, including support from rulers and colleges, belongs to the Seljuk period (Chapter 58). The notion of a uniform "Sunni orthodoxy" in this era is misleading: Sunni scholars disagreed about reason and revelation, divine attributes, human action, and whether faith increases and decreases. What united them was a shared reverence for the Qurʾan, the Prophet, and the early community, and a growing sense of a middle path between the Muʿtazila on one side and the Shiʿa and Khārijites on the other. The term "ahl al-sunna wa-l-jamāʿa" gradually became a self-description of this broad community.

Division IV · §§47.14–47.18Shiʿi Communities, the Imamate, and Religious Identity
Section 47.14

The Imamate and the Foundations of Shiʿi Religious Authority

The word Shīʿa derives from shīʿat ʿAlī, the "party of ʿAlī." In the first century after the Prophet's death, supporters of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and his descendants were a political and religious current (Chapters 40 and 43). The Shiʿi position, in its various forms, held that leadership of the community belonged to the Prophet's family, beginning with ʿAlī, his cousin and son-in-law, and ʿAlī's descendants through Fāṭima, the Prophet's daughter, and her sons al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn. Shiʿi tradition cites the Prophet's statements at Ghadīr Khumm and elsewhere as designating ʿAlī; Sunni scholars also report some of these traditions but interpret them differently. Karbala (680) and subsequent Alid uprisings, covered in Chapters 43–44, gave the movement martyrs and a lasting sense of grievance, and are mentioned here only as background.

Shiʿi doctrines of the Imamate developed gradually. Many Shiʿa came to hold that an Imam is divinely guided, possessing special knowledge of scripture's meaning and, in the Imami view, protected from serious error (ʿiṣma), and that the Imam was designated by the previous Imam (naṣṣ). The community therefore needed the Imam to understand religion rightly. Early Shiʿi groups were diverse. Some believed in rising against unjust rulers, while others emphasized teaching and caution. Others, labeled ghulāt ("exaggerators") by their critics, attributed extraordinary qualities to Imams, and mainstream Imamis often distanced themselves from them.

The Abbasid victory in 750 complicated matters, since the dynasty rose to power on Hāshimite claims and then repressed Alid rivals. Persecution encouraged prudence. Imami teaching stressed the permissibility of concealing one's beliefs under threat (taqiyya). Because Shiʿi communities lived in Iraq, Iran, Arabia, and elsewhere, often alongside Sunnis and under varying rulers, their religious authority took shape through networks of followers, teachers, and agents, with the Imams or later their representatives at the center.

Section 47.15

Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, Imami Scholarship, and the Development of Shiʿi Traditions

Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq (d. 765), a descendant of al-Ḥusayn through his father Muḥammad al-Bāqir, lived mainly in Medina during the turbulent transition from Umayyad to Abbasid rule. Unlike some relatives, he did not lead an uprising, and he taught students in law, hadith, and theology. Later Imami tradition credits him with extensive teaching, and he is also cited by Sunni transmitters. He was therefore a revered figure beyond the Imami community. Among his associates were scholars such as Zurāra ibn Aʿyan and Hishām ibn al-Ḥakam, a theologian active in Kufa and Baghdad.

Much Imami hadith is traced to him and to other Imams, and Imami legal doctrine is sometimes called "Jaʿfarī." This labeling should be used carefully. Hadith attributed to the Imams were collected by followers in the ninth century in works such as al-Ṣaffār al-Qummī's Baṣāʾir al-darajāt (d. 903) and, later, al-Kulaynī's al-Kāfī (d. 941), and a systematic legal school developed later still. Historians must consider how far later compilations reflect the Imam's own teaching and how far they reflect the needs of later communities. The attribution of a body of law to Jaʿfar is real in tradition but is difficult to verify in detail.

His death was followed by disagreement about his successor. His son Ismāʿīl, whom some regarded as designated, predeceased him, and different followers recognized different heirs. The majority of the Imamis followed Mūsā al-Kāẓim, who died in custody in Baghdad in 799 under Hārūn al-Rashīd, while others followed the line of Ismāʿīl (§47.17). The later Imams, including ʿAlī al-Riḍā (d. 818), lived in an Abbasid world of surveillance and, occasionally, as with al-Maʾmūn's appointment of al-Riḍā, of political courtship. Those later Imams continued to teach, and their followers were organized through agents who collected religious dues and conveyed instructions.

Section 47.16

Zaydi Shiʿism: Leadership, Political Action, and Religious Scholarship

The Zaydis take their name from Zayd ibn ʿAlī, a grandson of al-Ḥusayn who led an uprising against the Umayyad caliph Hishām in Kufa in 740 and was killed. Where Imami Shiʿism emphasized designation by the previous Imam, Zaydi doctrine held that any qualified, learned descendant of al-Ḥasan or al-Ḥusayn who rose publicly against unjust rule (khurūj) could claim the Imamate. This emphasis on active leadership and scholarly merit meant that Zaydi imams could be many, and sometimes rival claimants, and that the community did not expect a hidden Imam.

Early Zaydis disagreed about the first caliphs. Batrī currents could regard Abū Bakr and ʿUmar as legitimate while placing ʿAlī above them; Jārūdī currents challenged the legitimacy of predecessors who had not recognized ʿAlī. The later descriptions of these currents also require caution because heresiographers imposed labels on a fluid early movement. Later Zaydi scholars adopted many Muʿtazilī theological positions on divine justice and human responsibility. Zaydi law developed through such figures as al-Qāsim al-Rassī (d. 860) and later Yaḥyā ibn al-Ḥusayn, called al-Hādī ilā l-Ḥaqq, and took its own distinctive shape.

Zaydism became a state tradition in two peripheral regions. In Tabaristan, on the southern Caspian coast, an Alid state was founded in 864 under al-Ḥasan ibn Zayd. In Yemen, al-Hādī established an imamate at Ṣaʿda in about 897, which gave rise to a Zaydi tradition lasting, with interruptions, for over a thousand years. The later political history of Yemen is revisited in Chapters 87 and 134, but those much later periods should not be confused with the ninth-century origins described here. For this chapter the Zaydis show that Shiʿi religious thought was not limited to the hidden-Imam model and that its communities developed far from the Abbasid capital.

Section 47.17

Ismaili Shiʿism: Succession, Missionary Networks, and the Emergence of the Fatimids

The Ismailis trace their line to Ismāʿīl, the son of Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq who died before his father, and to Ismāʿīl's son Muḥammad. The early history of the movement is obscure because few contemporary sources survive and later accounts were shaped by both supporters and bitter opponents. Ismaili doctrine developed a layered interpretation of scripture, in which an apparent (ẓāhir) meaning accompanies a hidden (bāṭin) one that the Imam and his representatives understand. In the later ninth century some Ismaili leaders were believed to be in concealment (mastūr) and to work through missionaries.

The summons, or daʿwa, was a network of missionaries who recruited followers across Iraq, Iran, Yemen, and North Africa. In Yemen, missionaries were active from the 880s. In 899 the movement split over a change in doctrine about the leadership. One group, often called Qarmatians, rejected the claims of the leader in Syria and, in eastern Arabia (Bahrayn), established an autonomous community whose forces raided Iraq and in 930 sacked Mecca and removed the Black Stone, which was returned in the 950s. Another branch recognized ʿUbayd Allāh, who established a base in North Africa.

In 909 Abū ʿAbd Allāh al-Shīʿī, a missionary working among the Kutāma Berbers, overthrew the Aghlabid rulers of Ifriqiya, establishing the new Ismaili-led state. ʿUbayd Allāh, known as al-Mahdī, entered Qayrawān and was proclaimed caliph in January 910, challenging the Abbasids. Historians dispute aspects of the Fatimid claim to descent from Ismāʿīl, which opponents already challenged. The Fatimid caliphate's subsequent conquest of Egypt, foundation of Cairo, and political history belong to Chapter 54, and the diverse later Ismaili traditions, including those of the Qarmatians, lie beyond this chapter. For our purposes, Ismailism shows how a religious interpretation of the Imamate, combined with organized missionary work, could produce a state.

Section 47.18

Twelver Shiʿism, the Occultation, and the Authority of Religious Scholars

In 874, the eleventh Imam of the Imamis, al-Ḥasan al-ʿAskarī, died in Samarra, then the Abbasid capital, held under surveillance. His death precipitated a crisis, because the Imamate was expected to pass to a son, and few people had seen one. Later Imami sources say that he had a son, Muḥammad, who had been concealed to protect him, and that the son became the twelfth Imam, the Mahdi expected to return and restore justice. Many Imamis, however, were uncertain or recognized other claims, and the early heresiographies list numerous groups that emerged after 874. The doctrine now called Twelver Shiʿism was one outcome of this uncertainty and was not an immediately agreed system.

According to the tradition, during the Lesser Occultation, traditionally dated 874 to 941, the hidden Imam communicated with the community through a series of deputies (nuwwāb or sufarāʾ), who conveyed questions and funds. Four are named, among them members of the Nawbakhtī circle in Baghdad, and the last died in 941. According to the tradition, he was told that no further deputy should be appointed, which inaugurated the Greater Occultation, during which no direct contact is possible. Scholars believe the period was marked by organization and argument within the Imami community in Baghdad, Qum, and Rayy as it adapted to the Imam's absence, and that the clear division into two occultations was formulated with hindsight.

The gradual consolidation of belief in the twelve Imams is tied to the writings of Imami scholars. Al-Kulaynī's al-Kāfī collected hadith, including those arguing that the earth is never without a divine proof (ḥujja); Ibn Bābawayh (d. 991) wrote Kamāl al-dīn to explain and defend the Occultation to confused believers. In the absence of the Imam, the authority of scholars who preserved and interpreted the transmitted teachings grew, a trend that the later eleventh-century scholars al-Mufīd, al-Sharīf al-Murtaḍā, and al-Ṭūsī developed further, with more emphasis on reason and legal method. Their later developments, and the relationship between scholars and states from the Buyids onward, appear in Chapters 53 and 77. Twelver doctrine was thus a long historical formation shaped by a crisis, by scholarship, and by changing political circumstances.

Analysis and limitations: The work illustrates how a scholar in the tenth century argued for the necessity of the Imamate. It cannot be used simply as a record of Imams' own words: its chains and arrangement reflect a compiler's choices, and Imami scholars later debated the reliability of individual reports. Historians also debate how fully the doctrine of exactly twelve Imams was articulated at the time he compiled the work, and later scholars refined the framework.

Section 47.19

Sunni and Shiʿi Communities: Shared Foundations, Distinct Authorities, and Internal Diversity

Sunni and Shiʿi Muslims shared the Qurʾan as scripture, reverence for the Prophet Muḥammad, the core ritual obligations of prayer, fasting, almsgiving, and pilgrimage, and many ethical teachings, although they differed in details of practice. Many reports and many scholars were common to both communities, and Sunni scholars revered the Prophet's family. The principal differences concerned authority: Shiʿi traditions held that divinely guided Imams from the Prophet's family were necessary interpreters of religion, while Sunni traditions located authority in the Qurʾan, the Prophet's example as transmitted by the community of scholars, and consensus. Different hadith collections, legal methods, and theological emphases followed from these positions.

Both traditions were internally diverse. Sunni communities included Ḥanafīs, Mālikīs, Shāfiʿīs, and Ḥanbalīs, Ashʿarīs, Māturīdīs, and traditionalists, with disputes among them. Shiʿi communities included Zaydis, Ismailis, and Imamis, whose views of the Imamate and the community's future differed profoundly. Other groups, such as the Ibāḍīs, were neither Sunni nor Shiʿi. The boundaries between traditions were also porous in the period. Zaydis shared positions with Sunnis, and individual scholars often belonged to more than one network.

Tension and conflict did occur, notably in tenth-century Baghdad, where riots involving Ḥanbalī and Shiʿi groups are recorded. These conflicts need political explanation, such as the weakened Abbasid caliphate, competing patrons, and neighborhood loyalties (see Chapters 50 and 53), rather than being treated as the automatic result of doctrine. The sectarian boundaries familiar in later periods were the product of centuries of institutional growth, not a fixed starting point, and the history of Sunni–Shiʿi relations includes long stretches of coexistence and scholarly exchange.

Section 47.20

The Formation of Islamic Religious Traditions: Continuity, Change, and Long-Term Significance

Between about 750 and 1050, scholarly networks, legal interpretation, hadith transmission, theological debate, and competing models of leadership reshaped Islamic religious life. In law, a set of scriptural and methodological arguments, with the competing schools' lineages of teachers, created a stable framework for judges and jurists. In hadith, techniques of critical evaluation and widely accepted collections gave Prophetic reports their central role. In theology, debates sparked by questions of justice, divine attributes, and human action produced the Sunni theological traditions, while Zaydi, Ismaili, and Imami communities elaborated distinctive doctrines of the Imamate.

Religious authority was neither simply dictated by the Abbasid caliphs nor independent of political conditions. Caliphs and governors appointed judges, patronized scholars, and, in the case of the miḥna, tried to command belief; scholars adapted to, benefited from, and sometimes resisted them. Shiʿi communities developed their institutions under suspicion or persecution and in some regions created their own states. Political fragmentation of the caliphate (Chapter 50) made scholarly networks and personal schools all the more important as sources of continuity across the Islamic world.

The chapter shows that these traditions were formed gradually and with a good deal of debate. The next chapter turns from religious learning to the everyday lives of the people who lived in Abbasid cities. Later chapters follow the institutions that would give these traditions state backing: Buyid and Fatimid rule (Chapters 53 and 54), the Seljuk revival of Sunni institutions (Chapter 58), and the Safavid creation of a Twelver Shiʿi state (Chapter 77).

Multiple Perspectives

Who Decides What Islam Requires? Perspectives on Religious Authority

From the perspective of an Abbasid caliph and court: The ruler is responsible for the community's welfare, including its religious life, and relies on judges and scholars as officials. Doctrinal disunity may seem a threat to order, which helps explain why a caliph might attempt a test such as the miḥna.
From the perspective of a traveling hadith scholar: Authority lies in carefully transmitted reports and in the reputations of teachers. A scholar's independence from the court is a mark of integrity, and a ruler's doctrinal command has no standing against the transmitted tradition.
From the perspective of an Imami follower: The community needs a divinely guided Imam, or, after 874, the teachings the Imams left and those who preserve them. Caution under hostile rulers is a religious duty, and loyalty to the Imams defines belonging.
From the perspective of a provincial judge: Law must be applied to real disputes in a given place, using the doctrine of a recognized teacher. A judge is answerable both to the appointing authority and to the scholars' standards, and may feel pressure from each.
From the perspective of Jewish and Christian communities: Their own religious leaders and courts governed internal affairs, and they watched Muslim debates, sometimes as participants in theological argument, while living under rulers whose policies affected them regardless of the doctrine at issue.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Regional Practice to Scholarly Traditions

What Changed

  • Regional practice and personal reasoning gave way to organized legal schools with lineages of teachers.
  • "Sunna" shifted from community practice toward Prophetic reports evaluated by scholars.
  • Theological argument became a formal discipline, and caliphal efforts to enforce doctrine ended after the miḥna.
  • Shiʿi communities, once largely a political current, developed distinct doctrines, texts, and in some places states.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Qurʾan and the memory of the Prophet remained central to all traditions.
  • Disagreement among qualified scholars remained an accepted feature of legal and theological life.
  • Rulers continued to depend on scholars for judges and legitimacy, and scholars continued to depend on patrons and communities.
  • Learning continued to move through personal teaching relationships and travel.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two errors: treating later institutions as ancient and unchanging, or treating the early period as unstructured.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Religious Authority Pass to the Ulama?

Long-term causes

The Prophet's death left the question of authority unsettled; conquest created a diverse empire that needed rules for adjudication; and scripture's brevity required interpretation.

Short-term causes

Abbasid patronage and the need for judges, the growth of Baghdad as a scholarly center, the circulation of conflicting reports, and the crisis created by the miḥna.

Immediate effects

Personal schools of law, systematic hadith collections, formal theology, and distinct Shiʿi doctrines of the Imamate.

Long-term consequences

Scholars became a lasting source of religious authority independent of rulers, and the sectarian traditions later gained institutional backing from states (Chapters 54, 58, and 77).

Historical complexity: These developments were not inevitable. Scholars and rulers made choices under uncertainty, and the same period could have produced different arrangements.

Everyday Life and Social History

Law and Learning in Daily Life

Most Muslims encountered religious law and learning through local practice: prayer, fasting, marriage contracts, inheritance, and the market. The judge's court, often held in a mosque or home, was where disputes were heard, and witnesses of recognized probity were important. Teaching circles in mosques were open to listeners, and Friday sermons and preachers spread religious ideas to people who never studied formally. The detailed history of households, gender, slavery, and urban life belongs to Chapter 48.

Evidence about ordinary believers is scarce because most surviving texts were written by scholars. Papyri, legal formularies, and biographical dictionaries provide glimpses of students, craftsmen-scholars, and women transmitters, and historians read them with attention to what they omit.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how religious authority in Muslim societies developed beyond the political authority of the caliphs. Because most sources were compiled later and shaped by the traditions they describe, historians must distinguish traditional accounts from documented evidence and from modern reconstruction. The Qurʾan was the foundation of authority, and the Sunna, increasingly understood as the Prophet's example preserved in hadith, became a second pillar. The ulama emerged through teaching circles, travel, and reputation, not through appointment by a central church.

In law, a distinction between sharīʿa as divine guidance and fiqh as human understanding allowed disagreement among qualified scholars. Judges and jurists responded to the practical demands of a diverse empire, and methods of reasoning, including raʾy, analogy, and consensus, were refined. Abū Ḥanīfa and his students, Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, and Ibn Ḥanbal contributed distinct approaches. The four principal Sunni schools did not exist as organized institutions in their founders' lifetimes. They consolidated through generations of students, in the ninth and tenth centuries.

Hadith scholars developed methods for evaluating transmitters and reports, and compilers such as al-Bukhārī and Muslim produced collections that gradually became authoritative. Modern historians debate how early much hadith and legal material is. In theology, kalām arose from debates about justice, divine attributes, and human responsibility. The Muʿtazila developed influential positions, and their doctrine of the created Qurʾan became the basis of the miḥna, which was begun by al-Maʾmūn in 833.

The miḥna failed, and its end under al-Mutawakkil confirmed that caliphs could not dictate doctrine to the scholars. Sunni theology afterward developed along traditionalist lines and, through al-Ashʿarī and al-Māturīdī, in two systematic traditions, neither of which was dominant in the tenth century. Sunni identity was broad and internally diverse.

Shiʿi communities held that divinely guided Imams from the Prophet's family were necessary religious authorities. Imami teaching, associated especially with Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, was transmitted in hadith collections compiled by later followers. Disputes over succession produced the Ismaili line, whose missionary network established Fatimid rule in Ifriqiya in 909 and proclaimed its caliph in 910, and the Zaydi tradition, whose activist doctrine led to states in Tabaristan and Yemen. The crisis of 874 and the experience of the Occultation shaped Twelver Shiʿism over decades, and scholars gained increasing authority.

Sunni and Shiʿi Muslims shared the Qurʾan, the Prophet, and core practices, while differing on the authority of the Imams, the sources of transmitted tradition, and some legal and theological doctrines. Both traditions were diverse, and conflicts between their followers require political explanation. The formation of Islamic religious traditions was therefore gradual and contested, and the institutional backing that rulers later gave them is the subject of later chapters.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how Muslim societies developed religious authority that was neither a church nor a state doctrine. The ulama, the schools, the hadith collections, and the theological traditions created ways of preserving and debating religious knowledge across a vast, politically divided world. They gave Muslim communities continuity even as dynasties rose and fell.

The chapter also shows why caution is needed with later labels. The sectarian and legal categories familiar today took shape gradually, through debate, patronage, persecution, and the work of students and compilers. Sunni and Shiʿi traditions emerged in the same world, shared much, and were internally diverse.

The structures introduced here will recur. Chapter 48 turns to the lives of people in Abbasid cities, and later chapters follow the institutionalization of these traditions under Buyid, Fatimid, Seljuk, and Safavid rule. A student who understands how religious authority was formed in this period will better understand why later rulers sought the scholars' support and why religious learning remained a force in Middle Eastern history.
