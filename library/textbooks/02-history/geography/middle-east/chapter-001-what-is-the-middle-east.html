What Is the Middle East?

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

No inhabitant of ancient Uruk, Memphis, Persepolis, Jerusalem, or Byzantine Antioch ever called their home "the Middle East." The phrase belongs to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, first appearing in scattered strategic and geographic writing before being popularized by naval strategists thinking about sea power, telegraph lines, and imperial rivalry, and it has been applied, debated, expanded, and contracted ever since. This textbook nevertheless adopts "the Middle East" as its organizing geographic frame, because the term has become the dominant modern name for a real and historically significant region — while insisting, from its very first page, that the name is far younger than almost everything the book describes, and that using it responsibly requires understanding what it does and does not mean.

Consider a single traveler's problem. A merchant departing Basra on the Persian Gulf in the tenth century CE, bound for Cairo, would have crossed a shifting patchwork of provinces, caliphal territories, language communities, and religious jurisdictions — Iraq, then the Syrian desert, then Bilad al-Sham, then Egypt — without ever imagining that these lands, together with Anatolia, Iran, and Arabia, formed a single "Middle East." A British telegraph engineer laying cable through the same lands nine centuries later, in the nineteenth century, would instead have spoken of Persia, Arabia, Mesopotamia, the Persian Gulf, Egypt, or "the Near East," since "Middle East" had not yet entered common use; an American oil executive negotiating a concession several decades after that, in the mid-twentieth century, could plausibly have used "Middle East" itself. Each generation, in other words, used a different regional vocabulary, with different borders in mind, serving different purposes, and often excluding populations and places that this textbook insists belong within the story.

This opening chapter, then, does not begin with Sumer, the pharaohs, or the Prophet Muhammad. It begins with the term itself: where "Middle East" came from, what older and still-useful terms — Near East, Levant, Mesopotamia, Fertile Crescent, Arabia, Anatolia, Iran — mean and how they relate to it, how wide a geographic frame this textbook adopts, which peoples and places belong inside that frame, what kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct a past stretching back more than five thousand years before any of these names existed, and why applying modern political categories to ancient, medieval, or early modern societies risks badly distorting the history this textbook exists to tell.

The region this textbook studies has never possessed fixed natural borders. Unlike an island or a narrow peninsula, it blends gradually into North Africa across the Sinai land bridge, into the Caucasus and Central Asia across the Iranian plateau's northern reaches, into South Asia through Afghanistan and Baluchistan, and into the Mediterranean and Balkan worlds through Anatolia and Cyprus. Every one of these boundary zones has, at different times, been treated as belonging inside or outside "the Middle East," and this textbook must therefore be explicit, from the outset, about the choices it makes and the reasoning behind them.

A further complication concerns whose history counts as central. Arab societies are indispensable to this history and receive sustained attention throughout its nine volumes, but a history of the Middle East that treated Arab history as the whole story would misrepresent the actual past of the region, in which ancient Egyptians, Sumerians, Achaemenid Persians, Anatolian Hittites, Parthians, Sasanians, Byzantines, Seljuks, Ottomans, Safavids, Kurds, Armenians, Jews, Assyrians, and many other peoples built states, empires, cities, and civilizations of their own, sometimes overlapping with Arab history and sometimes running in parallel to it. This textbook's very first commitment, developed throughout this chapter, is that the Middle East is the organizing subject of the work — not a synonym for the Arab world, the Islamic world, or the modern states that presently occupy the region's map.

Finally, this chapter introduces the methodological caution that will recur across all nine volumes and one hundred sixty-two chapters of this textbook: modern regional and national labels are recent, contingent, and frequently contested, and historians must use them carefully, as analytical tools for the present-day reader, rather than as descriptions of how people in the ancient, medieval, or early modern Middle East actually understood their own world. Getting this foundation right — precisely, and without false simplicity — makes it possible to read everything that follows with the discipline serious history requires.

The geographic frame adopted here begins with a core of interconnected lands — Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia and Iraq, the Arabian Peninsula, Anatolia and Turkey, Iran and the Iranian plateau, and the lands where Kurdish, Armenian, and other communities have long lived across these borderlands — while remaining attentive to the region's continuous relationships with North Africa, the Caucasus, Central Asia, South Asia, the Mediterranean world, the Horn of Africa, and, particularly from the twentieth century onward, Europe, Russia, and the United States. No chapter of this textbook treats the Middle East as a sealed or self-contained world; connection across these frontiers has been the historical rule, not the exception.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define "the Middle East" as a modern geographic and geopolitical term, and explain when it emerged and who was most influential in popularizing it.
  2. Distinguish "Middle East" from related terms including Near East, Levant, Mesopotamia, Fertile Crescent, Orient, and Southwest Asia, and explain the historical circumstances in which each term developed.
  3. Describe Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran as distinct historical regions with their own long-term trajectories rather than as peripheries of a single center.
  4. Explain the historical significance of the region's major waterways, including the Mediterranean, the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, the Bosporus, and the Dardanelles, for trade, strategy, and political power.
  5. Identify the major peoples of the region's history — including Arabs, Persians and other Iranian peoples, Turks, Kurds, Jews, Armenians, Assyrians and other Syriac Christians, Copts, Greeks, and others — and explain why the textbook treats the region's history as belonging to all of them rather than to any single group.
  6. Assess how wide a geographic frame is appropriate for a history of the Middle East, and explain the region's historical relationships with North Africa, the Caucasus, Central Asia, South Asia, and Europe.
  7. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of archaeological, textual, epigraphic, numismatic, and later documentary evidence for reconstructing the region's history across more than five thousand years.
  8. Explain the danger of anachronism — projecting modern national, religious, or ethnic categories backward onto periods that did not recognize them.
  9. Apply careful periodization concepts appropriate to a nine-volume, chronologically organized history, while recognizing periodization's interpretive limits.
  10. Contextualize the Middle East within wider Mediterranean, Eurasian, African, and Indian Ocean historical processes.
  11. Analyze why "Middle East" remains a contested and variably defined term among historians, geographers, and the region's own inhabitants.
  12. Articulate the organizing commitments — stated in this chapter — that will govern the textbook's treatment of ancient civilizations, religious traditions, empires, and modern states across all nine volumes.

Historical Context

Before students can engage with the specific content of this textbook — the first civilizations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant examined across Volume I; the Iron Age kingdoms, Persian Empire, and Hellenistic and Roman worlds of Volume II; the emergence of Islam and the caliphal age of Volume III; the medieval world of Turks, Crusaders, Mongols, and regional states of Volume IV; Ottoman and Safavid early modernity in Volume V; nineteenth-century reform and imperialism in Volume VI; the World War I era, mandates, and new nation-states of Volume VII; Israel, Palestine, Arab nationalism, oil, and the Cold War in Volume VIII; and revolution, political Islam, and the contemporary Middle East through 2026 in Volume IX — they need a shared, carefully defined geographic and evidentiary foundation.

The lands this textbook studies span an extraordinary range of environments and, across more than five millennia, an extraordinary range of political forms: village communities, city-states, territorial kingdoms, tributary empires, universal caliphates, sultanates, gunpowder empires, colonial mandates, and modern nation-states. No single environment, language, religion, or political system has ever unified the entire region, and none does so today. The Nile Valley's annual flood, the twin rivers of Mesopotamia, the arid plateau of Iran, the mountainous spine of Anatolia, the deserts of Arabia, and the narrow, fertile arc of the Levant have each supported distinct but repeatedly interconnected societies since long before written history begins.

Historians studying the Middle East must resist two opposite errors, familiar from the study of any large and long-inhabited world region. The first error treats the modern Middle East as though its current national borders, sectarian alignments, or ethnic geography were ancient and essentially permanent features of the landscape, reading twenty-first-century Iraq, Syria, or Turkey backward into Sumerian, Roman, or Ottoman history. The second error, a reaction against the first, denies that the region possesses any coherent long-term history at all, treating it as nothing more than an arbitrary sequence of unrelated episodes forced together by colonial cartography. This textbook adopts neither error. It treats the region's long-term environmental, commercial, religious, linguistic, and political connections as historically real and significant — the same river systems, mountain passes, sea lanes, and trade routes recur across chapter after chapter, connecting Sumerian merchants to Abbasid caravans to Ottoman pilgrims to twentieth-century oil pipelines — while insisting that political unity across the whole region this textbook defines as its core was rare, partial, and always temporary — no single early Islamic caliphate, Ottoman sultan, or any other ruler ever simultaneously controlled Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran together for any sustained period — and that regional, religious, linguistic, and ethnic diversity has been the historical norm, not an exception to an otherwise unified story.

A further complication concerns the very act of naming the region, which this chapter treats as a subject of historical inquiry in its own right rather than a neutral label to be adopted without comment. "Middle East" became established in English usage only around the turn of the twentieth century, in a specific strategic and imperial context; "Near East" is somewhat older but has narrowed in scholarly use. The regional names this textbook also relies on — Mesopotamia, the Levant, Arabia, Anatolia, Persia or Iran, the Fertile Crescent — are not all equally old: some, such as Mesopotamia and Arabia, are ancient or premodern names with long histories of their own use, while others, including the Levant in its modern geographic sense and the Fertile Crescent, are themselves comparatively recent European or American coinages. What unites them is not necessarily greater age but greater geographic precision, and, for many periods, greater historical appropriateness than the modern umbrella term. Section 1.1 examines the term "Middle East" itself in depth; Section 1.2 turns to these older and still indispensable regional names.

Section 1.1

"Middle East" as a Modern Term

Every regional history textbook must, at some point, explain what its subject actually is. For a history of France or Japan, the geographic referent, while not without its own complications, is at least anchored to a single, currently existing state whose borders have been relatively stable for generations. A history of the Middle East has no such simple anchor, because the term itself was invented for reasons that had little to do with the self-understanding of the peoples who have lived in the region across the five thousand years this textbook covers.

The phrase "Middle East" entered English-language usage in a recognizably modern sense around the turn of the twentieth century. Scattered earlier uses have been identified by historians in British writing of the 1880s and 1890s, including in the work of the British officer T. E. Gordon around 1900, but the phrase's decisive popularization and strategic definition came in a 1902 essay by the American naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan, writing about British imperial naval strategy and the defense of sea lanes around the Persian Gulf. Mahan used "Middle East" to denote the lands between Arabia and India, distinguishing this zone from what British and European writers were already calling the "Near East" — chiefly the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Ottoman lands closest to Europe — and the "Far East" of China and Japan; Mahan appears to have intended the new term to supplement "Near East" for a specific strategic purpose rather than to replace it outright. Journalists and British officials, including strategists associated with The Times of London, adopted and broadened Mahan's usage in the years that followed. The term spread rapidly in strategic and diplomatic writing after 1902, gained considerably broader currency during and after World War I as mandate planning required a workable regional vocabulary, and became increasingly dominant in English-language current affairs, journalism, and military administration by the era of World War II.

From its origin, then, "Middle East" was chiefly a term of external strategic geography — popularized by outside observers, organized around the interests and vantage point of European and American sea power, and applied to a region whose own inhabitants did not choose the name and initially had little occasion to use it. This does not make the term illegitimate for a modern history textbook to adopt; the term and its equivalents have become widely used internationally, including in Arabic (al-Sharq al-Awsat), Persian, Turkish, and Hebrew usage today, where it has been substantially adopted and adapted by the region's own governments, media, and scholars. But responsible use of the term requires remembering that it is a modern convenience, not a description that ancient Egyptians, Sumerians, Achaemenid Persians, Romans, Byzantines, Abbasids, Seljuks, or Ottomans would have recognized or used of themselves.

The term's boundaries, moreover, have never been fixed. Some twentieth-century usages included North Africa's Mediterranean coast, others excluded it; some usages included Afghanistan or even Pakistan as part of a "Greater Middle East," while others confined the term to the Arab states, Israel, Turkey, and Iran alone; American military and diplomatic organizations have periodically redrawn the map of what counts as "Middle East" for administrative purposes, sometimes including Sudan or the Horn of Africa, sometimes not. This textbook does not pretend that these variations can be resolved into one correct definition. Instead, it adopts a working core geographic frame — set out fully in Section 1.6 — while treating the term's very instability as a historically meaningful fact about how outside powers have imagined, mapped, and intervened in this region across the past century.

Historical Vocabulary
Near East and Middle East

Before "Middle East" became widespread, European diplomats, geographers, and travelers most commonly used Near East (or, in earlier usage, "the Levant" or "the Orient") to describe the Ottoman-ruled lands nearest to Europe — the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Arab provinces of Syria, Palestine, and Iraq. "Near East" implied a geography measured from a European vantage point: lands "near" to Europe, as opposed to the "Far East" of East Asia. This usage dominated nineteenth-century diplomacy, particularly around what contemporaries called the "Eastern Question" — the diplomatic and military crisis produced by the gradual decline of Ottoman control over its European and Near Eastern territories, examined in Chapter 95.

Through the twentieth century, "Near East" narrowed considerably in general journalism and public discourse relative to the broadening "Middle East," but it did not disappear. It remains firmly established in academic disciplines such as "Near Eastern archaeology" or "ancient Near Eastern studies," which typically refer to the pre-classical civilizations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant, and it also survives in ongoing institutional usage — the United States Department of State, for example, has long organized its regional diplomacy through a Bureau of Near Eastern Affairs. This textbook uses "Near East" chiefly in its narrower, more technical sense when referring to ancient Near Eastern civilizations, while noting that the term remains institutionally current in some contexts, and uses "Middle East" as its primary organizing term for the region across all periods.

Section 1.2

Older Regional Names: Near East, Levant, Mesopotamia, Fertile Crescent

Long before "Middle East" existed, the lands this textbook studies were known by a variety of older, often more geographically precise names, many of which remain indispensable to historians and which this textbook continues to use throughout, particularly for ancient, medieval, and early modern periods when "Middle East" would be anachronistic.

Mesopotamia, from the Greek for "between the rivers," describes the lands watered by the Tigris and Euphrates, corresponding roughly to modern Iraq and parts of eastern Syria. The term was already in use among ancient Greek geographers describing the lands they knew lay between these two great rivers, and it remains the standard scholarly term for the civilizations of Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, and Assyria examined in Chapters 8 through 12 and 17 through 20.

The Levant, from French and Italian words meaning "the rising" (referring to the direction of the rising sun as seen from Mediterranean Europe), describes the eastern Mediterranean coastal lands — roughly modern Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel, and the Palestinian territories. Medieval and early modern European merchants, particularly Venetian and Genoese traders, used "the Levant" extensively to describe their eastern Mediterranean commercial partners, and the term survives in modern usage, including in the academic field of "Levantine archaeology" and in everyday geographic reference throughout this textbook.

The Fertile Crescent is a more recent coinage, introduced by the American archaeologist and historian James Henry Breasted in the early twentieth century to describe a crescent-shaped band of relatively well-watered, agriculturally productive land curving from the eastern Mediterranean coast through northern Mesopotamia and down toward Babylonia and the head of the Persian Gulf, with the arid Syrian and Arabian deserts lying inside the curve of the crescent. Later writers have defined the term's exact boundaries somewhat differently, and modern usage commonly extends it into southeastern Anatolia and, in some versions, includes Egypt as well. Breasted's term captured a genuine ecological and agricultural pattern important for understanding the origins of farming and urban civilization, examined in Chapters 5 through 8, though it should not be mistaken for a comprehensive description of the wider region, since Arabia and Iran fall largely, and Anatolia partly, outside it.

Historical Vocabulary
The Fertile Crescent

James Henry Breasted, an American archaeologist and historian associated with the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, introduced "Fertile Crescent" in the early twentieth century as a descriptive and pedagogical device for explaining why farming, cities, and writing first developed where they did. The term is a modern analytical tool, not a translation of any ancient name, and later scholars and textbooks have drawn its boundaries somewhat differently — some extending it into southeastern Anatolia, others including Egypt as a kind of southwestern extension along the Nile. Students should treat "Fertile Crescent" as a useful shorthand for a genuine ecological pattern rather than as a fixed or ancient geographic category.

The Orient, an older and now largely retired term derived from Latin for "the direction of the rising sun," was used broadly by earlier generations of European writers to describe a vast, loosely defined eastern zone encompassing the Middle East, North Africa, and often South and East Asia as well. "Orientalism" itself carries more than one meaning that students should distinguish. In its older sense, it denoted the Western scholarly discipline of studying the languages, texts, religions, and histories of Asian and Middle Eastern civilizations, a tradition that produced genuine philological and historical scholarship still consulted by historians today. Following the publication of the literary scholar Edward Said's influential 1978 book Orientalism, the term has also come to name a critical framework examining how Western writing about "the Orient" frequently generalized, essentialized, or stereotyped the region and its peoples, and how such representations were bound up with European colonial power and knowledge production. This textbook uses "Orientalism" in Said's critical sense when discussing the history of Western representations of the region, while continuing to draw on the genuine scholarly contributions of the older philological tradition where they remain sound; it avoids "Orient" itself as a geographic term except when discussing how earlier European writers used it.

Alongside these broader regional terms, this textbook makes extensive use of still more specific historical geographic names — Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia, Assyria, Elam, Egypt, Canaan, Phoenicia, Philistia, Israel and Judah, Syria, Palestine, Arabia, the Hijaz, Najd, Yemen, Anatolia, Armenia, Persia — introducing and explaining each as it becomes relevant in later chapters. No single umbrella term, ancient or modern, can substitute for this more precise historical vocabulary, and students should expect this textbook to shift between "Middle East" as an overall organizing frame and these older, period-appropriate regional names as the specific history requires.

Section 1.3

Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran as Distinct Historical Worlds

A recurring commitment of this textbook, stated explicitly in these opening pages and enforced throughout its nine volumes, is that Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran must each be treated as major historical regions with their own long-term trajectories — not merely as peripheries surrounding an Arab, Ottoman-Turkish, or Perso-Islamic center, and not merely as the later homelands of the Arab, Turkish, and Iranian nations in their modern political sense.

The Arabian Peninsula, bounded by the Red Sea, the Arabian Sea, and the Persian Gulf, was for most of ancient and late antique history a world of oasis towns, caravan cities, South Arabian kingdoms, pastoral and semi-pastoral tribal confederations, and maritime trading communities along its coasts — a history examined in its own right in Chapters 34 and 35 before Arabia becomes, with the rise of Islam in the seventh century CE, the point of origin for a transformation that would reshape the wider region. Arabia's pre-Islamic history, its South Arabian civilizations such as Saba and Himyar, and its diverse religious landscape of polytheistic, Jewish, and Christian communities on the eve of Islam deserve sustained attention rather than serving merely as prelude.

Anatolia, the peninsula that forms the core of modern Turkey, possesses a documented history extending many centuries before large-scale Turkic settlement: Hittite kings ruled from Hattusa more than a thousand years before the Common Era, Greek cities lined its Aegean coast, and Persian satraps, Roman governors, and Byzantine emperors ruled it in succession, while Turkic-speaking peoples began arriving in significant numbers only from the eleventh century CE onward, gradually transforming the peninsula's language, religion, and political culture over subsequent centuries. This textbook explicitly rejects the notion that Anatolian history "begins" with the Turks; Chapter 15 in Volume I, further chapters across Volume II treating Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine Anatolia, and numerous later chapters treat Hittite, Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Armenian Anatolia as integral to the region's history in their own right.

Iran and the Iranian plateau present a related but distinct case: unlike Anatolia, where an earlier linguistic and cultural landscape was substantially transformed by Turkic migration, Iran has sustained long continuities of Iranian languages, cultural traditions, and territorial conceptions — Median, Achaemenid Persian, Parthian, Sasanian, and, after the Arab-Islamic conquest, a Persian linguistic and cultural revival within the Islamic world — even as Iranian identity itself was repeatedly transformed and reconstructed across these centuries, from the explicit territorial and religious concept of Ērānšahr articulated under the Sasanians in the third century CE, through conquest, Islamization, Turkic and Mongol rule, Safavid state formation, and modern nationalism. This textbook traces this thread from Chapters 21 through 25 in Volume II, through the Parthian and Sasanian chapters of Volume II, into the Persianate revival examined in Volume III, and onward through the Seljuk, Mongol, Safavid, Qajar, and Pahlavi periods of Volumes IV through VIII, and into the Islamic Republic examined in Volume IX. Iranian history does not disappear after the Arab conquest and reappear only with the Safavids or the modern Islamic Republic; it remains present, though repeatedly reshaped, throughout this textbook.

Treating Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran as distinct historical worlds does not mean treating them as isolated from one another or from the wider region. Persian administrative traditions shaped Abbasid government in Arab-ruled Baghdad; Byzantine Anatolia and Sasanian Iran fought exhausting wars against one another on the eve of Islam; Ottoman Anatolia and Safavid Iran later contested Iraq and the Caucasus for centuries; and Arabian trade networks connected the peninsula to Sasanian Iraq, Byzantine Syria, and Aksumite East Africa long before Islam's rise. The point is not isolation but genuine historical distinctiveness within a densely interconnected regional system — the central theme of this entire textbook.

Section 1.4

The Gulf, the Red Sea, and Regional Waterways

Few features have shaped the Middle East's history, strategic significance, and modern definition more decisively than its surrounding and internal waterways. The Mediterranean Sea connected the Levant, Anatolia, and Egypt to Greece, Rome, and later European maritime powers across more than three thousand years of recorded trade, warfare, and cultural exchange. The Persian Gulf — called the Arabian Gulf in some regional usage, a naming dispute this textbook notes without adjudicating — linked Mesopotamia to the Indian Ocean world from the era of Sumerian trade with Dilmun and Magan through the medieval Islamic Indian Ocean trade examined in Chapter 51, to the twentieth-century oil economy examined throughout Volume VIII. The Red Sea connected Egypt and Arabia to East Africa, South Arabia, and, via the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, to the wider Indian Ocean, while also carrying the enormous seasonal traffic of the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina across many centuries.

Narrower passages carried strategic weight far beyond their physical size. The Bosporus and Dardanelles straits, controlling passage between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, made Constantinople and later Istanbul one of history's most consistently fought-over cities, and remain central to Turkish strategic thinking today. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869 and examined in Chapter 93, transformed the relationship between the Mediterranean and Red Sea worlds and became one of the most geopolitically significant waterways on earth almost immediately upon completion. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the Strait of Hormuz, at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, and the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, at the southern end of the Red Sea, have become central to global energy security, examined in Chapters 141 and 160.

These waterways help explain why "Middle East," as a strategic term, was popularized in early twentieth-century strategic writing, especially by Alfred Thayer Mahan in 1902, out of concern for securing sea lanes to British India. They also explain why the region's modern definition has so often tracked shifting strategic priorities rather than any fixed cultural or historical boundary: a region defined around securing sea lanes to India looks different from a region defined around oil production, which looks different again from a region defined around the postwar Arab-Israeli conflict or, more recently, around counterterrorism operations. This textbook's own definition, by contrast, is organized around the deeper and more durable patterns of settlement, state formation, religious development, and cultural exchange examined throughout its nine volumes, while remaining attentive, especially from Volume VI onward, to how strategic and economic considerations have shaped outside powers' shifting maps of the region.

Section 1.5

Who Belongs to This History? Peoples, Not Only States

A history organized around "the Middle East" risks two related distortions if it is not written carefully: reducing the region's history to the history of whichever group currently holds the most political power, or treating today's map of sovereign states as though it reflected the region's population and political geography across the whole of its past. This textbook resists both distortions by organizing its narrative, wherever possible, around peoples and historical processes rather than exclusively around present-day states.

Arabs are central to this history from the emergence of Islam in the seventh century CE onward, and Arabic-speaking societies, states, and cultures receive sustained attention throughout Volumes III through IX. But Arab history before Islam is itself a rich and independent subject, examined in Chapters 34 and 35, and Arab history after Islam's rise unfolds within a region that never became exclusively or uniformly Arab, even at the height of the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates.

Persians and other Iranian peoples — including, in various periods, Medes, Parthians, and the many regional and tribal groups of the Iranian plateau — sustained one of the region's longest-running linguistic and cultural traditions, discussed in Section 1.3 above and throughout this textbook. Turks and Turkic peoples entered the political and military life of the Islamic Middle East well before the era most associated with their arrival in popular memory: Turkic soldiers and commanders became increasingly influential in Abbasid government beginning under the caliph al-Ma'mun, and especially under his successor al-Mu'tasim, who founded a new capital at Samarra in 836 CE in large part to house the growing Turkic military households central to his power, examined in Chapter 45. From the eleventh century CE, much larger Seljuk migrations and conquests transformed Iran, Iraq, and Syria and began the long transformation of Anatolia into a predominantly Turkic-speaking land, a process extended still further by later Ottoman expansion; Turkic peoples remained central to Anatolian, Levantine, Mesopotamian, and Arabian political history for most of the following millennium, examined especially in Volumes IV and V.

Jews have a documented history in this region extending back to ancient Israel and Judah, examined in Chapter 18, continuing through the Babylonian exile, Second Temple Judaism, the Jewish-Roman wars, and centuries of diaspora and continued presence across the Middle East and North Africa under Islamic and later Ottoman rule, and culminating, in the twentieth century, in the Zionist movement and the establishment of Israel, examined across Volumes VI through IX. Kurds, an Iranian-language-speaking people concentrated across the mountainous borderlands of modern Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria, appear throughout the region's medieval and modern history, examined transnationally in Chapters 52, 56, 69, 74, 103, 137, and 149, among others, without assuming that Kurdish communities in different states have always shared identical political organizations or goals.

Armenians sustained ancient and medieval kingdoms in eastern Anatolia and the Caucasus and one of Christianity's oldest national churches, and experienced genocide during World War I, examined in Chapter 111. Assyrians and other Syriac Christians, Copts, Maronites, Druze, Yazidis, Greeks, Circassians, and numerous other communities appear throughout this textbook's chapters wherever they are historically relevant, rather than being confined to a single "minorities" chapter — a commitment stated in the textbook's governing outline and honored throughout its nine volumes. Palestinians, whose modern national identity developed through the late Ottoman and Mandate periods examined in Volume VI and VII, receive independent treatment as a society with its own towns, institutions, culture, and political history, not merely as a party to conflict with Israel.

This textbook's guiding principle, then, is that the Middle East's history belongs to all of these peoples together, in their genuine historical complexity, rather than to any single group understood as the region's true or permanent owner. Chapters throughout this work will return, repeatedly, to the relationships — sometimes cooperative, sometimes competitive, sometimes violent — among these communities across more than five thousand years.

Section 1.6

How Wide a Frame? Neighboring and Connected Regions

This textbook's core geographic frame consists of Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia and Iraq, the Arabian Peninsula, Anatolia and Turkey, and Iran and the Iranian plateau, together with the Kurdish, Armenian, and other communities whose homelands span these borderlands. This core region has, across the periods this textbook covers, contained the overwhelming majority of the political, religious, economic, and military developments the following volumes examine.

But this core region has never existed in isolation, and this textbook remains attentive throughout to several connected and neighboring regions. North Africa, particularly Egypt's role as a hinge between the Nile Valley and the wider Mediterranean and African worlds, receives sustained treatment, while the Maghreb — modern Libya, Tunisia, Algeria, and Morocco — appears wherever its history intersects directly with the Middle East's, including under the early Islamic conquests, the Fatimid Caliphate, and later Ottoman rule, without this textbook attempting a comprehensive history of North Africa in its own right, a task properly belonging to other MGU History Series volumes. The Caucasus — including Armenia, Georgia, and Azerbaijan — appears throughout the textbook's treatment of the Ottoman-Safavid and Ottoman-Russian frontiers, examined particularly in Chapters 78 and 89. Central Asia and Afghanistan appear wherever Persianate, Mongol, Timurid, and later Islamic history connects directly to Iran and the wider Middle East, examined especially in Chapters 49, 55, and 66. South Asia appears at points of direct historical connection, including Ghaznavid campaigns, Indian Ocean trade, and, in the twentieth century, British imperial strategy linking Middle Eastern and South Asian territories. The Horn of Africa, and particularly Aksumite and later Ethiopian involvement in Red Sea and Arabian affairs, appears in Chapter 34 and at later points of direct connection. Europe, Russia, and, from the twentieth century, the United States become increasingly central actors from Volume VI onward, without this textbook treating them as the protagonists of Middle Eastern history — a principle stated explicitly in this work's governing outline and enforced throughout.

This textbook does not attempt to fix these neighboring regions' boundaries with the Middle East permanently or precisely, because no such fixed boundary has ever existed historically. Instead, each volume and chapter draws its geographic frame according to the historical developments actually under discussion, following genuine historical connection rather than an artificially rigid map.

Section 1.7

Reconstructing the Region's Past: Kinds of Evidence

Historians reconstruct the Middle East's past using several distinct and complementary categories of evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations, and each becoming more or less central depending on the period under study.

Archaeological evidence — excavated settlements, artifacts, skeletal remains, pollen and sediment records, and increasingly, ancient DNA — provides the primary source of information for the region's earliest periods, examined in Chapters 3 through 8, and remains indispensable throughout later periods as a check on, and complement to, textual sources. Non-textual archaeological remains — pottery, architecture, tools, bones, and similar material culture — can establish settlement patterns, technology, diet, trade contacts, and, with careful interpretation, aspects of social organization and religious practice, but on their own they generally cannot recover language, personal names, or detailed political events. Archaeological excavation frequently does recover texts as well — tablets, inscriptions, papyri, and seals found in datable strata — and when it does, this textual evidence can establish exactly the kinds of information, such as language, names, and events, that non-textual remains alone cannot.

Textual and literary evidence takes strikingly different forms across this textbook's chronological span: cuneiform tablets from Mesopotamia, Egyptian hieroglyphic and hieratic texts, the Hebrew Bible and other ancient Israelite and Judahite writings, Zoroastrian religious literature, Greek and Roman historians describing the region from the outside, the Qur'an and early Islamic historical writing, Persian and Arabic chronicles, Ottoman administrative and legal documents, and, for the modern period, an enormous and rapidly expanding documentary, journalistic, and digital record. Each of these textual traditions carries its own conventions, purposes, and biases, and historians must read each according to the circumstances of its composition rather than treating any single text as a transparent window onto the past.

Epigraphic evidence — inscriptions on stone, clay, metal, and monuments — offers some of the most valuable contemporary documentation available to historians of the ancient and medieval Middle East, because a well-preserved inscription can preserve wording composed at or near the time of the events it describes. This evidence has real limits, however: many inscriptions are undated or only approximately datable through style, findspot, or associated material; some were deliberately reinscribed, defaced, reused as building material, or otherwise physically altered after their original carving; and royal and monumental inscriptions in particular were often composed as ideologically selective statements of a ruler's preferred version of events rather than neutral records. Royal inscriptions from Assyrian and Achaemenid kings, Nabataean and South Arabian inscriptions, and the vast corpus of Islamic-era foundation and endowment inscriptions all provide valuable evidence of political ideology, administrative practice, and religious patronage, though such evidence typically represents the perspective of rulers and elites rather than ordinary people, and must be read with these limitations in mind.

Numismatic evidence — coins and their inscriptions, imagery, metal content, and find-spots — allows historians to trace political authority, trade networks, and economic conditions across periods and regions with limited surviving textual documentation, from the post-Achaemenid successor states through the early Islamic caliphates and beyond.

For later periods, this evidentiary base expands enormously: Ottoman court and provincial archives, European diplomatic and consular records, colonial administrative files, oral history, photography, film, and, for the most recent decades examined in Volume IX, government publications, satellite imagery, digital communications, and a vast contemporary journalistic and human-rights documentation record. Each category of evidence answers some historical questions better than others, and responsible historical writing requires combining multiple kinds of evidence while remaining honest, throughout every chapter of this textbook, about what each source can and cannot establish.

Section 1.8

Anachronism and the Danger of Backward Projection

The single most important methodological caution this chapter can offer concerns anachronism — the error of applying a later concept, term, border, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply. This danger is especially acute for a history of the Middle East, where modern political, national, and sectarian categories are often projected backward onto ancient and medieval societies in ways that badly distort the historical record.

Modern national labels present one obvious risk. Describing the Achaemenid Persian Empire as "ancient Iran" in the sense of the modern nation-state, or describing the Ottoman Empire as simply "early Turkey," or describing the medieval Mamluk Sultanate as "medieval Egypt" in a modern political sense, would each misrepresent political realities that recognized no such state. The modern states of Egypt, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel, Turkey, Iran, and the Arabian Peninsula states are, on the timescale of this textbook, extremely recent — most taking their current form only in the twentieth century, several only as recently as the second half of that century — and applying their borders retroactively onto ancient or medieval history would misrepresent how the people of those periods actually understood their own political geography, a principle this textbook applies consistently from Chapter 2 onward.

Religious and sectarian categories present an equally serious risk. Describing every pre-Islamic Arabian society as though it anticipated Islam, describing ancient Israelite religion as though it were identical to later rabbinic Judaism, or describing medieval Sunni-Shia relations as though they mirrored twenty-first-century sectarian conflict, would each project later categories backward inappropriately. This textbook's governing outline states explicitly that sectarianism must never be used as a self-explanatory cause of conflict; political institutions, war, state policy, economic interests, inequality, foreign intervention, and political mobilization must instead be examined as the concrete historical forces that shaped sectarian identity and conflict in any given period, a principle developed at length in later chapters on the Sunni-Shia split, the Safavid-Ottoman rivalry, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century sectarian violence.

Ethnic and linguistic categories require similar caution. This textbook does not assume that "Arab," "Persian," "Turk," or "Kurd" denoted the same thing in the seventh century CE that they denote today, nor does it assume that modern ethnic or national communities extend unbroken and unchanged from antiquity to the present. At the same time, avoiding anachronism does not mean avoiding discussion of ethnicity, religion, or identity altogether; the goal, as this textbook's governing principles state, is precision rather than evasion — describing historical identities as they were actually organized and understood in their own time, while explaining clearly, wherever relevant, how those identities changed, fragmented, merged, or gave rise to later categories still in use today.

Section 1.9

Periodization in a Nine-Volume History

This textbook organizes its 162 chapters into nine volumes, each corresponding to a major stage in the region's political, religious, economic, and institutional development: landscapes, prehistory, and the first civilizations; the empires, peoples, and religions of the ancient world; Islam and the caliphal age; the medieval world of Turks, Crusaders, Mongols, and regional states; the Ottoman and Safavid early modern world; nineteenth-century reform and imperialism; World War I, imperial collapse, and the mandate system; Israel, Palestine, Arab nationalism, oil, and the Cold War; and revolution, political Islam, and the contemporary Middle East through 2026.

Like all periodization, discussed in the vocabulary box below, this nine-volume structure is an interpretive tool rather than a neutral or self-evident feature of the past itself. Other historians have periodized Middle Eastern history differently — for example, by organizing the whole narrative around the rise and fall of the Islamic caliphate, or around a simple story of Western intervention and regional response, or around the modern nation-state as the natural endpoint toward which all earlier history was heading. This textbook's governing outline explicitly rejects several such simplifications: it insists that ancient history receives full treatment rather than serving merely as background to Islam; that 1914, 1948, 1979, 2001, and 2011 must not become artificial starting points for "modern" regional problems whose deeper roots lie further back; and that the textbook must continue through 2026 while explicitly distinguishing events still unfolding from developments whose historical outcomes are already established.

This chapter's task has been to establish the geographic, terminological, and evidentiary foundation on which this periodization depends. Readers should understand, by this chapter's end, that "the Middle East" is this textbook's organizing modern frame, not a timeless identity; that older and more historically precise regional terms remain indispensable throughout; that Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran each possess long, partly independent historical trajectories; that the region's history belongs to many peoples rather than to any single group; that the region's geographic boundaries have always been somewhat fluid and contested; that historians must use different kinds of evidence, understood on their own terms, to reconstruct different periods; and that avoiding anachronism is a standing methodological discipline, not a one-time warning confined to this opening chapter.

Primary Source Window

Alfred Thayer Mahan, "The Persian Gulf and International Relations," National Review, 1902

Creator: Alfred Thayer Mahan, American naval officer, historian, and strategist · Date: published 1902 · Type: Strategic essay on British naval policy, published in a British periodical for a policy-oriented readership

Context: Mahan, already internationally influential for his writing on the historical importance of naval power, wrote this essay to argue that Britain needed to maintain naval superiority around the Persian Gulf in order to protect its position in India and counter growing Russian and German influence in the region. In making this argument, he used the phrase "Middle East" to denote the area between Arabia and India, distinguishing it from the "Near East" nearer to Europe.

Paraphrase: Mahan argued that the area he called the Middle East, centered on the Persian Gulf, held strategic importance for British imperial security comparable to other maritime chokepoints he had studied throughout naval history, and that failing to secure British naval predominance there risked ceding influence to rival European powers.

Analysis: This essay is valuable to students of this chapter because it demonstrates, in a single influential document, how "Middle East" originated as a strategic-geographic term serving specifically British and, by extension, Western imperial interests, organized around securing sea lanes rather than around any feature of the region's own history, religion, or self-understanding. Mahan's essay also illustrates how quickly a single influential writer's terminology could spread through diplomatic and journalistic usage in an era of expanding print media and imperial competition.

Historical Debate

Does the Middle East Have Real Boundaries?

The question: Is "the Middle East" a coherent historical region with meaningful, if permeable, boundaries, or is it primarily a projection of outside strategic and political interests onto a landmass that never possessed a single unifying identity? Historians, geographers, and the region's own scholars continue to disagree.

Interpretation A — The Middle East is a historically coherent region: Some historians argue that despite the modernity of the specific term, the lands it denotes share genuinely deep and long-standing historical connections — overlapping trade networks stretching back to the Bronze Age, recurring patterns of imperial rule linking Mesopotamia, the Levant, Egypt, and Anatolia, the interlocking and competing Hellenistic, Roman/Byzantine, and Iranian (Parthian and Sasanian) imperial systems that repeatedly fought over and divided the region between rival spheres of control, and, from the seventh century CE onward, a shared (though never uniform) experience of Islamic religious, legal, and administrative culture. On this view, "Middle East" gives a modern name to connections that were already historically real long before the term existed.
Interpretation B — The Middle East is primarily a construct of outside strategic interest: Other historians argue that treating the region as a natural historical unit risks obscuring how much of its apparent coherence was produced retroactively by European and American strategic thinking, colonial administration, and mid-twentieth-century academic area-studies programs, which grouped together societies — Moroccan, Egyptian, Iraqi, Iranian, Turkish — with substantially different histories, languages, and political trajectories for reasons of external convenience rather than internal historical logic. On this view, the term's shifting and contested boundaries across the twentieth century are themselves evidence that no natural regional boundary exists.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying historical record — the same trade networks, the same imperial systems, the same twentieth-century administrative and academic history — but differ in how much genuine internal coherence to attribute to the region as opposed to externally imposed coherence.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate practically rather than doctrinally: it adopts "Middle East" as an organizing modern frame while insisting, throughout its nine volumes, on the deep pre-modern connections (trade, empire, religion, migration) that make the frame historically defensible, and while remaining explicit, as in this chapter, about the term's modern origin and contested boundaries.

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which you find more persuasive and why. Consider what is gained and what is lost by each position.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Names and Bounds "the Middle East"? Perspectives on Regional Definition

From the perspective of a Kurdish, Armenian, or Assyrian community member: Regional definitions organized around the era's dominant states and languages can obscure the history of peoples whose homelands span several modern national borders and who have often stood at the margins of both regional and national historical narratives dominated by the majority populations of those states.
Continuity and Change Over Time

A Region under Many Names

What Changed

  • The dominant English-language name for this region shifted from largely unnamed classical description, to "Near East" and "the Orient," to "Middle East" over roughly two and a half millennia
  • Political control over Mesopotamia, the Levant, Egypt, Anatolia, Arabia, and Iran passed among many different states and empires across five thousand years
  • The geographic boundaries attributed to "the Middle East" have expanded and contracted repeatedly since the term's coining in 1902, according to shifting strategic priorities
  • Modern national borders divided river systems, mountain ranges, and cultural regions that had not previously been politically separated in the same way

What Stayed the Same

  • The Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, and Jordan river systems have consistently anchored dense settlement and political power in their respective valleys
  • The Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Persian Gulf have consistently connected this region to wider Afro-Eurasian trade and migration networks
  • Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran have each sustained distinct regional identities across every political system this textbook examines
  • Outside powers have repeatedly found this region strategically significant enough to name, map, and intervene in, across more than two thousand years of recorded history

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating "the Middle East" as an eternal and self-evident identity, or dismissing the region's real historical connections merely because its modern name is recent.

Cause and Effect

Why "Middle East" Displaced Older Terms

Long-term causes

Centuries of European commercial, diplomatic, and later colonial engagement with the Ottoman, Persian, and Arabian worlds produced an accumulating vocabulary — Levant, Near East, Orient — reflecting Europe's own shifting vantage point on the region.

Short-term causes

Early twentieth-century naval strategy, World War I mandate planning, and World War II military administration each required a workable term for the area between the eastern Mediterranean and India, and "Middle East" filled that administrative need.

Effect

"Middle East" became the dominant English-language term by the mid-twentieth century, was subsequently adopted and adapted into Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and Hebrew usage, and now organizes academic programs, media coverage, and diplomatic practice worldwide.

This textbook's response

This textbook adopts "Middle East" as its organizing modern frame while using older, more historically precise regional terms throughout, and while explaining, as in this chapter, the term's own historical origin rather than treating it as neutral or timeless.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the term's spread; strategic, administrative, academic, and media developments reinforced one another over several decades, a pattern this textbook will return to when tracing how other regional concepts and identities have formed and spread across later chapters.

Everyday Life and Social History

Why Naming and Region Belong in Everyday Life

Regional names were not abstractions to the people who lived within the lands they described; they shaped how merchants identified their trading partners, how pilgrims described their journeys, how officials organized taxation and administration, and, in the modern era, how ordinary people understand their own citizenship, identity, and place in the world. A Damascene merchant writing to a Cairene trading partner in the fourteenth century would have used regional vocabulary entirely different from the terms a Beiruti journalist or an Istanbul-based diplomat would use to describe the same geography six centuries later.

Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to society and everyday life examine how ordinary agricultural, pastoral, artisanal, mercantile, and, in the modern era, urban and professional communities across this region's many environments experienced village and city life, markets, taxation, religious practice, migration, and changing technology and administration. Because most surviving pre-modern written sources were produced by a literate religious, administrative, or commercial elite, historians must read these sources carefully for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own — peasants, pastoralists, artisans, laborers, women, enslaved people, and religious and ethnic minorities — while also drawing on archaeology, material culture, and, for later periods, administrative, statistical, and journalistic records to reconstruct their experiences more fully.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced "the Middle East" as this textbook's organizing geographic and historical frame while establishing, from the outset, that the term is a modern, externally coined convenience rather than an ancient or self-evident identity. The phrase entered widespread use only around the turn of the twentieth century, popularized by the American naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan in a 1902 essay concerned with British imperial strategy around the Persian Gulf, and it spread through subsequent decades of diplomatic, military, and journalistic usage before being substantially adopted and adapted, in the second half of the twentieth century, into Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and Hebrew usage by the region's own governments, media, and scholars.

The chapter has situated "Middle East" alongside older and, for many periods, more historically appropriate regional terms: "Near East," which became less common than "Middle East" in general public usage over the twentieth century while remaining established in several academic and institutional contexts; "the Levant," describing the eastern Mediterranean coastal lands; "Mesopotamia," the ancient Greek term for the lands between the Tigris and Euphrates; and "the Fertile Crescent," a twentieth-century coinage describing the agriculturally favorable arc running from the eastern Mediterranean through northern Mesopotamia toward Babylonia and the Persian Gulf. This textbook uses "Middle East" as its overall organizing frame while continuing to rely on these older, more precise terms throughout, particularly for ancient, medieval, and early modern history.

Students have been introduced to Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran as major historical regions possessing their own long-term trajectories rather than functioning merely as peripheries of an Arab or Ottoman-Turkish center — a commitment this textbook will honor across its nine volumes, tracing Arabian history from its pre-Islamic South Arabian kingdoms through the rise of Islam, Anatolian history from the Hittites through Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Armenian societies to the arrival of the Turks, and Iranian history as a persistent major historical strand from the Medes and Achaemenid Persians through the Islamic Republic of the twenty-first century.

The chapter has also introduced the region's defining waterways — the Mediterranean, the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, the Bosporus and Dardanelles, and the Suez Canal, opened in 1869 — explaining both their deep historical significance for trade, migration, and religious pilgrimage and their outsized role in shaping outside powers' shifting strategic definitions of "the Middle East" across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

Central to this chapter has been the insistence that the region's history belongs to many peoples together: Arabs, Persians and other Iranian peoples, Turks, Kurds, Jews, Armenians, Assyrians and other Syriac Christians, Copts, Palestinians, Druze, Yazidis, Greeks, Circassians, and numerous other communities, each of whom receives sustained, historically specific treatment throughout this textbook's later volumes rather than being subordinated to any single group's narrative or confined to a solitary chapter on "minorities."

The chapter established the evidentiary foundations on which the rest of this textbook depends: archaeological, textual, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence, each with distinct strengths and limitations, expanding over time into the vast documentary record of the Ottoman, colonial, and contemporary periods. It gave particular attention to the danger of anachronism — the error of projecting modern national borders, sectarian categories, or ethnic identities backward onto periods that did not recognize them — establishing this as a standing methodological discipline that will recur throughout the textbook's treatment of ancient empires, the rise of Islam, the medieval Sunni-Shia division, Ottoman and Safavid rule, European imperialism, and the modern state system.

Finally, the chapter explained the nine-volume architecture that organizes this entire textbook, clarifying that this structure reflects genuine historical turning points — from the first cities of ancient Mesopotamia through the rise and fall of successive empires, the emergence and spread of Islam, medieval Turkic and Mongol transformations, Ottoman-Safavid early modernity, nineteenth-century reform and European imperialism, the collapse of the Ottoman order after World War I, the establishment of Israel and the Cold War regional order, and the ongoing transformations of the post-1979, post-2001, and post-2011 Middle East through 2026 — rather than an arbitrary or externally convenient division.

The Historical Debate feature on whether the Middle East possesses genuine historical boundaries illustrated this challenge concretely: how much coherence should historians attribute to a region whose modern name and borders were substantially shaped by outside strategic interest, as against the deep, long-standing, and historically documented connections — commercial, religious, political, and migratory — that link the lands and peoples this textbook studies across more than five thousand years?

These definitions, distinctions, and commitments are not abstract housekeeping. They are the interpretive foundation on which the following one hundred sixty-one chapters are built, and they will recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of ancient civilizations, classical empires, the rise of Islam, medieval sultanates and khanates, the Ottoman and Safavid empires, European colonialism, nationalism, oil, the Arab-Israeli conflict, political Islam, and the unresolved geopolitics of the twenty-first-century Middle East.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Middle East
A modern geographic and geopolitical term, popularized chiefly after 1902, denoting the region encompassing Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran; this textbook's primary organizing frame, adopted with explicit attention to its recent and contested origins.
Near East
An older term, dominant in nineteenth-century European diplomacy, describing the Ottoman lands nearest Europe; narrowed in most later academic usage to denote the ancient pre-classical civilizations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant.
Levant
The eastern Mediterranean coastal lands, roughly modern Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel, and the Palestinian territories; a term derived from French and Italian words for "the rising" of the sun in the east.
Mesopotamia
Greek-derived term meaning "between the rivers," denoting the lands watered by the Tigris and Euphrates, roughly modern Iraq and parts of eastern Syria.
Fertile Crescent
A term coined by James Henry Breasted in the early twentieth century for the agriculturally favorable arc running from the eastern Mediterranean through northern Mesopotamia toward Babylonia and the Persian Gulf; later definitions vary and sometimes include southeastern Anatolia or Egypt.
Orient / Orientalism
An older, now largely retired European term for a broad eastern zone including the Middle East. "Orientalism" carries two related meanings: an older sense referring to Western scholarly study of Asian and Middle Eastern languages, texts, and history, and, following Edward Said's 1978 book of that title, a critical framework analyzing how Western representations of "the Orient" often generalized or stereotyped the region in ways bound up with colonial power.
Anatolia
The peninsula comprising most of modern Turkey, home in succession to Hittite, Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Armenian, and, from the eleventh century CE, increasingly Turkic societies.
Arabia
The peninsula bounded by the Red Sea, Arabian Sea, and Persian Gulf, home to pre-Islamic South Arabian kingdoms and caravan cities before becoming, with the rise of Islam, the point of origin for the Arab-Islamic conquests.
Iranian plateau
The high plateau and surrounding mountains extending from Mesopotamia to Central Asia and South Asia, home to long continuities of Iranian languages and cultural tradition from the Medes to the present, repeatedly transformed over time.
Persianate
Describing cultural, administrative, and literary traditions rooted in the Persian language and Iranian court culture that spread across much of the medieval and early modern Islamic world, independent of any single ethnic or political boundary.
Periodization
The historian's practice of dividing the past into named periods for organizational and analytical purposes; an interpretive tool rather than a neutral feature of the past itself.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, border, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Epigraphy
The study of inscriptions carved on stone, clay, metal, or monuments; a major source of contemporary evidence for ancient and medieval Middle Eastern history.
Numismatics
The study of coins and their inscriptions, imagery, and metal content; valuable for tracing political authority and trade, especially where texts are scarce.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of Middle Eastern history have changed over time and why.
Primary source
A document, object, or record created at or near the time being studied, by a participant or contemporary witness.
Secondary source
A later interpretation of the past, typically by a historian, drawing on primary sources.
Cuneiform
The wedge-shaped writing system developed in Mesopotamia, among the earliest writing systems in the world, examined further in Chapter 4.
Hieroglyphics
The ancient Egyptian writing system combining logographic and phonetic signs, examined further in Chapter 13.
Transregional connection
Historical linkages — commercial, religious, political, or migratory — that cross the boundaries of a single region, connecting the Middle East to North Africa, the Caucasus, Central Asia, South Asia, and Europe.
Regional diversity
The recognition that the Middle East varies significantly by sub-region, language, and religious community rather than forming a single uniform society, economy, or culture.
Mandate system
The post-World War I arrangement under which the League of Nations authorized British and French administration of former Ottoman territories, examined in Volume VII.
Strategic chokepoint
A narrow passage of outsized geopolitical importance relative to its physical size, such as the Bosporus, the Dardanelles, the Suez Canal, the Strait of Hormuz, or the Bab-el-Mandeb strait.
Sectarianism
Division or conflict organized around religious sectarian identity; this textbook treats sectarianism as a historical outcome shaped by political, economic, and social forces rather than as a self-explanatory cause of conflict.
Alfred Thayer Mahan
American naval strategist whose 1902 essay on British naval policy around the Persian Gulf is credited with popularizing the modern English term "Middle East," discussed in the Primary Source Window above.
Kurdish transnationalism
The historical condition of Kurdish communities living across the modern borders of Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria, examined without assuming shared organization or identical political goals across states.
Continuity and change
The historical thinking skill of identifying what persists and what transforms over a given period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire nine-volume textbook. The habits of mind it introduces — distinguishing "the Middle East" from older and often more precise regional terms, treating Arabia, Anatolia, and Iran as historical worlds in their own right, recognizing that this region's history belongs to many peoples rather than to any single group, reading evidence carefully according to its kind and its distance from the events it describes, and refusing to project modern national borders and sectarian categories backward onto periods that did not recognize them — are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

The Middle East's history rests on one of the world's longest and richest archaeological and textual records, and it is among the most environmentally varied and most linguistically, religiously, and ethnically diverse historical subjects any textbook can undertake. It has included some of the world's earliest cities and writing systems, some of history's largest pre-modern empires, the birth of three major world religious traditions, centuries of Islamic imperial civilization, one of history's most consequential early modern imperial rivalries between the Ottomans and Safavids, two centuries of European imperial intervention, a world war that destroyed a centuries-old imperial order, the establishment of new nation-states and one of the twentieth century's most enduring conflicts, and, in the contemporary era, the ongoing negotiation of political Islam, oil wealth, regional rivalry, and unresolved questions of sovereignty, security, and peace. Understanding this diversity is not a challenge to the region's historical significance — it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than flattened into a simplified or politically convenient story.

Students who understand that "the Middle East" is a modern, externally coined geographic frame rather than a timeless identity, that evidence must be read according to its kind and its distance from the events it describes, and that the region's many peoples, languages, religions, and states are integral rather than peripheral to its history, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook — from the ancient civilizations of Volume I through the empires and religions of Volume II, the Islamic caliphal age of Volume III, the medieval world of Turks, Crusaders, Mongols, and regional states of Volume IV, Ottoman-Safavid early modernity of Volume V, nineteenth-century reform and imperialism of Volume VI, World War I and the mandate system of Volume VII, Israel, Palestine, Arab nationalism, oil, and the Cold War of Volume VIII, and the revolution, political Islam, and contemporary geopolitics of Volume IX — with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians bring to the subject.

The story this textbook tells extends from the deep prehistoric and early agricultural past of Chapters 2 through 8 through the unresolved questions of the contemporary Middle East examined in Chapter 162. It is a story of many regions, many peoples, and many religious and linguistic communities, joined by shared geography, long-standing connection, and a continuous, often contested, historical experience — not a single unbroken thread and not a predetermined national or civilizational outcome. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, diversity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
