Part VIII — Rome, Sasanian Iran, Christianity, and Late Antiquity
31

Rome, Judea, and the Jewish-Roman Wars

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Among the Judean Desert caves above the Dead Sea, archaeologists have recovered letters sent by a commander in the 130s CE who gave orders about supplies and discipline. They are among the very few documents in this region written by participants in a war with Rome rather than by elite observers. Their existence reminds us that the history of Roman Judea was lived by farmers, officials, priests, soldiers, and rebels, not only by emperors.

Chapter 30 traced the establishment of Rome's eastern frontier system, in which client kings, provincial governors, and legions managed a boundary with Parthia. This chapter asks what that system meant on the ground in one small and religiously charged territory. Judea was small, but its location between Syria and Egypt and the importance of Jerusalem to Jews across the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia gave it unusual weight.

Judea passed through three stages of Roman relationship. After Pompey's intervention in 63 BCE it became a reduced successor to the Hasmonean state, then under Herod a client kingdom, and from 6 CE largely a province governed by Roman officials, with a brief interlude of Agrippa I. These stages involved different mixes of Roman power, local elites, and institutions, and they should not be collapsed into a single story of occupation.

Twice, Judea rose in major revolt. The first war, 66–70 (with fighting ending at Masada in 73 or 74), ended with the burning of the Second Temple. The second, 132–135, ended with devastation in Judea and the completion of Roman Jerusalem as Aelia Capitolina, a project begun earlier. Between these wars lay decades in which Jewish communities adapted to new conditions, and after them a century of institutional change.

The argument is that Roman rule did not produce a simple, continuous struggle between Rome and a unified Jewish population. Judea was governed through changing combinations of client kings, Roman officials, priestly elites, municipal authorities, and local communities, while Jewish society remained politically, socially, and religiously diverse. The revolts were not inevitable, and the defeats did not end Jewish life but transformed its institutions, geography, and religious authority.

The evidence is uneven. For the first war we depend heavily on Josephus, an interested participant. For the second we have almost no continuous narrative but remarkable documents and coins. The chapter uses each kind of evidence for what it can show.

The chapter treats Christianity only where it bears on Judean conditions, since Chapter 33 examines its spread, and it stops at the Roman side of the frontier, since Chapter 32 turns to Sasanian Iran.

Historical Context

The Hasmonean state described in Chapter 29 had expanded across much of Palestine, but dynastic civil war between Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II invited Roman arbitration. Chapter 30 treated Pompey's eastern settlement regionally. For Judea the consequence was that Jewish politics now operated inside a Roman imperial system whose priorities were security, tribute, and stable client relations.

That system included both provinces governed directly by Roman officials and client kingdoms ruled by local kings under Roman supervision. Judea moved between these categories across the period, and the difference mattered: a client king collected taxes and kept order himself, while a province required Roman officials and, in an emergency, Roman legions from neighboring Syria.

Judea's geography and society shaped its politics. The territory included Jerusalem and the hills of Judea, Galilee to the north, and coastal plains, and it bordered Samaria, Idumea, and the Nabataean kingdom. Jerusalem was the religious center, while Caesarea on the coast became the administrative seat. Hellenized cities such as Caesarea and Sebaste had mixed populations, and Samaritans, Idumeans, Greeks, and Jews often had tense relations.

Beyond Judea lived large Jewish communities in Egypt, Syria, Anatolia, Babylonia, and Rome. These diaspora communities were connected to Jerusalem by pilgrimage and the Temple contribution, but they also had their own institutions, and they matter to the story long before the wars begin.

Our evidence changes across the period. For the first century BCE and the first century CE the central narrative is Josephus, supplemented by Roman historians, coins, inscriptions, and archaeology; the New Testament offers some incidental evidence for Judean conditions. Rabbinic texts were compiled generations later and reflect later concerns, so they must be used carefully for earlier events. For the second war the narrative sources are thin and the documentary evidence from the Judean Desert and coins carries unusual weight.

Section 31.1

Rome and the End of Hasmonean Independence

Chapter 29 followed the Hasmonean state to the point of Rome's arrival. After the death of Salome Alexandra in 67 BCE, her sons Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II fought for the throne. Hyrcanus, supported by the Idumean official Antipater and by the Nabataean king Aretas III, was besieged by Aristobulus's forces, and both brothers eventually appealed to Pompey, who was reorganizing Rome's eastern holdings (Chapter 30).

In 63 BCE Pompey, after hearing both sides, favored Hyrcanus. Aristobulus's supporters held the Temple Mount, and Pompey took it after a siege of about three months. Josephus reports that Pompey entered the sanctuary but did not plunder it, an act many Jews nonetheless remembered as a profanation. Aristobulus was taken to Rome as a captive.

The settlement reduced Judea sharply. Coastal and inland cities were detached from Jewish rule, Samaria and Decapolis cities came under direct Roman oversight, and the remaining territory paid tribute. Hyrcanus was confirmed as high priest and ethnarch but not king. The title meant that he led the community without royal status, and that his authority was now subordinate to Rome and to the governor of Syria.

Judea was not annexed as a province in 63. Rome often preferred dependency without provincial administration, which kept costs low and let local elites do the governing. The distinction explains why Judea could remain a distinct political entity, with its own high priest and traditions, while no longer independent.

Antipater's role grew in this system. He supported Roman commanders with supplies and troops, and when Caesar defeated Pompey, Antipater aided Caesar in Egypt. In 47 BCE Caesar confirmed Hyrcanus as ethnarch and high priest, gave Antipater Roman citizenship and a fiscal role, and allowed the rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls. These measures show how Roman favor could elevate a local family.

The arrangement was unstable. Rome's civil wars brought repeated shifts of patronage, and Antipater's sons, including Herod, held posts in Galilee and Jerusalem. In 40 BCE a Parthian invasion installed Antigonus, a Hasmonean rival, and captured Hyrcanus, and Herod fled to Rome. This crisis opened the way to Herodian client kingship.

Section 31.2

Herod and Roman Client Kingship

Herod was the son of Antipater, the Idumean official who had supported Hyrcanus. The Idumeans had been absorbed into the Hasmonean state, and Josephus treats Herod's family as Jewish, though many Judeans regarded the Idumean origin with suspicion. This legitimacy problem shaped his rule. Herod served as governor of Galilee in the 40s BCE and gained Roman favor through suppressing banditry and delivering tribute.

In 40 BCE, after fleeing the Parthian-backed Antigonus, Herod went to Rome, where the Senate, with the support of Mark Antony and Octavian, named him king of Judea. He had to conquer the kingdom, and in 37 BCE he took Jerusalem with Roman troops under Sosius. He married the Hasmonean Mariamme to strengthen his claim. After Jerusalem fell, Sosius took Antigonus prisoner, and Mark Antony later had him executed, reportedly at Herod's urging, ending Hasmonean royal rule.

Herod's survival depended on Roman patronage. After Antony's defeat at Actium in 31 BCE he secured Octavian's confirmation, and Augustus later added territories east and north of the Jordan. In return Herod supplied military aid, political loyalty, diplomatic cooperation, and stability on a sensitive frontier, and he supported Roman operations such as the Arabian expedition of 26 BCE. A later quarrel with the Nabataeans strained his relationship with Augustus, showing that a client's freedom of action was bounded.

Client kingship had mechanics. The king raised revenue, maintained a military force, and managed relations with neighboring rulers, while Rome expected loyalty, military or diplomatic support, and deference on questions of war and succession. Herod appointed high priests from outside the Hasmonean line, including a Babylonian Jew, which weakened a potential rival power base and signaled his control over the sacred institutions.

He relied on mercenaries, fortresses, informers, and loyalty oaths. His court politics were violent: Mariamme was executed in 29 BCE, and several sons followed in later years, according to Josephus. Josephus drew on the court historian Nicolaus of Damascus, who wrote for Herod, so the portrait mixes official admiration with later hostility and must be read critically.

Herod was therefore neither a Roman puppet nor an independent Jewish monarch. He had room to pursue his own projects, and his policies reflected his priorities, but Roman backing set the limits and many Jews questioned his legitimacy. His death in 4 BCE exposed how dependent the kingdom was on one ruler's skill.

Section 31.3

Herodian Building, Economy, and the Temple

Herod's most famous project was the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple, begun around 20/19 BCE. He expanded the Temple Mount into an enormous platform, supported by massive retaining walls, and rebuilt the sanctuary itself on a larger scale. Josephus says that priests were trained as builders so that the sanctuary could be constructed without profanation. Much of the work continued after Herod's death, and Josephus says it was completed only in the 60s CE, shortly before the revolt.

The project required large amounts of labor, stone, and money. Construction employed many workers, and when it ended, Josephus says, many were left without work, an indication of how important building had become to the economy. The scale of the platform and the surviving retaining walls show the ambition of the enterprise.

Herod also built extensively beyond Jerusalem. Caesarea Maritima, with its large artificial harbor, was dedicated about 10/9 BCE and became the Roman administrative center and a major port linking Judea to Mediterranean trade. Samaria was rebuilt as Sebaste, and fortress-palaces such as Masada, Herodium, and the Jericho complex combined security with luxury. These sites show how construction served both defense and display.

The projects were funded by taxation, royal estates, and trade revenue, including balsam, dates, and Dead Sea products. They created employment but also imposed burdens, and Josephus reports that Herod reduced taxes and imported grain during a famine around 25 BCE. Pilgrimage added to Jerusalem's economy, and the harbor integrated Judea more deeply into the Roman eastern economy.

Herod built or supported temples to Augustus and civic buildings in non-Jewish cities, while in Judea his coins mostly avoided human images. Jewish piety and Greco-Roman monumentality were not contradictory in his politics: the Temple secured religious prestige among many Jews, while Caesarea and imperial temples tied him to Rome. Both expressed a king who needed to appeal to two audiences.

Not all Jews accepted this combination. Josephus reports that near the end of Herod's life, students tore down a golden eagle placed over a Temple gate, and the king punished them severely. The episode suggests that Herodian building could provoke objections about imperial symbols, a theme that returns in later conflicts.

Section 31.4

Jewish Society under Herod

Jewish society under Herod was socially layered. At the top stood a priestly and landed aristocracy centered in Jerusalem, including high priestly families whose fortunes depended on both the Temple and Roman or Herodian favor. Below them were landowners, tenants, peasants, artisans, builders, and traders, and a population of pilgrims and workers swelled the city at festivals.

The Temple was a major economic and social institution. Priests served in rotating courses, sacrifices and offerings required animals, grain, and oil, and the Temple contribution brought funds from across the diaspora. Its construction and maintenance employed many workers, and the city's trade depended in part on pilgrimage.

Rural life was different. Galilean and Judean villages farmed grain, olives, and vines, with fishing around the Sea of Galilee, and managed affairs through local elders and assemblies. Antipas's rebuilding of Sepphoris and foundation of Tiberias brought administration and urban demand to Galilee. Tenancy, debt, and the burden of dues weighed on many families.

Chapter 29 introduced the major religious currents, and the same caution applies here. Josephus and later rabbinic sources describe Pharisees as bearers of ancestral traditions of interpretation, but both are later and partly interested witnesses. Sadducean teaching must be reconstructed almost entirely through outsiders and opponents, since no Sadducean writings survive. Essenes are described by Josephus, Philo, and Pliny, and many scholars link a related movement to the Qumran library, though the identification is debated. Other teachers, prophets, and popular leaders also appear. These were currents with fluid boundaries, not fixed denominations.

The archaeological evidence is valuable but limited. Ritual baths, stone vessels, and ossuaries, along with inscriptions and settlement remains, indicate practices of purity and burial among some households, especially in and near Jerusalem. They do not tell us everything about belief, or about the many poorer rural families whose lives left few traces. Historians therefore describe diversity without assuming that any single group spoke for Jewish society.

Households and gender also shaped society, though evidence for Herod's reign is limited. Later Roman-period evidence helps illuminate possibilities that are poorly documented for that time. The Babatha archive, whose surviving documents date from 94 to 132 CE and come from the southern Dead Sea region, shows a Jewish woman owning and managing property and pursuing legal disputes under Roman provincial institutions. Because these documents postdate Herod by more than a century, they cannot simply be projected backward, but they demonstrate the kinds of legal and economic activity available to some Jewish women in the broader Roman-era society. Women took part in Temple worship and pilgrimage, with access to the more sacred zones graded for different categories of people, and they worked in agriculture, textile production, and trade. Most evidence for women comes through male authors or legal documents, and ordinary women's views are largely lost.

Everyday Life and Social History

Jerusalem, a Galilean Village, and a Diaspora Community

A Jerusalem household before 70: Life in the city depended in part on the Temple and its festivals. Families of priests, merchants, builders, and servants supplied pilgrims with lodging, food, and animals for sacrifice, and the city's workshops produced stone vessels and other goods tied to purity practice. Wealthier houses in the upper city had mosaics and ritual baths, while poorer families lived in crowded quarters. The Temple's construction and maintenance employed many workers.

A Galilean village: Families farmed grain, olives, and vines, tended small flocks, and fished the lake, paying rents, tolls, and dues in kind or coin. The village managed disputes through elders and gathered for Scripture reading and communal meals. Pottery, coins, oil presses, and house remains show a modest but connected economy that traded with towns such as Sepphoris and Tiberias.

A diaspora community: In a city such as Alexandria or Rome, a Jewish household might speak Greek, work in trades or commerce, and belong to a synagogue or association. It paid the annual Temple contribution, observed festivals and food customs, and negotiated its standing with civic and imperial authorities. Inscriptions and papyri show variety of status and wealth.

Evidence includes ossuaries, stone vessels, ritual baths, papyri, and inscriptions, which favor the better-off, so historians must read them cautiously.

Section 31.5

From Client Kingdom to Province

Herod's death in 4 BCE led Augustus to divide his kingdom. Archelaus received Judea, Samaria, and Idumea with the lesser title of ethnarch, Antipas became tetrarch of Galilee and Perea, and Philip received lands in the northeast. Archelaus's rule provoked complaints from Jewish and Samaritan leaders, and Augustus removed him in 6 CE.

Judea came under direct Roman administration by an equestrian prefect, later styled procurator, who resided mainly at Caesarea and traveled to Jerusalem for festivals. The precise constitutional relationship with Syria is debated, but the Syrian legate commanded the nearest legions and could intervene in major crises. Pontius Pilate, known from Josephus and from an inscription at Caesarea, held the office from 26 to 36.

A census conducted under the Syrian legate Quirinius established the assessment for direct Roman taxation and sparked unrest, according to Josephus. Direct taxes were transmitted to Rome under the supervision of local elite councils, while indirect levies such as tolls and customs were often collected by tax-farmers and their agents. The census therefore made direct Roman government more visible and became a flashpoint for opposition to submission to Rome.

Caesarea and Jerusalem played different roles. Caesarea, a largely non-Jewish port city with a Herodian harbor, was the administrative capital and military base, while Jerusalem remained the religious center where the high priest and the Temple authorities operated. The separation meant that Roman officials were physically absent from Jerusalem for much of the year, except at major festivals, when Roman troops from the Antonia fortress were deployed along the Temple cloisters to watch for disorder.

The administrative apparatus was small. The prefect had a limited staff and auxiliary troops recruited largely from non-Jewish cities such as Caesarea and Sebaste, a fact with social consequences because the soldiers' loyalties and the local Jewish population's often diverged. Roman officials relied on local elites for tax collection and routine justice.

The high priest was the main intermediary. Governors such as Valerius Gratus replaced high priests frequently, which tied the priesthood to Roman favor and gave elite families strong reasons to compete for office. Jewish courts and councils handled much internal law, though their precise powers remain debated.

Agrippa I, a grandson of Herod raised in Rome, received territories from Caligula and Claudius and ruled as king over most of the old kingdom in 41–44. He cultivated Jewish leaders and observant practice. After his sudden death, Judea returned to procurators, and his son Agrippa II later held northern territories and influence over the Temple's priestly appointments. The interlude showed that Rome would still entrust Jewish rule to a trusted client.

Historical Vocabulary
Prefect, Procurator, and Legate

A prefect was the equestrian official who governed Judea from 6 to 41 CE; Pontius Pilate is known from an inscription as a prefect. After the reign of Agrippa I (41–44), Judea's equestrian governors were styled procurators. Both titles belong to the equestrian order, ranking below senators.

A legate was a senatorial governor commanding legions. The legate of Syria commanded the nearest legions and could intervene in Judea in a major crisis. By the early second century Judea itself had a consular-level governor and a second legion, a change that preceded the Bar Kokhba revolt.

Geography Study

Caesarea on the coast was the Roman administrative base, Jerusalem in the hills the religious center, and Galilee to the north the region that later became the center of Jewish learning. The Judean Desert on the east preserved documents from the second revolt, and Babylonia beyond the Roman frontier hosted a large Jewish community under other rulers. Section 31.6

Roman Government and Sources of Tension

Roman rule in Judea did not make revolt automatic. The provincial apparatus was small: a prefect or procurator at Caesarea with a modest staff and auxiliary troops, and the legate of Syria with legions nearby. Order depended on cooperation from the high priest, aristocratic families, city councils, and village leaders, who collected taxes, judged disputes, and mediated with Rome. Most years passed without mass violence, and many Jews used petitions and embassies rather than arms.

Economic pressure was real but varied. Tribute, tolls, and temple and local dues fell on a population that also bore the costs of Herodian-era building and, later, of debt and land consolidation. Josephus reports debt-record burning at the start of the revolt, which suggests that credit and land were sources of resentment, though his explanations are not neutral. Violence also arose from banditry, rural rivalries, and the movement of armed bands, and prophetic or apocalyptic figures drew followers with expectations of divine deliverance.

Religious and symbolic sensitivities mattered. Roman standards, imperial images, and the emperor's cult could provoke protest where Temple purity was at stake. Pilate's tenure, 26–36 CE, produced confrontations over standards and the use of Temple funds, and Caligula's attempt around 40 CE to place his statue in the Temple was averted partly through diplomacy and his death. Such episodes showed how governors' misjudgments could escalate quickly, but they were also resolved without general war.

Elite politics were equally important. High priestly families competed for office and income, sometimes violently, and Roman appointment and removal of high priests turned that competition into a Roman concern. Greek and Jewish city populations in Caesarea and other mixed cities quarreled over civic rights. Under the later procurators in the 50s and 60s, Josephus portrays worsening banditry, factional violence, and misrule, though his emphasis on a few bad governors serves his argument that responsibility lay with a minority.

The revolt of 66 thus resulted from the interaction of structural strain, elite and civic rivalry, popular expectation, and a sudden crisis under Florus. Some Jews worked to prevent it, and Roman officials made choices that made it more likely but not certain.

Section 31.7

The First War, 66–70 CE

Open revolt began in 66 CE. A dispute in Caesarea, where Jews and Greeks quarreled over civic rights, was followed by the procurator Florus's seizure of Temple funds. In Jerusalem, Josephus reports that Eleazar son of Ananias, a young Temple official, and his supporters persuaded those conducting the sacrifices to stop accepting offerings from foreigners, which ended the daily sacrifice for the emperor. Josephus treats this as a decisive step toward war, and he says that leading priests and notables opposed it. Roman troops in the city were then overwhelmed. Some leaders, especially among the priestly aristocracy, opposed war, and Josephus presents these debates as a split between a peace party and militants.

The legate of Syria, Cestius Gallus, marched on Jerusalem in autumn 66 but withdrew and was badly defeated at Beth Horon. This victory convinced many that resistance could succeed. Meanwhile, Jewish communities in Caesarea, Damascus, and other mixed cities suffered violence, and in places Jews attacked non-Jewish neighbors, as the war became partly a conflict of cities and regions.

A provisional government in Jerusalem divided the country into military districts and appointed commanders. Josephus took charge of Galilee, where he faced rival leaders such as John of Gischala. Josephus's own later account of these months is self-justifying, and his actions are hard to reconstruct independently.

Nero sent Vespasian, who with his son Titus advanced from the north in 67. Roman forces took the Galilean fortresses, including Jotapata, where Josephus surrendered and later predicted that Vespasian would become emperor. Refugees and rebel leaders, including John, fled to Jerusalem, and Roman troops continued the reconquest of much of Judea in 68.

In Jerusalem, factional conflict intensified. Radical groups, sometimes labeled Zealots in the sources and joined by Idumean fighters, seized the city and killed leading moderates such as Ananus. Simon bar Giora, who commanded a separate force, entered the city in 69, and rival groups held different parts of Jerusalem and the Temple. The Sicarii, a separate group, held Masada and were not part of these Jerusalem factions. Josephus's labels are polemical and imprecise.

Rome's own civil wars halted the campaign. Nero died in 68, and in 69 several claimants fought for power until Vespasian was proclaimed emperor and went to Rome, leaving Titus to finish the war. For civilians in Galilee and Judea the war meant destroyed villages, flight, enslavement, and shortages. The rebels were not a unified bloc, and the war divided Jewish society itself.

Historical Vocabulary
Zealots, Sicarii, and Rebel Labels

Josephus uses several labels for opponents of Rome, and they are polemical and imprecise. The Zealots were a specific Jerusalem faction in his account, associated with the Temple fighting and with Eleazar son of Simon, not a name for every rebel. The Sicarii, from a Latin word for a short dagger, were a separate group associated with Masada.

Josephus also calls many rebels brigands. Historians therefore treat his labels as evidence of his own viewpoint and of real factional distinctions, not as neutral descriptions of unified movements.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Crisis of 66–70: Four Reconstructed Viewpoints

Note: these viewpoints are analytical reconstructions that synthesize patterns historians infer from Josephus, inscriptions, archaeology, and later rabbinic texts. They are not quotations or the recorded views of any named person, and each group was itself divided.

The same events looked very different depending on where a person stood. Rome's rule, the Temple, taxation, and the approach of war carried different meanings for those who profited from the existing order, those who bore its costs, those who lived far from Judea, and those charged with preserving order. Comparing them helps explain why the revolt of 66 was neither unanimous nor inevitable.

From the perspective of a Jerusalem priest before 70:A priest in one of the rotating courses owed his status to descent and to service at the Temple, which also gave him a share of offerings and a place in an economy built on pilgrimage. His position depended on keeping the sanctuary functioning and on the working relationship between high priestly families and Roman or Herodian authorities. Many priests had good reason to fear that war would endanger the Temple. Yet priests were not unanimous: Josephus himself was a priest who first joined the revolt, and the decision to stop the sacrifice for the emperor was pressed by Eleazar son of Ananias and his supporters, while Josephus says leading priests opposed it. Sensitivities about purity and sacred space could make symbolic offenses by Roman officials feel like attacks on the center of communal life.
From the perspective of a rural Judean family:A tenant or small-holding family lived by grain, olives, and vines, and its main concerns were the harvest, rents, tolls, and the dues owed to landlords, the Temple, and the state. Debt could lead to the loss of land, and Josephus's report that rebels burned debt records suggests that economic grievance mattered, though his explanations are not neutral. Violence by armed bands, Roman troop movements, and requisitions threatened villages whether or not they supported the rebels. Such a family might welcome the promise of relief, fear the consequences of rebellion, or be coerced by local fighters, and its voice survives mainly in indirect traces rather than in its own words.
From the perspective of a Jewish resident of Alexandria or Rome:A Jew in the diaspora might never have seen Jerusalem but still sent the annual Temple contribution and thought of the Temple as part of his community's identity. His daily concerns were his local standing, his relations with Greek or Roman neighbors, and his community's privileges. News of revolt created danger: Jews in cities such as Caesarea and elsewhere suffered violence in 66, and later the fiscus Judaicus affected Jews across the empire. At the same time, diaspora communities had their own synagogues and leaders and did not depend on Judea for every part of religious life, a fact that shaped how they adapted after 70.
From the perspective of a Roman provincial official:A prefect or procurator governed with a small staff and auxiliary troops and was judged by his ability to collect tribute and keep order, not to understand local religion. He relied on the high priest and aristocratic families as intermediaries, and a sudden crisis could leave him dependent on soldiers who were often drawn from local non-Jewish cities. Josephus portrays some governors as corrupt or provocative, and officials sometimes misjudged the symbolic weight of standards, funds, or festival crowds. From this vantage, challenges seemed like threats to Roman authority that required firm response, which helps explain both the harshness of Roman reprisals and the hope of some Jews for negotiation.

Comparing the perspectives: Priests, villagers, diaspora Jews, and Roman officials shared the same events but did not share the same interests, information, or fears. Their differences show that the war emerged from interaction among groups with divergent stakes, and that responsibility cannot be assigned to a single actor.

Section 31.8

Jerusalem and the Destruction of the Temple

Titus began the siege of Jerusalem in the spring of 70 with four legions, including Legio X Fretensis, and auxiliary forces. The city, crowded with pilgrims at Passover according to Josephus, was defended by three lines of walls on its northern side and by the Antonia fortress adjoining the Temple. The Temple Mount itself was a fortified compound and one of the principal remaining defensive positions; resistance continued in the Upper City after the Temple fell.

Roman engineers raised siege ramps, used battering rams and stone-throwing engines, and built a wall around the city to cut off supplies and flight. The outer walls fell in stages through May, and the Antonia was taken in July. Josephus's account of the siege works and their speed reflects his interest in presenting Roman organization, and some of his details cannot be checked.

Inside, the divisions described in §31.7 persisted. At the siege's opening three factions existed, those of John of Gischala, Simon bar Giora, and Eleazar son of Simon, who held the inner Temple court. During Passover, however, John's men infiltrated the inner court and absorbed Eleazar's group, leaving John and Simon as the two principal blocs, and rebels burned grain stores in the factional fighting. Hunger and disease spread, and Josephus describes extreme famine and the killing of those who tried to flee, including Roman crucifixions at the walls. The numbers he gives for the dead are very large and cannot be treated as reliable, but civilians clearly suffered enormously.

The Temple was burned in late summer, traditionally dated to the ninth or tenth of Av, around August. In Jewish War 6 Josephus presents Titus as trying to save the sanctuary and as overruled by events, while later writers present him as intending its destruction. We can establish what happened, that the sanctuary was destroyed, but not Titus's intentions with confidence. Archaeology supports the destruction, with burned layers in the city and large quantities of fallen masonry around the Temple Mount. Resistance continued in the Upper City after the Temple fell, and the city was taken in the following weeks.

The religious consequences were immediate. Sacrifice ended, and the priesthood lost the institution that gave it its central political and economic role. Priests continued as a status group and kept their claims to descent, but without Temple service. Pilgrimage in its old form ended, though the Temple remained a focus of memory, law, and hope, and many Jews continued to study the rules of sacrifice and purity.

Rome presented the victory as a dynastic triumph. Vespasian and Titus celebrated a triumph in Rome in 71, Flavian coins proclaimed Judea captured, and the Arch of Titus later showed spoils from the Temple. Prisoners were enslaved or sent to Rome and to the games, the Temple contribution was redirected into the fiscus Judaicus paid to Jupiter Capitolinus, and Legio X Fretensis remained in the ruined city. Masada fell in 73 or 74, a date that remains debated, as an aftermath rather than the climax. The destruction transformed Jewish life but did not end it: the next sections show what replaced Temple-centered Judea.

Primary Source Window

Josephus, Jewish War 6, on the Burning of the Temple

Creator: Flavius (Yosef ben Matityahu) Josephus · Date: composed in Rome, c. 75–79 CE · Type: Narrative history in Greek, written under Flavian patronage

Context: Josephus was a priest from Jerusalem who was appointed to command in Galilee in 66, surrendered to Vespasian at Jotapata in 67, and later lived in Rome as a client of the Flavian emperors. The Jewish War is the only continuous narrative of the first revolt, and Book 6 covers the final siege and the burning of the sanctuary.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): In Book 6, Josephus describes fire spreading through the Temple during fighting, presents Titus as trying to save the sanctuary and as overruled by events and by soldiers' anger, and assigns responsibility to rebel factions whose actions he says polluted the holy place.

Analysis: The account is indispensable, since without it we would know little of the war's course, but it is not neutral. Josephus wrote for a Roman audience under the family that had won the war, and he had reasons to portray Titus favorably, to present Jewish aristocrats such as himself as opponents of revolt, and to blame a minority of fanatics. These apologetic aims affect his portrayal of both Roman intentions and rebel groups.

Corroboration and limits: Other evidence can be set against him. Roman commemoration, including Flavian coins proclaiming Judea captured and the Arch of Titus, shows how Rome presented the victory. Archaeology confirms destruction layers in Jerusalem and the collapse of masonry around the Temple Mount. Later writers give a different account of Titus's intentions, and Josephus's casualty and famine figures cannot be verified.

Section 31.9

After 70: Rebuilding Jewish Life

The destruction of the Temple ended a central institution, but Jewish life did not stop. Communities in Judea, Galilee, Babylonia, Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, and Rome continued to observe Torah, keep festivals, settle disputes, and care for their poor. What changed was the loss of sacrifice and pilgrimage as the focus of religious life, and the loss of a Jerusalem institution that had tied communities together.

Synagogues, as places of assembly, Torah reading, and communal business, existed before 70. Excavated buildings at Gamla, Masada, and Herodium, together with a Jerusalem inscription naming a synagogue builder and diaspora inscriptions, show this. After 70 they gained importance as regular centers of worship and study, though the architecture, functions, and degree of standardization varied widely from place to place. Prayer, Scripture reading, and study took on greater weight, but historians are cautious about assuming a single new liturgy appeared at once.

Priests did not disappear. They continued to hold status, to receive certain dues in rabbinic law, and to claim descent, and many rabbinic texts preserve extensive discussion of Temple service and purity as a subject of study and of future hope. Memory of the Temple became a way of keeping a lost institution present.

Tradition links Yohanan ben Zakkai with a school at Yavne, a coastal town in Roman Judea, and his escape from besieged Jerusalem is told in later stories of uncertain historicity. Gamaliel II is also associated with Yavne in later generations. The point to remember is that our evidence is rabbinic literature compiled generations later, shaped by retrospective needs, so it cannot simply be read as a report of events in 70.

Rabbinic authority developed gradually. Teachers of the generations after 70 debated law in small circles, and their traditions were collected in the Mishnah only around 200 CE under Judah ha-Nasi; the Talmuds came later still. For much of this period rabbis were one group among several, with influence that varied by place, and many Jews, especially in the diaspora, lived by local custom with little reference to them. Greek-speaking and other forms of Judaism continued.

Rome's policies shaped this adaptation. Jews retained the right to practice their ancestral customs in most places, but paid the fiscus Judaicus, and the military occupation of Judea marked defeat. The result was not a sudden creation of rabbinic Judaism at Yavne but a long transformation of religious leadership, text, and community.

Historical Debate

What Changed at Yavne after 70 CE?

The question: After the Temple's destruction, teachers gathered at Yavne, but what exactly happened there, and how much of later rabbinic Judaism did it create?

Older view: A “Council of Jamnia” convened under Yohanan ben Zakkai, converted Pharisaic Judaism into rabbinic Judaism, and fixed the boundaries of the Jewish Scriptures.
Current view: Yavne was an important gathering place for teachers, but there is no evidence for a formal council. Canon formation and rabbinic institutions developed gradually, and the importance of Yavne has to be separated from later stories about it.
Why the interpretation changed: The older model combined traditions from rabbinic texts compiled generations after 70 into a more institutionalized first-century council than the surviving evidence supports. No contemporary record describes a council closing the canon, and scholars note that discussions about particular books continued for long afterward. The Pharisee-to-rabbi equation has also been qualified, since rabbinic authority spread unevenly over generations. Scholarship increasingly distinguishes the historical significance of Yavne from retrospective narratives about it.

Evidence: The debate depends on rabbinic literature, later reports about Yohanan ben Zakkai and Gamaliel II, discussions of Scripture, and the absence of contemporary documentation.

Why it matters: The case illustrates how historians treat later retrospective sources and why transformation after 70 is described as gradual.

  2. What evidence would a formal council have left?

  4. Why is it misleading to equate Pharisees with rabbis?

Section 31.10

Diaspora, Identity, and Jewish Life

The Jewish diaspora long predated 70 CE. Babylonia, where communities descended from the deportations discussed in Chapter 20 lived, remained a major center. In Egypt, Alexandria held one of the largest Jewish populations in the Mediterranean, with roots in the Persian and Ptolemaic periods. Other communities lived in Cyrenaica, Syria, Asia Minor, Greece, and Rome, where a Jewish presence is attested from the second century BCE.

These communities were not uniform. They spoke Greek, Aramaic, and Hebrew in varied proportions, and held a range of civic statuses, from resident aliens to, in some places, citizens. Their institutions included synagogues, associations, and councils, and Roman authorities generally allowed them to follow ancestral customs, though this tolerance was negotiated and sometimes withdrawn.

Alexandria offers a case study. Jews formed a large, organized community with their own quarters and leaders, and writers such as Philo engaged Greek philosophy while defending Jewish practice. Tension with Greek neighbors produced violence in 38 CE, after which Philo led an embassy to Caligula. The episode shows both the vitality of Greek-speaking Judaism and the vulnerability of diaspora communities to local conflict.

Before 70 the diaspora was tied to Jerusalem through pilgrimage and the annual Temple contribution, though many communities also developed their own religious life. Diaspora Jews were participants in these events, and some took part in the revolts, but were not simply passive recipients of decisions made in Judea.

The destruction of Jerusalem did not create the diaspora. It changed the balance between Judea and communities elsewhere by ending the Temple as an institutional center, redirecting the Temple contribution into the fiscus Judaicus, and, with the defeat in 135, further reducing Judea's weight. Synagogue life, local leadership, and study continued, and these institutions became more central.

Babylonia differed because it lay outside Roman rule, under Parthian and later Sasanian authority, and its Jewish communities were not subject to the Roman measures that followed the wars. Chapter 32 treats the Iranian side of the frontier.

Large Jewish uprisings erupted in Cyrene, Egypt, and Cyprus in 115–117, with severe violence and harsh Roman repression, while Jewish resistance also occurred in Mesopotamia during Trajan's Parthian war. The relationship between the Mesopotamian fighting and the Mediterranean uprisings remains uncertain, and the sources are limited. These events formed part of the background to later tensions.

Section 31.11

The Bar Kokhba Revolt, 132–135

Hadrian visited the eastern provinces around 129–130 CE. On increasingly strong archaeological and numismatic evidence, Aelia Capitolina appears to have been begun then, before the revolt, although older reconstructions placed its establishment after the suppression. If the earlier date holds, the project is a plausible contributing cause of the revolt. A late and unreliable source says that a Hadrianic ban on circumcision caused the war, while other evidence suggests a ban may have followed it; the date and role of such a measure remain debated.

Other proposed factors include Roman administrative and military presence, land and economic pressures after the first war, and restorationist or messianic expectations. The causation and the order of events are not settled, and the chapter does not present any single cause as established.

The literary evidence is thin and late. Cassius Dio and Eusebius provide brief accounts, and Dio's extremely high figures for destroyed fortresses and villages and for the dead are treated with caution. They tell us little about operations and nothing from the rebels' side. The documents and archaeology therefore change the picture.

Letters and legal documents from caves in the Judean Desert, including Nahal Hever and Wadi Murabba'at, are written in Aramaic, Hebrew, and Greek. They show a functioning administration: orders to local commanders, supply requests, land leases, and dated contracts. The leader appears in them as Simon bar Kosiba, and some documents give him the title nasi. Early Christian sources preserve the star-name form Bar Kokhba (“son of the star”), while rabbinic tradition preserves forms such as Bar Koziba and a later tradition that Rabbi Akiva read a messianic prophecy about a star as applying to him.

Rebel coins were minted by overstriking Roman issues, with Hebrew inscriptions that refer to the redemption or freedom of Israel. Imagery commonly interpreted as Temple-related appears, though the identification and meaning of some designs remain debated. Underground hiding complexes in the Judean foothills and refuge caves in the desert show how communities prepared for Roman reprisal, but they do not tell us about the course of battles.

Rome brought in reinforcements, including commanders from other provinces, and fought a costly war of attrition. Betar, where the revolt's last stand is traditionally placed, fell in 135. Rural settlements were devastated and many people died, were enslaved, or fled. Rome then prohibited Jewish residence in Aelia Capitolina and its environs and completed its reorganization, as the next section discusses.

Primary Source Window

The Bar Kokhba Letters from the Judean Desert

Creator: Simon bar Kosiba and subordinate officials · Date: 132–135 CE · Type: Administrative letters and legal documents in Aramaic, Hebrew, and Greek, found in desert caves such as those at Nahal Hever and Wadi Murabba'at

Context: Unlike Josephus, these documents were produced inside the revolt, not written afterward for a Roman audience. They were found in caves where refugees and fugitives sheltered during the revolt and the Roman campaign to suppress it.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Surviving letters attributed to Bar Kosiba give orders to local commanders about the movement of men and the supply of goods, and warn officials against negligence.

What they reveal: They show an organized administration with officials, leases, and requisitions, and they use the title nasi for the leader. The use of Aramaic, Hebrew, and Greek shows the multilingual environment of the movement and its administration.

Limits and preservation: The documents survive by accident, because people carried them into refuge caves. They are not a representative sample of rebel correspondence, they concern routine matters, and they say little about strategy, ideology, or the overall course of the war. Interpretation depends on difficult texts and on dating.

Comparison with literary evidence: Cassius Dio and Eusebius give brief later narratives from outside the movement, while these letters offer participant-level administrative evidence. Together they show why different kinds of sources answer different questions.

Section 31.12

Judea after 135

Immediately after 135, Rome completed the reorganization of Jerusalem as Aelia Capitolina, a Roman colony with pagan cult buildings. Jews were prohibited from residing in Aelia Capitolina and its environs, while later sources also describe restrictions on access. This was part of a project begun earlier, as §31.11 explained, and it removed the possibility of Jerusalem as a Jewish center for generations.

Roman militarization of the province had already intensified before the revolt: by the early second century Judea had a consular-level governor and a second legion. After 135, Rome maintained this enlarged military presence while reorganizing the province, commonly associated with the name Syria Palaestina, though the details and timing are debated. Garrisons and veteran settlements strengthened control and shaped the landscape and economy of the region.

Judea suffered demographic devastation. Villages in the Judean hills were destroyed, many people died or were enslaved, and others fled. Jewish settlement was concentrated afterward in the southern edges of Judea, along the coast, and especially in Galilee, where it had always been strong. Non-Jewish populations also became more prominent in many areas.

The later second century saw a gradual shift of Jewish intellectual life toward Galilee. Teachers gathered at Usha and other Galilean towns in the generation after the war, and as restrictions eased under later emperors, rabbinic circles became more established. Judah ha-Nasi, active in the late second and early third century, is associated with the compilation of the Mishnah and with the patriarchate, a recognized leadership role whose powers are debated.

In the third century, institutions moved toward Tiberias, while Sepphoris and Beth Shearim, with its extensive burial caves, became prominent. This was a process of roughly a century, not an immediate postwar change, and the evidence for it comes largely from rabbinic texts, inscriptions, and archaeology, which must be read together.

Beyond the Roman frontier, Babylonia remained a major Jewish center under Parthian and then Sasanian rule, with its own leaders and later academies. The geography of Jewish intellectual life was therefore widening, not collapsing. Overall the result was transformation, not disappearance: new centers, texts, and forms of authority replaced Temple-centered Judea. Chapter 32 turns to Sasanian Iran, and Chapter 33 to the Christianization of the region.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 31 examined how Roman power operated in Judea and how two wars transformed Jewish life. In 63 BCE Pompey's intervention in a Hasmonean civil war ended full independence, reduced the territory, imposed tribute, and placed Judea within Rome's eastern client system under the oversight of Syria's governor.

Herod, named king by Rome in 40 BCE and ruling Jerusalem from 37, governed as a client king. He depended on Roman patronage and used mercenaries, fortresses, and dynastic violence, yet he also pursued his own policies and was never simply a puppet. His legitimacy among many Jews remained contested.

Herod's building programs, including the Temple's reconstruction, Caesarea, Sebaste, and the fortress-palaces, served his prestige, his Roman patrons, and the economy. They show that Jewish piety and Greco-Roman monumental culture could be combined in one political project, at a cost borne by taxpayers.

Jewish society was diverse, with priestly aristocrats, peasants, artisans, urban workers, and diaspora pilgrims, and with currents such as Pharisees, Sadducees, and Essenes whose character is known mostly through interested or later sources.

After Herod's death in 4 BCE and Archelaus's removal in 6 CE, Judea came under direct Roman administration by prefects and, after Agrippa I, procurators, with a small Roman apparatus that relied on local elites and the high priesthood. Tensions arose from taxation and debt, elite rivalry, religious and symbolic sensitivities, popular expectations, and governors' misjudgments, but revolt was not inevitable.

The revolt of 66 began after crises in Caesarea and Jerusalem and the defeat of Cestius Gallus. The rebels were deeply divided, and Josephus, our main source, was an interested participant. Vespasian and Titus reconquered Galilee and Judea, and in 70 Jerusalem fell and the Temple was destroyed. Sacrifice ended and the priesthood lost its central function, and Rome celebrated the victory and imposed the fiscus Judaicus.

Jewish life continued. Synagogues, Torah study, prayer, and communal institutions gave continuity, and rabbinic authority developed gradually, culminating in the Mishnah around 200. Yavne was important, but no formal council reorganized Judaism in 70. The diaspora, which long predated 70, remained vital.

The Bar Kokhba revolt of 132–135 is documented by coins and Judean Desert letters rather than a continuous narrative. Its causes are debated, including Hadrian's Aelia Capitolina, which appears to have been begun around 129–130. Its suppression devastated Judea and led to a prohibition on Jewish residence in Aelia Capitolina and a reorganized province.

In the century after 135, Jewish intellectual life shifted toward Galilee, with Usha, Judah ha-Nasi, and later Tiberias and Beth Shearim, while Babylonian and other diaspora communities flourished. Rome's military victory ended one political order but not Jewish history, and the stage is set for Chapter 32's turn to Sasanian Iran.

Reading a Revolt Through Its Sources

The history of the Jewish-Roman wars depends on very different kinds of evidence: a long narrative by an interested participant for the first war, and documents, coins, and archaeology for the second. Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how an imperial system's everyday workings, and not only its armies, shaped a provincial society. Rome ruled Judea with few officials by relying on kings, priests, and local elites, and when those arrangements failed, the results were catastrophic for many. The story cannot be reduced to a single oppressor and a single oppressed people, because Roman officials, Herodian rulers, priestly elites, and diverse Jewish communities each acted within their own interests and constraints.

It also shows the difference between military defeat and communal survival. The destruction of the Temple and the failure of two revolts ended a political and ritual order, but Jewish communities adapted through texts, study, and local institutions rather than disappearing. The changes were gradual, as the history of rabbinic authority and of Galilean and Babylonian centers shows, and they should not be compressed into a single moment at Yavne or at Betar.

The chapter illustrates how evidence shapes memory. Josephus, rabbinic tradition, coins, Roman monuments, and desert letters each tell different parts of the story, and historians must weigh them against each other. Their differences warn against treating any one source, especially an interested narrative, as the history itself.

The shift of Jewish geography from Judea toward Galilee and the diaspora, and the gradual growth of new forms of religious authority, shaped Jewish life across the Roman, Sasanian, and Islamic worlds treated in later volumes. Chapter 32 now turns to the Persian rival across the frontier, Sasanian Iran, whose Mesopotamian territory also contained a large Jewish community, and Chapter 33 to the broader religious transformation of the late antique Middle East.
