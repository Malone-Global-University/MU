Part XIV — The Seljuks, Anatolian Transformation, and the Crusades
61

Zengids, Nur al-Din, Saladin, and the Ayyubid Middle East

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 2 October 1187, after a siege of about two weeks, the Latin defenders of Jerusalem surrendered the city to Saladin on terms negotiated by Balian of Ibelin. Muslim chroniclers noted that the date fell in the month of Rajab, the month associated in tradition with the Prophet Muhammad's night journey to Jerusalem. The episode is often told as the climax of a single tidy story: a divided Muslim world is gradually united by pious and farsighted leaders, and then triumphs. This chapter argues that the real story is more interesting, and considerably less tidy.

The surrender of Jerusalem was a decisive event, and it reshaped the political and religious map of the eastern Mediterranean. But it was the product of nearly nine decades of political competition, institution-building, diplomacy, and warfare, much of which was fought between Muslim rulers rather than between Muslims and Latin Christians. In the century after the First Crusade, examined in Chapter 60, the Muslim states of Syria, the Jazira, Iraq, and Egypt were ruled by competing households. Their commanders fought one another over cities, revenues, and recognition from distant sultans and caliphs. Some of them negotiated truces and alliances with the Latin states when it suited them, just as the Latin rulers negotiated with Muslim neighbors when it suited theirs.

Out of this crowded political field emerged two dynasties. The Zengids, descended from the Turkish commander Imad al-Din Zengi, built a regional power centered on Mosul and Aleppo, extended it to Damascus under Zengi's son Nur al-Din, and used it to intervene in Egypt. The Ayyubids, a family of Kurdish origin that rose within Zengid service, inherited and then superseded that power. Under Saladin they won Egypt, much of Syria, the Jazira, and Yemen, defeated a Latin field army at Hattin, and recovered Jerusalem. Neither dynasty created a single unified Muslim realm. The Ayyubid state that survived Saladin was a family confederation of principalities, held together by kinship, shared revenues, military households, and negotiation.

This chapter treats these developments as a gradual process rather than a providential sequence. Muslim religious responses to Latin rule began almost at once, in the sermons and treatises of scholars such as the Damascene al-Sulami, well before Zengi or Nur al-Din rose to prominence. Rulers drew on the language of jihad, but they did so selectively, and they used it against Muslim as well as Christian rivals. The chapter therefore pays close attention to the distance between ideology and practice, and between the idealized portraits of Nur al-Din and Saladin created by admiring chroniclers and the documented decisions that these rulers actually made.

It also insists that the Zengid and Ayyubid world was not made only by rulers and armies. Its institutions were inherited from the Seljuk system of military land assignments and Sunni colleges examined in Chapters 57 and 58, and from the Fatimid administrative apparatus examined in Chapter 54. Its cities, including Damascus, Aleppo, and Cairo, had their own scholarly families, merchants, judges, and neighborhood networks, whose cooperation was essential. Its populations included Sunni and Shiʿi Muslims, Orthodox, Armenian, Syriac, and Coptic Christians, Jews, and Latin Christians living under Muslim rule or on its frontiers. Kurdish military households, whose historical significance is a major theme here, served within a wider army of Turks, Arabs, Kurds, and others, and must not be read through the lens of modern nationalism.

The geography of power mattered as much as its language. Egypt's Nile revenues supported armies operating far from Cairo, but the road to Damascus crossed difficult frontier zones and depended on ports and fortified towns. Aleppo linked northern Syrian markets to the Euphrates and Jazira; Mosul faced toward Iraq and eastern Anatolia; and control of Damascus gave access to the approaches to Palestine. No ruler could simply erase the military households and city interests along those routes. That is why the acquisitions of 1154, 1174, 1183, and 1186 were different political operations, even though chroniclers sometimes presented them as stages of one campaign.

The sources for this century also need to be compared rather than harmonized automatically. Damascene chronicles record the concerns of city officials, court biographies celebrate patrons, and accounts of the siege of Jerusalem give unequal space to rulers, ransom payments, and captive families. Jewish records from the Cairo Geniza and surviving buildings reveal economic and social practices less visible in accounts of battles. Historians must ask when a writer observed an event, what earlier reports were available, and why a particular outcome mattered to a patron or community. These questions shape the five source-study windows and the chapter's assessments of religious language, diplomacy, and violence.

The chapter follows five divisions. The first examines the political fragmentation after 1099, the intellectual and religious responses to Latin conquest, and the rise of Zengi. The second treats the conquest of Edessa, the consolidation of Nur al-Din's authority, the incorporation of Damascus, and his government. The third turns to Fatimid Egypt, Kurdish military networks, and Saladin's rise from vizier to ruler. The fourth covers Saladin's regional coalition, Hattin, Jerusalem, and the diplomacy of the Third Crusade. The fifth examines Ayyubid government, society, succession, and legacy. The comprehensive history of the Third Crusade as an expedition, and of the later Latin states, belongs to Chapter 62; here the emphasis is on how Saladin's government experienced and managed it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Muslim states of Syria, the Jazira, and Egypt were politically fragmented after 1099, and why that fragmentation delayed any sustained counteroffensive.
  2. Analyze early Muslim religious responses to Latin rule, including al-Sulami's Kitab al-Jihad, and distinguish religious exhortation from political and military coordination.
  3. Describe the institutions of atabegs, military land assignments, and nominal Seljuk and Abbasid recognition that structured twelfth-century politics.
  4. Trace the career of Zengi, including his campaigns against Muslim rivals and his capture of Edessa in 1144.
  5. Assess the different experiences of Latin, Armenian, and Syriac Christians at Edessa and the political consequences of its fall.
  6. Evaluate Nur al-Din's consolidation of Aleppo and Damascus and the institutional basis of his legitimacy, separating contemporary evidence from later hagiography.
  7. Reconstruct the crisis of Fatimid Egypt and the expeditions of Shirkuh and Saladin between 1164 and 1171.
  8. Explain the role of Kurdish military households in Zengid and Ayyubid state formation without projecting modern ethnic nationalism backward.
  9. Distinguish the stages by which Saladin became ruler of Egypt and then of Syria, including 1169, 1171, 1174, 1183, and 1186.
  10. Analyze the political, logistical, and military circumstances of Hattin and the capture of Jerusalem in 1187, and the unequal consequences of the surrender for the city's inhabitants.
  11. Characterize the Ayyubid political system, its fiscal and military foundations, its society, and the role of women and religious institutions within it.
  12. Evaluate how Saladin's later reputation was constructed and how it differs from the documented historical record.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did Zengi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin each combine religious legitimacy, military force, and diplomacy, and how did their methods differ?
  3. Why was control of cities such as Mosul, Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo more important to these rulers than any single campaign against Latin territory?
  4. What was the role of Kurdish and Turkic military households in building new dynastic power, and what does it mean to say this without anachronism?
  5. How did Egypt's agricultural wealth and strategic position transform the balance of power in Syria and Palestine?
  6. What were the causes of the Latin defeat at Hattin, and why did the destruction of a field army lead to the loss of most Latin cities?
  7. How should historians evaluate sources written by court officials and admirers of Nur al-Din and Saladin?
  8. Was the Ayyubid state an empire, a confederation, or something else, and what evidence best answers that question?

Historical Context

By 1099 the Great Seljuk Empire, whose rise and institutions were examined in Chapters 57 and 58, had already fragmented. After the death of Sultan Malikshah in 1092, rival sons and relatives contested the succession, and Seljuk princes in Syria, Anatolia, Iraq, and Iran increasingly governed separate territories. The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad retained religious prestige and some ability to confer legitimacy, but little independent military power. Egypt remained under the Ismaili Fatimid caliphate (Chapter 54), whose own government had passed into the hands of powerful military viziers. Anatolia was being transformed by Turkic migration and the rise of the Sultanate of Rum (Chapter 59).

Into this divided landscape came the First Crusade (Chapter 60), which by 1100 had established the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Edessa, and the Principality of Antioch, with the County of Tripoli following in 1109. Muslim rulers did not respond as a bloc. The Seljuk princes of Aleppo and Damascus, the Artuqid commanders of Upper Mesopotamia, the governors of Mosul, and the Fatimid vizier in Cairo all pursued separate interests. Some fought the newcomers, some bought them off, and some made agreements with them against Muslim rivals. The Latin states in turn became players in a regional game whose rules existed before they arrived.

This chapter begins in that world and follows it for roughly a century, from about 1100 to about 1200, with selective glances into the early thirteenth century where needed to explain Ayyubid continuity. The Zengid and Ayyubid dynasties were products of the Seljuk military-political system as much as reactions to the Crusades. They inherited its atabegs, its land assignments, its Sunni colleges, and its habit of treating the Abbasid caliph as the source of legitimacy. They also inherited the Fatimid state in Egypt, which they dismantled in its political form while preserving much of its administration. The next stage of the story, the later Crusades and the Mamluk transformation, belongs to Chapters 62 and 65, and the Mongol catastrophe to Chapter 63.

Political Fragmentation, Jihad Discourse, and the Rise of Zengi

Section 61.1

The Middle East after the First Crusade: Latin States, Muslim Rulers, and Regional Rivalries

The political map that emerged after 1099 was not a frontier between two blocs. On the Latin side were the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, and, from 1109, the County of Tripoli. Their rulers expanded along the coast in the following decades, taking Acre in 1104, Tripoli in 1109, Beirut and Sidon in 1110, and Tyre in 1124, usually with the help of Italian fleets. Fatimid Egypt, which had lost Jerusalem just before the crusaders took it, kept the southern port of Ascalon until 1153. These Latin polities were small, dependent on a thin ruling class and on local Christian and Muslim subjects, and they were constantly short of manpower.

On the Muslim side the political units were smaller and more numerous. Aleppo and Damascus, once held by rival sons of the Seljuk prince Tutush, passed to military commanders and their descendants. Mosul and the northern Jazira were governed by appointees of the Seljuk sultans in Iraq, who changed often. Upper Mesopotamia was divided among Turkmen and Artuqid lords. Baghdad housed the Abbasid caliph and, at intervals, a Seljuk sultan. Cairo was ruled in the name of a Fatimid caliph by viziers who controlled the army. These centers differed in ruling households, military resources, and interests. A ruler in Damascus feared an ambitious neighbor in Aleppo or Mosul at least as much as he feared the king of Jerusalem.

For this reason relations across the religious boundary were varied. In June 1119 the Artuqid commander Il-Ghazi, then ruling Aleppo, destroyed an army of Antioch at the battle later called the Field of Blood; Latin chroniclers and Muslim sources alike regarded it as a major defeat. Yet Damascene rulers made truces with Jerusalem, and border districts could be governed through shared revenue arrangements. As late as 1140 the Damascene atabeg Muʿin al-Din Unur concluded an agreement with King Fulk against Zengi, whom he regarded as the greater threat. Historians, notably Michael Köhler in his study of treaties and alliances, have shown that such arrangements were routine rather than scandalous.

The key point for this chapter is chronological. The emergence of Zengid power followed several decades of competition and did not represent an immediate, coordinated Muslim counteroffensive. The Latin states survived their first half-century largely because their neighbors were divided, and Muslim unity, where it came, was built around particular cities and families rather than around a shared project. The following sections show how that gradual process unfolded.

The territory separating the Latin-held coastal towns from the inland cities was not governed as a continuous, fixed border. Farmers sometimes cultivated land while paying revenues to competing claimants; merchants traveled between markets under safe-conducts; and raiding forces crossed districts whose nominal rulers could not always protect them. Fortified sites controlled roads and water rather than every field between them. These arrangements allowed mixed populations to continue their lives, but exposed them to sudden demands for tribute, provisions, and military service. A treaty between a Damascene official and the Kingdom of Jerusalem might settle a dispute over harvest revenue without implying agreement in religion or permanent friendship. The same political pragmatism helps explain why the Latin states could persist in the midst of more populous Muslim-ruled territories.

Section 61.2

Seljuk Fragmentation, Atabegs, the Burids, and the Artuqids

The institutional background is the weakening of Great Seljuk authority described in Chapter 57. After Malikshah's death in 1092 his sons and nephews fought for the sultanate, while Sultan Sanjar governed Khurasan from the east until his defeat by the Qara-Khitai at Qatwan in 1141 and his death in 1157. In Iraq and western Iran successive sultans such as Muhammad Tapar, Mahmud, and Masʿud maintained courts at Baghdad or in Iranian cities but depended on the loyalty of their commanders. In Syria Seljuk princes had little effective control from the beginning.

Into this gap stepped atabegs. Originally military guardians appointed to supervise young Seljuk princes, atabegs often married the prince's mother, controlled his army, and governed in his name. Many became hereditary rulers while still invoking Seljuk legitimacy. In Damascus the atabeg Tughtekin dominated the Seljuk prince Duqaq and then his heirs; his descendants, the Burids, ruled the city from 1104 until 1154. Contemporary and later sources show how real power could rest with a vizier or senior commander while a young Burid held the title, a pattern visible in Unur's dominance in the 1130s and 1140s and in the nominal rule of Mujir al-Din Abaq.

Historical Vocabulary
Atabeg

From Turkish elements meaning roughly "father-lord," an atabeg was a military guardian and tutor of a young Seljuk prince. In practice atabegs controlled armies, tax revenues, and appointments, and several founded dynasties. The word is therefore both an office and a description of a political style: rule by a military guardian who governs in the name of a nominal superior. Zengi's Mosul and Aleppo lordship and the Burid regime in Damascus are the principal examples in this chapter.

In Upper Mesopotamia and eastern Anatolia the Artuqids, descended from the Turkmen commander Artuq, held Hisn Kayfa, Mardin, and Amid (modern Diyarbakır) and sometimes extended their power to Aleppo. Il-Ghazi and his nephew Balak achieved notable successes against Antioch and Edessa in the 1110s and 1120s, including the capture of Count Joscelin I and King Baldwin II in 1122 and 1123. Their rule shows that effective resistance could come from small military lordships. It also shows why such lordships could not sustain a long-term offensive: their armies were largely Turkmen cavalry that dispersed after campaigns, their revenues were limited, and the death of a leader often ended his achievements.

The relationships among commanders, ruling families, urban elites, and the Abbasid caliphate were fluid. Recognition by a sultan or caliph conferred prestige and sometimes a formal diploma of investiture, but it did not guarantee obedience. Historians must therefore distinguish nominal recognition from effective local control. Fragmentation created opportunities for Latin expansion, because no single neighbor could threaten the Latin states for long, and for ambitious Muslim commanders, because cities and revenues were up for competition. Zengi was the first to exploit these opportunities on a large scale.

The vocabulary of sovereignty could also conceal competing claims. A Friday sermon might name an Abbasid caliph and a recognized Seljuk ruler, while soldiers in the marketplace obeyed a local atabeg who paid them. Coins, public investiture, titles, and marriages thus offer different kinds of evidence from chronicles of battles. An appointment from a nominal superior was valuable because it made a seizure of power look like an authorized office; yet a diploma could not by itself collect taxes or command garrisons. The historical challenge is to reconstruct who could supply and dismiss troops, appoint governors, manage the citadel, and negotiate terms in a particular city. These practical tests of power are more illuminating than the presence of an impressive dynastic title alone.

Section 61.3

Muslim Responses to the Crusades: Al-Sulami, Jihad, and Religious Mobilization

Muslim reaction to the Latin conquests began quickly and took several forms. After the fall of Jerusalem in 1099, refugees, including scholars and jurists, reached Damascus and Baghdad, and a delegation from Syria protested in Baghdad about the failure of the Seljuk and Abbasid authorities to respond. Between about 1110 and 1115 the Seljuk sultan sent several commanders from Mosul, including Mawdud and later Bursuq, against the Latin states in Syria. These expeditions failed to achieve lasting gains, partly because local rulers in Aleppo and Damascus distrusted the sultan's agents and feared that an eastern army might replace them.

The most important early intellectual response came from the Damascene scholar Ali ibn Tahir al-Sulami (d. 1106), a teacher of Arabic grammar and Sunni law who taught in the city's great mosque. Around 1105 he delivered the public lectures that became his Kitab al-Jihad ("Book of Jihad"). The work survives only in a fragmentary manuscript and has been edited and translated by modern scholars, including Niall Christie. Al-Sulami argued that the Latin conquests, which he linked to wider Christian advances in Sicily and Spain, were a divine response to the Muslims' disunity and moral failings, and he called on rulers to cooperate. He also treated jihad as a broad ethical and spiritual obligation, including struggle against one's own faults, and not only as warfare. Both themes would resurface in later propaganda.

Historical Vocabulary
Jihad

Arabic jihad means "striving" or "exertion." Classical Islamic law and ethics used it for several things: the inner struggle against sin, the effort to promote righteous conduct, and, in legal manuals, military effort on behalf of the Muslim community under rules governing who may fight, whom, and how. Twelfth-century writers, preachers, poets, and rulers invoked it in different ways. A ruler might call a campaign against crusaders "jihad," but so might a ruler fighting Muslim rivals who allegedly made treaties with the Latins. Historians therefore treat jihad rhetoric as evidence of how legitimacy was argued, not as proof of a unified military program.

Editorial paraphrase, not a quotation: Drawing on religious tradition, the author tells his audience that Muslim fortunes have declined because of disunity and neglect of duty, urges rulers to put aside quarrels and cooperate against the invaders, and describes the struggle for moral improvement as the basis for success in any outward struggle.

Analysis: The treatise is evidence for a moral and legal argument about Latin rule and for an audience in Damascus willing to hear it. It is not evidence that rulers adopted this program, and the contemporary political record shows that most of them did not.

Limitations: Fragmentary survival; a scholar's perspective rather than a ruler's; uncertain size and composition of the audience; and modern editions that must be consulted for exact wording.

Questions for students:

  1. What kinds of evidence would show that rulers actually acted on al-Sulami's appeals?
  2. Why might a scholar emphasize moral reform alongside military action?
  3. What does the fragmentary survival of the text suggest about the limits of the evidence?

Modern research has thus shown that Muslim ideological responses emerged well before Zengi or Nur al-Din came to prominence, which challenges the older assumption that resistance began only after the fall of Edessa in 1144. The appearance of jihad rhetoric, however, does not prove a unified military coalition. The expeditions of the 1110s, the career of Il-Ghazi, and Damascus's treaties with Jerusalem all show that political interests came first. What the early period produced was a vocabulary, and a group of scholars, that later rulers could use to claim that their wars served a larger religious purpose.

The audiences of such religious teaching were themselves politically situated. Mosques gathered legal scholars, artisans, merchants, and officers, but a preacher's audience was not identical to the ruler's council. A call to set aside disputes might command admiration while leaving merchants concerned about interrupted trade and commanders fearful of a rival's troops. The fragmentary survival of al-Sulami's treatise prevents an exact reconstruction of its reception. Nevertheless, its insistence on moral reform and collective obligation provides a benchmark against which to compare later official pronouncements. When Nur al-Din or Saladin presented campaigns as a defense of the Muslim community, they drew on a discourse that scholars had developed before those rulers secured their political bases.

Section 61.4

Imad al-Din Zengi: Mosul, Aleppo, and the Formation of Regional Military Power, 1127–1144

Imad al-Din Zengi was born around 1085, the son of Aq Sunqur, a Turkish military governor who served the Seljuk sultan Malikshah. After his father's death Zengi rose through the Seljuk military system as an officer of the sultans in Iraq. In 1127 Sultan Mahmud appointed him governor of Mosul and atabeg of two Seljuk princes, an office that gave him a base in the Jazira. Contemporary and later chroniclers agree that he combined skill with a reputation for severity toward his own soldiers and towns.

In 1128 Zengi entered Aleppo, a city then weakened by successive changes of ruler and by Latin pressure. Local notables, including the qadi Ibn al-Khashshab, favored a strong protector, and the arrangement joined the Jazira to northern Syria. Over the next decade he took Hama and several smaller fortresses, defeated the army of King Fulk at Barin in 1137, and exploited the Byzantine emperor John II Komnenos's campaign in northern Syria in 1137–1138. Sultan Masʿud and the caliph al-Mustarshid sometimes opposed him; in 1132 he suffered a defeat near Tikrit by the caliph's army, from which he escaped in part through the help of the local commander, Najm al-Din Ayyub, whose family appears again in this chapter.

Zengi's priorities cannot be reduced to holy war. For most of his career his main enemies were Muslim rulers: the Artuqids, the Burids of Damascus, local Turkmen lords, and, at times, the caliph and sultan. His siege of Damascus in 1139–1140 failed, and it pushed Unur into the arms of Jerusalem. Consolidating his position against Muslim competitors was central to his politics, and his propaganda, when it invoked jihad, did so to justify war against whichever opponent stood in his way, including fellow Muslims who allied with Latins.

The circumstances that made Edessa vulnerable in 1144 were political. Count Joscelin II preferred to live at Turbessel west of the Euphrates, leaving the city thinly garrisoned. The Latin states were divided: King Fulk had died in 1143 and was succeeded by a child king under his widow Melisende, while Antioch and Edessa quarreled. Zengi, meanwhile, was campaigning against the Artuqid ruler of Hisn Kayfa, whom Joscelin had allied with. When Joscelin led much of his force out of the city, Zengi turned west.

Aleppo itself illustrates why Zengi needed more than an army. A stronghold on the northern Syrian trading routes depended upon provisioning from the countryside, the security of caravans, the confidence of merchants, and the cooperation of judges and household commanders. Its people had suffered from contested government and military pressure; promises of protection therefore possessed immediate practical value. Zengi could present himself as a restorer of order even while subjecting opponents to harsh coercion. The same city could welcome a powerful defender and resent his extraction of money or soldiers. This tension between urban security and the fiscal needs of a campaigning ruler persisted under the Zengids and the Ayyubids.

Zengid Expansion and Nur al-Din's Political and Religious Program

Section 61.5

The Conquest of Edessa, 1144, and the Changing Balance of Power

Zengi arrived before Edessa at the end of November 1144 and besieged it for about four weeks. The Latin garrison was small, Archbishop Hugh of Edessa and the city's remaining defenders organized resistance, and the Armenian and Syriac Christian inhabitants contributed to the defense. Sappers undermined a section of the walls, and the city fell on or about 24 December. In the confusion many people died, including the archbishop, and crowds in the citadel and towers suffered in the crush as the attackers entered.

The experiences of the city's communities differed. Latin Christians, as the ruling elite, were killed, enslaved, or held captive. Our evidence for Armenian and Syriac Christians comes from Syriac and Armenian chroniclers and from Muslim writers, who describe Zengi ordering an end to the killing and allowing local Christians to remain; some sources report that deportations and enslavements affected them as well. Students should therefore avoid universal claims. What can be said is that Edessa's population was mixed, that Latin political authority was ended by the capture, and that Armenian and Syriac Christians, who had lived under Muslim, Byzantine, Armenian, and Latin rulers in turn, experienced the conquest as civilians in a siege, with outcomes that varied by status, location, and luck.

Chroniclers also diverge in distinguishing deaths during the breach, deaths from panic and crowding, execution, enslavement, and flight. Their numerical totals were not compiled through modern censuses. Armenian and Syriac accounts preserve fears and communal losses that Latin narratives often subordinate to the fate of the county. A sound textbook should let the different episodes and witnesses stand separately, explain the uncertainty in any numbers, and acknowledge that civilians could endure multiple successive crises.

Section 61.6

Zengi's Death in 1146 and the Rise of Nur al-Din

On 14 September 1146, Zengi was murdered during the siege of Qalʿat Jaʿbar on the Euphrates, reportedly by a servant of his own household. His empire was at once divided. His elder surviving son, Sayf al-Din Ghazi I, took Mosul and the Jazira. His younger son, Nur al-Din Mahmud, then about twenty-eight, secured Aleppo. The division was not a catastrophe; it followed the ordinary logic of dynastic succession and reflected the different resource bases and military followings of the two cities. It also meant that Mosul and Aleppo would follow separate paths for a generation, and that Nur al-Din never ruled Mosul as an unchallenged overlord until 1170.

Nur al-Din quickly met a crisis. In late 1146 Joscelin II, supported by Armenian residents of Edessa, tried to retake the city; Nur al-Din recaptured it and, according to later sources, destroyed its fortifications. In 1149 he defeated and killed Raymond of Antioch at Inab. Joscelin was captured in 1150, and the remaining Edessene territories were soon absorbed or abandoned. Nur al-Din also forged alliances through marriage: in 1147 he married a daughter of Unur of Damascus, a politically significant link with a ruler he later displaced.

Compared with Zengi, Nur al-Din relied more on negotiation, religious patronage, and the careful courting of scholars and urban elites. He also drew from the Turkmen cavalry and from officers such as Shirkuh and Ayyub, whose Kurdish background is discussed in Section 61.10. The contrast must not be exaggerated: he fought Muslim rivals as readily as his father had. But his reputation for justice and piety, repeated in later chronicles such as Ibn al-Athir's, requires care. Praise written after his death, often by people connected to his successors or to the religious institutions he sponsored, shaped the image of a model ruler. Historians treat the evidence of his foundations, inscriptions, and appointments as firmer than the anecdotes about his personal conduct.

Nur al-Din inherited a distinctive political problem: a celebrated victory at Edessa had enhanced the dynasty's name, but its resources remained divided. An officer loyal to the Mosul branch might cooperate against the Latin states while resisting an Aleppine demand for revenues or appointments. In Syria, every extension of Nur al-Din's authority required negotiation with existing households; in the Jazira his brother's and later nephews' claims could not simply be erased. The ruler's reputation for piety helped justify his position, but victories, diplomatic assurances, and the distribution of rewards were also necessary. Reading the panegyrics alongside the movements of armies and the decisions of city elites makes both the religious and political aspects of his career visible.

Section 61.7

The Incorporation of Damascus in 1154 and the Consolidation of Zengid Syria

Nur al-Din's acquisition of Damascus in April 1154 was the product of years of political pressure and negotiation. After Unur's death in 1149, Mujir al-Din Abaq, the Burid ruler, depended on his advisers and on an uneasy balance between Nur al-Din and the Latin kingdom. In the early 1150s Latin expeditions in the Hauran and Mujir al-Din's payment of tribute to Jerusalem undermined his standing among many Damascenes. The fall of Ascalon to King Baldwin III in 1153 showed that the Latin kingdom was growing stronger, and made Latin protection less attractive.

Sources indicate that Nur al-Din applied economic and diplomatic pressure, cultivated sympathizers within Damascus, and relied on the cooperation of local leaders and military families. Among them was Najm al-Din Ayyub, who had served in Damascus and whose family connections made him a useful intermediary. When Nur al-Din arrived, part of the city opened its gates and the Burid ruler left, receiving an iqtaʿ at Homs that was later exchanged for lands elsewhere. This was not a simple assault. It was a negotiated transfer of power in which urban notables, military commanders, and the ruler weighed their interests.

Control of both Aleppo and Damascus substantially strengthened Nur al-Din. He held the two largest cities in Syria, the routes between them, and the resources of the Orontes and the Damascus oasis. He could now press the Latin states from the north and east and look toward Egypt. The extent of his consolidation, however, should not be overstated. Mosul remained in the hands of his brother and then his nephews. Artuqid and other lords in the Jazira and Upper Mesopotamia kept their autonomy. The Seljuk sultanate of Rum, under Kilij Arslan II, remained an independent neighbor. Nur al-Din's authority was a layered system of direct governorships and negotiated relationships.

For Damascenes, the choice in 1154 was not between independent national sovereignty and foreign occupation in a modern sense. Households were evaluating which military patron could maintain the walls, uphold contracts, restrict pillage, and defend the orchard lands of the Ghuta oasis. A ruler who paid for soldiers by coercive levies could lose the support of merchants and jurists; one who promised safety might be accepted even when his troops came from another city. Accounts written under later Zengid and Ayyubid patrons had reason to depict the transfer as an orderly welcome. That literary interest does not invalidate the evidence of negotiation, but it warns against assuming that every inhabitant supported the change. The continued functioning of Damascene courts and markets mattered as much as the entry of new guards into the citadel.

Section 61.8

Nur al-Din's Government: Justice, Sunni Patronage, Hospitals, and Political Legitimacy

Nur al-Din's government rested on three supports: an army of Turkish, Turkmen, Kurdish, and other soldiers paid largely through land assignments; a civil administration of secretaries and judges; and a network of religious scholars and institutions that gave his rule an Islamic legitimacy. Sources describe him hearing petitions personally and establishing in Damascus a Dar al-ʿAdl, a "house of justice," where complaints, including those against officials and military men, could be heard with the participation of judges. Later writers claimed he abolished non-Islamic taxes. Such reports express the ideal of the just ruler, and they should be balanced against the fact that his army required revenue and that evidence for the actual operation of these measures is limited.

Patronage of Sunni institutions was the most visible element of his legitimacy, building on the Seljuk-era traditions of Nizam al-Mulk examined in Chapter 58. Nur al-Din founded or supported madrasas in Aleppo, Damascus, and other cities, a dar al-hadith (a center for the study of Prophetic traditions) in Damascus, charitable endowments, and law colleges that taught different Sunni legal schools. He supported scholars such as Ibn Asakir, whose enormous biographical dictionary of Damascus and whose writings on jihad belong to this environment. Sources also report that he promoted Sunni practice in cities with substantial Shiʿi populations, such as Aleppo, though students should treat accounts of religious policy as partly polemical. Another surviving witness is the elaborate wooden pulpit (minbar) commissioned in Aleppo in the late 1160s for the al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem, which was installed there after Saladin recovered the city in 1187. It shows that Nur al-Din's circle publicly anticipated a Muslim Jerusalem, though no evidence shows that this was his only or immediate aim.

The best-documented material monument of his patronage is the Bimaristan Nur al-Din in Damascus, a hospital founded in 1154 CE, as established by dated architectural and epigraphic evidence. The building has a monumental muqarnas portal, a courtyard organized around a central pool and vaulted halls, and its operation as a hospital and medical teaching site continued for centuries. Funded by a waqf (a charitable endowment), it combined care for the sick with medical teaching. It shows how rulers used institutions to link charity, learning, and urban prestige. Its architecture also shows the reuse of established regional building traditions. This evidence supports the conclusion that Nur al-Din's rule included effective investment in public institutions. It does not by itself prove that he personally administered justice with the saintly consistency described by later admirers.

The hospital offers a rare place where written history and surviving material evidence can be compared. Its original foundation is securely dated to 1154 CE; museum catalogues give 549 AH, while architectural records also employ an adjacent Hijri-year conversion, so the Common Era date is the clearest form for this chapter. Subsequent additions and repairs should not be mistaken for the original building. Its endowed medical functions, books, and teaching spaces show deliberate provision for urban health and learning, not simply a monument advertising victory. Yet the surviving fabric cannot reveal exactly who received treatment, whether patients paid anything, or how the institution managed epidemics. Those questions require documents beyond an architectural description. The institution therefore supports a limited but important claim: Nur al-Din's patronage created enduring urban resources, while the actual social reach of these resources must be investigated rather than assumed.

Historical Debate 1

Was Muslim Political Consolidation Driven Primarily by Jihad or Dynastic Ambition?

The question: To what extent did religious mobilization, as opposed to political competition, explain the rise of Zengi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin?

Interpretation A — Religious mobilization mattered decisively: Some scholars argue that jihad rhetoric, patronage of Sunni scholars, and the cause of Jerusalem gave Nur al-Din and Saladin a basis of legitimacy that rivals lacked, and that sermons, poetry, and treatises helped recruit soldiers, justify taxes, and unite political followings.
Interpretation B — Dynastic and fiscal ambition drove events: Others emphasize that these rulers spent much of their careers fighting Muslim rivals, that they negotiated truces with Latin neighbors, and that control of Aleppo, Damascus, Mosul, and Egypt was a precondition for any war on Latin territory. On this view, religion was a language of legitimacy that served political goals.

Evidence: Both sides rely on chronicles, chancery letters, inscriptions, and the careers of the rulers. The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive, because political and religious motives could reinforce each other.

Student task: Evaluate Zengi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin separately before generalizing. Which of them provides the strongest evidence for each interpretation, and why?

Division III · Sections 61.9–61.12

Fatimid Egypt, Kurdish Military Networks, and Saladin's Rise

Section 61.9

Fatimid Egypt in Crisis: Cairo, Vizieral Rivalries, and the Struggle for Political Control

Egypt was an established political and social system with its own institutions, not simply a prize waiting for rulers from Syria. Its wealth came from the Nile flood, which watered a densely settled valley and delta and supplied grain, flax, and sugar. Taxes on land and trade supported a bureaucracy that Chapter 54 described. Cairo was the Fatimid capital and seat of the caliph; Fustat was the older commercial city; Alexandria was the main Mediterranean port; and Red Sea ports such as Aydhab connected Egypt to Arabia, East Africa, and the Indian Ocean. Merchants, including Jewish traders whose papers survive in the Cairo Geniza, moved goods and credit through this network.

Politically, the Fatimid caliphate was in decline. After the long reign of al-Hafiz (d. 1149) the caliphs were often children or youths, and real power belonged to viziers who commanded the regiments of Armenian, Sudanese, Turkish, and Berber soldiers. Talaʿi ibn Ruzzik (vizier 1154–1161) and his son Ruzzik were followed by Shawar, who seized the vizierate in 1162 or 1163 and was ousted within months by his rival Dirgham. The caliph al-Adid, who succeeded in 1160 at about the age of eleven, never controlled the state. The loss of Ascalon in 1153 had deprived Egypt of its principal forward base against the Latin kingdom.

Shawar fled to Nur al-Din's court in Damascus and asked for military help against Dirgham. King Amalric I of Jerusalem, who took the throne in 1163, saw an opportunity to make Egypt a tributary and also feared a Syrian takeover. Nur al-Din, for his part, wished to prevent Egypt from falling under Latin influence and to use its revenues. These calculations, rather than any single religious program, set the stage for the intervention described next. The chapter's later sections show how the rivalry among Shawar, Amalric, and Nur al-Din's commanders turned Egypt into the central prize of the 1160s, and how Egyptian elites, including Fatimid officials, soldiers, merchants, and Coptic and Jewish administrators, participated in the outcome.

Control over Egypt also depended upon the willingness of agricultural and fiscal officials to keep revenue moving during regime change. A commander could capture Cairo, but he could not feed an army indefinitely if irrigation works fell into neglect, merchants withheld credit, or soldiers seized grain before taxes were assessed. The Nile flood introduced an annual rhythm that was only partly responsive to political orders. The Fatimid government possessed experienced secretaries, revenue records, and links to provincial notables; incoming Syrian forces needed their expertise even while seeking to replace hostile military formations. Cairo's religious institutions likewise had constituencies that could not be erased by an order from the vizier. The resulting transition was therefore a struggle for administrative cooperation as well as control of palaces and garrisons.

Section 61.10

Shirkuh, Ayyub, and the Kurdish Military Networks of the Zengid World

The family that would found the Ayyubid dynasty belonged to the Rawadiya, a Kurdish tribal group from the region of Dvin in the Armenian highlands. Najm al-Din Ayyub and his brother Asad al-Din Shirkuh entered the service of Seljuk governors and later of Zengi. Ayyub governed the citadel of Tikrit and, according to the sources, helped Zengi escape across the Tigris after his defeat in 1132; he later governed Baalbek and served in Damascus. Shirkuh, a short, stout, and formidable officer, became one of Nur al-Din's leading commanders. Saladin, Ayyub's son, was born at Tikrit around 1137 or 1138 and grew up in Syria, serving his uncle and then Nur al-Din.

The Kurdish origin of this family was historically significant, but it should be understood in medieval terms. Kurdish soldiers and tribal leaders served in Seljuk and Zengid armies alongside Turks, Turkmen, Arabs, Armenians, and others. Kurdish families offered kinship networks, military experience, and reputations that made them useful to rulers who needed trusted officers. The Ayyubid dynasty did not operate as a modern Kurdish national movement. Its legitimacy rested on Sunni Islam, the Abbasid caliphate, and military success, and its officer corps was ethnically mixed. Later chroniclers occasionally noted Kurdish identity, sometimes with prejudice, but contemporary political loyalties ran through households, patrons, and oaths rather than ethnic nationalism.

Shirkuh's three expeditions to Egypt were military and political contests with King Amalric. In 1164 he went with Saladin and restored Shawar to the vizierate by defeating Dirgham, who was killed. Shawar then turned against Shirkuh and allied with Amalric, besieging Shirkuh at Bilbays until Nur al-Din's capture of Harim, in Antioch's territory, drew Amalric away and both armies withdrew. In 1167 Shirkuh returned, fought Amalric and Shawar at the indecisive but costly battle of al-Babayn in March, and left Saladin to hold Alexandria under siege until a treaty allowed both armies to withdraw. Amalric left a garrison and an annual tribute arrangement in Cairo. In 1168 the king attacked again, sacked Bilbays with great bloodshed, and approached Cairo; Shawar ordered Fustat burned to keep it from the Latins. The Fatimid caliph appealed to Nur al-Din, and Shirkuh entered Cairo in January 1169. Shawar was executed, and Shirkuh was appointed Fatimid vizier. He died on 23 March 1169, only about two months later.

The distinction between Shirkuh's brief vizierate and Saladin's succession is important. Shirkuh's appointment was the Fatimid caliph's choice in an emergency, but Shirkuh was also Nur al-Din's commander. His death left the army, which included Turkish officers sent by Nur al-Din, Kurdish followers of Shirkuh, and Fatimid regiments, without an obvious leader. The decision to appoint Saladin, a relatively junior figure, was a compromise among competing groups that none of them necessarily expected to last.

Section 61.11

Saladin's Vizierate, the End of the Fatimid Caliphate, and the Reorganization of Egypt, 1169–1171

Saladin took office as vizier in March 1169 with the title al-Malik al-Nasir and nominal subordination to both the Fatimid caliph al-Adid and Nur al-Din. His first tasks were to manage the competing military factions and to defend Egypt. In the summer of 1169 he suppressed a revolt by the Sudanese regiments of the Fatimid army, which left a lasting mark on Cairo's military structure. In the autumn a combined Byzantine and Latin fleet attacked Damietta, and Saladin's defense, supported by supplies and reinforcements from Nur al-Din, held the port. In 1170 his father Ayyub and other relatives arrived in Egypt, giving him a family core on which to build.

The end of Fatimid political authority was a process, not a single moment. Saladin cultivated Sunni religious institutions in Cairo and Fustat, founding colleges for Shafiʿi and Maliki law, and put trusted officers in command of the armies and key offices. Nur al-Din, whose own legitimacy depended on recognition by the Abbasid caliph, pressed him to end the khutba (Friday sermon) in the name of the Ismaili Fatimid caliph. In September 1171 the sermon was changed to name the Abbasid caliph al-Mustadi. The change in caliphal recognition preceded, by only a few days, the death of the last Fatimid caliph, al-Adid, whose sickness was reportedly kept from him until the sermon had been delivered, according to later accounts. The sequence matters: the Abbasid name was introduced first, and al-Adid died afterwards.

Changes followed in administration, army, and patronage. The Fatimid palaces were sealed and their treasures dispersed or sold; the family of al-Adid was confined; and the dynasty's political ceremonial ended. Saladin used members of his own household and trusted emirs to hold key provinces and sent his brother Turan-Shah to conquer Yemen in 1174. He also promoted a Sunni religious establishment: colleges, Sufi lodges (khanqahs), and endowed institutions that gave scholars and soldiers a stake in his government. A Fatimid-sympathizing conspiracy in 1174, led by a court poet, ended in executions.

It would be wrong to imagine the instant disappearance of Egypt's Ismaili population, cultural inheritance, or administrative traditions. Many officials, including Coptic and Muslim scribes, remained in post, and the fiscal system, with its tax registers and land surveys, continued with modifications. The Fatimid institution of land grants for military service informed the later system of iqtaʿ assignments. Ismaili communities persisted in Egypt, Yemen, and Syria, as did Christians and Jews. What ended was the Fatimid caliphate's role as a rival source of religious and political legitimacy. The recovery of a unified Sunni political order within the old Fatimid domains was a major change, but it did not erase the society in which the Fatimids had ruled.

Changing the formula of the Friday sermon was symbolically powerful because the public naming of a caliph acknowledged the source of rightful religious sovereignty. It did not, however, transfer the working institutions of state with a single utterance. Fatimid soldiers had to be controlled, provincial tax officials retained or replaced, endowments redirected, and judicial appointments renegotiated. The steps could be cautious because Saladin still faced both internal opponents and danger from the Latin kingdom. In this setting the end of the Fatimid caliphate represented a decisive constitutional break combined with considerable bureaucratic continuity. It also shows why the survival of Shiʿi and Ismaili communities cannot be inferred simply from the religious identity of the new rulers: different levels of political and social change have different chronologies.

Section 61.12

From Zengid Subordinate to Ayyubid Ruler: Nur al-Din's Death and the Struggle for Syria, 1171–1183

As Saladin consolidated Egypt, tension with Nur al-Din grew. Nur al-Din wanted Egyptian revenue and troops for his own Syrian campaigns and was unwilling to see an independent power in Cairo. Saladin was reluctant to bring his forces to Syria for joint expeditions, as in 1173 when he withdrew from a planned campaign against Kerak, citing concerns about unrest at home. Sources written after the fact claim that Saladin's family debated whether to resist Nur al-Din openly, and that Ayyub urged restraint. These reports reflect later efforts to explain the relationship and cannot be taken as verbatim.

Nur al-Din died in Damascus in May 1174, leaving a young son, al-Salih Ismaʿil, and an untested regency. Saladin entered Damascus in October 1174, at the invitation of part of the city's elite, claiming to protect the heir and to defend the Muslim cause. He took Homs and Hama in the following weeks. But taking Damascus did not make him ruler of Syria. Aleppo, where al-Salih lived under the care of powerful emirs, resisted, and Nur al-Din's nephews at Mosul supported the Zengid cause. At the Horns of Hama in 1175 Saladin defeated Zengid forces; he then took the title of sultan with the Abbasid caliph's recognition. A truce with Aleppo in 1176 left al-Salih in place.

The next several years were marked by campaigns against both Zengid and Latin opponents. Saladin suffered a serious defeat by the young leprous King Baldwin IV at Montgisard in November 1177, but later captured the new Latin fortress at Jacob's Ford in 1179. He also faced attempts on his life by Ismaili assassins, to whom he responded with military pressure in the Syrian highlands. When al-Salih died in December 1181, rival Zengid princes quarreled over Aleppo, which led to the events of 1183 described in the next section. Saladin's rise as an Ayyubid ruler was thus contested and gradual: the Zengid inheritance was divided among many hands, and legitimacy had to be argued, negotiated, and enforced.

At Damascus Saladin had to present his intervention as the protection of Nur al-Din's minor heir rather than a direct usurpation of his former patron's inheritance. Such claims mattered because commanders, judges, and townspeople possessed memories of Nur al-Din's gifts and authority. The competing Zengid court at Aleppo could portray Saladin as an ambitious Egyptian upstart, and some regional leaders preferred that interpretation. A campaign might consequently be accompanied by letters and religious argument as well as siege machinery. Diplomatic recognition, marriage alliances, and the assignment of cities to relatives made progress possible without eliminating the independence of every neighboring prince. The expansion after 1174 was a sequence of bargains and contests, not an automatic unification after Nur al-Din's death.

Division IV · Sections 61.13–61.16

Ayyubid Consolidation, Hattin, Jerusalem, and Diplomacy

Section 61.13

Saladin's Regional Coalition: Aleppo, Mosul, Egypt, Syria, and the Road to 1187

Between 1174 and 1186 Saladin assembled a regional coalition by a combination of force, negotiation, and marriage. His principal resource was Egypt, whose revenues supported troops and gifts; he also assigned commands and revenues to relatives and trusted officers, including his brothers al-Adil and Turan-Shah, his nephews, and his sons. In 1183 he acquired Aleppo from the Zengid prince Imad al-Din Zengi II, who preferred to exchange it for Sinjar and other lands in the Jazira. The agreement followed political and military pressure rather than automatic inheritance, and local feeling in Aleppo was divided.

Mosul was the remaining major Zengid center. Saladin besieged it in 1182 and again in 1185 without success. In 1186 a settlement left Mosul's ruler, Izz al-Din Masʿud, in place but recognized Saladin's suzerainty: Saladin's name appeared in the sermon and on coinage, and the Zengid ruler owed military service. Students should distinguish this from direct annexation. Suzerainty meant that a subordinate ruler governed his territory and sent troops, but remained a separate political actor. Similar arrangements held with Artuqid and other lords in the Jazira and Upper Mesopotamia.

Saladin's expansion should not be presented as a universally welcomed reunification of Muslim territories. His campaigns against Muslim rivals were controversial among contemporaries, and chroniclers disagreed about whether he was protecting the Zengid heir or usurping him. His own letters, drafted by secretaries such as al-Qadi al-Fadil, justified his actions as necessary for jihad and for the Abbasid caliph's honor. Historians treat these as political communications that reveal how he wished to be seen, not as neutral accounts of his motives. Egypt remained the fiscal and military foundation for his ambitions, and it also set limits: he could draw only so much from the Nile valley, and his emirs, many of whom had their own territorial interests, expected rewards.

The Aleppo settlement of 1183 has its own political history. After the death of Nur al-Din's son al-Salih Ismaʿil, rival members of the Zengid house competed over the city's inheritance. A prior exchange among them had made the Aleppine prince Imad al-Din Zengi II particularly concerned with securing Sinjar and other holdings. The 1183 agreement with Saladin should be read against that bargaining, rather than as a peaceful handover from an uncontested ruler. For merchants and governors, the practical questions were whose officials would collect dues, whose garrison would hold the citadel, and whether caravan roads would stay open. At Mosul in 1186 the distinction was even sharper: the ruler remained on his throne, while language of suzerainty and specified obligations recognized Saladin's wider position. Territorial control, religious commemoration in the sermon, and the loyalty of an armed household remained related but separate forms of authority.

Section 61.14

The Campaign of 1187 and the Battle of Hattin

The Kingdom of Jerusalem in the mid-1180s was deeply divided. King Baldwin IV, ill with leprosy, died in 1185, and his nephew Baldwin V died the following year. The crown passed to Sibylla and her husband Guy of Lusignan, whom a faction of barons, including Raymond III of Tripoli, distrusted. Raymond, regent under Baldwin V, had earlier arranged a four-year truce with Saladin. That truce was broken in late 1186 or early 1187 when Reynald of Châtillon, lord of Kerak, attacked a caravan traveling between Egypt and Syria. Saladin demanded redress, Guy could not or would not compel Reynald, and the truce was lost.

Saladin gathered forces from Egypt, Syria, and the Jazira, supported by contingents from allied rulers, and the army assembled east of the Sea of Galilee in the early summer. In May a Muslim reconnaissance force under his son al-Afdal, passing through Raymond's territory by agreement, destroyed a Templar and Hospitaller detachment at the Springs of Cresson, a prelude to the main campaign. In July Saladin attacked Tiberias, whose citadel, held by Raymond's wife Eschiva, was under siege. Guy decided to march to its relief from his base at Saffuriyya, against the advice of Raymond and others who feared that the open country and summer heat would favor the Muslim cavalry.

On 3 July the Latin army moved across a dry plateau without secure water supplies, harassed by mounted archers, and camped for the night near the twin hills called the Horns of Hattin. On 4 July Saladin's troops surrounded them. Contemporary accounts describe thirst, brush fires lit to add to the discomfort of the Latin infantry, repeated charges, and the collapse of cohesion among the army. Guy, Reynald, the Templar master Gerard of Ridefort, and many other leaders were captured, as was the relic of the True Cross. Raymond escaped. Saladin personally executed Reynald, and he had the captured Templars and Hospitallers killed, treating them as especially dangerous enemies. Guy was held and later released.

The battle's logistics, command, and terrain explain the result, not a mere superiority of conviction. Latin leaders chose a risky march, lacked water, and were divided; Saladin had used truce diplomacy, reconnaissance, and a flexible army to draw them onto ground of his choosing. Hattin destroyed the kingdom's field army, which the Latin states could not replace quickly, because the garrisons of many castles had been stripped to form it. The subsequent surrender of the towns followed from that destruction: Tiberias fell, Acre surrendered on 9–10 July, and Jaffa, Sidon, Beirut, Ascalon, and many smaller places followed in the next weeks. Tyre, reinforced by Conrad of Montferrat, held out. The immediate destruction of a field army must therefore be distinguished from the sieges and negotiated surrenders that followed.

Operational decisions at Hattin reveal why geography mattered. The Latin army left the relative security of its water sources and marched in the July heat toward Tiberias. Mounted forces could harass the column, interfere with access to wells, and slow movement toward a defensible position. Saladin's commanders sought to exploit fatigue and disorder, but the sequence is reconstructed from partisan accounts written after the result was known. The defeat cannot be reduced to a single mistaken decision by Guy or to the bravery of one commander. It reflected the interaction of contested leadership among the Latin nobles, Saladin's ability to concentrate regional contingents, and the vulnerability of a large force far from reliable water. Students should treat colorful speeches and precise troop figures with particular caution.

Section 61.15

Saladin's Recovery of Jerusalem, 1187: Siege, Surrender, and Civilian Consequences

Saladin reached Jerusalem on 20 September 1187. The city was crowded with refugees and had few trained fighters. Balian of Ibelin, who had escaped from Hattin, obtained Saladin's permission to enter and bring his family out, and then stayed to organize the defense when the patriarch and the citizens pressed him to do so. After an initial assault failed, Saladin's sappers undermined the northern walls, and Latin leaders recognized that the city could not hold. Balian came out to negotiate. Saladin at first said he would take the city as the Latins had taken it in 1099, but he agreed to terms: the inhabitants could ransom themselves at fixed rates. Muslim sources record that the city surrendered on 2 October 1187, corresponding to 27 Rajab 583.

The chronicles report ransom rates for men, women, and children, and a collective sum for the poor, but their figures and the numbers released differ, and students should treat any precise totals as uncertain. Many inhabitants could not pay. Some were freed through the generosity of Saladin, of his brother al-Adil, or of the Latin leaders and the patriarch Heraclius, who paid for some people; others were led into captivity or enslaved. Latin Christians left under escort to Tyre, Tripoli, or Egyptian ports, and some found the reception hostile. Eastern Christians, including Greek and Syriac believers, were permitted to stay and remained a part of the city's life. The negotiated surrender thus prevented a general massacre comparable to that of July 1099, but ransom, captivity, displacement, and enslavement affected many people, and the burden fell unequally on the poor.

Saladin's government restored Muslim religious institutions. The Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque, which the Latins had used as a church and as a Templar headquarters, were cleansed and reconsecrated; the cross was removed from the Dome, and the minbar commissioned in Aleppo by Nur al-Din was installed in al-Aqsa. Some Latin churches were converted to Muslim use, including the church of St. Anne, which became a madrasa, while the Church of the Holy Sepulchre remained a Christian site under arrangements that allowed pilgrims to visit. The comparison with 1099 is tempting, but it should not become a simple contrast between brutality and perfect clemency. Saladin's terms were negotiated under the shadow of a threat, and they were limited by the needs of his army, the resistance of the defenders, and the expectations of his allies.

The surrender terms are often reported in a graduated schedule: ten dinars for an adult man, five for an adult woman, and two for a child. These are figures preserved in narrative sources, not an audited register of payments, and chroniclers differed about collective arrangements for poorer residents. Balian's negotiation and contributions by Muslim and Christian patrons could release particular groups, while others who could not pay faced captivity or enslavement. Wealth, gender, age, and the availability of assistance therefore shaped an inhabitant's immediate future. The relative absence of a general massacre of the kind documented in 1099 should not obscure such inequalities. Conversely, the existence of captivity does not erase the political significance of a negotiated capitulation that kept much of the city's fabric and population intact.

Section 61.16

The Third Crusade, Richard I, and Saladin's Diplomatic Settlement, 1189–1192

The loss of Jerusalem produced a Latin response: the Third Crusade, whose expedition history, western leadership, and consequences for the Latin states belong to Chapter 62. For Saladin's government, it meant a long campaign on the Palestinian coast. Guy of Lusignan, released in 1188, began the siege of Acre in August 1189, and Saladin's forces besieged the besiegers for nearly two years, strained by supply problems and by the fatigue of troops from distant provinces who expected to return home. Acre surrendered on 12 July 1191, after the arrival of Richard I of England and Philip II of France. When disputes over ransom payments delayed implementation of the surrender terms, Richard ordered the execution of a large group of Muslim prisoners in August.

Richard then marched south along the coast. At Arsuf in September 1191 Saladin's forces failed to break the Latin column, though the Latin army suffered heavy losses. Saladin chose to dismantle Ascalon rather than risk its capture, a decision that shocked many of his followers but reflected his judgment that he could not hold every fortress. Richard rebuilt some coastal positions and pushed toward Jerusalem twice, but he judged that holding the city against Saladin's army would be impossible without a larger force. Meanwhile, negotiations went on through envoys, including Saladin's brother al-Adil and Richard's representatives, and covered the division of the coast, the status of Jerusalem, and even an imaginative proposal of a marriage union.

The settlement of September 1192, commonly called the Treaty of Jaffa and sometimes associated with Ramla, was a truce of about three years. It left a Latin strip of the coast from Tyre to Jaffa, kept Jerusalem and the interior in Saladin's hands, and arranged for Christian pilgrims to visit Jerusalem and other holy places. Ascalon was to be dismantled. Conrad of Montferrat had been assassinated in April 1192, and Henry of Champagne became the Latin leader in Acre. The settlement shows both Saladin's achievements and their limits. He had recovered Jerusalem and most of the interior, but he had not expelled the Latins from the coast, and his coalition was tired. The outcome was a negotiated division, not the final disappearance of Latin Syria.

From Saladin's position, the prolonged struggle for Acre was a fiscal problem as well as a contest of prestige. Keeping armies in the field required grain shipments, horse fodder, naval coordination, and the continued loyalty of emirs who were not salaried servants of a single centralized treasury. The fall of Acre in July 1191 weakened his reputation and exposed the coast, yet the crusaders also faced the difficulty of supplying inland operations. Diplomacy thus grew out of limits experienced on both sides. Messages passed through interpreters and envoys and addressed the practical terms of ports, roads, prisoners, and pilgrim access. The settlement of 1192 was neither the recovery of all Latin territory nor a definitive religious peace; it was a negotiated end to a campaign neither side could complete on its preferred terms.

Ayyubid Government, Society, Succession, and Historical Legacy

Section 61.17

The Ayyubid Political System: Dynastic Principalities, Military Finance, and Provincial Government

The political system that Saladin established and his successors developed is best described as a loosely integrated collection of hereditary principalities held by members of one family, not as a single centralized imperial bureaucracy. Egypt was the largest and richest unit, with its revenues organized through a bureaucracy that Saladin had inherited from the Fatimids and staffed with experienced secretaries, notably al-Qadi al-Fadil, who directed his chancery. Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, Baalbek, Kerak, Yemen, and territories in the Jazira were held by sons, brothers, nephews, and other relatives, each with his own household, army, and local alliances.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqtaʿ

An iqtaʿ was an assignment of the right to collect revenue from a defined area, given to a military officer in return for service and the upkeep of troops. Developed under the Buyids and expanded by the Seljuks (Chapters 53 and 58), the system spread across Syria and Egypt. The holder generally did not own the land and could be moved, but powerful officers sought stability and passed rights to protégés. Ayyubid rulers used iqtaʿs to pay armies without cash and to reward kin and loyal emirs, but the system also gave soldiers a stake in local revenue and could weaken a ruler's direct control of the treasury.

Military finance relied on iqtaʿ assignments, cash payments from the treasury, and the household followers of each prince, including mamluks, military slaves bought and trained for service. Saladin's own mamluks and his relatives' soldiers formed the core of his army, alongside Kurdish, Turkmen, and Arab contingents. Provincial governors collected taxes through the established Fatimid and Seljuk offices, issued coinage, and arranged for the Friday sermon in the name of the Abbasid caliph and the leading Ayyubid. Relations among the principal centers depended on family seniority. The leading ruler, usually in Cairo, was acknowledged as senior, but his authority over other branches depended on persuasion, military strength, and the expectations of kinship, and could be tested in every crisis.

The system had advantages. It spread risk, provided trusted governors in widely separated regions, tied family members to the dynasty's success, and permitted local adaptation. It also had weaknesses. Princes fought for shares of territory, military leaders switched allegiance, and succession disputes could produce civil war. The same decentralization that made the Ayyubid state flexible made it difficult to concentrate resources for long campaigns, and Saladin's relatives sometimes pursued their own objectives. Historians therefore describe the Ayyubid polity as a family confederation whose cohesion depended on its members' sense of shared interest.

Administration varied from province to province. A fiscal order written in Cairo might be implemented through established Egyptian clerks and survey practices, while an appointment in northern Syria depended on the emirs, judges, and city families already entrenched there. Military revenue assignments could fund soldiers without transferring full ownership of the soil, as Chapter 58 explained, but the rights exercised by an assignee required scrutiny in each locality. Provincial governors were expected to sustain garrisons, account for revenue, protect routes, and show appropriate dynastic loyalty. When a prince died, the same administrative network could serve his successor or become an asset contested by relatives. The Ayyubids therefore inherited governmental capacity along with administrative limits: the dynastic family did not have to invent fiscal offices, but it could not command uniform obedience by kinship alone.

Section 61.18

Ayyubid Society: Religious Institutions, Women, Commerce, and Urban Life

Ayyubid society rested on agriculture, trade, and urban crafts. In Egypt the Nile flood determined the harvest and the tax base, and peasants paid land taxes to officials or to iqtaʿ holders. In Syria rain-fed grain, olives, fruit, and irrigated gardens, such as those of the Damascus oasis, supported cities and armies. Merchants moved goods through Alexandria, Damascus, and Aleppo, and Italian city-states such as Pisa, Genoa, and Venice traded with Egypt under agreements that Saladin's government confirmed, including a treaty with Pisa in 1173. The Spanish Muslim traveler Ibn Jubayr, who visited Egypt, Mecca, and Syria in 1183–1185, described the busy ports and markets and reported the abolition of a specific levy at the Red Sea port of Aydhab and the arrangements that replaced it, though one must read his admiring account with caution. Red Sea trade, including the spice and luxury commerce associated with the Karimi merchants, linked Cairo to Yemen and India.

Religious and educational institutions flourished. Madrasas, founded by rulers, emirs, and wealthy patrons and supported by waqf endowments, trained jurists and officials, and they multiplied in Damascus, Aleppo, and Cairo. Sufi lodges (khanqahs) offered communal worship and hospitality; Saladin converted a former Fatimid palace into the khanqah Saʿid al-Suʿada in Cairo. Michael Chamberlain's study of Damascus shows how families of scholars used these institutions to build local influence, intermarry, and sustain themselves through generations, and how patronage by the military elite made the ulama partners in governance. Medical institutions, including hospitals in Damascus and Cairo and the physicians who worked in them, were part of this scholarly network.

Not all inhabitants shared the same position. Sunni scholars were the main beneficiaries of Ayyubid patronage. Shiʿi Muslims, including Twelver communities in Aleppo and Syria and Ismailis in Egypt, Yemen, and the Syrian mountains, faced a political order that favored Sunni institutions, though many continued to live and worship. Christians, including Greek Orthodox, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic communities, paid the jizya tax under Muslim law and worked as scribes, physicians, merchants, and farmers. Jewish communities, such as that of Fustat, whose records survive in the Geniza, also flourished; the philosopher and physician Moses Maimonides, who lived in Fustat, was associated with the medical and patronage circle of al-Qadi al-Fadil, Saladin's influential chief secretary and adviser. A letter of about 1191 documents his high standing as a physician among Egyptian elites; the chronology and formal title of his communal leadership remain matters for careful historical interpretation. The relationship between rulers and religious minorities varied with place and time, and the evidence does not support a picture either of uniform tolerance or of uniform persecution.

Women of the ruling households took part in public patronage. Sitt al-Sham, Saladin's sister, founded colleges and a tomb complex in Damascus, including the Shamiyya madrasa, whose architectural and documentary records show her use of waqf endowments to support legal education, and she was buried in that complex. Other female relatives of rulers founded religious and charitable institutions in Damascus, Aleppo, and Cairo, and women could hold property, create endowments, and influence succession. This evidence concerns elite women. Evidence for ordinary women, enslaved women, and the wives of peasants and artisans is scarce and mostly indirect, from legal texts and incidental references. The same limitation applies to cultivators, artisans, soldiers, enslaved laborers, and urban households. Most of what we know about them comes from sources written by elites and must be read against the grain.

Ibn Jubayr's praise of Saladin's fiscal measures should be connected to the actual route he described. He reported the removal of a particular burdensome exaction on pilgrims passing through Aydhab on the Red Sea, together with an alternative arrangement for sustaining the route. It would be inaccurate to conclude that all fees on pilgrims everywhere disappeared. His observations tell us how one educated traveler perceived reform and which abuses he considered important. Similarly, the commercial agreement with Pisa in 1173 shows that Muslim rulers could seek maritime trade with Latin merchants even amid warfare; it does not establish unrestricted peace between governments. Such cases offer concrete evidence of economic pragmatism alongside political and religious rivalry.

Everyday Life and Social History

Markets, Taxes, Hospitals, Captivity, and Charity

In Cairo and Damascus the market was the center of urban life: merchants and artisans worked in specialized streets, hired workers, and paid fees and taxes that supported the state and its soldiers. Cultivators in the Nile valley and in Syrian villages paid land taxes in kind or cash, and their fortunes depended on the harvest and on the demands of local officials and iqtaʿ holders. Hospitals such as Nur al-Din's in Damascus offered treatment funded by endowments, and the physicians' training linked medical practice to written scholarship.

War brought other experiences. Soldiers served on long campaigns and expected pay and booty. After Hattin and during the capture of coastal towns, many Latin Christians became captives; in Jerusalem the ransom system meant that wealthier families could buy freedom while poorer residents risked enslavement. Refugees streamed from the coast to Tyre and other ports. Women in elite families endowed colleges and charities, while many other women, including enslaved domestic workers, left few records. Historians must reconstruct these lives from endowment deeds, legal opinions, chronicles' passing remarks, the Geniza papers, and archaeology.

Section 61.19

Saladin's Death in 1193, Ayyubid Succession, and Regional Politics after the Third Crusade

Saladin died at Damascus on 4 March 1193, only months after the truce with Richard, and was buried near the Umayyad Mosque. Sources report that he left little wealth, having spent heavily on soldiers and gifts. His territories were divided according to the principle of shared family rule: his eldest surviving son al-Afdal took Damascus and its dependencies; al-Aziz Uthman took Egypt; al-Zahir Ghazi held Aleppo; and other relatives held places such as Hama, Homs, Kerak, and Yemen. His brother al-Adil, a skilled administrator and negotiator, held lands in the Jazira and Transjordan and had great influence in the family.

The 1190s were marked by dynastic competition rather than collapse. Al-Afdal and al-Aziz quarreled over the balance of authority, and in 1194–1195 al-Adil mediated and then sided with al-Aziz. In 1196 al-Adil took Damascus, sending al-Afdal to a smaller lordship. When al-Aziz died in November 1198 after a riding accident, leaving a young son, the Egyptian emirs invited al-Afdal to serve as regent. Al-Adil, however, campaigned against his nephew, and in 1200 entered Cairo and took the Egyptian sultanate himself. He placed his own sons in provincial governorships, notably al-Kamil in Egypt, and made himself the dynasty's senior ruler.

The dynasty did not collapse after Saladin's death; its branches reorganized. Al-Adil's accession was a major development, because it created a new center of seniority and shifted the family's weight from Saladin's sons to his brother's line. In Aleppo, al-Zahir Ghazi ruled until 1216 and developed the city's institutions. The Latin states, weakened by the loss of Jerusalem but sustained by the coastal strip and by crusading reinforcements, remained neighbors, with whom al-Adil negotiated truces. Later crusades, al-Adil's reign, and the mature thirteenth-century system are introduced here only as transitions: Chapter 62 treats the later crusading expeditions and Latin states, and later chapters follow the Ayyubids' eventual replacement by the Mamluks (Chapter 65).

One reason the Ayyubid dynasty endured was that succession could be negotiated even after severe rivalry. The senior prince who controlled Egypt commanded unusually rich fiscal resources, but his relatives retained troops, endowments, and regional allies that he had to respect. Al-Adil's achievement was not the simple revival of an undivided empire: it was a rebalancing of the confederation in which the next generation could be placed strategically in Cairo, Damascus, and other centers. Urban notables were affected because each change of prince could bring new governors, military obligations, and patronage opportunities, while established judges and secretaries often provided continuity. This pattern foreshadowed thirteenth-century Ayyubid diplomacy, which the next chapter approaches from the perspective of the continuing Latin states.

Section 61.20

Historical Synthesis: Zengid Consolidation, Ayyubid State Formation, and the Transformation of the Medieval Middle East

The rise of Zengi, the institutions of Nur al-Din, the Kurdish military networks of Shirkuh and Ayyub, Saladin's consolidation, the recovery of Jerusalem, and the Ayyubid political order form one connected story. It began in a divided landscape and moved through successive consolidations: Zengi's regional military power, Nur al-Din's union of Aleppo and Damascus with a program of Sunni patronage, the Egyptian expeditions that placed a Syrian-trained army in Cairo, and Saladin's creation of a family confederation stretching from Yemen to the Jazira. Each stage depended on cities, revenues, and negotiations as well as on battles.

Jihad rhetoric was an important part of this story, but it was not the sole explanation for military campaigns or dynastic legitimacy. Scholars such as al-Sulami and Ibn Asakir, preachers, poets, and secretaries supplied a vocabulary of religious purpose that rulers used to justify taxation, recruitment, and war, including war on Muslim rivals. The same rulers made truces with Latin neighbors, accepted ransoms, and negotiated with Richard. Military conflict and diplomacy coexisted across religious boundaries, as they had since 1099. The continued existence of a Latin coastal strip after 1192 reflects how much of the twelfth-century Middle East was shaped by compromise.

Zengid and Ayyubid rulers adapted rather than invented their institutions. The atabeg system, iqtaʿ assignments, madrasas, and loyalty to the Abbasid caliph came from the Seljuk world; the Egyptian fiscal bureaucracy and many of its officials came from the Fatimids; the ideal of the just ruler and patron of scholars came from long Islamic traditions. What the new dynasties added was a more effective combination of these elements under rulers who controlled both Syria and Egypt, and a political presentation that tied dynastic authority to the defense of Muslim holy places.

Saladin's reputation requires special care. Contemporary writers such as Imad al-Din and Ibn Shaddad, who depended on him, praised his piety, generosity, and mercy. Later writers elaborated these themes, and European authors turned him into a chivalrous enemy, a figure used in medieval and modern literature, including Walter Scott's The Talisman (1825). In the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries Arab, Turkish, and Kurdish nationalists and political leaders claimed him for their own purposes; Kaiser Wilhelm II's visit to his tomb in Damascus in 1898 and its restoration are part of that modern history. The historical Saladin made hard political choices: he fought Muslim rivals, executed some prisoners, accepted ransom for others, and negotiated a truce that left the Latin coast in place. He was neither a flawless nor a universally merciful ruler, and his achievements are better understood through his decisions than through idealization.

The final historical conclusion is that the strength of Ayyubid rule depended on flexible regional political networks and institutional continuity, even though competing dynastic interests limited centralization. This structure allowed the dynasty to survive the death of its founder, to adapt to changing circumstances, and to leave institutions in Damascus, Aleppo, and Cairo that outlasted its political authority. It also set the stage for the thirteenth century, when continuing Latin presence, new crusading expeditions, and the Mongol advance (Chapter 63) would test the Ayyubid confederation in new ways.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Inherited Institutions and New Patronage

What Changed

  • The Fatimid caliphate ended as a political institution, and Egypt's rulers recognized the Abbasid caliph
  • Egypt and Syria came under a single family confederation
  • Jerusalem returned to Muslim rule in 1187, while a Latin coastal strip remained
  • Sunni colleges, hospitals, and Sufi lodges expanded under Zengid and Ayyubid patronage

What Stayed the Same

  • Iqtaʿ assignments and military households continued to structure armies and revenue
  • Many Fatimid-era officials and fiscal practices persisted in Egypt
  • Egyptian agriculture, Syrian trade, and Red Sea commerce remained the economic foundation
  • Religious diversity continued, including Ismaili, Christian, and Jewish communities

Why the pattern matters: The Ayyubid achievement was an adaptation of inherited institutions to new political circumstances, which explains both its resilience and its limits.

Cause and Effect

Fragmentation, Opportunity, and the Recovery of Jerusalem

Long-term causes

Seljuk fragmentation, atabeg rule, the weakness of the Fatimid state, and the small size of the Latin states created a competitive environment in which military commanders could build regional power.

Short-term causes

The Fatimid crisis of the 1160s, the deaths of Nur al-Din and Baldwin IV, the Latin succession crisis of 1185–1186, and Reynald's attack on a caravan in 1186–1187 created specific openings.

Immediate effects

The destruction of the Latin field army at Hattin and the surrender of most Latin towns, including Jerusalem, followed within months.

Long-term and regional consequences

The Third Crusade and a coastal Latin presence, a family confederation under Saladin's heirs, and a changed religious geography in Jerusalem shaped the thirteenth century.

Historical complexity: No single cause, whether religious zeal, personal ambition, or Latin weakness, explains the outcome; the combination of fiscal resources, diplomacy, institutional adaptation, and opportunity does.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Muslim political transformation of Syria, the Jazira, and Egypt between about 1100 and 1200. After the First Crusade the region was divided among Seljuk-descended princes, atabegs, Artuqid lords, the Abbasid caliph, and the Fatimid caliph's viziers. The Latin states survived their first half-century largely because their neighbors competed with one another, and Muslim rulers fought, negotiated with, and sometimes allied with the Latin states according to local interest.

Muslim religious responses to Latin rule began early. Al-Sulami's teaching of jihad in Damascus around 1105 shows that scholars interpreted the conquests in moral and political terms decades before Zengi. But a vocabulary of jihad was not a coalition, and expeditions in the 1110s achieved little. The early evidence cautions against assuming that religious language automatically produced unified action.

Zengi built a regional power by combining Mosul and Aleppo, and he spent much of his career fighting Muslim rivals. His capture of Edessa in 1144 ended Latin rule there, with different consequences for Latin, Armenian, and Syriac Christians, and prompted the Second Crusade, which failed before Damascus in 1148. The victory raised the prestige of his house.

After Zengi's assassination in 1146, his territories were divided. Nur al-Din secured Aleppo, defeated Antioch at Inab, and in 1154 acquired Damascus through pressure and negotiation with local elites. His government combined justice, Sunni patronage, colleges, and the Damascus hospital. Evidence from inscriptions, buildings, and endowments is firmer than the later anecdotes of exemplary piety.

Fatimid Egypt in the 1160s was an established but weakened system, with a child caliph, rival viziers, and a divided army. King Amalric's interventions and Nur al-Din's concern about Latin influence led to Shirkuh's three expeditions. Kurdish military households, which served alongside Turks, Arabs, and others, were important in this process, but the Ayyubid dynasty was a medieval household and not a modern national movement.

Saladin became vizier in March 1169, after Shirkuh's brief vizierate and death. In September 1171 the Abbasid caliph was named in the Friday sermon, just before the death of al-Adid ended Fatimid rule. Administrative continuity, religious diversity, and Ismaili communities survived, even as the Fatimid caliphate ended.

After Nur al-Din's death in 1174, Saladin entered Damascus and then fought for years to establish his position in Syria. Aleppo came to him by exchange in 1183, and Mosul recognized his suzerainty in 1186. These were negotiated and contested settlements, not smooth reunification.

Hattin on 4 July 1187 destroyed the Latin field army, and Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October after Balian's negotiations. The ransom system spared the city a general massacre but left many inhabitants captive, enslaved, or displaced. The Third Crusade and the truce of 1192 showed the limits of Saladin's achievement, which left a Latin coastal strip.

Saladin's death in 1193 led to dynastic reorganization, not collapse. Al-Adil's rise, culminating in 1200, created the mature Ayyubid confederation, a family system of principalities supported by iqtaʿ and Egyptian revenue. The period's legacy includes institutions, a changed religious geography, and a powerful but idealized memory of Saladin.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how political change in the medieval Middle East could be gradual, negotiated, and uneven, even when it culminated in a dramatic event. Jerusalem's recovery in 1187 was a major achievement, but it followed decades of consolidation and compromise, and it left a Latin coastal strip in place.

The chapter also shows the importance of institutions. The Zengids and Ayyubids governed through inherited Seljuk and Fatimid structures, such as iqtaʿ assignments, chanceries, and colleges, and through new patronage of hospitals, madrasas, and Sufi lodges. Their cities, scholars, merchants, and diverse communities made their rule possible.

Finally, the chapter shows that history is shaped by memory. Admiring chroniclers, European romancers, and modern nationalists have all reshaped the image of Nur al-Din and Saladin. Distinguishing documented decisions from later idealization is an essential skill for the study of the whole region.

The Ayyubid confederation would soon face new challenges: later crusades and the Latin states of the eastern Mediterranean (Chapter 62), the Mongol conquests and the fall of Baghdad (Chapter 63), and the Mamluk seizure of power in Egypt (Chapter 65). Understanding the compromises and structures of the twelfth century is essential for understanding how the region met them.
