Part IV — Egypt, Anatolia, the Levant, and the Bronze Age International System
15

Hittites, Hurrians, Canaanites, and the Bronze Age Levant

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the archives of Hattusa, the Hittite capital on the Anatolian plateau, and in the ruins of Ugarit on the Syrian coast, excavators have recovered thousands of clay tablets written in half a dozen languages and several different scripts — cuneiform syllabic writing borrowed from Mesopotamia, Anatolian hieroglyphs generally used to write the Luwian language, and, closely associated with Ugarit in particular, an alphabetic cuneiform system whose precise origins remain debated among specialists. No single one of these archives, by itself, could explain the Late Bronze Age Near East. Read together, they reveal a world of kings who addressed one another as "brother," diplomats who negotiated marriages across a thousand miles, merchants whose ships carried copper from Cyprus and tin whose ultimate sources are still argued over by archaeologists, and treaties whose broken clauses could mean war.

Chapters 13 and 14 traced Egypt's transformation from a unified river-valley kingdom into an imperial power projecting force into the Levant. This chapter turns to the peoples and states that Egyptian pharaohs encountered, fought, married into, and negotiated with beyond the Nile Valley: the Hittites of central Anatolia, whose kings built one of the era's most formidable military and administrative systems; the Hurrians, a population whose language groups with Urartian in a small and otherwise isolated family, and whose political expression, the kingdom of Mitanni, briefly rivaled Egypt and the Hittites for control of northern Syria; and the city-states of the Levantine coast and interior, whose rulers wrote anxious letters to Egyptian pharaohs even as their merchants profited from a Mediterranean trading world stretching from the Aegean to Mesopotamia.

This chapter examines one of the earliest extensively documented international diplomatic systems in world history — not the first such system in any absolute sense, since the eighteenth-century BCE archives of Mari, examined briefly in Chapter 12, already preserve thousands of letters showing many of the same diplomatic conventions four centuries earlier, but a system whose internal workings, across a wider set of interacting great powers, can be reconstructed in unusually rich documentary detail. The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean and Near East was not a set of isolated civilizations that occasionally collided. It was a network of interdependent courts, cities, and trade routes bound together by treaties, royal marriages, gift exchange, shared administrative language, and a common diplomatic vocabulary, even as its constituent states remained fierce military rivals.

Anatolia's Hittites present a particular challenge to the assumptions introduced in Chapter 1: the term "Middle East" did not exist for them, and neither, in any meaningful sense, did later categories such as "Turkey" or a specifically Anatolian political identity distinguishable from other regional identities. The Hittites were an Indo-European-speaking population that had settled in Anatolia over an already ancient substratum of Hattian and other populations, and their kingdom absorbed elements of Hattian, Hurrian, Mesopotamian, and local Anatolian culture into a distinctive royal ideology and legal tradition. Understanding the Hittites on their own terms, rather than as a preview of later Anatolian history, is essential to this chapter's approach.

The Hurrians and their kingdom of Mitanni introduce a further complication: a major regional power whose internal administrative archive has never been excavated, whose capital city has never been definitively located, and whose history must therefore be reconstructed largely from the records of its rivals, its allies, and its eventual conquerors. Mitanni's difficult evidentiary record is itself an important historical lesson about how unevenly the ancient Near East's history survives to be studied, and this chapter gives that kingdom, and the debated evidence for an Indo-Aryan element within it, dedicated attention rather than treating it as a footnote to Hittite or Egyptian history.

The city-states of the Levant, by contrast, are unusually well documented for exactly the reason introduced in Chapter 14: their rulers wrote frequently, and often desperately, to the Egyptian court that claimed authority over them, and roughly three hundred and eighty of these letters, discovered at Amarna in Egypt, survive to describe a fragmented political landscape of competing city rulers, local wars, banditry, and appeals for military support that Egyptian administration did not always provide. Chapter 14 already introduced this correspondence from the Egyptian side; this chapter instead foregrounds Hittite treaty documents and the documentary world of Ugarit, sources that let the region's other major participants speak in their own administrative and legal voices.

Finally, this chapter gives dedicated attention to Ugarit, a coastal city whose scribes left behind not only administrative and commercial records but also religious and literary texts that illuminate the religious and social world of the Northwest Semitic Levant more directly than almost any other Bronze Age source. Ugarit's cosmopolitan population, its participation in long-distance maritime trade, and its eventual destruction at the end of the Bronze Age make it one of this chapter's central case studies and a bridge to Chapter 16's examination of the wider system's collapse.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political and cultural landscape of Anatolia and the Levant before the consolidation of the Hittite kingdom and the major Canaanite city-states.
  2. Trace the formation of the Old Hittite Kingdom and explain the succession crises and reforms that shaped early Hittite kingship.
  3. Explain the institutions of the Hittite Empire, including its kingship, law code, treaty practice, and provincial administration, and distinguish Old Kingdom from Empire-period institutions.
  4. Identify the Hurrians as a distinct linguistic and cultural population and explain the rise and structure of the kingdom of Mitanni.
  5. Evaluate the evidence for Indo-Aryan linguistic and religious elements within Mitanni and the scholarly debate over their significance.
  6. Explain how Suppiluliuma I's campaigns transformed Mitanni's position and reorganized Hittite control over northern Syria.
  7. Analyze the political fragmentation of the Levant into competing city-states and their relationship to Egyptian overlordship.
  8. Describe Ugarit's economy, society, religion, and script, and explain both the significance and the limits of the city as a source for the wider Northwest Semitic world.
  9. Analyze the diplomatic conventions binding Egypt, Hatti, Mitanni, Babylonia, and Assyria, including treaties, royal correspondence, and dynastic marriage.
  10. Explain the causes and terms of the Battle of Kadesh and the subsequent Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty from a multicausal, multi-sided perspective.
  11. Evaluate the limitations and biases of the surviving evidence, including Hittite treaty archives, Ugaritic texts, and the Amarna letters, for reconstructing this period.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Hittite kingdom emerge from, and eventually govern, a diverse Anatolian population encompassing multiple languages and older local traditions?
  2. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct Hittite kingship, law, and administration in unusual institutional detail, and how did those institutions change between the Old Kingdom and the Empire period?
  3. Why does so much of what is known about the Hurrians and Mitanni come from outside sources rather than the kingdom's own administrative records?
  4. What can and cannot be concluded from the Indo-Aryan linguistic and religious evidence associated with Mitanni's ruling circles?
  5. How did Suppiluliuma I's campaigns against Mitanni reshape the political map of northern Syria?
  6. Why did the Levant remain politically fragmented into competing city-states rather than forming a unified kingdom?
  7. What does Ugarit's cosmopolitan population and trade reveal about the interconnected character of the Late Bronze Age Mediterranean and Near East, and what are the limits of treating Ugarit as representative of the wider region?
  8. How did diplomatic marriage, gift exchange, and treaty-making function as tools of great-power relations, and what did smaller states gain and risk from participating in this system?
  9. What combination of military, political, and strategic factors led Egypt and Hatti from war at Kadesh to a negotiated peace roughly fifteen years later?

Historical Context

Chapter 14 examined New Kingdom Egypt's military and diplomatic expansion into the Levant following the expulsion of the Hyksos, establishing Egyptian claims over Levantine city-states as far north as the Syrian coast. That expansion did not occur in a vacuum. By the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BCE, Egypt operated within a genuinely multipolar system whose other major participants — the Hittite kingdom in Anatolia, the kingdom of Mitanni in northern Syria and upper Mesopotamia, and, more distantly, Babylonia and Assyria in Mesopotamia — possessed their own royal ideologies, administrative traditions, military capacities, and diplomatic protocols. This chapter examines those other participants and the smaller Levantine polities caught between them, reserving the fuller assembly of the whole interconnected system for Chapter 16's account of its collapse.

Anatolia in this period had no political unity comparable to Egypt's Nile Valley kingdom. Instead, a patchwork of city-states and small kingdoms, some tracing their origins to the earlier Hattian population examined archaeologically at sites such as Kanesh and Alaca Höyük, gradually came under the control of an Indo-European-speaking dynasty centered on the city of Hattusa, in north-central Anatolia. The resulting Hittite kingdom absorbed Hattian religious and cultural elements even as its royal family spoke a distinct Indo-European language, Nesite, conventionally called Hittite by modern scholars.

To the southeast, in the region encompassing upper Mesopotamia and northern Syria, a Hurrian-speaking population had long been present in smaller polities before coalescing, by the sixteenth century BCE, into the kingdom of Mitanni. Hurrian belongs, together with the later-attested Urartian language of the Armenian highlands, to a small family conventionally called Hurro-Urartian; proposed connections to more distant language families remain unproven. Mitanni's ruling circles are further associated with a layer of Indo-Aryan personal names and specialized vocabulary, a combination examined in detail in Section 15.5.

The Levant itself, examined geographically in Chapter 2 as a narrow corridor between the Mediterranean and the desert lacking a single dominant river, supported dozens of city-states — Byblos, Ugarit, Qatna, Kadesh, Megiddo, Jerusalem, Gezer, and many others — whose rulers periodically owed tribute or allegiance to whichever larger power, Egyptian, Hittite, or Mitannian, could project the most effective military and diplomatic pressure into the region at a given moment. This chapter examines how these local rulers navigated great-power rivalry, and how their cities functioned as economic and cultural centers in their own right rather than merely as pawns in larger contests.

Section 15.1

Anatolia and the Levant before the Great Kingdoms

Long before the Hittite dynasty consolidated power at Hattusa, the Anatolian plateau supported a diverse array of populations and small polities documented in some of the earliest surviving Anatolian written sources: the archives of Assyrian merchant colonies, known as karum, established at sites such as Kanesh in the nineteenth and eighteenth centuries BCE. These merchant archives, written in Old Assyrian cuneiform, record commercial transactions between Assyrian traders and local Anatolian rulers and communities rather than an indigenous Anatolian bureaucracy, but they nonetheless preserve some of the earliest attested Hittite, Luwian, and Hattian personal and place names, allowing historians to trace the presence of Indo-European-speaking populations in Anatolia well before the emergence of a unified Hittite state.

The Hattians, whose language is unrelated to the Indo-European family and whose name gave the later kingdom its geographic designation "Hatti," represented an older substratum population whose religious traditions, including major deities and cultic centers, were substantially incorporated into later Hittite religion rather than displaced by it. This pattern — a ruling dynasty and elite speaking an intrusive Indo-European language governing a population whose religious and cultural traditions predated their arrival — should caution students against assuming that "the Hittites" refers to a single ethnically or linguistically homogeneous population; Hittite civilization was, from its formation, a layered synthesis of Hattian, Hittite (Nesite), Luwian, Hurrian, and other Anatolian elements.

The archaeological record from sites such as Alaca Höyük, with its elaborate royal tombs containing metalwork of exceptional craftsmanship, indicates that complex, stratified societies existed in central Anatolia well before written Hittite records begin, though connecting these earlier material remains to specific named populations known from later texts remains difficult and, in many cases, uncertain.

The Levant, meanwhile, entered this period already dotted with walled towns and small polities whose roots extended back through the Middle Bronze Age, examined in Chapter 14 in connection with the Hyksos, without ever consolidating into a single Levantine kingdom. This persistent political fragmentation, examined further in Section 15.7, is a structural feature of Levantine history across the entire period this chapter covers, not a temporary condition awaiting unification.

Section 15.2

The Old Hittite Kingdom

The Old Hittite Kingdom is conventionally dated to its formation under Hattusili I, who established or consolidated Hattusa as his capital and campaigned aggressively into northern Syria, bringing Hittite forces into conflict with city-states such as Aleppo. His successor, Mursili I, extended these campaigns dramatically, leading a Hittite army down the Euphrates to sack Babylon around 1595 BCE — a raid that ended the Old Babylonian dynasty descended from Hammurabi, examined in Chapter 12, but that did not result in sustained Hittite control over Mesopotamia. The episode illustrates an important feature of early Hittite military capability: the kingdom could project devastating force across enormous distances, but its early political institutions struggled to translate military success into durable territorial administration.

That institutional weakness became apparent in the succession crises that followed. Mursili I was assassinated by a relative shortly after his return from Babylon, beginning a pattern of court intrigue, assassination, and disputed succession that historians describe as characteristic of the kingdom's early decades. This instability culminated in the reign of King Telipinu, who issued an edict, preserved in later copies, that regulated royal succession and addressed the responsibilities of palace personnel and the royal council, or panku, in suppressing the dynastic violence that had destabilized earlier reigns. The Edict of Telipinu is among the most important surviving Hittite political documents, both for what it reveals about actual succession practices and for what it reveals about how Hittite kings themselves understood and sought to correct their kingdom's political vulnerabilities. Separately from the panku's role in this edict, the tawananna — the formal title of the Hittite queen — held significant, often lifelong religious and political authority in her own right across much of Hittite history, an office whose powers were not created by Telipinu's edict but that the edict's broader concern with orderly succession helps illuminate.

Following Telipinu's reign, Hittite power entered a period of relative eclipse, sometimes called the Middle Kingdom, during which the kingdom faced pressure from Kaska tribal populations to the north, Hurrian and Mitannian expansion to the southeast, and internal political fragmentation, setting the stage for the reorganization and expansion examined in Section 15.3.

Section 15.3

The Hittite Empire: Kingship, Law, and Institutions

Beginning with Tudhaliya I/II in the fifteenth century BCE and reaching its fullest development under Suppiluliuma I in the fourteenth century BCE, the Hittite kingdom entered what modern scholars call its New Kingdom or Empire period, marked by renewed military expansion, administrative reorganization, and the decisive campaigns against Mitanni examined in Sections 15.5 and 15.6. Hittite kingship in this period combined military leadership, religious authority as chief priest of the storm god and other deities, and a legal role as supreme judge. The panku, so important to the succession politics of the Old Kingdom discussed in Section 15.2, is only sparsely attested in Empire-period sources and appears to have functioned, where it functioned at all, as a body subordinate to and convened by the king rather than as an institution that constrained royal authority in a constitutional sense; students should not imagine Hittite Empire-period kingship as checked by a standing council in the way the Old Kingdom evidence might suggest.

Hittite kingship also operated within established legal traditions reflected in the collection conventionally called the Hittite Laws, a set of case-based legal rulings covering matters including personal injury, property, marriage, and agricultural disputes, notable for its relatively restrained use of capital punishment and mutilation compared with some contemporary Mesopotamian law collections. The king functioned as supreme judge within this legal tradition rather than as a ruler bound by an external institution capable of constraining him in a constitutional sense. The Hittite state governed a large and ethnically diverse territory through a combination of directly administered core provinces and a network of vassal kingdoms bound to the Hittite king through treaties, examined further in Section 15.10.

Hittite administrative correspondence, preserved in the royal archives at Hattusa, reveals a sophisticated bureaucracy capable of managing military logistics, diplomatic correspondence in multiple languages, religious festivals, and legal appeals across a geographically extensive and politically heterogeneous empire. Hittite scribes were trained in cuneiform script and in multiple languages, including Akkadian, the diplomatic lingua franca discussed in Section 15.10, alongside the native Hittite (Nesite) language and Luwian, spoken widely in southern and western Anatolia and written, in monumental Empire-period inscriptions, in a distinct hieroglyphic script conventionally called Anatolian or Hieroglyphic Luwian.

Hittite religion, examined further in Section 15.9, has sometimes been described by modern scholars as a "thousand gods of Hatti," reflecting the kingdom's practice of incorporating local deities from conquered or allied territories into an expanding state pantheon rather than imposing religious uniformity — a pattern with significant implications for how the Hittite state managed its diverse population and one that should caution students against assuming ancient empires necessarily sought religious homogeneity.

Section 15.4

The Hurrians and the Kingdom of Mitanni

The Hurrians appear in Mesopotamian and Syrian records from at least the late third millennium BCE, but they achieved their most significant political expression in the kingdom of Mitanni, which coalesced by the sixteenth century BCE across a broad territory encompassing northern Syria and upper Mesopotamia, with its likely core centered somewhere along the upper Khabur river system, though the location of its capital, Washukanni, has not been securely identified archaeologically. At its height in the fifteenth century BCE, Mitanni contested control of northern Syria with Egypt, drawing Egyptian and Mitannian forces into repeated conflict before the two powers concluded a peace settlement reinforced by dynastic marriage, examined further in Section 15.10.

Hurrian was prominent among Mitanni's population and court culture and is directly attested in major diplomatic and religious evidence connected to the kingdom, including personal names, deity names, and administrative vocabulary preserved in outside archives at Hattusa, Amarna, and elsewhere. It is important, however, not to overstate the precision of this picture: because Mitanni's own capital has never been excavated, the kingdom's internal administrative archive — the kind of source base that has allowed historians to reconstruct Hittite institutions in such detail in Sections 15.2 and 15.3 — remains almost entirely unknown, and much of what can be said about Mitannian society depends on inference from the records of its neighbors and eventual conquerors. A further distinctive layer of evidence, examined in Section 15.5, consists of Indo-Aryan personal names and specialized vocabulary associated with Mitanni's ruling circles, a pattern that has generated extensive and still unresolved scholarly discussion.

Section 15.5

Indo-Aryan Elements in Mitanni: Evidence and Debate

Among the most discussed puzzles in Late Bronze Age history is the appearance, within a kingdom in which Hurrian was prominent in the attested population and court culture, of a distinct layer of evidence pointing toward Indo-Aryan language and religion. Three kinds of evidence support this observation. First, a number of Mitannian royal names, including those of several kings, appear to derive from Indo-Aryan rather than Hurrian roots. Second, a treatise on training chariot horses attributed to a certain Kikkuli, preserved among the Hittite archives at Hattusa, uses specialized technical vocabulary — including numeral-based terms for stages of training — that scholars have identified as Indo-Aryan rather than Hurrian or Hittite. Third, and most strikingly, the treaty concluded between the Hittite king Suppiluliuma I and the Mitannian prince Shattiwaza, examined further in Section 15.6, invokes among its divine witnesses a set of deities corresponding closely to Mitra, Varuna, Indra, and the Nasatya twins — deities also known from the Vedic religious tradition of South Asia.

What this evidence establishes with reasonable confidence is that Indo-Aryan linguistic and religious elements were present, and evidently prestigious, within Mitanni's ruling circles. What remains genuinely debated is the social and political significance of that presence: whether it reflects a distinct ruling lineage or warrior elite of Indo-Aryan origin superimposed on a Hurrian population, a smaller group of specialists (particularly associated with chariotry) whose prestige vocabulary and religious practice were adopted more broadly by a Hurrian elite, or some other historical process altogether. This chapter's Historical Debate feature examines this question in more detail; students should resist the temptation to convert a demonstrated linguistic layer into a confident narrative of conquest, migration, or ethnic hierarchy without acknowledging how thin the underlying evidence actually is.

Section 15.6

Suppiluliuma I and the Restructuring of Northern Syria

Mitanni's political fortunes declined sharply over the course of Suppiluliuma I's reign, through a process that unfolded in stages rather than in a single decisive stroke. During the campaign conventionally called the One-Year Campaign, Suppiluliuma crossed the Euphrates and advanced as far as Mitanni's capital, Washukanni, while the reigning Mitannian king, Tushratta, avoided a pitched battle, before Suppiluliuma turned west to bring numerous Mitannian-aligned Syrian states under Hittite control. Mitanni's internal dynastic crisis deepened afterward, especially following Tushratta's murder in a subsequent succession struggle. Only later, toward the end of Suppiluliuma's reign, did Shattiwaza — a son of Tushratta who had sought refuge and support at the Hittite court — return with Hittite backing to be installed as ruler of a much-reduced Mitannian state under Hittite protection, an arrangement sealed with the treaty examined in Section 15.5 and with a dynastic marriage between Shattiwaza and a Hittite princess.

The consequences of this campaign extended well beyond Mitanni's own reduced territory. Suppiluliuma used his victory to reorganize the political map of northern Syria more broadly, installing Hittite princes as vassal rulers over major Syrian centers, including Aleppo and Carchemish, and binding a wide array of smaller Syrian city-states — some of which had previously answered to Mitanni or Egypt — into the Hittite vassal-treaty network described in Section 15.10. This reorganization is what brought Hittite power into direct and sustained contact, and eventually conflict, with Egyptian interests in Syria, setting the stage for the confrontation examined in Section 15.11.

A reduced Mitannian state, sometimes called Hanigalbat in Assyrian sources, survived for a time as a much smaller polity increasingly pressured by a resurgent Assyria, examined further in Chapter 19, before disappearing as an independent political entity. Its fifteenth-century territorial rival Egypt, meanwhile, found itself confronting not the familiar power of Mitanni but a newly assertive Hittite presence directly on the northern edge of its Syrian sphere of influence.

Section 15.7

Levantine City-States of the Bronze Age

Unlike Egypt, Hatti, or Mitanni, the Levant during the Late Bronze Age was not organized into a single centralized kingdom but instead consisted of dozens of city-states, each ruled by a local dynast controlling a walled city and its surrounding agricultural hinterland. It is useful to distinguish two overlapping zones within this fragmented landscape. Farther south, in the region more conventionally described as Canaan, cities such as Megiddo, Gezer, Jerusalem, and Shechem maintained their own ruling families and local administrations. Farther north, in inland and coastal Syria, cities such as Qatna and Kadesh, and the coastal city of Ugarit examined in detail in Section 15.8, occupied a position more directly exposed to the Hittite and Mitannian spheres of influence examined in Sections 15.4 through 15.6. Byblos, a major timber-exporting port with especially long-standing ties to Egypt, occupied an intermediate position along the coast. All of these cities, whatever their zone, maintained their own ruling families, local administrations, and, frequently, rivalries with neighboring city-states.

This fragmented political landscape made the Levant unusually susceptible to great-power intervention. Egyptian pharaohs, beginning with the campaigns examined in Chapter 14, claimed overlordship over many of these cities, requiring tribute, military cooperation, and diplomatic loyalty in exchange for recognition of local rulers' authority and, at least intermittently, military protection. The Amarna letters, introduced in Chapter 14 and examined there as an Egyptian-facing source, reveal that this Egyptian protection was frequently unreliable, leaving local rulers to manage rival neighbors, roaming groups referred to in the correspondence as habiru — a term whose relationship, if any, to the later biblical term "Hebrew" remains a subject of ongoing and unresolved scholarly discussion — and periodic incursions from Hittite-aligned Syrian powers largely on their own.

Despite this political fragmentation and frequent insecurity, Levantine cities sustained substantial economic and cultural achievements, including sophisticated urban architecture, extensive craft production, and, as Section 15.8 examines in detail, significant experiments in writing that occurred alongside, rather than as a simple ancestor of, the wider Mediterranean world's later alphabetic traditions.

Section 15.8

Ugarit: A Northwest Semitic Port City

Ugarit, located on the Syrian coast near modern Ras Shamra, occupied a strategically valuable position linking maritime trade routes connecting Cyprus, the Aegean, and Egypt with overland routes reaching into inland Syria and Mesopotamia. Excavations beginning in the 1920s uncovered an extensive royal palace, private houses belonging to wealthy merchant and scribal families, temples, and, crucially, archives containing thousands of clay tablets written in multiple languages and scripts, including Akkadian cuneiform used for international correspondence and administration, Hurrian, Hittite, Egyptian material reflecting trade and diplomatic contact, and, most distinctively, an alphabetic cuneiform system used to write the Ugaritic language. Ugaritic is unambiguously a Northwest Semitic language with close affinities to the traditions conventionally described as Canaanite, though its precise classification relative to other Northwest Semitic languages, including whether it should itself be labeled a Canaanite language in the strict linguistic sense, remains a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion; this textbook therefore describes Ugarit as a Northwest Semitic city-state whose texts illuminate religious and cultural traditions closely related to those of the wider Canaanite world, rather than treating "Canaanite" and "Ugaritic" as simple synonyms.

The Ugaritic script consists of roughly thirty cuneiform signs, most representing single consonantal sounds, though three separate signs for aleph additionally encode information about an adjacent vowel — a detail that shows the system was not a purely and uniformly consonantal alphabet in the strictest sense. Compared with the hundreds of signs required for full Akkadian cuneiform or Egyptian hieroglyphic writing, this represents a significant simplification, and it is reasonable to conclude that the script reduced the barrier to acquiring basic literacy in the local language. It would, however, overstate the evidence to conclude that this simplification produced widespread or popular literacy across Ugaritic society: surviving evidence for scribal training, including identifiable school exercise texts, indicates that writing at Ugarit remained substantially embedded in specialized scribal and administrative contexts, and the demographic reach of literacy beyond those trained circles cannot be confidently estimated from the evidence currently available.

Tablets written in the Ugaritic script preserve important religious and mythological literature, including the Baal Cycle, a series of texts describing conflicts among deities including Baal, a storm and rain deity whose power was closely bound up with agricultural fertility and the seasonal cycle; Yam, associated with the sea; and Mot, associated with death — texts that provide historians with a rare direct window into the religious world of the Northwest Semitic Levant, independent of the often hostile or distorting later accounts preserved in other traditions.

Ugarit's economy depended heavily on maritime and overland trade, examined further in Section 15.12, and its population included resident merchants, diplomats, and craftsmen from Cyprus, the Aegean world, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and elsewhere in the Levant, reflected in loanwords, foreign personal names, and imported material culture recovered throughout the site. Ugarit's rulers maintained vassal status under Hittite overlordship during the period examined in this chapter, a relationship documented through surviving treaty texts, while continuing to pursue their own substantial independent commercial interests.

Section 15.9

Religion and Society across the Bronze Age Levant

The Late Bronze Age Near East and eastern Mediterranean exhibited substantial religious and cultural exchange without producing a single unified regional religion. Hittite religion, as noted in Section 15.3, absorbed Hattian, Hurrian, and Luwian deities and cultic practices into an expanding royal pantheon, while Hurrian religious influence, transmitted partly through Mitanni's cultural prestige, shaped Hittite religious practice significantly, including the incorporation of Hurrian deities such as Teshub, a storm god, and Hebat into official Hittite state cult, particularly evident at the rock sanctuary of Yazılıkaya near Hattusa.

The religious world illuminated by the Ugaritic textual discoveries examined in Section 15.8 centered on a pantheon including El, the senior god and patriarch of the pantheon; Baal, the storm and rain deity discussed above; Athirat, a major goddess closely related to the figure later known in Hebrew tradition as Asherah, though the two should not be treated as simply identical across thoroughly different periods and religious contexts; and other deities whose worship, iconography, and mythology would later intersect in complex ways with the religious history of ancient Israel and Judah, examined in Chapter 18. Students should resist projecting later biblical polemics against Canaanite religion backward as a neutral historical description of what Levantine religious practice actually involved in this earlier period; the Ugaritic texts provide a rare opportunity to examine this religious world through its own surviving literature rather than solely through later, often hostile, outside accounts.

Language and writing systems also crossed political boundaries extensively. Akkadian cuneiform served as the shared diplomatic medium discussed in Section 15.10; Hittite scribes maintained fluency in Akkadian, Hurrian, and Luwian alongside their native language; and Ugarit's alphabetic cuneiform system, discussed in Section 15.8, represents one important second-millennium experiment in alphabetic writing. It existed alongside older and contemporary linear alphabetic traditions used elsewhere in the Levant; the later Phoenician alphabet, examined in Chapter 17, developed within that broader linear tradition rather than descending straightforwardly from Ugaritic cuneiform, even though both traditions shared the same underlying West Semitic alphabetic principle.

Historical Vocabulary
Vassal Treaties and Suzerainty

A vassal treaty in the Late Bronze Age Near East formally bound a subordinate ruler to a more powerful overlord, typically requiring tribute, military service, extradition of fugitives, and diplomatic loyalty, in exchange for recognition of the vassal's continued local authority and, at least nominally, protection. Hittite vassal treaties, well preserved in the Hattusa archives, followed a recognizable structure including a historical preamble justifying the relationship, specific obligations, lists of divine witnesses, and curses and blessings contingent on compliance. This structure differed sharply from parity treaties concluded between roughly equal great powers, such as the Egyptian-Hittite treaty discussed in Section 15.11, which lacked the language of subordination and instead emphasized mutual obligation between formal equals. Historians use the term suzerainty to describe this asymmetrical but still formally negotiated relationship, distinguishing it from direct territorial annexation.

Historical Vocabulary
Akkadian as a Diplomatic Lingua Franca

A lingua franca is a language adopted for communication between groups who do not share a native language. Although Egypt, Hatti, and Mitanni each possessed their own native languages and, in Egypt's and Hatti's cases, their own scripts, their courts conducted virtually all surviving international diplomatic correspondence in Akkadian cuneiform, the language of Babylonia and Assyria. This practice was not new to the Late Bronze Age great powers: the earlier Mari archives, noted in this chapter's opening, already show Akkadian functioning in a broadly comparable diplomatic role in the eighteenth century BCE. Its continued use across the Late Bronze Age required extensive scribal training in foreign courts far from Mesopotamia itself and illustrates how a shared administrative technology could bind together politically and militarily rival states into a single functioning diplomatic system.

Section 15.10

Egypt, Hatti, and Contested Syria

Suppiluliuma I's reorganization of northern Syria, examined in Section 15.6, brought Hittite vassal networks into direct proximity with territories Egypt considered its own sphere of influence following the campaigns examined in Chapter 14. Historians often describe the resulting diplomatic system linking Egypt, Hatti, Mitanni (before its subordination), Babylonia, and Assyria as a "great powers club" or "family of nations," terms that capture the surviving correspondence's striking convention of addressing fellow great-power rulers as "brother" regardless of actual kinship, while addressing subordinate vassal rulers, such as the Levantine city rulers examined in Section 15.7, in a distinctly hierarchical register as "servant" or "son." This linguistic convention reflected and reinforced a real distinction in Late Bronze Age diplomatic practice between the parity treaties concluded between roughly equal great powers and the vassal treaties, discussed in this chapter's Historical Vocabulary feature, imposing formal subordination on smaller states.

Dynastic marriage functioned as a central diplomatic tool across this system: Egyptian pharaohs received foreign princesses from Mitanni, Babylonia, and other courts as diplomatic wives, reinforcing alliances and signaling status, though Egyptian pharaohs conspicuously refused to send Egyptian princesses in the reverse direction, a practice that generated recorded diplomatic friction in surviving correspondence. Gift exchange, involving gold, textiles, horses, chariots, and other luxury goods, accompanied these marriages and other diplomatic occasions, with rulers frequently registering complaints in surviving letters about gifts perceived as insufficiently generous compared with those exchanged with rival courts.

As Hittite influence over Syrian vassal states expanded through the arrangements examined in Section 15.6, competition for the loyalty of individual Syrian city-states intensified between the Egyptian and Hittite spheres. By the decades leading up to the confrontation examined in Section 15.11, this competition centered especially on control of Kadesh itself and the neighboring territory of Amurru, both of which shifted allegiance between Egyptian and Hittite overlordship more than once. This competition, layered onto the older instability of Levantine city-state politics examined in Section 15.7, forms the immediate political background to the military confrontation examined in Section 15.11.

Section 15.11

Kadesh and Its Aftermath from a Hittite and Regional Perspective

Chapter 14 examined the Battle of Kadesh, fought around 1274 BCE between Ramesses II of Egypt and the Hittite king Muwatalli II, chiefly from the perspective of Egyptian royal inscriptions, which present the battle as a personal triumph for Ramesses despite an engagement that most historians regard as militarily inconclusive. Hittite and broader regional evidence complicates and enriches this picture. Muwatalli II commanded a large coalition of forces drawn from across the Hittite vassal network assembled through Suppiluliuma I's earlier reorganization of Syria, examined in Section 15.6, including contingents from vassal states throughout Anatolia and northern Syria — a detail that itself illustrates how thoroughly the vassal-treaty system had bound these territories into Hittite military mobilization by the mid-thirteenth century BCE.

In the years following the battle, control over the contested Syrian city-states, including Kadesh itself, remained substantially with the Hittites, suggesting that whatever tactical claims Egyptian inscriptions made about the battle's outcome, it did not translate into a decisive Egyptian strategic victory in Syria. Continued but indecisive friction between the two powers over the following decade and a half, combined with a set of pressures specific to each court, eventually produced the negotiated settlement examined in this chapter's Cause and Effect feature: Hattusili III, who had acceded to the Hittite throne through an irregular succession that displaced his nephew, had strong incentives to secure external recognition and stability for his rule. Assyria's growing power in northern Mesopotamia, examined further in Section 15.12 and in Chapter 19, posed an especially serious strategic problem for Hatti and altered the wider balance of power on its eastern and southeastern frontiers, while Egypt had its own separate incentives to stabilize its northern frontier and regularize relations with the Hittite court rather than continue an open-ended and unproductive rivalry.

The resulting treaty, concluded around 1259 BCE and surviving in both Egyptian hieroglyphic and Akkadian cuneiform versions, formalized peace and mutual nonaggression between the two courts, provided for mutual military assistance in the event that either party or its territory came under attack, and established procedures for the extradition of political refugees, while leaving the precise boundary between Egyptian and Hittite spheres of influence in Syria without a single explicit territorial line. It is often cited as one of the earliest surviving international peace treaties whose text can be read in something close to its original form, and it was reinforced some years later by a further dynastic marriage between Ramesses II and a Hittite princess.

Section 15.12

The Connected Region on the Eve of Crisis

By the second half of the thirteenth century BCE, the developments examined throughout this chapter had produced a region bound together by overlapping and interdependent systems: Hittite vassal networks stretching from central Anatolia through northern Syria; the former Mitannian territories, examined in Sections 15.4 through 15.6, increasingly incorporated into the expanding Middle Assyrian state rather than surviving as any meaningfully independent polity; a fragmented but economically vigorous Levantine city-state world epitomized by Ugarit; and a formal peace between the two leading Mediterranean and Near Eastern land powers, Egypt and Hatti.

Binding these political relationships together was an extensive maritime and overland trading network connecting Egypt, the Levant, Cyprus (referred to in contemporary texts as Alashiya and identified as a major source of copper), the Aegean world of Mycenaean Greece and Minoan Crete, and, through overland and river routes, Mesopotamia and more distant sources of tin, apparently including regions of Anatolia and parts of Central Asia, though precise provenance and the exact overland and maritime routes involved remain actively debated among archaeometric researchers. Shipwreck evidence, most famously the Uluburun wreck discovered off the southern coast of Anatolia and dated to roughly the late fourteenth century BCE, provides an unusually direct archaeological window into this trade, having yielded a ship's cargo including copper oxhide ingots (large, distinctively shaped metal ingots facilitating standardized bulk transport), tin ingots, Cypriot pottery, Canaanite jars likely containing wine or resin, Egyptian and Near Eastern jewelry and raw materials including ivory, ebony, and glass ingots, and even a folding wooden writing board, illustrating the geographic range of materials moving through a single Late Bronze Age vessel's cargo, whether that vessel operated as an independent merchant venture, a royally sponsored voyage, or some combination of the two.

This trade required specialized institutions and personnel: merchants operating with royal sanction or under royal oversight in some kingdoms, standardized weights and measures facilitating exchange across different political systems, multilingual scribes capable of drafting contracts and correspondence, and port facilities capable of handling substantial cargo volumes. Coastal cities such as Ugarit, Byblos, and various Cypriot centers functioned as emporia — trading centers where merchants from multiple regions congregated to exchange goods under some degree of royal or civic regulation.

This economic interdependence coexisted with, rather than eliminated, the military and political rivalries examined throughout this chapter: rulers of great powers had incentives to maintain functioning trade routes even while competing for territorial influence, since disrupted trade threatened access to strategically important materials, particularly tin and copper required for bronze production. Chapter 16 now turns to the still-debated question of how rapidly this apparently durable network was disrupted and transformed around 1200 BCE, why some states and cities collapsed while others survived, contracted, or were transformed, and why historians continue to debate the causes.

Primary Source Window

The Suppiluliuma–Shattiwaza Treaty, 14th century BCE

Creator: Issued in the name of the Hittite king Suppiluliuma I, concerning the restored Mitannian prince Shattiwaza · Date: mid-to-late fourteenth century BCE · Type: Vassal treaty, cuneiform tablet, preserved in the Hittite royal archives at Hattusa

Context: Following Suppiluliuma I's campaign against Mitanni, examined in Section 15.6, this treaty formalized Shattiwaza's restoration as a subordinate Mitannian ruler under Hittite overlordship. Like other Hittite vassal treaties, it follows a recognizable structure: a historical preamble justifying the relationship, specific obligations placed on the vassal, a lengthy list of divine witnesses, and curses and blessings contingent on compliance.

Paraphrase: The treaty recounts the circumstances of Mitanni's internal crisis and Shattiwaza's flight to the Hittite court, describes Suppiluliuma's decision to restore him to a reduced Mitannian throne bound by marriage and obligation to Hatti, and invokes a long list of divine witnesses to guarantee the agreement — a list that includes, alongside Hurrian and Mesopotamian deities, a set of names corresponding closely to the Vedic deities Mitra, Varuna, Indra, and the twin Nasatyas.

Analysis: This treaty is central evidence for two distinct historical questions examined in this chapter: the structure of Hittite vassal diplomacy, discussed in Section 15.10 and the Historical Vocabulary feature, and the debated Indo-Aryan element within Mitanni's ruling culture, discussed in Section 15.5. The appearance of these particular divine names in a Hittite-authored treaty concerning Mitanni is among the strongest pieces of evidence for that Indo-Aryan religious element, though, as the Historical Debate feature explores, historians disagree sharply about what social or political conclusions this evidence supports.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a Hittite-authored treaty concerning Mitanni include deities associated with an Indo-Aryan religious tradition?
  2. What does the treaty's structure reveal about how Hittite vassal relationships were formalized and enforced?
  3. What are the limitations of using a treaty imposed by the victorious power as evidence for the internal culture of the defeated one?
  4. How does this document differ in perspective and purpose from the Amarna correspondence examined in Chapter 14?
Historical Debate

How Much Does the Indo-Aryan Evidence from Mitanni Tell Us?

The question: How should historians interpret the presence of Indo-Aryan royal names, specialized horse-training vocabulary, and treaty deities corresponding to later Vedic gods within a kingdom in which Hurrian language and culture were prominent in the attested population and administration, even though Mitanni's own internal archive remains unexcavated?

Interpretation A — A distinct Indo-Aryan ruling or military element: Some historians and linguists argue that Mitanni's royal dynasty and a specialized military elite associated with chariot warfare, the maryannu, descended from, or were strongly influenced by, an intrusive Indo-Aryan-speaking group connected to a wider Indo-Aryan movement whose linguistic traces also appear far to the east in early South Asian sources, suggesting a small but historically significant ruling and military minority superimposed on the predominantly Hurrian population.
Interpretation B — Limited technical and religious borrowing without a distinct ruling ethnicity: Other historians caution that the evidence is limited to a small number of royal names, specialized technical vocabulary related to horses and chariotry, and a set of divine names in a single treaty, and that this could reflect selective cultural, religious, or technical borrowing and prestige practice by a Hurrian elite rather than a distinct conquering or ruling population, cautioning against overstating what a handful of names and terms can reveal about ethnicity, migration, or the internal structure of Mitannian society.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited body of evidence: royal names and treaty deities preserved mainly in outside sources such as the Suppiluliuma–Shattiwaza treaty examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, the Kikkuli horse-training text, and the absence of any directly excavated Mitannian royal archive, since Mitanni's own capital has not been securely identified or excavated.

Why it matters: This debate illustrates a recurring challenge in Bronze Age history: reconstructing a major regional power almost entirely from the records of its neighbors and eventual conquerors, and the danger of overinterpreting a small set of linguistic and religious data points into confident claims about ethnicity, migration, or political structure.

Student task: Using the evidence described above, evaluate which interpretation the available evidence more strongly supports, and explain what kind of additional evidence — for instance, the eventual excavation of a Mitannian administrative archive — would help resolve the debate.

Multiple Perspectives

Living amid the Great Powers

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many kinds of evidence, not quotations from a single named individual.

From the perspective of a Hittite provincial official: Governing a distant province means balancing loyalty to a distant king at Hattusa with the practical realities of managing local populations, collecting tribute, maintaining garrisons, and responding to unrest without the resources or authority to act entirely independently.
From the perspective of a Levantine city ruler: Survival depends on skillfully balancing formal submission to a distant Egyptian court, or to a Hittite vassal network extending from northern Syria, against the practical need to manage local rivals and threats largely without reliable outside support, while never appearing disloyal enough to invite punishment or hostile enough to invite a neighboring ruler's attack.
From the perspective of a merchant at Ugarit: Prosperity depends on maintaining commercial relationships across political boundaries — with Cypriot copper suppliers, Aegean traders, and Egyptian buyers — regardless of which great power currently claims overlordship over the city, since trade routes and profit margins matter more immediately than distant dynastic politics.
From the perspective of a scribe trained in multiple cuneiform traditions: Professional value depends on mastering not only one's native language and script but also Akkadian and its administrative conventions, since royal correspondence, treaties, and trade agreements all require fluency in a shared diplomatic language regardless of which kingdom employs the scribe.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Levant between Empires

What Changed

  • Overlordship over Levantine city-states shifted repeatedly among Egyptian, Mitannian, and Hittite spheres of influence across the period examined in this chapter
  • Mitanni rose from a regional power rivaling Egypt to a much-reduced vassal state within roughly a century
  • Hittite kingship evolved from a turbulent, assassination-prone institution to a more stable succession system, and its Empire-period council structures no longer functioned as they had under the Old Kingdom

What Stayed the Same

  • The Levant's underlying political fragmentation into competing city-states persisted regardless of which great power claimed overlordship
  • Maritime and overland trade networks linking Egypt, the Levant, Cyprus, and the Aegean persisted across much of the period despite shifting political control
  • Akkadian remained the diplomatic lingua franca across every major realignment of power discussed in this chapter

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity helps students avoid assuming that shifts in formal political control necessarily disrupted the deeper economic and cultural connections binding the region together.

Cause and Effect

The Making of the Egyptian-Hittite Peace

Military background

The militarily inconclusive Battle of Kadesh and the years of continued friction that followed left neither power able to decisively resolve control over contested Syrian city-states such as Kadesh itself.

Political background

Hattusili III's irregular accession to the Hittite throne gave him a strong personal incentive to secure external recognition and a stable frontier rather than continued conflict with Egypt.

Strategic background

The growing power of Assyria in northern Mesopotamia gave both Egypt and Hatti reasons not to leave themselves exposed by continuing to exhaust one another.

Effect

The 1259 BCE treaty formalized peace, mutual nonaggression, mutual defense obligations, and extradition arrangements, later reinforced by dynastic marriage between the two courts.

Historical complexity: This chapter treats the treaty as the product of several converging military, political, and strategic pressures rather than attributing it to any single cause, such as war-weariness or trade interests alone; the peace served the specific needs of each court's ruler as much as any general preference for negotiated settlement over war.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Merchant Household at Ugarit

Excavated houses at Ugarit belonging to prosperous merchant and scribal families reveal multi-room residences, private archives of commercial and legal documents, and imported goods from Cyprus, the Aegean, and Egypt, indicating households whose daily economic life was directly bound up with long-distance trade rather than solely local agriculture. Marriage contracts, loan documents, and property records surviving from such households show structured legal arrangements governing inheritance, debt, and commercial partnership, while religious texts and household shrines indicate the coexistence of official state cult with more localized household religious practice.

Because most surviving Ugaritic and Akkadian documents were produced within trained scribal and administrative circles, as Section 15.8 discussed, historians must read them carefully for indirect evidence of the far larger population of agricultural laborers, sailors, and craftspeople whose labor underpinned the wealth visible in merchant and royal archives, a method this textbook continues to apply throughout its treatment of Bronze Age social history.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Hittite kingdom, the Hurrian-speaking kingdom of Mitanni, and the city-states of the Levant as principal participants, alongside Egypt, in one of the earliest extensively documented international systems in Near Eastern history. Section 15.1 established that both Anatolia and the Levant entered this period already characterized by political and cultural diversity rather than blank slates awaiting the arrival of a single dominant power. Sections 15.2 and 15.3 traced the Old Hittite Kingdom's turbulent formation and its later Empire-period institutions, distinguishing the Old Kingdom's panku and succession politics from the more centralized, less council-constrained kingship of the Empire period.

Section 15.4 introduced the Hurrians and the kingdom of Mitanni, emphasizing how little is directly known of Mitanni's own internal administration. Section 15.5 examined in detail the debated evidence for Indo-Aryan linguistic and religious elements within Mitanni's ruling circles, including royal names, the Kikkuli horse-training text, and the divine witnesses of the Suppiluliuma–Shattiwaza treaty, while cautioning against overconfident conclusions about ethnicity or political structure. Section 15.6 traced how Suppiluliuma I's campaign against Mitanni reorganized the political map of northern Syria and brought Hittite power into direct contact with Egyptian interests.

Section 15.7 examined the persistent political fragmentation of the Levant into competing city-states, and Section 15.8 presented Ugarit as an unusually well-documented, though not simply representative, case study of Northwest Semitic urban life, religion, and commerce, correcting overstated claims about both its script and the extent of literacy it produced. Section 15.9 examined religious exchange across Hittite, Hurrian, and Levantine traditions while cautioning against collapsing them into an undifferentiated composite.

Section 15.10 examined the diplomatic system linking Egypt, Hatti, and the other great powers, including parity and vassal treaties, dynastic marriage, and gift exchange, while Section 15.11 examined the Battle of Kadesh and the subsequent peace treaty from a Hittite and regional perspective, emphasizing the treaty's multicausal origins in military stalemate, Hattusili III's political needs, the growing Assyrian challenge to Hatti, and Egypt's separate interest in stabilizing its northern frontier. Section 15.12 drew these threads together to describe a genuinely interconnected region on the eve of the crisis examined in Chapter 16, bound together by trade in copper, tin, and other goods whose precise routes and sources remain subjects of ongoing archaeological research.

The Historical Debate feature on Mitanni's Indo-Aryan evidence, paired with the Suppiluliuma–Shattiwaza treaty as a primary source, illustrated the interpretive challenges historians face when working with limited and indirect evidence. The Multiple Perspectives feature showed how officials, city rulers, merchants, and scribes each experienced this interconnected world differently, while the Continuity and Change and Cause and Effect features demonstrated that political overlordship shifted repeatedly, and that the Egyptian-Hittite peace emerged from several converging pressures rather than a single cause.

These developments set the stage directly for Chapter 16, which examines how this elaborate, interdependent system — Hittite, Levantine, Cypriot, Aegean, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian alike — was rapidly disrupted and transformed around 1200 BCE, why some states and cities collapsed while others survived, contracted, or adapted, and why the causes of this transformation remain actively debated among historians and archaeologists.

Key Terms

Nesite (Hittite)
The Indo-European language of the Hittite royal dynasty and elite, conventionally called "Hittite" by modern scholars.

The pre-Hittite Anatolian population and language whose religious and cultural traditions were substantially absorbed into Hittite civilization.
Panku
The Hittite royal council, most significant as a judicial and advisory body in the Old Kingdom period and scarcely attested as an active political institution in the Empire period.

The formal title of the Hittite queen, who held significant and often lifelong independent religious and political authority.

The small language family, comprising Hurrian and the later-attested Urartian, to which the language of Mitanni's core population belonged; more distant relationships remain unproven.

Suzerainty
An asymmetrical but formally negotiated relationship of overlordship distinguished from direct territorial annexation.
Parity treaty
A treaty concluded between roughly equal great powers, emphasizing mutual obligation rather than subordination.
Great powers club
A modern historians' term describing the Late Bronze Age diplomatic system linking Egypt, Hatti, Mitanni, Babylonia, and Assyria.

A shared language of communication among groups without a common native language, exemplified by Akkadian in Late Bronze Age diplomacy and already visible in the earlier Mari archives.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Late Bronze Age Near East and eastern Mediterranean functioned as an interconnected international system whose participants were simultaneously fierce military rivals and dependent economic and diplomatic partners. The Hittites, the Hurrians of Mitanni, and the city-states of the Levant each developed distinctive political institutions, religious traditions, and cultural achievements, even as they were bound together by shared administrative technologies, trade networks, and diplomatic conventions that this chapter has examined in detail — and, as this chapter has repeatedly cautioned, much of what can be confidently said about these societies is shaped by which archives happened to survive and be excavated, and which did not.

Understanding this system matters for the chapters that follow. Chapter 16 will show how quickly this apparently durable network of treaties, trade routes, and courts was disrupted, and how unevenly its member states and cities fared in the aftermath — some destroyed, some transformed, and some, including Egypt and Assyria, enduring as states even as the wider international system examined in this chapter did not survive intact. That transformation remains debated precisely because the system examined in this chapter was so genuinely interconnected: a crisis in one part of the network could propagate rapidly to affect the others, even without producing a uniform outcome everywhere.

Students who understand that ancient states could be simultaneously rivals and partners, that diplomatic correspondence and treaties must be read critically for bias and self-interest, and that major historical actors such as Mitanni can remain only partially recoverable from the surviving evidence, are well prepared to approach the collapse examined in Chapter 16 and the many later instances of regional interdependence examined throughout this textbook's remaining eight volumes.
