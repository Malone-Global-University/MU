The Arab-Islamic Conquests and the End of the Byzantine-Sasanian Regional Order

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A small piece of papyrus from Egypt, written in Greek and Arabic and dated to the spring of 643, records the delivery of sheep to the Arab authorities who now ruled the province. It is not a victory monument or a chronicle of battles. It is a receipt. Its Greek half follows the conventions of the Byzantine bureaucracy that had governed Egypt until the previous year; its Arabic half is among the earliest dated Arabic documents to survive. Together they show something that no battle narrative can: that the conquest of a province was also a transfer of obligations, supplies, and paperwork, carried out by officials who in many cases had served the previous regime.

In the roughly eighteen years between the Prophet Muhammad's death in 632 and the death of the last Sasanian king around 651, armies drawn from Arabia and organized under the first caliphs overran Byzantine Syria and Palestine, took Egypt from the Roman Empire, destroyed the Sasanian imperial state, and began the incorporation of Iraq and Iran into a new political order. Two empires that had fought each other, with interruptions, for centuries, and whose final war had ended only in 628, were each transformed within a generation. Byzantium survived as a smaller state centered on Constantinople and Anatolia. The Sasanian monarchy, which had ruled Iran and Mesopotamia since the third century, ceased to exist.

Chapter 38 examined how Abū Bakr consolidated the Muslim community in Arabia during the Ridda Wars. This chapter follows the armies that moved beyond Arabia. It reconstructs four main theaters of operation, Syria and Palestine, Iraq, Egypt, and Iran, and it follows the campaigns from their uncertain beginnings in 633–634 through the death of Yazdegerd III in 651. It also looks at what the conquests meant for the people who lived through them: how surrender agreements were negotiated, how soldiers were settled in new garrison cities, how revenue was collected, which officials remained in place, and how Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others experienced the new rulers.

Three processes that later tradition often runs together must be kept apart. Military conquest was the defeat of imperial armies and the occupation of cities. Political incorporation was the slower work of collecting revenue, installing governors, and securing rural districts, and in much of Iran it continued for decades. Conversion to Islam and adoption of Arabic were different again: they unfolded unevenly over centuries and are the subject of Chapter 42. Winning a battle did not make a province Muslim, and a province that accepted a tribute agreement was not necessarily quiet.

The historical record imposes a second discipline. The fullest narratives of the conquests, those of al-Balādhurī and al-Ṭabarī, were compiled in the ninth and early tenth centuries, two or three centuries after the events. Their authors drew on oral reports, family traditions, and earlier written collections shaped by tribal pride, legal argument, and the memory of a heroic age. Contemporary and near-contemporary sources do exist, among them Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic writings, papyri, coins, and archaeology, but they are fragmentary and have their own concerns. Section 39.1 explains why dates, army sizes, speeches, and treaty texts so often differ from one source to another, and why this chapter marks a date as "traditional" when it rests on the later Arabic narrative alone.

The conquests also have to be explained without reducing them to a single cause. Arabian military experience, religious commitment, the prospect of plunder and stipends, and effective command all mattered. So did the exhaustion of Byzantium and Iran after their last war, the political crisis of the Sasanian monarchy, plague, fiscal strain, religious conflict within the Christian churches, and the willingness of many local elites to negotiate rather than fight to the end. Different regions responded differently. Syria's cities, Egypt's villages, Iraq's landowners, and the Iranian plateau's aristocrats each had their own interests, and the speed of conquest varied accordingly.

The chapter is organized in seven divisions. The first establishes the sources, the preceding crisis of the two empires, and the military and geographic conditions of the campaigns. The next four follow the conquests of Syria and Palestine, Iraq, Egypt, and Iran. The sixth turns to the arrangements that made conquest sustainable: treaties, garrison cities, taxation, administration, religious communities, and everyday life. The seventh evaluates the explanations historians offer and describes the new regional order that emerged by mid-century, one whose unresolved tensions lead into the First Fitna examined in Chapter 40.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the principal sources for the conquests, Arabic narrative histories, non-Muslim chronicles, papyri, coins, and archaeology, and explain why their accounts of dates, numbers, and events often diverge.
  2. Explain the condition of the Byzantine and Sasanian empires after the war of 602–628 and why each was vulnerable in different ways.
  3. Trace the principal campaigns in Syria and Palestine, from the first engagements of 633–634 through Yarmūk, Jerusalem, and the Syrian cities to the Byzantine frontier in Anatolia.
  4. Trace the conquest of Sasanian Iraq, including the Battle of the Bridge, al-Qādisiyya, the fall of Ctesiphon, and the consolidation of Mesopotamia.
  5. Describe the conquest of Egypt and the negotiated Byzantine withdrawal from Alexandria, and assess what Egyptian documents reveal about the transition.
  6. Analyze the uneven conquest of Iran, from Khūzestān and Nihāvand to Khurāsān, and distinguish the end of Sasanian sovereignty from the incorporation of Iranian society.
  7. Distinguish conquest by agreement from conquest by force, and assess the reliability of surviving treaty texts.
  8. Explain the formation of garrison cities and military districts and how early taxation and stipends financed the new order.
  9. Assess the continuity of provincial administration and the varied experiences of Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, villagers, women, and enslaved people under early Muslim rule.
  10. Compare military, political, economic, religious, and environmental explanations for the speed of the conquests, and justify a multi-causal account.
  11. Distinguish Byzantine territorial contraction from Sasanian collapse and describe the new political geography that resulted.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did armies organized under the early caliphs defeat or displace the established powers of the Middle East, and how did the victories reshape government, taxation, settlement, and relations among the region's populations?
  2. What can historians know about the conquests when the fullest narratives were written two or more centuries afterward?
  3. Why did Byzantium lose Syria and Egypt yet survive as an empire, while the Sasanian dynasty lost everything?
  4. How did conquest by negotiated agreement differ from conquest by force, and why did later jurists care about the difference?
  5. Why did the conquerors build new garrison cities instead of occupying existing capitals, and what consequences followed?
  6. How much changed in provincial administration immediately after conquest, and how much stayed the same?
  7. Why is it misleading to treat military conquest, conversion to Islam, and Arabization as a single process?
  8. What geographic, environmental, political, and religious conditions explain the differing pace of conquest in Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and Iran?

Historical Context

Volume II traced the long rivalry between Rome and Iran. By the late sixth century the Roman Empire in the East, usually called Byzantine by modern historians, and the Sasanian Empire were the two great powers of Southwest Asia. Their frontier ran through Armenia, Upper Mesopotamia, and the Syrian steppe, and each relied on Arab client kingdoms, the Ghassānids for Byzantium and the Lakhmids of al-Ḥīra for the Sasanians, to manage the desert margins (Chapter 34). The last and most destructive of their wars began in 602 and ended in 628. Sasanian armies occupied Syria, Palestine, and Egypt for a time, and Byzantine forces under Heraclius ultimately marched into Mesopotamia and forced the overthrow of Khusrau II. The victory restored Byzantium's provinces but left both states financially and militarily drained.

In Arabia, the Prophet Muhammad had by his death in 632 created a political community that extended across much of the peninsula (Chapters 36 and 37). Chapter 38 examined the succession crisis, the caliphate of Abū Bakr (632–634), and the Ridda Wars, which restored the community's authority over tribes that had withdrawn from it. Those wars produced experienced fighters, working relationships between Medina and the tribes, and a pattern of paying and rewarding participants from the proceeds of campaigns. They also left the new polity with the question of what to do next.

This chapter treats the conquests as a regional event that reordered the world described in Chapter 2: the Euphrates and Tigris, the Nile, the Zagros, the Taurus, and the desert routes that linked them. Armies crossed those spaces along established roads and caravan routes, used the Syrian and Iraqi desert margins as bases, and, in Egypt, followed the Sinai coastal route that earlier invaders had used. The ancient geography of connection described in Chapter 2 made the rapid movement of armies possible, while the difficulty of the Iranian plateau and its mountains explains why the conquest of the east was prolonged. The chapter ends around 651, with limited discussion of later consequences. The political crisis of the caliphate under ʿUthmān and ʿAlī belongs to Chapter 40; the mature Umayyad state to Chapter 41; and conversion and Arabization to Chapter 42.

Section 39.1

Reconstructing the Arab-Islamic Conquests: Sources and Historical Problems

The central narrative sources for the conquests are Arabic and were compiled long after the events. Al-Balādhurī (died c. 892) wrote the Futūḥ al-buldān ("Conquests of the Lands"), organized by region and rich in details about treaties, taxation, and the foundation of garrison cities. Al-Ṭabarī (died 923) incorporated conquest narratives into his great universal chronicle, arranged year by year. For Egypt, Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam (died 871) is the earliest extensive account. These authors worked from earlier collections that no longer survive in their original form, and they cite chains of transmitters (isnāds) that reach back to participants or their descendants. Much of al-Ṭabarī's account of Iraq, including al-Qādisiyya, derives from Sayf ibn ʿUmar (died c. 796), a storyteller whom later critics regarded as unreliable and whose narratives are rich in vivid detail that other sources do not confirm. Later romances falsely attributed to al-Wāqidī, an earlier historian, embroider the Syrian campaigns with duels and heroines and cannot be used as evidence for what happened.

Modern scholars have shown that the Arabic tradition is shaped by recurring literary forms: speeches before battle, single combats, prophetic dreams, schematic troop numbers, and a tidy sequence of decisive victories. Tribes and families preserved the memory of their ancestors' roles and had reasons to enlarge them. Jurists later turned the memory of conquest into legal precedent, because the status of land and its inhabitants, whether taken by treaty or by force, affected taxation. The result is that the same event may appear with different dates, different commanders, and different terms. This does not mean the Arabic accounts are fiction. It means that their outlines are often credible while their details need independent confirmation.

Independent evidence exists, though it is scattered. The Doctrina Jacobi, a Greek Christian dialogue composed in the mid-630s, refers to the Saracen invasion of Palestine as it was happening. Sophronius, patriarch of Jerusalem, mentions Arab raiders in a Christmas sermon of 634. The Syriac priest Thomas the Presbyter, writing around 640, appears to record a battle between Romans and Arabs in Palestine in early 634. The Armenian history attributed to Sebeos, composed in the 660s, offers an account of the conquest of Iran and the Caucasus from an Armenian perspective. The Syriac Khuzistan Chronicle, probably written in southwestern Iran in the 660s by a member of the Church of the East, provides a rare account from within the conquered Sasanian world. John of Nikiu, a Coptic bishop writing in Egypt in the late seventh century, describes the Egyptian conquest in a text that survives only in an Ethiopic translation of an Arabic version of the lost original. Byzantine chroniclers, notably Theophanes in the early ninth century, preserve earlier Syriac and Greek material. None of these writers is neutral. They wrote from within religious communities that interpreted the conquests as divine punishment, as a test, or as an extension of existing church conflicts.

Documentary and material evidence adds a different kind of information. Greek and Arabic papyri from Egypt and Palestine, including the Nessana documents from the Negev, record taxes, requisitions, and local arrangements. Coins show the new rulers adopting existing designs: Byzantine-style bronze issues with Arabic additions in Syria, and Sasanian-style silver drachms with Arabic phrases and the names of Muslim governors in Iran. Very few Arabic inscriptions survive from before the 650s. Archaeology demonstrates that many Levantine cities, including Jerash, Pella, and Scythopolis (Beth Shean), continued to function after the conquest, though excavators' interpretations of destruction layers vary and many seventh-century levels are hard to date precisely. For Iraq and Iran, excavations at Ctesiphon and other Sasanian sites document the earlier state, but the conquest-period record is thin.

This chapter therefore proceeds in three registers. Where several independent sources agree, as for the fact of a decisive Muslim victory at Yarmūk, it states the event plainly. Where only the later Arabic tradition supplies a date, it says so. Where army sizes or speeches are given, it treats them as literary conventions, not data. Modern scholarship has moved between interpretations that follow the Arabic narratives closely and interpretations that reconstruct events principally from contemporary non-Muslim and documentary sources. This chapter draws on both, and it notes where the choice matters.

Historical Vocabulary
Futūḥ, Akhbār, and Isnād

Futūḥ (singular fatḥ, literally "openings") is the Arabic term for the conquests, and also the name of a genre of narrative. It reflects the tradition's own understanding of the events, as the "opening" of lands to Islam. Modern historians use "conquests" as an analytical label but remember that the Arabic term carries the memory of the victors.

Akhbār (singular khabar) are individual reports, often short stories or anecdotes, that early Arabic historians collected and arranged. An isnād is the chain of named transmitters attached to a report. Chains can reflect real transmission but can also be constructed or improved later, so an impressive isnād does not by itself prove reliability.

Section 39.2

From the Ridda Wars to Expansion beyond Arabia

The Arabic tradition presents the move beyond Arabia as the natural sequel to the Ridda Wars (Chapter 38). Once Abū Bakr had restored the community's authority over the Arabian tribes, it says, he sent armies north to Syria and east to Iraq. Several features of the situation made this plausible. The Ridda campaigns had produced seasoned commanders and mobilized tribal contingents, and fighters who had been defeated or brought back into the community were available for new service. Tribes on the northern fringes of Arabia already raided and traded in the Syrian and Iraqi borderlands. Al-Muthannā ibn Ḥāritha, of the Bakr ibn Wāʾil, is said to have been raiding the Sasanian frontier in the Sawād before the caliph committed forces there, which suggests that some of the earliest pressure on the empires came from tribal initiative as well as from Medina.

What the evidence does not establish is a single, centrally designed program of imperial conquest. The tradition itself shows armies moving in response to opportunity, tribal rivalry, and shifting reports of enemy strength. Some modern historians, notably Fred Donner, argue that the earliest campaigns were extensions of a movement of religious "Believers" whose leaders sought to bring neighboring peoples under their authority, with the larger ambition of conquering empires emerging gradually in response to success. Others place more weight on the economic incentives of raiding and tribute and on the fluid character of the Arabian frontier. The two views are not incompatible. What matters for the narrative is that early objectives appear to have been limited: raids, tribute, and the occupation of frontier districts, not yet the destruction of empires. Only after repeated victories did the ambition grow.

The tradition's picture of command is also uncertain. It credits Abū Bakr with sending four columns to Syria under Yazīd ibn Abī Sufyān, Shuraḥbīl ibn Ḥasana, Abū ʿUbayda ibn al-Jarrāḥ, and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, and it sends Khālid ibn al-Walīd to Iraq and then to Syria. The chronology of Khālid's movements is among the most disputed questions in the early sources, and some historians doubt that he fought in Iraq at all before moving to Syria, suspecting that later tradition created a unified "Khālid campaign" by combining separate memories (Section 39.12). Abū Bakr died in 634 and ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who succeeded him, is the figure to whom tradition attributes the establishment of the dīwān, the garrison cities, and much of the early administrative framework. These attributions are plausible in outline but may compress developments that unfolded over a longer period and under several leaders. What is clear is that by ʿUmar's reign Medina had become a command center, issuing orders and receiving reports from commanders at distances of hundreds of kilometers, and that the caliph exercised real, though not complete, control over the armies.

Section 39.3

The Byzantine and Sasanian Empires after the War of 602–628

The war of 602–628, examined in Chapter 34, left the two empires in very different conditions. Byzantium emerged victorious, but at a cost. Sasanian forces had occupied Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, in some cases for more than a decade. Jerusalem fell to the Sasanians in 614, and contemporary and later sources report massacres, the damaging of churches, and the removal of the relic known as the True Cross. Heraclius, after campaigns in Anatolia, Armenia, and northern Mesopotamia, defeated the Sasanian army near Nineveh in 627 and the war ended with Khusrau II's overthrow. The emperor returned the True Cross to Jerusalem in a ceremony traditionally dated to 630. Yet the provinces had been devastated, tax revenue was disrupted, many soldiers had gone unpaid, and the ceremonial triumph of restored imperial authority rested on resources the state no longer had. The imperial army, though victorious, had been heavily taxed and depleted, and the frontier forces that had guarded the desert margins, including Arab allies who relied on imperial subsidies, were in some cases poorly supported. The tradition that Heraclius reduced payments to Arab frontier groups is reported but cannot be confirmed in detail.

Religious conflict added to the strain. Byzantine emperors sought unity among Christians divided over the Council of Chalcedon (451), and in the 630s Heraclius and Patriarch Sergius of Constantinople promoted a compromise doctrine known as Monoenergism, later Monotheletism, which pleased few. In Syria and Egypt, where Miaphysite Christians, who rejected Chalcedon, formed large communities, imperial attempts to enforce Chalcedonian authority were sources of resentment. Egyptian Copts, in particular, had a long grievance against Chalcedonian rule that reached a high point under Cyrus, appointed patriarch and prefect of Alexandria around 631 (Section 39.17). Heraclius is also reported to have taken measures against Jews after the reconquest of Palestine, for example by authorizing or requiring baptisms, although the exact scale and the chronology of these measures are debated. Such evidence shows tension within Byzantine society without proving that any group welcomed an invader. Byzantium also remained an institution with an army, a treasury, an administrative system, a capital, and a navy. Its crisis was serious but not systemic.

The Sasanian crisis was of a different kind. Khusrau II had been deposed and executed in 628 by his son Kavād II (Shērōē), who immediately made peace with Heraclius. Kavād II died within months, reportedly of plague, and in the following four years some eight or more rulers or claimants took the throne, among them Ardashir III, a child; the general Shahrbarāz, who briefly seized power and was murdered; Khusrau II's daughters Būrān and Āzarmīdukht; and several short-lived kings. Yazdegerd III, a grandson of Khusrau II, was enthroned around 632, still very young, and relied on powerful aristocrats, including the general Rostam Farrokhzād. The monarchy depended on cooperation among a handful of noble families and regional military commanders, the spāhbeds, and on the tax revenues of the rich Mesopotamian plain. Civil war, plague, and the empire's inability to agree on a king weakened the very institution that had held the empire together. The Lakhmid dynasty of al-Ḥīra had been abolished by Khusrau II around 602, and the Sasanians thus lacked the frontier Arab client kingdom that might have helped manage the western desert fringe. Reports of severe floods in Mesopotamia in the late 620s, which damaged embankments and irrigation channels, if accurate, would further have hurt the tax base (Section 39.11).

The contrast matters because it shapes everything that follows. Byzantium had an institutional center that could survive provincial losses. The Sasanian state was a dynastic monarchy whose center was Ctesiphon in the Mesopotamian lowlands, the very region the Muslim armies first conquered. When it was lost, the dynasty had no second heartland to which to retreat, although it did have the plateau of Iran, whose mountains and distances would delay the conquest for years.

Section 39.4

Armies, Commanders, Tribal Networks, and the Geography of Conquest

The Muslim armies were drawn from tribes across Arabia and organized by tribal contingents under commanders whose rank depended on a mixture of seniority in the community, military reputation, and tribal standing. The earliest Muslims from Mecca and Medina, the muhājirūn and anṣār, held high prestige, and Qurashī commanders such as Khālid, Abū ʿUbayda, and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ held many senior posts. Tribal contingents from Bakr, Tamīm, Thaqīf, Azd, Madhḥij, and Ḥimyar were crucial, and the former Ridda tribes contributed substantial numbers. Arab tribes already living in Syria and Iraq played several roles, some of them Christian, such as those of Ghassān, Lakhm, Judhām, and Taghlib. Some fought for the empires, some changed sides, and some stayed neutral. The reported size of Muslim armies is small by imperial standards. Arabic sources give figures of a few thousand for early expeditions and tens of thousands for the great battles; modern estimates for individual battles are generally much lower than the figures in the sources, and the Byzantine and Sasanian numbers in the tradition are usually greatly exaggerated.

The armies were fast-moving and relied on a combination of cavalry, camel transport, infantry, and archers. Camels allowed movement through arid country and the carrying of water and supplies over long distances, while horses supplied shock and pursuit power. The Muslim forces at first lacked siege equipment, which helps explain why they often bypassed fortified cities, ravaged the countryside, and negotiated tribute, and why prolonged sieges, such as Caesarea and Babylon Fortress, took many months. They learned quickly. They used local guides and captured specialists and, in time, adopted the techniques and engineers of the empires they were fighting. Communication relied on couriers carrying letters between Medina and the commanders, supplemented by informal intelligence from allies and captured informants.

Geography shaped each theater. In Syria, the desert fringe from the Ḥijāz through the Wādī ʿAraba and the Syrian steppe let Arab forces approach Byzantine territory from several directions while remaining close to their own base. The Byzantine army, by contrast, was dispersed in fortified cities and depended on roads, supply, and coordination across a broad territory. In Iraq, the Euphrates and the canals of the Sawād both aided and obstructed movement; armies could march along the desert edge, where heavy Sasanian cavalry and elephants operated at a disadvantage, and use the Euphrates as a supply line. In Egypt, the Sinai coastal route led directly to the Delta, but the Nile, its branches, and canals created defensive lines at Babylon Fortress and the Delta. On the Iranian plateau, mountain barriers, long distances, and dispersed oasis settlements (Chapter 2) meant that no single victory could bring the whole region under control, and campaigns depended on securing individual cities and negotiating with local rulers.

The Conquest of Byzantine Syria and Palestine

From the first engagements of 633–634 through Yarmūk, Jerusalem, and the Syrian cities to a new frontier with Anatolia.

Section 39.5

The First Campaigns into Syria and Palestine, 633–634

The Syria that Muslim forces entered was a region of Byzantine provinces: Palaestina Prima and Secunda, Phoenice, Arabia, and Syria, with their cities, estates, monasteries, and fortified frontier posts. Roman military organization along the southern desert fringe had long depended on a mixture of regular troops and Arab federates. The Ghassānid phylarchs had been the most prominent of these, though their authority had weakened by the early seventh century. Arab tribes such as Judhām, Lakhm, and Quḍāʿa lived in the borderlands and served as soldiers, guides, and mediators. For Arab tribal networks, the frontier was not a line but a zone of kinship, trade, and shifting loyalty, and early Muslim forces could draw on these ties. Some tribes of the zone joined the invaders, some fought for Byzantium, and some seem to have changed sides more than once.

The earliest campaigns, traditionally placed in 633–634, were raids and probes into southern Palestine and Transjordan. Arabic tradition says that Abū Bakr dispatched forces under Yazīd ibn Abī Sufyān, Shuraḥbīl ibn Ḥasana, Abū ʿUbayda, and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, and that Byzantine officials marshaled local garrisons in response. A non-Muslim source offers a rare early check. Thomas the Presbyter, writing around 640, appears to date a clash between Romans and Arabs near Gaza to early February 634, and the Doctrina Jacobi reports the killing of a Byzantine official in Palestine. The Arabic tradition places a battle in the Wādī ʿAraba region, and a Byzantine commander, Sergius, the patrikios of Caesarea, is reported killed in one account. These clues suggest that fighting in southern Palestine began early and that Byzantine forces suffered real defeats from the beginning. The details, however, cannot be fully harmonized.

The tradition gives Khālid ibn al-Walīd a major role, reporting that he crossed the Syrian desert from Iraq, in a march of several days, to reinforce the armies in Syria. Whether he came from Iraq at all, and when, is disputed (Section 39.12). Some of the earlier Syrian successes attributed to Khālid, among them the surrender of Bosra in the Ḥawrān, which the tradition places in 634, are likely to have involved several commanders. The Byzantine government meanwhile, under Heraclius, who was at Ḥimṣ (Emesa) or in northern Syria, reacted by sending forces, including the emperor's brother Theodore. The first period of campaigning ended in 634 with the death of Abū Bakr, which the tradition places in August of that year, and the accession of ʿUmar. The war in Syria would be decided in the following years.

Section 39.6

Ajnādayn, Faḥl, and the Contest for Southern Syria

The battle of Ajnādayn, traditionally placed in July 634, is described as the first major pitched battle between Muslim and Byzantine armies in Palestine. Arabic sources locate it somewhere between Ramla and Bayt Jibrīn in the Judean lowlands. They report a Muslim victory over a substantial Byzantine force, and some later accounts blur it with other engagements. Contemporary Christian writers refer to heavy fighting in Palestine in these years, but none provides a clear, datable account of Ajnādayn itself, and some scholars suspect that the sources have telescoped several clashes. The consequences were clear: Byzantine forces in Palestine were compelled to retreat toward fortified towns, and the open countryside of southern Palestine passed largely out of imperial control.

The next major engagement was at Fiḥl (Pella) in the Jordan Valley, which tradition dates to about January 635. The Jordan Valley mattered strategically: it controlled the route between Galilee and the Transjordanian highlands, connecting Palestine with Damascus, and nearby Scythopolis (Beth Shean) was one of the region's most important cities. Arabic accounts describe a Byzantine attempt to hinder the Muslim advance by flooding low ground, and the Muslims fighting in difficult, muddy terrain before winning. As with other early battles, such details may reflect later storytelling. Archaeological work at Pella and Scythopolis indicates that these cities were not simply destroyed in the 630s; rather, they continued with some change in occupation, as did many others. Some towns surrendered by agreement; others were taken later.

By 635, then, the Byzantine position in southern Syria had been significantly weakened, but not broken. Damascus remained under siege, the northern cities and the coast were still in imperial hands, and Heraclius could assemble reinforcements. The Muslim forces held ground in the countryside but did not yet have a secure base. Uncertainties of sequence, whether Ajnādayn preceded or followed the fall of Damascus, and whether the battle of Marj al-Ṣuffar belongs before or after Fiḥl, are typical of the problems that make a precise reconstruction of these years impossible. The overall direction, however, is consistent across the sources: Palestine and the south were contested from 634, and the Byzantine response was neither absent nor ineffective, but it failed to prevent the loss of the countryside.

Section 39.7

The Battle of Yarmūk, 636: A Decisive Byzantine Defeat

In 636 the Byzantines mounted a major counteroffensive. Arabic sources report that Heraclius assembled a large army from Syria, Armenia, and Arab allies, and that the Muslim commanders, in the face of this threat, withdrew from Ḥimṣ and Damascus toward the southern frontier, returning at least part of the tribute they had collected, an incident that later tradition emphasized to show the Muslims' good faith. The Muslim forces gathered in the valley of the Yarmūk River, a tributary of the Jordan that forms a deep gorge in the Ḥawrān and Transjordan area. The Byzantine army included regular troops, Armenian contingents under a general named Vahan, Arab allies led by Jabala ibn al-Ayham, and a senior commander named Theodore, called the sakellarios. The Muslim leadership comprised Abū ʿUbayda, Khālid, ʿAmr, Yazīd, and Shuraḥbīl, and the tradition says Khālid took tactical command.

The armies faced one another in the Yarmūk valley for weeks, the tradition reports, with skirmishing and some negotiation. The decisive fighting is said to have lasted about six days in August 636. Accounts describe cavalry charges, infantry stands, and the use of the ravine and the river on the Byzantine flank. Theophanes, drawing on earlier Syriac or Greek material, mentions a dust storm or strong wind that disadvantaged the Byzantines. Arabic sources describe the Byzantine retreat becoming a rout when Muslim cavalry cut the army off, and many Byzantine soldiers are reported to have perished in the ravines. Arabic tradition gives the Byzantine army as one hundred thousand or more, and the Muslim as twenty to forty thousand; modern historians such as Walter Kaegi consider these figures far too high and think that the armies were probably each considerably smaller, perhaps in the range of tens of thousands at most, and possibly considerably fewer. Detailed battle narratives, the ruses, the heroic duels, and the speeches, should be treated as literary.

What can be said with confidence is that at Yarmūk the Byzantines lost their principal field army in Syria, and that this loss removed the means by which the empire might have recovered the province. Several consequences followed. Byzantine garrisons, deprived of relief, were increasingly isolated. Armenian, Arab, and other contingents were dispersed or demoralized. Heraclius, who was in northern Syria, withdrew toward Anatolia and Constantinople, and later Greek and Arabic traditions provide the emperor with a dramatic farewell to Syria, a story that probably reflects later reflection more than a recorded event. The empire did not collapse: coastal cities and some fortified towns held out, and the Byzantine navy still controlled the sea. But the military initiative had passed to the Muslims. The battle is rightly regarded as one of the great turning points of the conquest, but it was one stage in a longer process, not its end.

Section 39.8

Jerusalem and the Conquest of Palestine

Jerusalem was both a provincial city and one of the most important sacred places in the Christian world, the site of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and a city of special significance for Jews and Muslims. It had been conquered by the Sasanians in 614, with reported destruction and killing and the removal of the True Cross, and recovered by Byzantium, with the Cross restored in a ceremony traditionally dated to 630. Its patriarch in the 630s, Sophronius, was a learned theologian who preached in 634 that Saracens were preventing pilgrims from reaching Bethlehem. By late in the sequence of campaigns, Muslim forces were laying siege to Jerusalem. Arabic sources describe a siege of some months, followed by a negotiated surrender in which Sophronius played the leading role for the city. The date is uncertain; traditional dates range from 636 to 638, and the most commonly cited fall in 637–638.

Tradition says that the caliph ʿUmar traveled to Jerusalem in person to receive the surrender and that he issued an assurance of safety, a document known as the covenant of Iliyāʾ (the Roman name Aelia Capitolina). The earliest surviving versions of the text, in al-Ṭabarī and others, promise protection of lives, property, churches, and crosses; prohibit the conversion of churches into dwellings or their destruction; and impose payment of the poll tax. The text and the story of ʿUmar's visit are widely questioned: the text comes down in versions that differ, and the journey itself, while possible, is told in legendary form. Some later versions of the text add that Jews were not to reside in the city, a clause that appears in some versions and not others, which has led historians to doubt that it was part of the original. What can be said is that Jerusalem passed to Muslim rule by negotiated surrender, and that the Christian community, led by its patriarch, was able to continue its worship. A Jewish presence in Jerusalem in the following decades is attested by later sources, including documents from the Cairo Geniza.

The fate of the Temple Mount, the site of the destroyed Jewish Temple, was a matter of particular significance. Traditions say that ʿUmar had the site, which had been used as a refuse heap under the Byzantines, cleared and that a prayer place was established there. Around 670 the Frankish pilgrim Arculf, whose account is preserved in a work by Adomnán of Iona, described a rough rectangular house of prayer on the platform that could accommodate a large number of people. This supports the view that Muslims used the site early, although the construction of the monumental Dome of the Rock under ʿAbd al-Malik in the 690s was a later development examined in Chapter 41. In the rest of Palestine, many towns surrendered by agreement, among them Lydda and Gaza according to tradition. Caesarea, the provincial capital and a port, held out much longer (Section 39.9).

Section 39.9

Damascus, Ḥimṣ, Antioch, Caesarea, and the Syrian Cities

The conquest of Syria's cities followed no single pattern, and the sources preserve distinct traditions for each. Damascus, the largest city of Roman Syria, was besieged in 634–635 and fell, by the traditional chronology, around 635; some scholars place it in 636 after Yarmūk. Arabic sources contain two competing accounts. One says that Khālid entered by force at the eastern gate, while Abū ʿUbayda took another part of the city by agreement; the other describes a negotiated settlement. The tradition resolved the conflict by treating the entire city as taken by agreement, a legal solution that reveals how later jurists shaped the memory of conquest (Section 39.26). The cities of Ḥimṣ, Baʿlabakk, Aleppo, Qinnasrīn, and Antioch, the great Byzantine administrative center in the north, are reported to have capitulated, generally after Yarmūk, on terms that included tribute and guarantees. Antioch's surrender is dated by tradition to about 637–638; Byzantine counterattacks and Muslim retaliation in this region may have complicated the sequence.

The coastal cities were more difficult. The Byzantine navy supplied them by sea, and the Muslims had no fleet. Caesarea, the capital of Palaestina Prima, resisted for years and fell by tradition after a long siege led by Muʿāwiya in about 640–641. Other ports, including Tyre, Acre, Ashkelon, and Tripoli, passed to Muslim control at different times, and some of them were reoccupied by Byzantium and retaken. The development of a Muslim naval capacity, which began under Muʿāwiya in the 640s, was a direct response to this problem (Chapter 40).

Cities that surrendered mostly continued as before. Damascus, Ḥimṣ, Jerusalem, and Aleppo retained their Christian populations, churches, and local administrations. In some, a part of the city or an adjoining space was assigned to Muslim use. Later traditions describe Muslims and Christians using portions of the church complex associated with Saint John in Damascus before the Umayyad caliph al-Walīd I rebuilt the site as the Great Mosque in the early eighth century; the precise arrangements and the narrative of shared use are debated (Chapter 41). Muslim troops were often settled not within the old cities but in camps nearby or at selected sites, such as Jābiya in the Golan, an early base. The picture that emerges is one of Syrian cities reaching accommodation with new masters who initially had few administrators of their own and depended on local officials. The speed of the conquest, and the shifting pattern of capitulation and resistance, were partly a result of local calculations that varied from city to city.

Section 39.10

Northern Syria, the Jazīra, and the Byzantine Frontier

After the fall of Antioch and the cities of northern Syria, Muslim armies moved into the Jazīra, the upland plain of Upper Mesopotamia between the Euphrates and the Tigris. Arabic tradition attributes the conquest of the region, including Raqqa, Edessa (al-Ruhā), Ḥarrān, Samosata, and Amida (Āmid), to ʿIyāḍ ibn Ghanm around 639–640. Edessa and other cities are said to have capitulated on terms. The population of the Jazīra included Miaphysite (Syriac Orthodox) Christians, East Syriac Christians of the Church of the East, Chalcedonian communities, Arab Christian tribes such as Taghlib, and Jews. The Taghlib, according to tradition, were permitted to remain Christian on condition that they paid a double rate of an alms tax levied on Muslims, an arrangement that shows how the new authorities adapted fiscal categories to groups that were not simply "conquered subjects."

North of the Jazīra, the line between Muslim and Byzantine power began to settle along the Taurus and Anti-Taurus ranges, introduced in Chapter 2 as a long-standing frontier. Armenian sources describe Muslim raids on Armenia, with the capture of Dvin in 640, and Muslim columns reached into Anatolia, including Cappadocia, in the 640s and later. The Byzantines adopted a strategy that avoided pitched battles with the Muslims, relying on fortified places and mountain refuges and allowing the plains of Cilicia to become a depopulated buffer. Whether this was a deliberate policy or an improvised response is debated. Over the coming decades this frontier zone, with its raids, fortresses, and shifting populations, became a permanent feature of the region, and it was the origin of the later Muslim-Byzantine border systems.

The conquest of Syria also involved a human catastrophe unrelated to warfare. A severe plague, remembered in Arabic sources as the plague of ʿAmwās, struck Syria and Palestine in about 638–639 and killed many people, including senior commanders reported to have died, such as Abū ʿUbayda and Yazīd ibn Abī Sufyān. After this, ʿUmar is said to have appointed Yazīd's brother Muʿāwiya to the governorship of Damascus and the Syrian territories, giving the family a position that would shape later history (Chapter 40). The plague, together with a famine in Arabia remembered as the "year of ashes" in about 638, reminds students that epidemic disease and food shortage were part of the background of the conquests, for victors and vanquished alike. By around 640 the Byzantine Empire had lost Syria, Palestine, and the Jazīra, and its government at Constantinople, still struggling with the succession to Heraclius, who died in February 641, was faced with the loss of Egypt next.

The Conquest of Sasanian Iraq and Mesopotamia

The loss of the Sasanian heartland, distinguished from the destruction of the entire Sasanian state.

Section 39.11

Sasanian Iraq before the Conquest: Cities, Agriculture, and Imperial Government

Lower Mesopotamia was the economic heart of the Sasanian Empire. The alluvial plain between the Tigris and Euphrates, which Arabic sources call the Sawād ("the dark land") and Persian administrators called Āsōristān, was crossed by a dense network of canals, among them the great Nahrawān canal east of the Tigris, which supported intensive cultivation of grain, dates, and other crops (Chapter 2). The Sasanian state taxed this land heavily. The reforms attributed to Khusrau I in the sixth century, including a land survey and a more regular tax on land and persons, rested on the productivity of this region. The loss of it would be a fiscal as well as a military blow. Environmental fragility mattered, too: Mesopotamian irrigation required continual maintenance, and later Arabic and Persian sources describe severe flooding in the late 620s that broke embankments and left areas of marsh. The scale of the damage is uncertain, but it is plausible that the civil wars and the floods together disrupted agriculture and revenue.

The twin capital of Ctesiphon (Ṭaysafūn), on the Tigris, and its neighbor Veh-Ardashīr (Seleucia) formed an urban complex that Arabic sources call al-Madāʾin, "the cities." The royal palace and the great vaulted hall known as the Ṭāq-i Kisrā, still standing today, marked the monarchy's seat. The population was diverse. Zoroastrian priests and aristocrats formed the ruling elite. A large Christian population belonged to the Church of the East, whose catholicos resided at Seleucia-Ctesiphon, and there were also Miaphysite Christians, many of them descended from people deported from Roman cities during earlier wars. The Jewish communities of Babylonia, home of the Babylonian Talmud and its academies, lived under Sasanian rule with their own institutions. Landowning gentry, the dihqāns, managed estates and collected taxes for the state. Further north and west lay Aramaic-speaking villages, Arab tribal groups, and towns such as Anbār and ʿAyn al-Tamr on the Euphrates.

Al-Ḥīra, on the western edge of the Sawād near the Euphrates, had been the capital of the Lakhmid Arab kingdom, a Sasanian client state and a center of Arab Christianity of the Church of the East, with monasteries, churches, and a literate elite. Khusrau II had deposed the last Lakhmid king around 602, and al-Ḥīra was thereafter governed by Sasanian officials, though its Arab and Christian character persisted. After the Lakhmid collapse, Arab tribes along the frontier had greater freedom to raid, a development that helps explain why the first Muslim forces in Iraq could find allies and information among tribes familiar with the Sasanian frontier. For the Sasanian empire, in sum, Iraq was both its richest province and its most exposed. Its defenders would have to hold it with armies drawn from a monarchy still divided by civil conflict.

Section 39.12

The First Campaigns in Iraq and the Battle of the Bridge

The Arabic tradition describes Khālid ibn al-Walīd moving from eastern Arabia into the Sawād in about 633 and winning a series of engagements: the battle of the Chains (Dhāt al-Salāsil), the victory at Ullays, the capitulation of al-Ḥīra, and the capture of Anbār and ʿAyn al-Tamr. In the Ḥīra story, the city's notables, led by a man called Ṣalūbā ibn Naṣṭūnā in some accounts, agree to pay an annual sum, with a figure of 190,000 dirhams often reported, in return for protection. Whether Khālid actually conducted this campaign, and in this order, is disputed. Some historians consider that the Iraqi campaign has been shaped by later tradition, which gathered memories of several tribal leaders and raids into a coherent story centered on Khālid. Al-Muthannā ibn Ḥāritha and his Bakr ibn Wāʾil tribesmen were active in the region, and it seems likely that early operations in Iraq involved tribal raids and negotiations with local leaders, not a single systematic campaign.

The Sasanian response gathered strength during 634. Under the leadership of Rostam Farrokhzād and others, the empire assembled armies, and the Muslims, reinforced from Medina, sent a force led by Abū ʿUbayd ibn Masʿūd al-Thaqafī. At the Battle of the Bridge, traditionally dated to October 634 and fought on the Euphrates near al-Ḥīra, a Sasanian force under a commander called Bahman Jādhūya, which included elephants, defeated the Muslims. Abū ʿUbayd was killed, and the Muslim army retreated across a pontoon bridge in disorder, with heavy losses, including many who drowned. It was a significant setback, and a reminder that the Muslim conquests were not a triumphal progress. The Sasanian state, divided as it was, could still defeat the invaders in the field.

The tradition continues with a Muslim recovery at Buwayb in late 634 or 635, a victory under al-Muthannā over a Persian force. Yet the Sasanian court's own political divisions limited its ability to exploit its successes. Contested accession struggles and competition among the nobility, in which Rostam and others played large roles, diverted resources and attention. Meanwhile ʿUmar, after the defeat at the Bridge, drew on recruits from the Arabian tribes and sent substantial new forces to Iraq under Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ. The first phase of the Iraqi war thus ended without a decisive result, though it demonstrated that Muslim forces could operate in the Sawād and that its Arab population and many local communities were prepared to negotiate. The decisive confrontation was still to come.

Section 39.13

The Battle of al-Qādisiyya, c. 636–637

Al-Qādisiyya was fought near a settlement of that name on the western edge of the Sawād, close to the Euphrates and the desert, a position that guarded the approach to al-Ḥīra and the road to Ctesiphon. Arabic tradition gives the Muslim commander as Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, an early Companion of the Prophet who is said to have directed the fighting from a nearby fortified building because of illness. The Sasanian army was led by Rostam Farrokhzād, who held the high rank of spāhbed and whom Persian tradition remembers as an astute and cautious general. Both sides are said to have exchanged envoys before the battle, and the speeches and encounters attributed to these envoys are among the best-known episodes in Arabic literature, though they are almost certainly stylized.

The date is disputed. Al-Ṭabarī and much of the tradition give 15 AH (636–637), but other reports give 14 or 16 AH, and modern scholars have proposed dates from about 636 to 638. The tradition describes several days of fighting, with names such as the Day of Armāth, the Day of Aghwāth, and the Day of ʿImās, and a final "Night of Clamor" (laylat al-harīr). Persian elephants, again, are reported to have caused the Muslims difficulty, and the battle turned when Rostam was killed. The Sasanian army broke and fled. The figures for the armies, tens of thousands on each side, again should be treated with caution: modern estimates are considerably lower, but no consensus has been reached.

Whatever the details, the consequences were clear in the sources. The Sasanian field army that had defended southern Iraq was defeated, its leading general killed, and the road to Ctesiphon opened. The battle was not the end of Sasanian resistance, as the continuing fighting at Jalūlāʾ and elsewhere would show. But it deprived the monarchy of its best chance to defend the Sawād, and in later Persian memory Rostam's death at Qādisiyya became a symbol of the fall of the Sasanian order, a memory that was reshaped in later Persian literature (Chapter 49). It is hard to find a clearer example of a battle whose importance is secure while its details are not.

Primary Source Window I

Two Ways of Remembering the Fall of Sasanian Iraq: al-Ṭabarī and the Syriac Khuzistan Chronicle

Creators: al-Ṭabarī (Baghdad, died 923), compiling reports largely from Sayf ibn ʿUmar; an anonymous East Syriac Christian writer of southwestern Iran, c. 660s · Type: a long Arabic annalistic narrative and a short Syriac ecclesiastical chronicle

Context: The most detailed account of al-Qādisiyya comes from al-Ṭabarī's History, written about three centuries later. A much shorter account of the same period comes from the Khuzistan Chronicle, composed roughly a generation after the events by a Christian from within the conquered Sasanian world.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Al-Ṭabarī narrates the battle over several named days, with exchanges of envoys, descriptions of Persian elephants, the heroism of individual warriors, and the death of Rostam, and he provides a precise year according to the Hijrī calendar. The Khuzistan Chronicle, by contrast, as commonly read by modern scholars, treats the Arab victories in summary terms within a narrative whose principal concern is the fortunes of the church and the end of Persian royal rule, and it does not offer a battlefield narrative of comparable detail.

Analysis: The contrast illustrates how different the two traditions are in purpose. The Arabic narrative commemorates the founding victory of a conquering community and the ancestors of its tribes, and its details are shaped by oral storytelling and literary convention. The Syriac chronicle was written by a member of a minority church to make sense of a political collapse, and it records it briefly. Neither is a neutral report. The Arabic account's wealth of detail does not prove accuracy; the Syriac account's brevity does not prove ignorance. Historians use the Syriac text to confirm that a decisive defeat and the fall of the Sasanian monarchy occurred, and the Arabic text to reconstruct the likely framework of events, while treating specifics, such as troop numbers, speeches, and exact dates, as uncertain.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might an author writing three centuries after an event, from the victorious side, preserve more detail than an author writing a generation after it from the defeated side?
  2. What kinds of detail in al-Ṭabarī's account of al-Qādisiyya are most likely to reflect literary convention rather than reliable memory?
  3. What can a brief, ecclesiastical account of the conquest tell historians that a long battle narrative cannot?
  4. How should a historian decide which source to prefer when two accounts differ in date and detail?
Section 39.14

The Fall of Ctesiphon and the Loss of the Sasanian Imperial Heartland

After al-Qādisiyya, Muslim forces moved against al-Ḥīra and then toward the Tigris. They met resistance at Bābil and elsewhere, and tradition records a siege of Veh-Ardashīr, the western half of the capital complex, which lasted some weeks or months. The Sasanian government's armies had been broken, but the Tigris itself and the city's fortifications offered some defense. Arabic accounts say that the Persians withdrew across the river and broke the bridges, and that the Muslims, finding the river in flood, crossed it by swimming their horses, an episode that became a heroic set piece. Whether or not the crossing happened as told, the sources agree that the Muslims occupied the capital, traditionally dated to about 637, when Yazdegerd III and the court had already withdrawn into the Iranian highlands.

The occupation of Ctesiphon had several consequences. The treasuries, palaces, and storehouses, accumulated through centuries of imperial tax collection and plunder, were captured, and the Arabic tradition speaks of fabulous booty, including the great carpet of Khusrau, said to have been cut into pieces and divided. The tradition says that the fifth portion due to the central treasury was sent to Medina and that the remainder was divided among the soldiers, an event that, whatever the precise amounts, gave the conquest a material reward that fed further recruitment. Saʿd is said to have used the audience hall of the Ṭāq-i Kisrā for prayer, a detail that reflects the symbolic value the site acquired. The Church of the East, whose catholicos had its seat in the capital, and the Jewish and Christian populations of the twin cities, began to deal with new rulers.

The loss of Ctesiphon deprived the Sasanian monarchy of its principal administrative center, its treasury, and much of its tax base. Yet the Muslims did not remain there. Instead, on ʿUmar's instruction according to tradition, they founded a new garrison city at Kūfa, on the Euphrates near al-Ḥīra, which became the center of Muslim government in Iraq (Section 39.27). The twin cities declined, and the old Sasanian capital was never again an imperial seat. The monarchy's survival now depended on its ability to relocate and mobilize resources in the Iranian highlands, an effort that could draw on the loyalty of the great houses, the provincial governors, and the memory of dynastic legitimacy.

Section 39.15

Jalūlāʾ, the Iraqi Interior, and the Consolidation of Mesopotamia

The fall of Ctesiphon did not end organized resistance. Persian forces regrouped at Jalūlāʾ, on a tributary of the Diyāla river northeast of the capital, where they fortified a position on the road toward the Zagros and Ḥulwān. Arabic sources report that the Muslims, under Hāshim ibn ʿUtba and with ʿUmar's authorization, besieged this position for several months and finally defeated the Persian forces, traditionally in 637 or 638. The victory secured the Muslim hold on the central Mesopotamian plain and opened the way to Ḥulwān, the gateway to the Iranian plateau. Yazdegerd III is said to have retreated at this point to Rayy and Isfahan.

Consolidation involved a wider set of tasks. Muslim forces took Tikrīt, which held out for some time with the help of Byzantine and Arab Christian allies according to the tradition, and secured the main routes of the Sawād. The conquest in the interior depended on local agreement as much as on arms. Landowners, village leaders, and town notables negotiated payments, and many continued to collect taxes from peasants under the new rulers. Individual forts and towns that resisted might be taken by force, and the sources report episodes of plunder, enslavement, and punishment. The difficulties of extending control were substantial: the Sawād was a landscape of canals, estates, and villages that could not easily be garrisoned, and its management depended on cooperation from people familiar with its irrigation and its tax records.

ʿUmar's administrative decisions in Iraq are among the best-known in the tradition. Soldiers reportedly asked that the conquered land of the Sawād be distributed among them as booty, and ʿUmar is said to have refused, preferring that the cultivators remain on the land and pay a tax to support the community as a whole. The story is preserved in later legal and historical sources whose interest in the question was legal, and the precise outcome of the debate probably took shape over time (Section 39.28). What is clear is that the main economic pattern of the Sawād was maintained: the peasants continued to farm, and the state continued to tax them. The conquerors drew on this revenue to pay stipends to their soldiers, a decision with large consequences for the political history of the next decades.

Section 39.16

Northern Iraq and the Transformation of the Mesopotamian Frontier

North of Ctesiphon, Muslim forces extended their control up the Tigris to Tikrīt and Mosul and into the foothills of the Zagros. Arabic sources describe agreements with local leaders at Mosul and in the plain of Nineveh and report campaigns in the region of Shahrazūr and the Zagros approaches, where Kurdish groups and other highland communities lived. Kurdish tribes are mentioned in the tradition as opponents and as parties to agreements, and they appear as one of several highland populations whose relations with Sasanian authority had been loose and negotiated. The sources, however, are brief and do not allow a detailed reconstruction of Kurdish society at this period. It would be anachronistic to treat these groups as a single political unit; the Arabic sources use the word "Kurd" for various highland and pastoral populations whose boundaries are uncertain, a point developed in later volumes on Kurdish history.

The Mesopotamian frontier that emerged was a zone of interaction among several communities. Arab tribes, including Taghlib, Namir, and Iyād, many of them Christian, lived in the northern Jazīra. Syriac-speaking Christians of the Church of the East lived in Mosul and the Tigris basin, and Miaphysites in other towns. Muslim garrisons at Mosul and elsewhere linked the Iraqi and Syrian fronts. The Jazīra connected Syria with Iraq, so that the two armies of conquest were joined by a corridor under Muslim control.

Control of Mesopotamia provided the conquerors with a military and financial base. Kūfa and Baṣra became the staging points for campaigns into the Iranian highlands, with Kūfa orienting toward the north and the Jibāl and Baṣra toward Khūzestān and Fārs. The revenue of the Sawād supported the stipends of the soldiers and helped finance continuing war. In this sense the conquest of Iraq changed the strategic position of the Muslim polity: it was no longer a force dependent on Arabian resources but a state with access to one of the richest agricultural regions of the Middle East. How that wealth would be distributed became a major question of the next decades (Section 39.35 and Chapter 40).

Division IV · Sections 39.17–39.20

Egypt and the End of Byzantine Rule in the Nile Valley

A compact military campaign with extensive administrative and religious consequences, documented by papyri.

Section 39.17

Egypt before the Conquest and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ's Invasion

Egypt was among the wealthiest provinces of the Byzantine Empire. Its Nile-fed agriculture, described in Chapter 2, produced the grain that fed Constantinople and a large share of imperial tax revenue, and Alexandria was the empire's second great city and a major Mediterranean port. The province had been conquered by the Sasanians in about 618–619 and remained under their rule for roughly a decade, until Heraclius recovered it around 629. Sasanian occupation left papyri written in Middle Persian, and the return of imperial rule brought renewed taxation and administrative reorganization after a period of upheaval. The province's population was overwhelmingly Coptic-speaking in the countryside, with Greek the language of administration and of the urban elite of Alexandria and the provincial towns, and with substantial Jewish and other communities in the cities.

The Christian church was divided. The great majority of Egyptians were Miaphysite, adhering to the Coptic Church, while the imperial administration supported the Chalcedonian church. After the reconquest, Heraclius appointed Cyrus, who had been bishop of Phasis in the Caucasus, as both patriarch and prefect of Alexandria, around 631, uniting religious and civil authority in one person and charging him with advancing the compromise doctrine of Monoenergism. Coptic sources, notably the later tradition about the Coptic patriarch Benjamin, who fled into hiding, portray this period as one of persecution. Coptic hostility to Chalcedonian rule is thus a real factor, though it would be wrong to equate it with support for the Muslim invaders, a point taken up in Section 39.33.

Arabic tradition says that ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, who had played a major role in Palestine, urged an invasion of Egypt on the caliph ʿUmar, who hesitated because of the risks, and that ʿAmr set out with a small force, reported as three or four thousand men, in about December 639 (traditionally late 18 AH). The tradition adds that ʿUmar sent a letter that ʿAmr deliberately did not open until he had crossed the border. Such stories are likely to be later embellishments, but the idea of an initiative from the field commander seems plausible. Reinforcements followed, including a force under al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām. The army followed the Sinai coastal route, took al-ʿArīsh, and invested Pelusium (al-Faramā), the fortress guarding the eastern Delta, which fell after a siege of about a month in early 640. Bilbays followed. The Byzantine administration in Egypt was weakened by the death of Heraclius in February 641, by the succession struggle that followed, and by the divisions within Alexandria's leadership.

Section 39.18

Heliopolis, Babylon Fortress, and the Struggle for the Nile Valley

From the Delta's eastern edge the Muslim army moved toward the apex of the Delta, where the Nile divides and where the Roman fortress of Babylon (in the area of modern Old Cairo) controlled the river crossing and the route to Upper Egypt. Arabic sources describe a battle at Heliopolis (ʿAyn Shams) in July 640 against a Byzantine force, which the Muslims won, after which they besieged Babylon. The Byzantine defenders held the fortress for several months, according to the sources about seven, and the siege was complicated by the Nile flood, which in late summer filled the surrounding channels and made movement difficult. The Byzantines used boats on the river and the Muslims received reinforcements. During this time Muslim detachments raided the Fayyūm and the Delta, and the operations around Nikiu, a town in the Delta, are described in John of Nikiu's chronicle as involving violence against villages.

John of Nikiu, writing decades later, provides the most vivid account from within Egypt, and his attitude toward the Byzantine officials, especially Cyrus, is bitterly critical, as he blames them for the loss of Egypt. His chronicle also describes Muslim exactions and mistreatment in the villages. It is a valuable but partisan source, and it survives in a translation of a translation, so some details may be distorted. The Arabic sources by contrast give a more orderly account in which Cyrus, called al-Muqawqis, negotiates an agreement with ʿAmr, and in which Egyptians accept an annual payment in return for security. The negotiation, whatever its form, took place in the spring of 641, and Babylon surrendered, according to the traditional dating in April of that year, soon after the death of Heraclius.

The burdens fell heavily on village populations. Armies passing through a region required food, fodder, transport, and housing, and the people who supplied them were the farmers and villagers of the Nile Valley. Villages were obliged to provide labor for bridges and the repair of canals, and the requisitions of the first years later appear in the documentary record as regular payments. The campaign was thus experienced as a mixture of fighting, requisition, and negotiation, with many communities making terms with whichever force was nearby.

Section 39.19

Alexandria, Negotiated Surrender, and the Withdrawal of Byzantine Forces

With Babylon taken, ʿAmr advanced on Alexandria, the last major Byzantine stronghold in Egypt. The city, protected by walls and supplied by sea, was a formidable position. Its defense was complicated by politics in Constantinople. After Heraclius's death the court was split between his widow Martina and her son Heraklonas, on one side, and the supporters of his eldest son Constantine III and later his grandson Constans II, on the other, and the government was in no position to send substantial reinforcements. Cyrus, who had returned to Alexandria, was engaged in negotiations with the Muslims, and he faced hostility from both Byzantine officers and Egyptians.

According to John of Nikiu, a treaty was concluded at Alexandria in November 641. Its terms included a truce of about eleven months during which the Byzantine army would withdraw by sea, payment of tribute by the city, and an agreement about the movements of the forces. In Arabic sources the negotiation is again associated with al-Muqawqis, who is said to have agreed to pay a fixed tax on every adult male, traditionally two dinars a head, with provisions for the army, while excluding women, children, and the elderly. This tax figure is recorded in Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam, a ninth-century source, and its attribution to the original treaty is uncertain. The Byzantine evacuation of Alexandria took place in September 642, according to the usual reconstruction, and the Muslims entered the city soon after. Byzantine writers blamed Cyrus for treason or cowardice, though this accusation may reflect later polemic and the search for a scapegoat. The chronology and details of the negotiations are among the most uncertain parts of the conquest, with different sources placing the surrender of Alexandria in 641, 642, or even later.

The result was the end of Byzantine rule in Egypt after more than three centuries of Roman and Byzantine government. The Egyptian church returned to Coptic leadership: Benjamin, who had been in hiding, resumed his place at the head of the Coptic Church in about 643–644, according to Coptic tradition. This change, which ended the imperial attempt to impose Chalcedonian unity, had significant consequences for the Coptic community, even though Coptic leaders had not necessarily preferred Muslim rule as such.

Section 39.20

Egypt after the Conquest: Resistance, Reoccupation, and the New Political Order

Byzantine rule was not quite over. In about 645–646 a Byzantine fleet under a commander named Manuel recaptured Alexandria, probably with the support of part of the city's population, and ʿAmr, who had been replaced as governor of Egypt shortly before by ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd ibn Abī Sarḥ, the caliph ʿUthmān's foster-brother, was recalled to deal with the emergency. He defeated the Byzantines at Nikiu, reoccupied Alexandria, and, according to the tradition, had the city's walls demolished, a measure intended to prevent future resistance. The episode shows both that Byzantine power remained capable of a seaborne counterattack and that the Muslim hold on Egypt depended on holding the coast and on the cooperation of the Delta population. After this the Byzantines made no further serious attempt to recover Egypt for some time.

The new rulers built a base at Fustat, a military camp founded around 641–642 next to the Babylon Fortress, at the head of the Delta. The choice reportedly reflected ʿUmar's wish that the Muslims not be separated from Arabia by a body of water, and Fustat was easier to supply from the Nile and the canal that was reopened to carry grain to Arabia. A mosque associated with ʿAmr stood at its center. Alexandria, though the largest city, remained Greek in speech and Christian in population, with a Muslim garrison, and the Arab governor resided at Fustat. The pattern of a new military city beside an older one was typical of the conquest (Section 39.27).

Egypt's contribution to the new empire was large. It provided grain and a rich tax base. Following the conquest, ʿAmr is traditionally credited with reopening the Nile–Red Sea canal around 641–642 to facilitate shipments to the Ḥijāz, a response to food shortages associated with ʿUmar’s reign; this must not be confused with shipments already taking place during the earlier famine. The Muslim state extended its authority southward by a treaty, the baqt, with the Christian kingdom of Nubia in about 652, which regulated trade and provided for an annual delivery of slaves and goods. Campaigns also moved west into Cyrenaica and, in later years, toward Ifrīqiya, which belongs to the subsequent history of North Africa. Egypt's administration, as the papyri show, retained its Greek and Coptic officials, tax categories, and local institutions for a considerable time (Section 39.29).

Division V · Sections 39.21–39.25

The Collapse of the Sasanian Empire and the Conquest of Iran

An uneven, decade-long process that ended Sasanian sovereignty without immediately subduing Iranian society.

Section 39.21

Sasanian Political Fragmentation and the Survival of Yazdegerd III

The loss of Iraq did not end the Sasanian state, but it stripped away the resources on which the monarchy had depended. Yazdegerd III withdrew with his court into the highlands, and Arabic and Persian sources place him successively at Ḥulwān, Rayy, Isfahan, and later cities of the plateau and the east. The monarchy still had considerable assets: the dynasty's prestige, the Zoroastrian priesthood, the great noble houses, the armies of regional commanders, and the plateau's geography. Persian tradition reflected in later sources lists the leading families, among them the Mihrān of Hamadan and Rayy, the Kārin, the Spandiyād, and others, whose heads commanded forces and held provincial governorships. Whether they would fight for Yazdegerd depended on their own interests as much as on loyalty to the crown.

Rostam's death at al-Qādisiyya had removed the leading general, and the years of civil war had left the nobility divided. Yazdegerd, still young, lacked the authority to coordinate the great houses. Some regional commanders continued to resist, others negotiated separate terms with the Muslims, and some acted as independent rulers. The monarchy's weakness is visible in its coinage: silver drachms continued to be struck in Yazdegerd's name for years, and later issues, the so-called Arab-Sasanian coins, retained the Sasanian design while adding Arabic phrases and the names of Muslim governors. This continuity shows that the Sasanian monetary system, with its mints and its expectations of the monarchy's image, survived the political collapse and was taken over by the conquerors.

Yazdegerd is reported to have sought assistance from beyond the empire, including from Turkic rulers of Central Asia and from the Tang court of China, to which his family sent embassies, according to Chinese records. These appeals produced no decisive help. The monarchy's survival after 637 thus rested on a fragile alliance of aristocrats whose own regions were threatened in turn. The conquest of Iran would be the story of the progressive separation of those aristocrats from the crown and the gradual absorption of their territories.

Section 39.22

Khūzestān and Fārs: War, Resistance, and Regional Political Change

The first Iranian region to come under Muslim attack was Khūzestān, the lowland province of the southwest adjoining Iraq, with cities such as Ahwāz, Susa (Sūs), Shūshtar (Tustar), and Jundīshāpūr. Muslim forces from Baṣra under commanders such as ʿUtba ibn Ghazwān and later Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī began to raid the area in the late 630s. The province was led by Hormuzān, a magnate from one of the great Iranian families, who fought the invaders and, according to tradition, made agreements that he later broke. The campaign, between about 639 and 642, involved sieges of Susa and Shūshtar, and Shūshtar, a fortified town on a river, reportedly resisted for a long period. Hormuzān eventually surrendered and was sent to Medina, where, according to the tradition, he converted to Islam. Jundīshāpūr, known as a center of learning with a Christian medical tradition, surrendered. The Khuzistan Chronicle, written by a Christian from this region, is a rare local voice on these events, though a fragmentary one.

Fārs, the heartland of the Persians and the ancient home of the Achaemenids (Chapter 22), was a different matter. Its population was Persian-speaking and strongly Zoroastrian, with a priesthood centered on Iṣṭakhr near the ruins of Persepolis, and its nobility had long supported the dynasty. Muslim forces approached from Baḥrayn across the Persian Gulf, in raids on the coast in the late 630s, and from Baṣra by land. Resistance was strong, and the conquest was achieved only in the late 640s. The tradition reports that Iṣṭakhr fell around 649–650 to ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir, the governor of Baṣra, and that the region rebelled soon afterward, with the Muslim governor killed in one report, requiring a second campaign. Such rebellions were typical: a city taken by force or agreement might rise again once the army moved on.

The chronology of these operations is much less secure than the overall sequence. Different sources give different dates and different commanders, and the line between negotiated settlement and military occupation is hard to draw. What the evidence shows is a pattern of regional campaigns conducted largely from Baṣra, with local magnates making agreements that preserved a good deal of their authority in exchange for tribute, and with repeated resistance in the most strongly Persian regions. The conquest of southwestern Iran changed the political relations of the region more slowly than the fall of Ctesiphon had done in Iraq.

Section 39.23

The Battle of Nihāvand, 642, and the Opening of the Iranian Plateau

Nihāvand lies in the Zagros highlands, southeast of Hamadan, on the road that links the Mesopotamian lowlands with the central plateau. According to the Arabic tradition, Yazdegerd and his commanders organized a large army, led by a general named Fīrūzān (Pērōz) and drawn from many provinces, to defend the plateau, and the caliph ʿUmar sent forces from Kūfa and Baṣra under Nuʿmān ibn Muqarrin. The battle, traditionally dated to 642 (21 AH), though some sources give an earlier or later year, ended in a Muslim victory, with Nuʿmān killed and the Persian army dispersed. The tradition calls it the "Victory of Victories" (fatḥ al-futūḥ), reflecting its perceived importance. The numbers (some sources give sixty thousand or more Persians) are not credible, but the sources agree that a major Persian army was assembled and defeated. The role of stratagems, such as feigned retreats, in the Arabic accounts is again likely to be literary.

The strategic effect of Nihāvand was to open the plateau. Hamadan, one of the great cities of western Iran, passed to Muslim control, and the road to Rayy, near modern Tehran, and to Isfahan lay open. The tradition says that after the battle ʿUmar, who had previously been reluctant to send armies farther into Iran, authorized further advances. In the next two years Muslim forces took Hamadan, Rayy, Isfahan, and parts of Azerbaijan, often through agreements with local rulers. Not everything went smoothly: Hamadan and other cities rose in revolt and had to be retaken.

It would be an error to treat Nihāvand as the end of the Sasanian Empire. The Persian army that fought it was probably the largest that the dynasty could still assemble, and its destruction ended the monarchy's capacity to resist in the field on a large scale. But the dynasty survived, and so did the regional powers of the east and the north. Tabaristan, on the southern shore of the Caspian, remained effectively independent under local rulers for generations, protected by its mountains and forests. The battle thus marks the point at which Muslim conquest of Iran became likely, not the point at which it was complete.

Section 39.24

From Western Iran to Kermān, Sīstān, and Khurāsān

The conquest of the Iranian interior and east proceeded through a series of regional campaigns, many of them conducted in the 640s and early 650s under ʿUthmān's governors at Kūfa and Baṣra. Azerbaijan, in the northwest, was brought into tribute through an agreement with its local ruler, Isfandiyādh, according to tradition, after campaigns from Kūfa. Further east, forces advanced to Qazvin, Zanjān, and the Caspian approaches, but the mountainous region of Tabaristan, Gīlān, and Daylam resisted, and its rulers, including the Dābūyids, retained local authority well beyond this period. Isfahan and the central plateau came under Muslim control through agreement with local governors. Kermān, to the southeast, and Sīstān, a region of oases and the Helmand river system, were attacked in the early 650s: Sīstān's ruler made terms, though the region later rebelled, and the city of Zaranj became a Muslim base.

Khurāsān, the northeastern region that had been the Sasanian frontier against Central Asia and the home of Rostam's family, was the last major province to fall. The tradition says that ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir, the governor of Baṣra, launched an expedition into Khurāsān around 651, taking Nishapur and Ṭūs, and that his lieutenant al-Aḥnaf ibn Qays took Herat, Marv al-Rūdh, and Balkh in the following years. In many cases, the Muslim commanders made agreements with local marzbāns (frontier governors) or dihqāns, who agreed to pay tribute and to host or supply garrisons, retaining their local positions. The Muslim presence in Khurāsān was at first modest, and the region revolted when the caliph ʿUthmān was killed in 656 (Chapter 40), requiring reconquest under later governors (Chapters 41 and 43).

The east shows most clearly that conquest and control were not the same. In the Iranian interior, Muslims governed through local elites in an arrangement more like suzerainty than direct administration: tribute was paid, garrisons were small, and the old structures of landed authority persisted. When the Muslim polity was weakened by civil war, the arrangement frayed. Historians such as Michael Morony have emphasized recurring resistance, changing tribute arrangements, and indirect control through local elites, and the incorporation of many eastern districts into the caliphate was a gradual process that continued for generations (Chapters 42 and 49).

Section 39.25

Yazdegerd III's Flight and Death: The End of Sasanian Imperial Sovereignty

Yazdegerd's last years are reconstructed from late and sometimes conflicting reports. He is said to have moved from Isfahan to Fārs, Kermān, and Sīstān, always one step ahead of the Muslim advance, and to have sought the support of regional rulers who in many cases refused him shelter. Eventually he reached Khurāsān and Marv, the main city of the northeast and home of the marzbān. In about 651 (some sources give 652), he was killed there. The most common version in the Arabic and Persian tradition says that he was murdered by a miller, to whose mill he had fled, though other accounts hold the local governor or other nobles responsible, and the circumstances are uncertain. The tradition says that his body was buried by the Christian community of Marv, an indication of the presence of the Church of the East in the city.

His death ended the Sasanian dynasty as a ruling house. His son Pērōz is recorded in Chinese sources as having been received by the Tang court and as holding a titular Persian command in exile; another descendant is said to have continued in Tang service. These exiles kept alive the memory of the dynasty but could not restore it. In Iran itself, a generation of Persian nobles would adopt new patterns of cooperation with the Muslim authorities.

The end of the dynasty was not the end of Persian society. Zoroastrian priests continued to function, fire temples continued to operate in many regions, and the old nobility, including dihqāns and local princes, retained land and local authority. Middle Persian (Pahlavi) continued as the language of administration in Iran for decades, and Sasanian coinage and fiscal practice continued under the new governors. In this sense, the chapter can speak of the destruction of Sasanian imperial sovereignty without suggesting that Persian civilization had come to an end, a theme that returns in the Persianate revival of Chapter 49 and in the long-term changes discussed in Chapter 42. What ended in 651 was a monarchy; what began was a long process of incorporation, adaptation, and renewal.

Division VI · Sections 39.26–39.31

Conquest, Settlement, Taxation, and Local Societies

How an army became a government: agreements, garrison cities, revenue, administration, religious communities, and daily life.

Section 39.26

Surrender Agreements, Treaties, and the Terms of Conquest

Many of the conquered regions came under Muslim rule through negotiated agreements, and the Arabic sources preserve texts or summaries of dozens of them. Later jurists classified conquest into two broad types. Land and cities that surrendered by agreement, called ṣulḥ, were held to retain the terms of their treaty, while land taken by force, ʿanwa, was subject to the community's disposal. Guarantees of safety, called amān, might accompany either. In practice the situation was less tidy. A town might first resist, then negotiate; commanders on different sides of a city might make different arrangements; and an agreement might be revised as circumstances changed. The documents that survive typically specify a payment, either a lump sum or an annual tax on persons or land; a guarantee of life, property, and, usually, places of worship; and an obligation on the inhabitants to provide support for the army, sometimes including food, guidance, and hospitality to Muslim travelers.

Specific examples from the tradition illustrate the range. Al-Ḥīra is said to have agreed to an annual payment in return for protection; Jerusalem to have received a covenant guaranteeing its churches; Damascus to have been given terms protecting its inhabitants and buildings; the cities of the Jazīra, including Edessa, to have agreed to payments; Azerbaijan to have paid a fixed annual sum; and Armenia, whose nobleman Theodore Rshtuni made an agreement with the Muslims in the early 650s that is described in Sebeos's history, to have retained its autonomy under tribute. Nubia's baqt was a different kind of treaty, between sovereign powers, regulating trade and exchange. Each arrangement reflected the local balance of power and the interests of the people who negotiated it.

Historians must be cautious about these texts. Most are preserved in works written in the ninth century or later, by authors with legal interests, and the wording may have been adapted to later categories. The famous "Pact of ʿUmar," a list of restrictions on non-Muslims, is not a document of the conquest period at all but a later legal composition (Chapter 47). Even for authentic agreements, we cannot be sure the transmitted wording matches the original. The safest conclusion is that surrender agreements were common, that they typically exchanged tribute for protection and the continuation of local religious life, and that their specific wording must be treated with care.

Section 39.27

Military Settlements and the Formation of the Amṣār

One of the most consequential decisions of the conquest period was where to put the army. In Iraq and Egypt, the Muslims built new garrison cities, called amṣār (singular miṣr), instead of occupying the existing capitals. Baṣra was founded around 635–638 at the head of the Gulf, near the older Persian outpost of al-Khuribah, and became the base for campaigns in Khūzestān, Fārs, and Khurāsān. Kūfa was founded around 638 on the Euphrates near al-Ḥīra, on the edge of the desert, and became the base for campaigns in the Jibāl and the north. Fustat was founded around 641–642 beside Babylon Fortress in Egypt (Section 39.20). The Arabic tradition attributes the choice of sites to ʿUmar, who reportedly wanted the troops to live in places reachable by camel from Arabia, with healthful air and good pasture, and not to settle among the existing populations or take up farming. The reasons, as reported, mix practical and political considerations: keeping the army cohesive and mobile, preventing assimilation, and maintaining the caliph's control.

The camps were initially simple: tents or reed huts, with a central mosque and the governor's residence. A fire in Kūfa is said to have prompted the use of brick, and the cities were laid out in quarters (khiṭaṭ) assigned to tribal groups. Kūfa's population was grouped into larger tribal divisions, and a distinctive local politics emerged, shaped by rivalries among tribal factions and between early settlers and later arrivals. Persian soldiers who had gone over to the Muslims, sometimes called Aḥmarī or Asāwira in the sources, settled in these cities, as did captives and, in time, converts and their descendants, who were attached to Arab tribes as clients (mawālī), a subject developed in Chapter 42. The garrisons depended on the surrounding Sawād for food and revenue, and the relationship between soldiers and cultivators was one of the central features of provincial life.

Syria followed a different pattern. There, Arab forces settled in and around existing cities, including Damascus, Ḥimṣ, and Jerusalem, and in military camps such as Jābiya. Over time, Syria was divided into military districts, called ajnād (singular jund), centered on Damascus, Ḥimṣ, al-Urdunn (Jordan), and Filasṭīn (Palestine), to which Qinnasrīn was later added; the soldiers of each district were paid from its revenues. Because the cities of Syria were already centers of administration and population, the conquerors did not create entirely new capitals there. This difference in settlement is one reason that later politics differed so strongly between Syria, where the army lived within a settled society, and Iraq, where the garrison cities developed as separate, volatile centers of tribal politics.

Historical Vocabulary
Jund, Miṣr, and Dīwān

A jund (plural ajnād) was a military district in which Arab troops were settled and paid from local revenue; the term was used especially for Syria. A miṣr (plural amṣār) was a garrison city founded for the army, as at Baṣra, Kūfa, and Fustat. A dīwān was a register of soldiers entitled to regular payments (ʿaṭāʾ), traditionally ascribed to ʿUmar around 640–641; the same word later came to mean a government bureau. Traditions about the dīwān record that payments were ranked according to precedence in Islam and military service, which tied the soldiers' income to the community's early history. The detail of these arrangements, as reported, may reflect later developments projected back onto ʿUmar.

Section 39.28

Land, Taxation, Tribute, and the Financing of Conquest

The conquests were financed from several sources. Plunder from battles and captured cities was divided, with a fifth, the khums, set aside for the community's central treasury. Lump-sum tribute was paid by cities and regions that capitulated. Regular taxes were collected from the conquered populations, usually through existing systems, and the proceeds were used to pay stipends to the soldiers and to support the governors. In the Arabic sources, the revenue from non-Muslim subjects is often described using two terms, kharāj and jizya, which in later law came to mean a land tax and a poll tax on non-Muslim men respectively. In the seventh century the words were less precise and could refer to tribute or taxation in general, and the documents, in Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian, use older terms from the Byzantine and Sasanian systems.

The Byzantine and Sasanian tax systems, in fact, continued. In Egypt, the Byzantine administration collected taxes on land and on persons, and the papyri show that tax assessments, receipts, and requisitions continued in forms familiar from the earlier period. The tradition says ʿAmr's treaty fixed a payment of two dinars per adult man, with provisions for the army, but this is recorded only in later sources (Section 39.19). In Iraq the Sasanian land survey and the tax records continued to be used, with local dihqāns collecting the revenue. The Arabic tradition of ʿUmar's decision not to divide the Sawād but to leave it with its cultivators, who paid a tax, is the best-known statement of the principle that conquered land would provide a continuing income for the community, not an immediate distribution to the soldiers. The decision's details, and whether it applied equally in all regions, are uncertain.

The conquerors thus lived on the taxes of the conquered. Their stipends, recorded in the dīwān, were paid in coin or in kind. In Egypt, Syria, and Iraq, this meant that the new regime depended on the collaboration of local tax collectors and on the continued productivity of the land. Historians caution against assuming that the new taxes were uniformly lighter or heavier than the old; Christian chroniclers voice both complaints and relief, and the evidence is uneven. What can be said is that the early conquest state was a tax-collecting state of the usual kind, drawing on inherited systems and local intermediaries. Its distinctiveness lay in the fact that the group receiving the revenue, the Arab-Muslim soldiery, was a separate, privileged community, and that its stipends were tied to religious status and early service, features that would shape later conflicts over who was entitled to the empire's wealth (Chapter 40) and the development of a more formal fiscal system (Chapter 41).

Historical Vocabulary
Kharāj, Jizya, Ṣulḥ, and the Danger of Anachronism

Kharāj and jizya are often defined as the land tax and the poll tax paid by non-Muslims, but this distinction was formalized by jurists in the eighth and ninth centuries (Chapter 47). In the 640s both words could mean tribute or tax in a broad sense, and the documents often use Greek or Persian equivalents. Ṣulḥ means a peace settlement or agreement; ʿanwa means taking by force. Jurists later used the distinction to decide what rights the inhabitants and the community had in land. When students meet these terms in a seventh-century context, they should ask whether the source uses them in a later technical sense.

Section 39.29

Continuity in Provincial Administration: Officials, Languages, and Institutions

Everywhere the conquerors went, they relied on people who already knew how to govern. The Muslim governor, called the amīr, commanded the troops, and a small group of Arab officials handled religious affairs and the distribution of payments, but the day-to-day administration, the survey of land, the collection of taxes, the maintenance of canals and roads, and the keeping of records, remained in the hands of local officials. In Egypt, Greek-speaking officials and Coptic-speaking village headmen continued to function, and the districts and their administrators, such as the pagarchs who supervised tax collection, remained in place. Greek continued as the language of the Egyptian and Syrian bureaucracies for decades, until reforms around the end of the seventh century introduced Arabic as an administrative language (Chapter 41). In Iraq and Iran, Middle Persian remained the language of administration, and Persian scribes and dihqāns continued to manage the estates and the tax records.

Coins offer a clear example. In Syria, the Muslims continued to use Byzantine gold coinage and issued bronze coins of Byzantine design, sometimes with Arabic additions. In Iran and Iraq, they struck silver drachms in the Sasanian style, adding Arabic phrases such as the invocation "in the name of God" and the governor's name to the Persian text. This continuity was practical, because the populations were accustomed to these coins, and political, because the conquerors did not yet claim a distinct monetary identity. The change to a purely Islamic coinage came later, under ʿAbd al-Malik (Chapter 41).

Some Christian officials rose to prominence under the new rule. The Christian administrative family of John of Damascus illustrates this continuity across generations: his grandfather Manṣūr ibn Sarjūn had served the Byzantine authorities in Damascus and is reported to have remained influential after the conquest; his son Sarjūn ibn Manṣūr subsequently served the Umayyad administration under Muʿāwiya and was the theologian John of Damascus’s father. The case is typical of a wider pattern in which local elites, bishops, landowners, and secretaries kept their positions in the new order. At the same time, the conquerors introduced new roles: the qāḍī (judge) for the Muslim community, the garrison commander, and the supervisor of the stipend register. The result was a hybrid administration in which an Arab-Muslim leadership oversaw a provincial machinery that it had inherited largely intact. This early arrangement, a form of indirect rule, was later modified as the Umayyads centralized and Arabized the government.

Section 39.30

Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Other Religious Communities under New Rule

The religious map of the conquered territories was crowded. In Syria and Palestine, Chalcedonian (Melkite) Christians, Miaphysites (often called Jacobites), and Maronites lived alongside Jews and Samaritans. In Egypt, the Coptic Miaphysite Church had the majority, with a Chalcedonian minority and Jewish communities. In Iraq and Iran, the Church of the East, Miaphysites, Jews, Zoroastrians, Manichaeans, Mandaeans, and others coexisted. The initial Muslim authorities were more interested in tribute and obedience than in religious conformity, and the early arrangements typically allowed communities to keep their worship and, within limits, their institutions. The Muslim community itself had not yet developed the elaborate law of non-Muslim status that later jurists would call the dhimma.

Experiences varied by place and by community. For Miaphysite Christians in Syria and Egypt, the end of Byzantine rule meant the end of imperial pressure to conform to Chalcedon, and in Egypt the return of a Coptic patriarch. For Chalcedonian Christians, the loss of the imperial state meant the loss of a protector. Jews, who had suffered under Byzantine policies and Sasanian-Byzantine wars, had access to Jerusalem under Muslim rule, according to later sources, and their communities in Babylonia continued their legal and scholarly work without major interruption. Zoroastrians, for whom the monarchy had been the guarantor of the priesthood and the fire temples, lost the support of a royal patron, but the religion continued to be practiced openly for centuries in Fārs and elsewhere. A tradition, whose early date is uncertain, treated Zoroastrians (called Majūs) as comparable to "People of the Book" for purposes of tribute.

A rare contemporary Christian voice is that of Ishoʿyahb III, the catholicos of the Church of the East (about 649–659), who in a letter described the Arabs, as the ruling power, as not hostile to the Christian religion and as respecting its clergy and churches, while lamenting that some of his own community had abandoned their faith. Other Christian writers read the conquests as divine punishment for sin or heresy, and apocalyptic works circulated, such as the later Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius. Jewish sources, similarly, show a mixture of hope and anxiety. These sources should not be read as showing that any group welcomed conquest; they show that communities adjusted to a political change they had not chosen. The more elaborate legal and social restrictions of later centuries, discussed in Chapters 42 and 47, are not a good guide to the practical arrangements of the 640s.

Section 39.31

Everyday Life under Conquest: Agriculture, Families, Labor, Slavery, and Displacement

For most people in the conquered territories, the conquests were experienced less as grand battles than as marching armies, requisitions, changed tax collectors, and the uncertainty of who held local power. Farmers continued to plant, irrigate, and harvest. The Nile flood, the canals of the Sawād, and the qanats of Iran (Chapter 2) kept their rhythms, and the markets and trade that depended on them continued. Villagers supplied grain, animals, fodder, and labor, and a papyrus like the 643 receipt shows how such burdens were recorded. Fighting caused destruction in places: Palestine and Syria had been ravaged by the Persian occupation and the Byzantine reconquest before the Muslims arrived, and the campaigns of the 630s compounded the damage. Yet archaeology shows that many cities and villages continued to be occupied and even to build or decorate churches in the following decades, which warns against assuming general devastation.

War also brought captivity and displacement. Arabic sources report that captives, women and children as well as men, were taken in campaigns and distributed or sold, and that some were settled in the garrison cities. Slavery was already a widespread institution in the Byzantine and Sasanian worlds, and the conquests expanded it, though the scale is impossible to measure from the sources. Some elite families fled: Byzantine landowners and officials left Syria and Egypt for Anatolia or Constantinople, and Persian nobles moved east or into exile. Plague and famine accompanied the war, and the epidemic of about 638–639 and the Arabian famine are well attested. Sophronius's sermon of 634, with its lament that the Saracens prevented pilgrims from reaching Bethlehem, preserves the voice of a Christian leader who experienced the early campaigns as a time of danger.

The experiences of women and families are poorly documented. Arab soldiers were often accompanied by wives and families, and in the garrison cities women formed part of the settlement. Later Arabic romances celebrate women warriors, but these accounts are unreliable. For the conquered populations, women's lives were shaped by the same economic and legal systems as before, with changes occurring gradually through marriage with the conquerors, concubinage, and the enslavement of captives. The picture that emerges is of societies undergoing disruption and adaptation, in which the continuities of agricultural labor and family life were as important as the political changes.

Everyday Life and Social History

What Conquest Meant to a Village

A village in the Egyptian Delta, a Syrian hill village, a Sawād estate, and an oasis on the Iranian plateau experienced the conquests differently, but several features recur. An army's arrival meant that the village had to supply food and animals, house or guide soldiers, and negotiate with an unfamiliar authority through whichever local leader, headman, priest, or landowner could speak for it. The surviving papyri show that such obligations became regular and were recorded in the same bilingual or inherited bureaucratic forms as before. The village's tax collector might be the same man who had served the earlier regime, or his successor, and the village might see little immediate change in its religion, language, or social order.

Because most surviving sources were written by churchmen, officials, and later Arabic historians, ordinary people appear mainly as the objects of requisition, captivity, or taxation. Historians reconstruct their experience from papyri, archaeology, and the comparative study of rural life. The conclusion that daily life continued in many places with relatively little immediate change should not obscure the suffering of those who were killed, enslaved, or displaced, nor the long-term transformations that would eventually change the religion and language of much of the region.

Section 39.32

Explaining the Rapid Conquests: Military Organization, Strategy, and Leadership

Several explanations focus on the invaders themselves. The Muslim armies were mobile, drawing on experienced tribal fighters, many of whom had campaigned in the Ridda Wars, and they could move across deserts that slowed their enemies. They did not depend on a single base but could shift their forces among the Syrian, Iraqi, and Egyptian theaters, and the caliph in Medina could in principle coordinate them. Their opponents, by contrast, were slower to concentrate, and when they did, as at Yarmūk and al-Qādisiyya, they risked everything on a pitched battle. The invaders were adaptable, learning siege techniques, using local guides and allies, and combining negotiation with force. Offers of terms that preserved life, property, and worship, in return for tribute, were attractive to cities that faced prolonged siege, and they limited the cost of conquest for the conquerors.

Motivation mattered. Participation in the campaigns was rewarded with plunder and, after the dīwān, with regular stipends, and the prospect of wealth drew recruits from across Arabia. Religious conviction, the belief that the community was fighting for God and would be rewarded in this world and the next, gave the armies cohesion and a sense of purpose. Historians disagree about how to weight the religious and material motives, and the tradition itself mixes them. The leadership of the early caliphs, who organized recruitment, sent reinforcements, and selected commanders, was a significant factor, as was the quality of some commanders, such as Khālid, ʿAmr, and Saʿd. Here, again, caution is needed. The Arabic tradition is inclined to credit conquests to the genius of individual commanders and to the personal decisions of ʿUmar, and this tendency conceals the broader conditions, including the work of thousands of unnamed soldiers, local allies, and negotiators.

The sheer speed of the conquests should not conceal their limits. The Muslims suffered defeats, as at the Battle of the Bridge. They took years to capture cities such as Caesarea and Alexandria, and parts of Iran required a decade or more. The wars were hard and costly. What distinguished them was the combination of effective mobilization and the weakness of the opposing states, which is the subject of the next section.

Historical Debate

Why Were the Conquests So Rapid?

The question: Why did armies from Arabia, which had no tradition of empire, defeat two long-established states within about a generation, and no single factor seems to be sufficient?

Interpretation A — Imperial exhaustion and weakness: Scholars such as Walter Kaegi have emphasized that Byzantium and Sasanian Iran were drained by their war, afflicted by plague and fiscal strain, and politically divided, so that even modest forces could overcome them. On this view the conquerors exploited a power vacuum and their success reveals as much about the empires as about the invaders.
Interpretation B — Religious and ideological cohesion: Others, notably Fred Donner, stress that the invaders were united by a common religious movement that gave them unusual cohesion and purpose, and that this movement, more than the weakness of their enemies alone, explains the organization and persistence of the campaigns. On this view the early community's beliefs and institutions mattered as causes, not as later decorations.
Interpretation C — Material incentives, local alliances, and negotiated rule: A third emphasis falls on the rewards of plunder and stipends, on the Arab tribal networks of the frontier, and on the readiness of provincial elites to negotiate. On this view the conquests succeeded because they offered many groups something: soldiers got pay, local leaders kept their positions, and cities avoided destruction.

Evidence and assessment: All three interpretations draw on the same limited and partly unreliable evidence, and they are not mutually exclusive. Most current work combines them. Environmental stress, including plague and climate fluctuation, has also been proposed as an additional factor, though its role is still debated and difficult to demonstrate.

Why it matters: The debate shows that explaining a historical event is not a matter of selecting a single cause. It also shows why the chapter keeps military, political, religious, and material explanations together and treats local responses as a cause, not just a consequence.

Section 39.33

Imperial Weakness, Local Responses, and the Limits of Single-Cause Explanations

The condition of the empires has already been described. Byzantium had a treasury drained by war, an army exhausted, provinces that had been occupied and recovered, a religious establishment that had alienated many of its subjects, and, after 641, a succession struggle. The Sasanian monarchy had a series of short-lived rulers, a nobility divided by civil war, a tax base disrupted by plague and flooding, and a young king without a secure base. Neither state was prepared to face a new, mobile enemy that came from a direction it had long regarded as peripheral. The Arab client kingdoms that had guarded the desert frontiers had been weakened or abolished, and some of the former allies joined the invaders.

Local responses varied widely. In places, communities resisted: cities such as Caesarea, Alexandria, Shūshtar, and Iṣṭakhr fought, sometimes for years. In others, local leaders negotiated promptly. Some groups probably saw an advantage in the change, for example Miaphysite Christians who had suffered under Chalcedonian rule, Arab Christians with kinship ties to the invaders, and landowners who gained from the arrangement. Others lost status or livelihood. The older view that conquered peoples uniformly welcomed the Muslims as liberators from oppressive empires does not survive close examination: it rests partly on later polemic and on selective readings of sources such as John of Nikiu, who is hostile to Byzantine officials but also describes Muslim violence. Reports that Jews aided the invaders appear in some Christian sources, but they are often polemical and cannot be generalized. The accurate picture is of diverse, mostly pragmatic responses, determined by local circumstances, with acceptance often following defeat in the field, not preceding it.

A multi-causal account, then, has to include the invaders' organization and motivation, the empires' exhaustion and division, the availability of negotiated surrender, and the role of local intermediaries who kept the provincial machinery running. Environmental and epidemic factors, such as the plague that struck in the 620s and the later ʿAmwās outbreak, are plausible contributors, but the evidence does not permit confident claims about their weight. What the evidence does support is that the conquests were not inevitable: the Muslim armies could have been checked, as at the Battle of the Bridge, had the circumstances been different, and the speed with which they were not is itself the central problem of the period.

Section 39.34

The End of the Byzantine-Sasanian Regional Order

For about four centuries before the conquests, Southwest Asia had been dominated by two great states, Rome and its Byzantine successor on one side and Parthia and then Sasanian Iran on the other. They fought over Armenia and Mesopotamia, relied on client kingdoms, and competed for the trade routes that linked the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean (Chapters 30, 32, and 34). The conquests ended this order, but in different ways. Byzantium lost Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and eventually North Africa, but it survived, and its response was to reorganize its remaining territories, especially in Anatolia, around a system of regional military commands, usually called themes, a process whose timing and character are debated. It would remain a major power for centuries and the principal opponent of the caliphate on the northern frontier, a rivalry that included naval warfare in the Mediterranean from the 640s and 650s.

The Sasanian Empire did not survive. Its imperial monarchy and central political sovereignty ended, while much of its provincial administration and the authority of local elites persisted under changing rulers; control of parts of Iran remained uneven for decades. Persian society, however, did not disappear: its aristocracy, priesthood, language, administrative traditions, and cultural memory continued, and they would reshape the Muslim state in the following centuries (Chapters 44 and 49). The contrast between Byzantine contraction and Sasanian collapse is one of the fundamental distinctions of the period.

The result was a new political geography. After centuries of division between Roman and Iranian imperial powers, Syria, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and much of Iran again came under a single overarching authority, recalling earlier periods of imperial unification rather than constituting a historical first (Chapters 2 and 22). The Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and overland routes became increasingly connected within the new caliphal political system, although authority remained uneven at its edges. Medina, a small Arabian oasis town, was the seat of this vast territory, a situation that would not last. The frontier with Byzantium shifted north to the Taurus, and a new frontier opened in the east, in Central Asia. The region's religious map also began a slow change, although, as emphasized throughout this chapter, conversion was a later and gradual process.

Section 39.35

The Conquest Empire by the Mid-Seventh Century: Achievements, Contradictions, and the Road to Civil War

By about 650, the Muslim polity ruled a territory stretching from Libya and Egypt through Syria, Mesopotamia, and Iran to the borders of Central Asia, and from the Taurus to Nubia and the Arabian Sea. It had an army paid from a central register, a revenue system based largely on inherited arrangements, garrison cities in Iraq and Egypt, military districts in Syria, and governors responsible to the caliph in Medina. It had achieved this with remarkable speed and with limited administrative apparatus. The achievement was real: one of the most extensive political unions of these regions since antiquity, though with a structure and ideology very different from those of earlier empires (Chapter 22).

The new empire also had deep contradictions. The soldiers settled in the garrison cities were paid according to their precedence in Islam, which gave early Muslims, especially Quraysh, a privileged position, while later arrivals and the Arab tribesmen who had joined after the Ridda wars resented their lower rank. Wealth flowed to Medina and the governors, while the settlers in Kūfa, Baṣra, and Fustat competed for resources. Governors enjoyed wide autonomy at a great distance from the caliph, and the provinces differed in their political cultures. Muʿāwiya in Syria commanded a loyal army settled in the midst of an existing society, while the tribal garrisons of Iraq were volatile. ʿUmar, who had presided over much of this expansion, was assassinated in November 644 by a Persian slave, and the caliphate passed to ʿUthmān, whose policies and appointments would generate substantial opposition.

The question of authority was unresolved. Who was entitled to rule, how the empire's wealth should be distributed, how much autonomy provincial governors should have, and how the early companions of the Prophet and their descendants should be treated were issues that the conquest era had raised but not settled. These questions, together with the grievances of provincial garrisons, led to the crisis that culminated in ʿUthmān's assassination in 656 and the first major civil war among Muslims. That story is told in Chapter 40. The mature administration of the Umayyad state, which emerged from that conflict, is the subject of Chapter 41, and the long-term transformation of the conquered societies, through conversion, Arabization, and the changing status of non-Arab Muslims, is the subject of Chapter 42.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how armies organized under the first caliphs, within roughly eighteen years of the Prophet's death, brought Byzantine Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, Sasanian Iraq, and then Iran under Muslim authority. It began with the sources, which are mostly Arabic narratives compiled two or three centuries later, supplemented by fragmentary Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Coptic texts, papyri, coins, and archaeology. These sources agree on the broad sequence but diverge on dates, numbers, and details, and the chapter has therefore marked traditional dates and treated battle speeches and troop figures as literary conventions.

The conquests grew out of the Ridda Wars, but they did not follow a single, fully formed plan. Early campaigns were raids and probes in which tribal initiative, opportunity, and the caliph's direction combined. Byzantium and Iran had been exhausted by the war of 602–628, and each faced its own crisis: Byzantium had a damaged treasury, religious divisions, and a succession struggle after Heraclius's death in 641, while the Sasanian monarchy suffered civil war, plague, and a fragile kingship. The two states were vulnerable in different ways, a difference that shaped the outcome.

In Syria and Palestine, victories at Ajnādayn and Fiḥl opened the south, and the Battle of Yarmūk in 636 destroyed the principal Byzantine army. Jerusalem, Damascus, and other cities passed to Muslim rule through negotiation or siege, while coastal cities such as Caesarea held out for years. Northward, the conquest reached the Jazīra and the Taurus frontier, where a long border zone between Muslim and Byzantine power took shape. A plague in about 638–639 and the death of senior commanders brought Muʿāwiya to the governorship of Syria.

In Iraq, the Sasanian state defeated the Muslims at the Battle of the Bridge in 634, but al-Qādisiyya, whose date is disputed, destroyed the Sasanian army, and Ctesiphon fell around 637. The monarchy lost its richest province and treasury, but not its existence. Muslim forces consolidated control of the Sawād, and the revenue of Iraq became the financial base for the army. ʿUmar's reported decision to leave the Sawād with its cultivators set a pattern for the taxation of conquered land.

In Egypt, a small force under ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ invaded in 639–640, defeated the Byzantines at Heliopolis, took Babylon Fortress after a lengthy siege, and negotiated the evacuation of Alexandria in 641–642. A Byzantine attempt to retake Alexandria in the mid-640s failed. Documentary papyri, such as the bilingual receipt of 643, show that Egyptian administration continued in Greek and Coptic under new authorities and that requisitions and taxes were quickly regularized.

Iran was conquered more slowly and unevenly. The victory at Nihāvand in 642 opened the plateau, but Fārs resisted into the late 640s, and Khurāsān was reached in the early 650s. Local magnates, marzbāns, and dihqāns often negotiated tribute arrangements that preserved their local power, and revolts followed when the Muslim polity was weakened by civil war. Yazdegerd III died near Marv around 651, ending the dynasty, but Persian administration, religion, and landed society continued.

The conquerors built their government on existing foundations. Surrender agreements typically exchanged tribute for security and the continuation of religious life, though their surviving texts must be treated with caution. New garrison cities, Baṣra, Kūfa, and Fustat, housed the soldiers, while in Syria they settled in and near existing cities and military districts. Taxation drew on inherited Byzantine and Sasanian systems, with later legal categories such as kharāj and jizya not yet fixed. Provincial administration remained largely in the hands of local officials who used Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian.

Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and other communities experienced the conquests differently. Copts saw the end of imperial pressure to conform to Chalcedon, Chalcedonians lost their imperial patron, Jews gained access to Jerusalem and retained their Babylonian academies, and Zoroastrians lost their royal protector. Ordinary people, including villagers, women, and the enslaved, bore the burdens of requisition, displacement, captivity, and plague, while the routines of agricultural life continued. The old claim that conquered populations uniformly welcomed the Muslims does not withstand scrutiny.

The conquests ended the Byzantine-Sasanian regional order. Byzantium survived in contracted form, while the Sasanian state was destroyed and its territory absorbed. For the first time, Syria, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Iran were united under one authority, with Medina as its distant center. The empire's success was explained by a combination of military mobility, religious and material motivation, imperial exhaustion, and local negotiation, and the chapter has shown why no single cause suffices. The new empire's contradictions, over the distribution of wealth, the autonomy of governors, and the question of legitimate authority, led to the civil war that Chapter 40 describes.

Chapter Reflection

The conquests of the 630s through 650s changed the political map of the Middle East more completely than any event since the Achaemenid conquests of the sixth century BCE. The old rivalry between Rome and Iran, which had structured the region's politics for centuries, ended. Syria, Egypt, Iraq, and Iran came under one authority, and the conditions for a new civilization, with its own religious, legal, and cultural institutions, were created. That civilization, as the next chapters show, would be built not on the ruins of the old order but on its continuing institutions.

The chapter has also shown how historians reason about a problem on which evidence is thin and traditions are contested. The habits of mind it asks for are to distinguish contemporary from later sources, to treat dates and numbers cautiously, to separate conquest from incorporation and from conversion, and to resist single-cause explanations. These habits apply to every later chapter, especially when memory and politics shape accounts of the past.

The new order carried the seeds of its own crisis. The questions of authority, wealth, and provincial autonomy raised by the conquests produced the civil war of Chapter 40, the Umayyad reorganization of Chapter 41, and the long social transformations of Chapter 42. The garrison cities, the stipend register, and the arrangements with provincial elites remained structuring features of the early caliphate.

Finally, the conquests link the Middle East's ancient and medieval histories. The Persian and Byzantine administrative traditions, the Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities, and the Arabian movement that brought them together all contributed to the world of the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates, to the Persianate revival of Chapter 49, and to the later history of the entire region. This chapter's account of the first generation of that world is the foundation for what follows.
