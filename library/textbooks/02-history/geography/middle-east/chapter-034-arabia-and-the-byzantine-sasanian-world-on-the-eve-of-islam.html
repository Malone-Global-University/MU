Arabia and the Byzantine-Sasanian World on the Eve of Islam

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the Himyarite year 658, which corresponds to 548 CE, a ruler named Abraha ordered an inscription carved at Ma’rib, in the highlands of Yemen, to commemorate the repair of the great dam that had watered the oasis for more than a thousand years. The text opens with an invocation of the Merciful, his Messiah, and the Holy Spirit. It then records, among the events of the celebration, the arrival of envoys from the king of Aksum across the Red Sea, from the Roman emperor, from the Persian king, and from the Arab rulers allied to each empire along the northern frontier. Few single documents show so economically what this chapter argues: that sixth-century Arabia was neither a remote and isolated peninsula nor a territory simply owned by the great powers, but a zone through which the political, commercial, and religious systems of late antiquity ran.

Chapter 33 traced the Christianization of the Roman East, Armenia, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Iran, and it deliberately left Arabia at its margin. This chapter picks up the Arabian threads. It asks how Arabia was connected to the political, commercial, and religious systems of late antiquity, and how competition among Byzantium, Sasanian Iran, the Christian kingdom of Aksum, the kingdom of Himyar in Yemen, and Arab frontier powers reshaped the peninsula and its borderlands in the century before the emergence of Islam. It is the capstone of Volume II. Having followed the region from the first Mesopotamian cities through Persian, Hellenistic, Parthian, Roman, and Sasanian empires, the volume ends by showing the late antique order at its most developed, and at the moment of its most violent disruption.

The chapter has two main movements. The first, Sections 34.1 through 34.13, looks at Arabia as part of a regional system. It follows the Red Sea, Gulf, and desert corridors; the late kingdom of Himyar and its Jewish and Christian communities; the persecution at Najran in the 520s; the Aksumite invasion and the rule of Abraha; the Sasanian intervention in Yemen; and the Arab federate dynasties of the Ghassanids and Lakhmids who mediated imperial power on the Syrian and Mesopotamian frontiers. The second movement, Sections 34.14 through 34.19, narrates the Byzantine-Sasanian war of 602–628, the longest and most destructive conflict between the two empires, which carried Persian armies to Jerusalem, Alexandria, and the Bosporus and then ended in the collapse of Khosrow II’s regime.

Two cautions govern the whole chapter. The first concerns the relationship between this chapter and the one that follows. Chapter 35 opens Volume III and turns inward to Arabia before Islam: pastoral and settled society, Mecca and Yathrib, tribal relations, poetry, internal Arabian religion, and the evidentiary problems of reconstructing pre-Islamic Arabia. This chapter therefore looks at Arabia from the standpoint of the wider late antique world, and it treats Mecca and the Hijaz only where they enter that larger story. The second caution concerns hindsight. It is tempting to read the events of the early seventh century as a prelude to the Arab-Islamic conquests, to see the empires as “exhausted” and Arabia as waiting. This chapter resists that reading. The outcome of the war of 602–628 was not obvious in 614, and the world of 630 did not point inevitably toward 634. Historians still debate how much the war weakened the two empires, a question taken up in the Historical Debate in Section 34.19.

The chapter also asks students to think carefully about evidence. For pre-Islamic Yemen, inscriptions in the South Arabian script carved in stone are among the most valuable sources, because they are contemporary, often precisely dated, and written by the people involved. Greek, Syriac, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Persian-language sources offer outside views, many composed by participants in the religious and political conflicts they describe. Later Arabic histories, written from the eighth century onward, preserve rich memories of South Arabian kings, Ghassanid and Lakhmid courts, and the great war, but they were composed in a very different world and with different purposes. A central skill of this chapter is learning to keep these classes of evidence distinct instead of blending them into a single smooth narrative.

The chapter’s central argument, then, is that Arabia on the eve of Islam was a connected space. Merchants, soldiers, bishops, rabbis, envoys, and kings moved between Ma’rib and Adulis, between al-Hira and Ctesiphon, between Bostra and Constantinople. Arab rulers were not passive buffer states, and Arab communities were not uniformly pagan, nomadic, or pastoral. At the same time, the older view that Arabia was a mere appendage of the empires does not survive the evidence either: Himyarite kings, Abraha, the Ghassanid phylarchs, and the Lakhmid monarchs pursued their own ambitions and exploited imperial rivalry to do so. The great war of 602–628 then shattered the equilibrium that had held, with some interruptions, since the sixth century, and left a region in which both empires survived but neither was secure.

Historical Context

By the early sixth century the Middle Eastern world was dominated by two empires that had faced one another across Mesopotamia, Armenia, and the Syrian desert for centuries. The Roman Empire, governed from Constantinople and usually called Byzantine by modern historians, held Anatolia, Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and the Balkans (Chapter 31, Chapter 33). The Sasanian Empire, founded in the 220s CE, ruled Iran, Mesopotamia, and the Caucasian marches from its capital at Ctesiphon (Chapter 32). Their rivalry was not constant warfare. Long stretches of peace, diplomatic exchange, shared concern about steppe peoples to the north, and a recognized if tense parity of status characterized much of the period. Chapter 32 explained how the Sasanians combined a Zoroastrian church, an aristocracy of great families, and an increasingly centralized fiscal and military system, and how by the sixth century Khosrow I had reorganized the tax system and the army.

Chapter 33 showed that Christianity had become the established religion of the Roman Empire and Armenia and had also acquired deep roots in Sasanian Mesopotamia, where the Church of the East developed as a distinct institution. Doctrinal divisions after the Councils of Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) split Christians into communities that would later be labeled Chalcedonian, Miaphysite, and Dyophysite, and those divisions shaped imperial politics in Syria and Egypt. Chapter 33 also noted, without developing, the presence of Arabic-speaking Christians in the desert and of Jewish communities across the region. This chapter develops that Arabian dimension.

The history of Arabia before the sixth century is largely the subject of Chapter 35, but a few essential background points are needed here. Southwestern Arabia had been home since the first millennium BCE to literate, urbanized kingdoms, among them Saba, Qataban, Hadramawt, and Himyar, whose wealth rested on irrigated agriculture in the highlands and on the production and carriage of frankincense, myrrh, and other aromatics. The Himyarites, whose capital was at Zafar, united most of the south by about the 270s CE, absorbing the older Sabaean center of Ma’rib. Roman authors since the first century had known a Red Sea trade conducted through Egyptian and Eritrean ports, and Aksum, the highland kingdom in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, emerged as a regional power with its own coinage, an Indian Ocean outlook, and, from the fourth century, an official Christianity. In northern Arabia, Roman and Persian authorities had long dealt with Arab leaders along the desert frontier, a relationship Chapter 30 introduced in connection with the Nabataeans and the Roman eastern frontier.

Finally, the sixth century saw global shocks that modern historians continue to assess. The plague that began in the 540s, known as the Justinianic plague, struck the Mediterranean world and neighboring regions, although its demographic scale is now debated more cautiously than it once was. Climatic fluctuations, harvest failures, and earthquakes also marked the period. Such events form a backdrop to the political developments in this chapter, but they should not be treated as self-sufficient causes. Political decisions, military organization, and religious commitments mattered at least as much.

Section 34.1

Arabia in the Late Antique Middle Eastern World

It is easy to imagine late antique Arabia as a vast desert separating two great empires and a few trading oases. The evidence supports a different picture. The peninsula’s western edge faced the Red Sea, along which ships linked Egypt, the Sinai coast, the Hijaz, Yemen, and the Eritrean and Ethiopian ports of Aksum. Its southern coast faced the Indian Ocean, with monsoon-driven voyages to the Horn of Africa, India, and Sri Lanka. Its eastern shore faced the Persian Gulf, a Sasanian waterway. Its northern edge merged into the Syrian steppe, where Roman provinces and Sasanian Mesopotamia met Arabic-speaking communities. At each of these edges, Arabians interacted with outsiders who traded with them, hired them, converted them, fought with them, and sent embassies to them.

This connectedness has to be reconciled with the fact that the evidence for it is uneven. The densest and most reliable sources concern Yemen, where kings and elites carved inscriptions in the monumental South Arabian script and, in the sixth century, sometimes dated them by the Himyarite era. These are contemporary documents written by participants, though they serve royal purposes and rarely give a balanced account. For the northern frontier, Greek authors such as Procopius, Malalas, and John of Ephesus, together with Syriac chronicles and Greek inscriptions and papyri, describe Arab leaders as seen from the imperial side. For al-Hira and the Sasanian frontier the evidence is thinner and often later: Syriac Christian sources, the Persian historical tradition as preserved in Arabic works such as al-Tabari’s history (compiled around 915), and Arabic poetry attributed to court poets. For the war of 602–628, there are Greek, Syriac, Armenian, Georgian, Coptic, and later Arabic and Persian accounts, as well as numismatic and archaeological evidence.

The distinction between classes of evidence matters because the later Arabic tradition remembers this world in a particular way. Early Islamic historians, writing in the eighth through tenth centuries, preserved narratives about South Arabian kings, about the Aksumite invasion, about Ghassanid and Lakhmid courts, and about the “Day of Dhu Qar,” a famous battle between Arab tribes and Persian-led forces. These narratives preserve real memories but they were shaped by genealogical interests, by the literary tastes of the Abbasid age, and by the later theological need to depict pre-Islamic Arabia as a time of ignorance (jahiliyya). Contemporary inscriptions, Greek chronicles, and Syriac letters do not support every detail of the later accounts, and a historian must say so.

Historical Vocabulary
“Arab” and “Saracen”

The words used by late antique observers for the peoples of the Arabian desert and its fringes were neither stable nor neutral. In the Sabaic inscriptions of South Arabia, “Arabs” (a‘rab) were mobile groups of the steppe and desert, distinct from the settled population of the highlands and often acting as soldiers or subjects of a king. Greek and Latin authors used Saracens for the same or overlapping groups, usually pastoral peoples on the imperial frontier, and by the sixth century often as a catch-all label for frontier Arabic-speakers. Syriac authors used Tayyaye, derived from the name of the tribe of Tayyi’, for Arabs in the Sasanian sphere.

These terms did not describe a single ethnic nation or a single religion. Later Arabic authors used “Arab” for an increasingly broad cultural and genealogical category, and modern nationalism has reinforced the assumption that ancient Arabians belonged to one people. This textbook uses “Arab” for Arabic-speaking communities where the sources permit and always notes that political loyalty, religion, and way of life varied within the category.

Section 34.2

Red Sea, Desert, and Gulf: The Geography of Connection

Chapter 2 introduced the geography of the Arabian Peninsula, and Section 2.6 and Section 2.7 should be recalled here. What matters for this chapter is how particular zones created different kinds of connection. The southwestern highlands of Yemen, with elevations above three thousand meters in places and seasonal monsoon rainfall, supported terraced agriculture, fortified towns, and relatively dense population. Ma’rib, at the desert edge, drew on floodwaters stored and distributed by the great dam, and Zafar, the Himyarite capital, sat in the highlands. Najran, farther north in a fertile valley between the highlands and the interior desert, was a major oasis and caravan station. The Tihama, the hot coastal plain on the Red Sea side, had small ports and was linked to the highlands by mountain tracks.

North of Yemen, the western Arabian highland chain runs along the Red Sea through the Hijaz toward the Sinai and the Gulf of Aqaba. The Hijaz comprises narrow coastal plains, volcanic fields, and valleys, among them the valleys in which Mecca and Medina (anciently Yathrib) stand. Those places belong chiefly to Chapter 35. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that this corridor connected the south with Syria and Palestine, and that at its northern end ports such as Aila (modern Aqaba) linked the overland route with maritime routes. Eastward, the central plateau of Najd and the great sand deserts gave way, along the Gulf, to the oases and shores of eastern Arabia and, farther south, to Oman, which looked toward the Indian Ocean.

The Syrian desert and steppe, extending from Palestine and Syria toward the Euphrates, was the northern theater of the frontier. Oases such as Dumat al-Jandal, passes like the Wadi Sirhan, and desert-edge cities such as Bostra, Palmyra (declining but still occupied), and al-Hira on the western edge of the Euphrates floodplain organized movement. Water sources, pasture, and the seasonal rhythms of herding shaped where armies and caravans could go. Pastoral groups moved across these areas in ways that did not respect imperial boundaries, and imperial authorities sought to influence them through payments, titles, and alliances.

Section 34.3

Caravan Routes, Ports, and Maritime Commerce

Frankincense and myrrh, harvested in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, had been among the best-known luxury commodities of the ancient world, and incense-bearing caravans along the western Arabian route are among the images most often associated with the peninsula. By late antiquity the picture was more complex. Aromatics remained in demand for temples, churches, funerals, and medicine, though historians debate how far the Christianization of the Roman world changed that demand, and some of the older arguments about a collapse of the incense trade have been revised. What did change was the broader structure of exchange: the Indian Ocean now brought spices, textiles, and other goods in large quantities through Red Sea and Gulf ports, and Mediterranean and Iranian markets drew on both sea and land routes.

On the Red Sea, the Aksumite port of Adulis was the principal gateway to the African highlands, exporting ivory, hides, and gold-linked trade goods and receiving cloth, wine, and metal wares. Egyptian Red Sea harbors that had thrived in the early empire, such as Berenike, had declined by the sixth century, and the port at Clysma near Suez and the northern Red Sea harbor of Aila were among those that connected the sea to Egypt and Palestine. The Monastery of Saint Catherine at Mount Sinai, built in the reign of Justinian, was a major point of pilgrimage and a destination for Arab pastoralists and traders in the Sinai. On the Yemeni side, the coastal ports and the harbor associated with Aden carried goods across the Bab-el-Mandeb and toward the Indian Ocean.

On the Persian Gulf, the Sasanian state controlled the northern and eastern shores and had projected power to eastern Arabia and Oman at several points, using the Gulf as a channel for commerce with India and Sri Lanka. The sixth-century Alexandrian merchant known as Kosmas Indikopleustes, who wrote about the Indian Ocean in the 540s, describes Sri Lanka as a crossroads where ships from Persia, Ethiopia, and elsewhere met, and he reports a Persian community there. That report, though from a Christian writer with his own interests, matches other evidence for Persian engagement in Indian Ocean shipping. The silk that Byzantine consumers prized came largely overland through Persian territory or by sea through Persian-controlled routes, and Byzantine emperors, especially Justinian, sought alternatives. A famous episode, recorded by Procopius and other writers, claims that monks smuggled silkworm eggs from Central Asia to Constantinople in the 550s.

Taxes and tolls linked commerce to politics. Rulers charged customs at ports and oases, and caravans needed protection and safe passage. Himyar’s kings controlled routes from the highlands to the coast and north into central Arabia. Aksum earned revenue from Adulis and from its role in Red Sea shipping. The Roman and Persian states taxed trade at frontier cities such as Nisibis and Dara, and they tried to regulate it by treaty. Arab leaders in the north benefited from caravans and from imperial subsidies, which is one reason why control of desert routes mattered. This section has deliberately avoided placing Mecca at the center of the story. Mecca’s commercial and social setting is treated in Chapter 35, and a later chapter on interregional commerce, Chapter 51, examines Mediterranean, Red Sea, Gulf, and Indian Ocean networks in the Islamic period.

Section 34.4

Himyar and the Transformation of Late Antique Yemen

The kingdom of Himyar emerged from the competition among South Arabian states that had marked the first centuries CE. By about 275, Himyarite kings had absorbed Saba and Hadramawt and claimed a composite royal title that included the highlands and the coastal plain as well as “their Arabs,” meaning the mobile groups on whom they relied for military support. From their capital at Zafar, while also controlling the older Sabaean center of Ma’rib, they ruled a heartland of intensive agriculture and fortified towns, and they also asserted authority over parts of central Arabia through alliances with confederations. The Murayghan inscription of 552, discussed in Section 34.8, shows a later ruler still dealing with a group called Ma‘add in central Arabia, evidence of continuity in this relationship.

The late Himyarite period, from about the fourth century to the 570s, saw major changes in royal ideology. Early Himyarite inscriptions invoke a range of local gods. From about 380, royal texts begin to refer to a single deity, called in various inscriptions “the Lord of Heaven,” “the Lord of Heaven and Earth,” or “the Merciful” (Rahmanan), and polytheistic dedications disappear from royal inscriptions. Historians read this shift as a monotheistic turn in the political culture of the kingdom, not necessarily as the adoption of a single named religion by every inhabitant. Local cults likely persisted in rural areas, and the evidence of private inscriptions and archaeology is more varied than the royal texts.

Himyar mattered strategically for several reasons. First, it controlled the southern end of the Red Sea and the approaches to the Bab-el-Mandeb. Second, its agricultural wealth, including the irrigation system at Ma’rib, supported a large population and a significant military. Third, its relationship with Arab groups in central and northern Arabia gave it influence along the caravan route toward Syria. Fourth, its position between Aksum across the water and the Roman and Persian empires to the north made it a natural focus for external diplomacy. A Greek source reports that in about 356 the emperor Constantius II sent an embassy to Himyar led by a bishop named Theophilus to encourage Christianity and permit church building at Zafar and Aden. The report, from a later ecclesiastical historian, is consistent with Roman interest but does not prove that Christianity then spread widely in the south.

Internal conditions also shaped the kingdom. The dam at Ma’rib required regular maintenance, and inscriptions record repair campaigns in the fifth and sixth centuries, which show both the state’s capacity to mobilize labor and the vulnerability of the irrigation system. Succession disputes grew sharper in the early sixth century, when a quick sequence of rulers and rival claimants reflected rivalries among aristocratic families and uncertainty about outside alignments. These circumstances formed the backdrop to the crisis of the 520s.

Section 34.5

Judaism and Christianity in South Arabia

Himyarite monotheism in the fifth century was not a blank. Jewish communities had existed in the Arabian Peninsula and in Yemen for a long time, and, by the fifth and sixth centuries, Jewish influence at the Himyarite court is visible in inscriptions that refer to “the God of Israel” and to a Jewish institutional presence. Some royal inscriptions show kings invoking the “Lord of the Jews” or using wording close to Jewish liturgical language, and the funerary inscriptions of Himyarite Jews at Beth She’arim in Galilee demonstrate that families from southern Arabia maintained links with Palestinian Judaism. Later Arabic tradition attributes a conversion to Judaism to a king called Abikarib As‘ad (also known in later sources as a Tubba‘), but later tradition and contemporary evidence differ in detail, and a safe statement is that Jewish identity and practice became more visible in the royal sphere from the fifth century.

Christianity also took root. After the fourth-century Roman and Aksumite missions, Christian communities emerged in Najran, in some coastal ports, and at Zafar, and Himyar’s Christians were in contact with the Syriac-speaking Church of the East and with Miaphysite and Chalcedonian churches. Najran in particular had a substantial Christian community with clergy and local leaders, as later sources about its martyrs indicate. Christians did not all share the same doctrinal allegiance, and some later martyr texts treat them as part of the wider Christian oikoumene without specifying communion with a particular church.

What can be inferred about royal religious policy? Here caution is essential. Contemporary Himyarite inscriptions show kings using monotheistic language, invoking a single God and sometimes associating their rule with Jewish expressions. They do not, in the surviving inscriptions, issue a policy of general conversion to Judaism, and they do not clearly describe the kingdom as a “Jewish state.” Greek and Syriac Christian sources, written by those who suffered under Dhu Nuwas or sympathized with them, tend to call him a Jew and a persecutor. Later Arabic accounts make Judaism the religion of the king and his people in more categorical terms. Historians now generally say that the kingdom’s religious situation was complex: a monotheist court, Jewish influence in high politics, an important Christian minority with foreign connections, and continuing traditional practice. The conflict of the 520s should be read in that setting.

The archaeological and epigraphic record is still growing. Excavations at Himyarite sites and the publication of new inscriptions have changed how scholars interpret the period, and several readings in older books have been revised. Students should therefore treat any confident description of Himyarite religion with skepticism, and look to see whether it rests on a dated inscription, a Greek or Syriac narrative, or a later Arabic tradition.

Section 34.6

Dhu Nuwas, Najran, and the Crisis of the 520s

In the early 520s a Himyarite aristocrat named Yusuf As’ar Yath’ar, known in Greek and Syriac sources as Dhu Nuwas, seized power. The circumstances are disputed. Some accounts represent him as a usurper who overthrew a king allied to Aksum, and others as the legitimate holder of power in an unstable system. Greek and Ethiopic texts connect his rise to the presence of an Aksumite-supported order in the highlands and on the coast, in which a Christian garrison at Zafar and Aksumite merchants were visible. Yusuf’s early moves included an attack on that garrison and on the church at Zafar, followed by a campaign against Najran, the northern oasis with its Christian population.

The conventional date for the events at Najran is 523. The sources are of several kinds. Himyarite inscriptions associated with the campaign, among them rock inscriptions in the Najran region, record military action by the king and his forces and make no sympathetic mention of Christians. A Syriac letter attributed to Simeon, bishop of Beth Arsham near Ctesiphon, was written in the mid-520s and reports that an embassy from the Himyarite king had come to the Lakhmid ruler al-Mundhir, at his camp, urging him to take similar action against Christians in his own realm. The letter is a Miaphysite Syriac source produced within the Sasanian-connected frontier world; Simeon was a Miaphysite polemicist and an opponent of East Syriac (Dyophysite) theology. Other accounts come from different milieus and should not be blended with it: the Syriac Book of the Himyarites, the Greek Martyrdom of Arethas (the Greek name of the martyr al-Harith of Najran), and Ethiopic martyrdom traditions. These accounts differ in details: the number killed, the sequence of events, the treatment of the city’s leaders, and the roles of women and children vary. Their authors wrote to commemorate martyrs, to inform allied rulers and bishops, and to mobilize action, and they shaped events as stories of faith.

Reading such texts requires distinguishing core claims from literary elaboration. The existence of a violent attack on Christians at Najran in the 520s is supported by Himyarite and Christian sources alike. The use of fire, the killing of clergy and other Christians, and a demand for apostasy or submission appear in many accounts. The detailed speeches, miracles, and exact numbers do not carry the same weight. The text of the Syriac letter, with its account of the king’s envoys and of Christian refugees, illustrates how quickly news traveled along the frontier networks: a bishop near Ctesiphon, a Lakhmid ruler on a Sasanian-Roman border camp, and a Himyarite court at Zafar were part of a single flow of information.

What was the episode? It should be treated simultaneously as religion, politics, and Red Sea geopolitics. For the Christians of Najran it was persecution and martyrdom, remembered in later liturgy. For Dhu Nuwas it may have been an attempt to eliminate a community that he saw as aligned with Aksum and Byzantium, at a moment when Himyarite independence was threatened. For Aksum, whose rulers and church identified with Christianity and whose merchants had interests in Himyarite ports, it was a provocation and an opportunity. For Byzantium, still engaged with Persia and interested in Red Sea trade and in Christian allies, it offered a chance to project influence. The diplomatic message sent to al-Mundhir shows that Dhu Nuwas was also seeking allies against the same Christians he attacked in Yemen. Later memory transformed these events into a founding scene in several traditions: martyr cults in Syriac and Ethiopic Christianity, and, in the Islamic tradition, an allusion often connected by commentators to the “companions of the trench” in the Qur’an, an association made by later exegetes and debated by modern scholars.

Section 34.7

Aksum Crosses the Red Sea

Aksum was one of the great states of late antiquity, and it should not appear as an external footnote. Located in the highlands of modern northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, with its port at Adulis on the Red Sea, it had minted gold coins since the third century, maintained inscriptions in Greek, Ge‘ez, and Sabaic, and by the mid-fourth century under King Ezana had adopted Christianity, probably through contacts with Alexandria and the Roman East. The Aksumite church later maintained a close relationship with the Coptic church of Egypt. Aksumite kings had also at times held territory or exercised influence in South Arabia before the sixth century, and, in the sixth century, their ambitions across the sea were linked to Christianity, commerce, and security.

Procopius, writing in Constantinople, says that the emperor Justinian sought the help of the Aksumite king Kaleb (called Elesboas in Greek sources) against Persia, and that the Aksumites controlled sea routes useful for silk and trade. Whatever the details of these diplomatic exchanges, Byzantium and Aksum had overlapping interests. In 525 or thereabouts, in response to the persecution at Najran and the fall of the Aksumite-backed order at Zafar, Kaleb led an expedition across the Red Sea. The evidence for the expedition comes from Greek, Syriac, and Ethiopic texts, and each has its own bias. Some of them mention Byzantine ships, supplied through merchants or through imperial support, though the extent of Byzantine assistance is unclear.

The invasion succeeded. Himyarite resistance collapsed, and Dhu Nuwas died in the fighting or by his own hand, according to the varying accounts. Kaleb installed a Christian Himyarite named Sumyafa‘ Ashwa‘ (Esimiphaios in Greek) as a vassal king, and Aksumite troops remained in Yemen. Ethiopic tradition recounts that Kaleb, after his victory, abdicated and became a monk, a story that suits a pious narrative and is not independently confirmed. The immediate result was an Aksumite-backed Christian order in South Arabia and a changed balance in the Red Sea. For a brief moment Aksum controlled the straits, the ports on both sides, and routes into Arabia. The success of the expedition also demonstrates the maritime and military capabilities of Aksum and the seriousness with which Mediterranean powers treated its rulers.

Including Aksum in the history of the Middle East corrects a Eurasian bias. The Red Sea was a connective space, not a barrier. Christianity, trade, diplomacy, and military power crossed it in both directions, and the political history of South Arabia cannot be understood without reference to the African shore. This is also why later chapters on Red Sea commerce and on modern Red Sea security, including Chapter 160, return to the strait that Kaleb’s ships crossed.

Section 34.8

Abraha and Aksumite Yemen

The Aksumite settlement did not last in the form Kaleb had arranged. According to Procopius, a man named Abraha, an Aksumite Christian whom the historian describes as a former slave of a Roman merchant at Adulis, rose against the king’s appointee in the 530s with the support of discontented Aksumite soldiers. Procopius’s story is hostile to Abraha and cannot be accepted uncritically, but the central point, that Aksumite troops and officers in Yemen turned an inherited arrangement into an autonomous rulership, is confirmed by Abraha’s own inscriptions. Kaleb sent forces to remove him. According to Procopius they either defected or were defeated, and Abraha eventually agreed to a nominal subordination and tribute, though in practice he ruled Yemen with extensive independence. He styled himself king of the highlands and the coast and their Arabs, and he took the traditional Sabaic royal title.

Abraha’s rule, which lasted into the 550s or perhaps the 560s, combined Christian patronage with the use of established Himyarite practices of kingship. He built or restored churches, and later Arabic tradition remembers a great church at Sana’a, but he also repaired the Ma’rib dam in the Himyarite manner, mobilized tribal levies, and negotiated with Arab and imperial powers. His inscriptions, in the Sabaic language and monumental script, are among the most important contemporary documents of the age. The Ma’rib inscription of 548 is the subject of the Primary Source Window below. A second inscription, found at Murayghan on the route into central Arabia and dated 552, records a campaign against the Ma‘add confederation, a settlement with groups including Kinda and others, and the movement of an army across the interior. It is the best contemporary evidence for Abraha’s military activity toward the north.

Primary Source Window

Abraha’s Ma’rib Dam Inscription (CIH 541), dated 548 CE

Creator: Abraha, ruler of Himyar under Aksumite suzerainty · Date: year 658 of the Himyarite era, corresponding to 548 CE · Type: Monumental royal inscription in Sabaic, carved in stone at the Ma’rib dam

Context: The Ma’rib dam was the infrastructural heart of the oasis, and its repair was a statement of royal capacity. The inscription records the restoration of the dam after a breach, the labor and supplies mobilized, and the ceremonies that followed. It also situates Abraha in a network of diplomatic relationships and offers evidence of a Christian royal language in South Arabia.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The text begins with an invocation of the Merciful God, his Messiah, and the Holy Spirit, a clear Trinitarian formula in the Sabaic idiom. It identifies Abraha as ruler of the highlands, the coastal plain, and their Arabs, and recounts that the dam had been breached and was repaired by large crews working over months, with food and materials supplied from across the kingdom. It also refers to a recent rebellion against his authority. When the work was completed, the inscription says, envoys came to celebrate and to deal with the king: representatives of the Aksumite ruler, of the Roman emperor, of the Persian king, and of the Arab rulers allied to the two empires, including the Lakhmid al-Mundhir and the Ghassanid al-Harith.

Analysis: The inscription is contemporary and was designed for public display, which is both its strength and its limitation. It shows that a ruler in highland Yemen could receive embassies from all the surrounding powers, and the list of envoys gives a political map of the Middle East in the mid-sixth century. The royal invocation shows that Christianity was the vocabulary of Abraha’s legitimacy, in a kingdom whose earlier kings had used Jewish or generic monotheistic language. The restoration of a dam first built many centuries earlier shows how Abraha sought continuity with Himyarite tradition. Finally, the text does not mention any expedition to Mecca, and this absence is significant for later traditions discussed below.

Limitations: The inscription is propaganda, speaking for the ruler. It tells us little about ordinary farmers, the laborers on the dam, or the Himyarite aristocrats who opposed Abraha. Its description of envoys reflects what Abraha chose to emphasize and may exaggerate his status. Some readings of individual words remain debated by epigraphers.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the list of envoys suggest about how neighboring powers regarded Abraha’s kingdom?
  2. Why might Abraha have chosen to commemorate a dam repair rather than a military victory?
  3. How does the Trinitarian invocation help historians understand the religious change in Yemen between the fifth and sixth centuries?
  4. What kinds of historical questions can this inscription not answer, and what other sources might be needed?

Later Islamic tradition tells of an expedition by Abraha against Mecca in the “Year of the Elephant,” traditionally placed around the time of Muhammad’s birth and usually dated to about 570 or in that decade. The tradition draws on the Qur’an’s short chapter on the Companions of the Elephant and on narratives preserved in biographies of Muhammad. Contemporary inscriptions do not mention such an expedition, and the epigraphic evidence for Abraha’s northern activity is the Murayghan inscription of 552. Moreover, chronological problems abound, because Abraha’s rule seems to have ended before the dates usually assigned to the elephant year. Historians generally agree that some memory of a Yemenite military expedition lies behind the tradition, but they differ on what happened and on how to date it. For this chapter the important point is methodological: the contemporary epigraphic sources and the later narrative tradition should be kept separate. Chapter 35 and Chapter 36 will return to the memory of these events in the context of Mecca.

Abraha’s Yemen also shows the commercial importance of the Red Sea. His rule linked Aksum and the highlands to the trade of the coast, and his court was a node for diplomacy. When Abraha died, probably in the 550s or 560s, the succession was contested. Procopius and later tradition mention sons and successors, but the political order was weakened, and the Aksumite presence became harder to sustain.

Section 34.9

Sasanian Intervention and the End of Aksumite Rule in Yemen

Chapter 32 mentioned Sasanian interests in southern Arabia, and Khosrow I’s reign (531–579) brought them into focus. The Sasanian monarchy had a long-standing interest in the Gulf and in routes to the Indian Ocean, and as a rival of Byzantium it saw in Aksumite Yemen a Christian state allied to its enemy and astride shipping lanes. In the 570s, according to Arabic and Persian traditions and with some corroboration from Byzantine sources, a Himyarite noble named Sayf ibn Dhi Yazan, from a family that had lost power under the Aksumites, appealed to Khosrow I. A Persian expedition under a commander called Wahriz, with troops that included, in the later tradition, men released from prison, sailed from the Gulf, defeated the Aksumite forces and local allies, and reorganized Yemen.

The dating of this intervention is debated. Traditional chronology places the expedition around 570 and some sources put it a few years later, which affects how it relates to the Persian-Byzantine war that began in 572. Some scholars argue the intervention was part of a general Sasanian strategy in the years before that war. Others emphasize that Persian action in Yemen responded to a request from Himyarite elites and to the collapse of Aksumite authority after Abraha. Both explanations can be true together. The last datable South Arabian royal inscriptions come from around the 560s, and after that the epigraphic record thins sharply, a sign of the profound political transformation underway.

Under the Sasanians, Yemen was governed by a local ruler under Persian authority and then, according to later sources, by Persian governors known by the Persian title marzban, the last of whom, Badhan, is remembered in the Islamic tradition. A community of Persian soldiers and settlers, later remembered as the Abna’ (“sons”), remained in Yemen. The Sasanian presence is thinner in documentary evidence than that of Aksum, but it brought the south into a network of trade and diplomacy centered on the Gulf and Ctesiphon. It also meant that, by the end of the sixth century, both ends of Arabia’s western and eastern seaboards had experienced Persian influence, and that Yemen no longer stood between two empires but within one of them.

The expulsion of the Aksumites also changed Red Sea politics. Aksum, after losing Yemen, faced internal difficulties and changes in trade networks, and its power gradually declined in the following centuries, though the causes of that decline were many and not confined to this event. For Byzantium, the loss of a Christian ally in Yemen was a strategic setback. For the Arab groups of the interior, the shift altered patterns of patronage and payment. The end of Himyar as an independent kingdom closes an era of South Arabian history that had lasted more than a thousand years.

Section 34.10

The Northern Arabian Frontier and the Politics of Federation

Along the long northern frontier, Rome and Persia confronted a similar problem. Their regular armies were organized to fight one another, not to control the desert, and the Arab groups of the steppe could raid cities, escort caravans, serve as scouts, or fight as light cavalry. Both empires therefore negotiated with Arab leaders. They paid subsidies, granted titles, supported church building, and incorporated Arab troops into campaigns. Arab leaders, in turn, used these relationships to build their own power, to resolve internal rivalries, and to protect their communities. It is misleading to see these arrangements merely as “buffer states” intended to absorb shocks for imperial benefit. They were negotiated, unstable, and shaped by Arab ambitions as much as imperial plans.

Historical Vocabulary
Federates, Phylarchs, and Frontier Kingship

A foederatus (plural foederati, “federates” in English) was an allied people or group that served the Roman state under a treaty rather than as regular troops. A phylarch (Greek phylarchos, “tribal ruler”) was a leader of an allied tribal group whom the empire recognized. In the sixth century, Byzantine authorities used these categories to describe Arab commanders such as the Jafnid leaders, whom they sometimes honored with high titles. Persian authorities used different terms and less documented arrangements for the leaders at al-Hira, whom Arabic and Syriac sources often call kings.

These categories describe imperial perspectives. From within Arab communities, leaders such as al-Harith ibn Jabala or al-Mundhir III might be seen as kings, military commanders, patrons, and tribal chiefs, depending on audience. Historians should therefore avoid treating the label “phylarch” as a complete description of a ruler’s authority, and should remember that the sources themselves reflect the interests of those who named them.

The northern Arab dynasties had earlier predecessors. In the fourth century, the Namara inscription in Arabic written in a Nabataean-derived script commemorates a king named Imru’ al-Qays, whose title and claims show an Arab ruler operating on the Roman frontier. In 378, a leader named Mavia (Mawiya) led a revolt against Roman authorities in the East, and a settlement involving a bishop acceptable to her community ended it, an episode that shows both the military strength of Arab groups and the religious dimension of frontier politics. In the fifth century, Arab groups under various leaders served the Roman Empire and were then replaced by others, and, on the Persian side, the Lakhmids of al-Hira rose to prominence. By the sixth century, the two best-documented systems were those led by the Jafnids (Ghassanids) for Byzantium and the Lakhmids (also called Nasrids) for Sasanian Iran.

The two systems were never mirror images. The Jafnids operated within a Roman provincial landscape that included cities, churches, and imperial roads, and their leaders were Christians who engaged in ecclesiastical politics. The Lakhmids operated in a Sasanian setting in which the king was a Zoroastrian monarch, al-Hira was a Christian and Arab city, and the Church of the East was a major institution. In both, the leaders were Arab commanders with local followings who could turn against imperial patrons when interests diverged. The rivalry between the two Arab dynasties sometimes escalated into war independent of imperial treaties, and the empires used that fact for their own ends.

Section 34.11

The Ghassanids/Jafnids and Byzantium

Modern historians often call the Arab frontier dynasty of Byzantine Syria the Ghassanids, after the confederation named Ghassan in later Arabic genealogies, which traced them to a migration from Yemen after the supposed collapse of the Ma’rib dam. That migration story belongs to later tradition and is not supported by contemporary evidence. The term Jafnids, from the ancestor Jafna in Arabic genealogical sources, is closer to the dynastic line, while Ghassan refers more broadly to the confederation. This chapter uses both, noting the difference between the ruling dynasty and the wider group.

The great figure of the dynasty is al-Harith ibn Jabala (called Arethas in Greek sources), who in 529 received from the emperor Justinian an exceptional status, including the Roman rank of patrikios and a kind of kingly authority over the Arab federates of the East. The grant reflected a Byzantine need for strong Arab allies against the Lakhmid al-Mundhir III, whose raids into Syria were a persistent threat. Al-Harith fought for Byzantium in the war of the 530s, and Arab contingents played a part at Callinicum in 531, where the imperial general Belisarius was defeated, and in other engagements. In 554 al-Mundhir III was killed in a battle with the Jafnids, an event celebrated in Arabic poetry and tradition, though the precise circumstances are less clear.

The Jafnids were also Miaphysites, adherents of the doctrinal tradition that rejected the formula of Chalcedon, and their patronage of Miaphysite clergy had political implications. Syriac sources, especially John of Ephesus, describe how, in the 540s, al-Harith and the empress Theodora supported the consecration of Miaphysite bishops, including Jacob Baradaeus, who built a clandestine church network across Syria and Mesopotamia. The patronage of a Miaphysite Arab elite by a Chalcedonian empire created recurring tensions. Justinian and his successors wanted unity in the church, and the Jafnid leaders were important allies who held positions that the imperial church condemned.

Material evidence confirms the dynasty’s Christian and monumental presence. At Rusafa (Sergiopolis), a pilgrimage center to the martyr Sergius in the Syrian steppe, a large building associated by scholars with al-Mundhir ibn al-Harith is interpreted as an audience hall or reception building. Greek and Arabic inscriptions, among them a trilingual text from Zebed in Syria of 512 and a Greek and Arabic inscription from Harran in southern Syria dated 568, show the use of Arabic script, the presence of Arab Christians, and the integration of Arab elites with imperial culture. Sources also mention Jafnid patronage of churches and monasteries and the dynasty’s use of seasonal camps and settlements, such as Jabiya in the Golan region.

The relationship with the empire eventually broke down. In the early 580s, after quarrels and suspicions about al-Mundhir ibn al-Harith’s loyalty, Byzantine authorities arrested him, and the unity of the Ghassanid leadership fragmented. Contemporary and later sources attribute this to the combination of Persian war, religious tension, and imperial mistrust, but historians remain uncertain about the immediate causes. Arab forces continued to serve Byzantium in various forms, but the great federation had been broken, and the northern Arabian frontier became less predictable, a condition that would shape the crisis after 602.

Section 34.12

The Lakhmids/Nasrids and Sasanian Iran

The Lakhmid dynasty ruled from al-Hira, a city near the Euphrates in what is now south-central Iraq, and acted as the principal Arab ally of the Sasanians on the western frontier. Some modern scholars prefer to call the later rulers Nasrids, after an ancestor Nasr in the genealogical tradition, and the double label reflects the difficulty of fitting dynastic names to evidence. The best-known ruler, the long-reigning al-Mundhir III, killed in 554, raided Syria, captured Byzantine hostages, and took part in the diplomacy that produced Persian and Byzantine settlements. The Syriac letter of Simeon of Beth Arsham, mentioned in Section 34.6, shows him at a frontier camp receiving Himyarite envoys and dealing with Christian and Persian clergy.

Al-Hira was a city of palaces, monasteries, and churches, and its Christians belonged largely to the Church of the East, whose bishops attended synods in Sasanian territory. Arabic literary tradition remembers the Lakhmid court as a center of poetry and patronage, hosting poets who later became famous, though the authenticity and date of much poetry attributed to them is a continuing problem for historians. The court also had Persian ceremonial connections. The Lakhmid kings accepted the overlordship of the Sasanian shahanshah, took part in Persian campaigns, and were responsible for guarding the western frontier and for controlling desert routes. They also collected tribute and mediated among Arab tribes.

The end of the dynasty came in about 602. Arabic and Persian tradition, found in sources such as al-Tabari, tells a dramatic story: Nu’man III ibn al-Mundhir, who had become a Christian, quarreled with Khosrow II, was imprisoned, and died, in one famous version trampled by elephants. The story’s details are conventional and must be treated as legend. What is plausible is that Khosrow II ended the Lakhmid monarchy and replaced it with direct Persian administration or with a governor drawn from another Arab family. Why he did so is debated: suspicion of the king’s loyalty, a dispute over succession, pressure from Persian aristocrats, or a general policy of centralization. The removal of a major frontier intermediary on the eve of the war is nonetheless striking, and it left the Arab frontier less controlled just as Khosrow turned his attention to Byzantium.

Later tradition associates the aftermath with a battle at Dhu Qar between Arab groups and Persian-led forces, in which Arab confederations, notably the Bakr ibn Wa’il, are said to have defeated a Persian army. The date is uncertain, traditionally placed in the early seventh century, and the account is embedded in later tribal memory, so the battle’s scale and significance cannot be fixed. Its reputation in Arabic tradition, however, shows how the dissolution of Lakhmid rule left Arab groups to fight their own conflicts with Persian authorities and with one another.

Section 34.13

Arab Christianity, Judaism, and Frontier Religious Networks

On the eve of Islam, “Arab” did not imply a single religion. Arab Christians lived on both sides of the Syrian desert and in Yemen. Fifth-century evidence mentions bishops for the “encampments” of Arab groups in Palestine, and the tale of Arab converts to the monastic life at Sinai and in the Judean desert is a feature of the lives of saints written by monastic authors such as Cyril of Scythopolis. In Syria, the Jafnids patronized Miaphysite churches and monasteries, and at al-Hira, the Church of the East had a bishop and a Christian population, including Arab clergy and aristocrats. Arab pilgrims visited shrines such as that of Sergius at Rusafa and the desert monasteries of the Sinai and Judea.

Historical Vocabulary
Chalcedonian, Miaphysite, and the Church of the East

After the Council of Chalcedon in 451 defined Christ as one person in two natures, Christians who accepted the definition are called Chalcedonians, and this was the position of the imperial church in Constantinople. Miaphysite Christianity understands the incarnate Christ as one united nature or reality possessing both divine and human attributes; the older and often pejorative label “Monophysite” is used less by modern scholars. The Miaphysite churches included Syriac Orthodox and Coptic communities. The Church of the East, centered in the Sasanian Empire, predated Nestorius and developed its East Syriac theology gradually, drawing particularly on Theodore of Mopsuestia and Antiochene traditions; its members are often called Dyophysites, and Roman opponents called them Nestorians, a label the church itself did not accept.

These labels simplify a complex process of doctrinal formulation, ecclesiastical politics, and communal identity. Each community also contained a range of positions. Arab leaders and communities could adhere to any of these traditions, and their choices responded to patrons, neighbors, and rivalries as well as to doctrine.

Jewish communities also formed part of these networks. In Yemen, Jewish presence at the court and in the cities has already been noted. In Palestine, Galilean Jewish communities, centered at Tiberias, were in touch with Himyarite Jews. In Babylonia, under Sasanian rule, rabbinic circles produced and transmitted traditions that eventually formed the Babylonian Talmud, although the later institutional form of the academies is debated (Chapter 31 traced the rabbinic transformation and Chapter 32 the Sasanian context). Jewish merchants and communities in oases of the Hijaz are treated in Chapter 35. At the level of imperial politics, Jews had a complicated status: in the Roman Empire they were a tolerated but restricted religion under Christian emperors, and in Iran they generally enjoyed more room under Zoroastrian rulers, though not without episodes of conflict. These differences figure in the Persian conquest of Jerusalem in 614 (Section 34.16).

The point of this section is not to catalogue every community but to show how religion operated as a network. Bishops traveled between Mesopotamian and Syrian sees; letters moved between al-Hira, Ctesiphon, and Himyar; Himyarite Christians looked to Aksum and Byzantium; and martyr stories were copied in Syriac, Greek, and Ge‘ez. This connectedness complicates the older picture of Arabia as the isolated setting of a purely “pagan” world awaiting revelation. It was a region of religious variety, within which monotheism, whether Jewish, Christian, or the local “Lord of Heaven” traditions, was already familiar. What Arabia’s internal religious life looked like in the Hijaz and central Arabia, including its polytheistic and monotheistic currents, is the work of Chapter 35.

Section 34.14

From the Peace of 591 to the Crisis of 602

The war that began in 602 followed a long sequence. The settlement that ended the war of 540–562 had been renewed and tested, and the war that began in 572 over Armenia, the Arab frontier, and the refusal of Justin II to continue payments lasted nearly two decades. Dara fell to the Persians in 573, Khosrow I died in 579, and the fighting dragged on, with Roman victories and defeats, Arab raids, and mutinies on both sides. The arrest of the Ghassanid leader in the early 580s weakened Byzantine arrangements in the south, while in the Sasanian realm, aristocratic factions pursued their own interests.

In 590 a Sasanian general, Bahram Chobin, rebelled against Hormizd IV and drove his successor, the young Khosrow II, to flee into Roman territory. The emperor Maurice (582–602) decided to support Khosrow, supplying troops and diplomatic backing. In 591, with Roman help, Khosrow regained the throne, and the resulting peace was highly favorable to Byzantium. Persia gave up Dara and Martyropolis and large parts of Armenia and Iberia, and Rome no longer had to pay annual subsidies of the kind earlier treaties had required. The settlement produced an equilibrium that lasted a decade, and it gave Maurice the freedom to turn his forces to the Balkans, where Avars and Slavs threatened the Danube frontier.

The peace also created a personal and political debt. Khosrow II owed his throne to a Christian emperor, a fact that Persian aristocrats and Zoroastrian clergy could regard as humiliating. Persian sources and later Arabic tradition, though shaped by hindsight, suggest tensions at court, and Khosrow’s own marriages and patronage, including favor to Christian communities and the cult of Saint Sergius, point to a complicated personal relationship with Christianity. Some stories in the later tradition even mention a Christian wife, Shirin, whose religious affiliation and influence are debated.

The crisis came in 602. Maurice, under pressure from financial difficulties and from a Balkan campaign that required winter operations beyond the Danube, faced a mutiny in the army. The rebels, led by a centurion named Phocas, marched on Constantinople. Maurice fled, was captured, and was executed along with his sons in November 602. Khosrow II declared that he would avenge his benefactor. He presented a young man as Maurice’s surviving son, Theodosius, and used the claim, whether sincerely or cynically, to justify war. In the same year or soon after, he removed the Lakhmid king at al-Hira, as described in Section 34.12. The combination of a legitimacy crisis in Constantinople and a confident Sasanian king, freed from the threat of domestic revolt and supported by a reformed army, set the stage for the war.

Section 34.15

Khosrow II’s Offensive and the Collapse of the Byzantine East, 602–614

Sasanian forces advanced methodically. In the first years of the war they overran the Roman frontier in Armenia and Upper Mesopotamia. The fortress city of Dara, symbol of the 591 settlement, fell in 604 after a long siege, and other fortified towns followed in succession, with Amida and Edessa and their regions coming under Persian control over the next several years. Earlier Sasanian wars had typically involved raids and sieges followed by negotiated settlement. As this war continued, the campaign increasingly shifted from frontier coercion toward territorial occupation; historians stress that it developed through distinct phases and not from a single plan fixed in 602. The Persian armies, which included units with siege experience and, increasingly, specialized forces, took cities one at a time rather than simply bypassing them.

The Byzantine response was hampered by internal turmoil. The regime of Phocas faced rebellion at home, including from provincial elites, and its response was often brutal. Heavy military commitments in the Balkans, where Avar and Slavic pressure continued, drew forces away from the East. In 610, Heraclius, the son of the exarch of Carthage, sailed with a fleet to Constantinople, overthrew Phocas, and was crowned emperor. He inherited a collapsing eastern front. Over the following years Persian armies took Antioch (about 611), and moved into Cappadocia and Anatolia, and in 613 they defeated the imperial army near Antioch and took Damascus. Persian commanders such as Shahrbaraz and Shahin led these campaigns.

Why did the Persian offensive achieve successes so far beyond earlier frontier wars? Several explanations work together. First, the Byzantine state was divided by civil conflict at the very moment the war began, and the unrest weakened command and logistics. Second, Khosrow II had a well-organized army and a fiscal system developed under Khosrow I, and he used both with determination. Third, the Byzantine defensive system in Syria and Mesopotamia, which depended on fortified cities and Arab allies, had been disrupted by the breakdown of the Jafnid arrangements. Fourth, the Persians exploited local conditions, including cities that preferred to negotiate rather than endure sieges. The religious divisions of the Roman East are sometimes invoked, because Miaphysite and Jewish communities may have seen some advantage in a more tolerant rule. This explanation has been used too broadly. Surviving evidence suggests that responses to the Persian advance varied among cities, communities, and individuals, and some Christians and Jews resisted, while others negotiated or collaborated.

The war was also fought as a contest of legitimacy. Byzantine writers presented Persian victories as divine punishment for sin, and Persian propaganda presented the king as avenger and restorer of order. In Constantinople, a diplomatic overture in 615, described in the Chronicon Paschale, brought Roman envoys to Khosrow with an appeal for peace, which he rejected. The conflict, in other words, was no longer a limited contest but a struggle over the survival of the Byzantine state in the East.

Section 34.16

Jerusalem, Egypt, and the High Tide of Sasanian Expansion, 614–619

In 614, a Persian army under Shahrbaraz moved into Palestine, took Caesarea, and besieged Jerusalem. The city fell after a siege of about three weeks. The accounts that survive, among them the Greek account attributed to Strategius of Mar Saba (preserved in Georgian and Arabic versions), the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos, and later chronicles, agree that the capture was accompanied by killing, destruction of churches, and the removal of relics and prisoners. They give large numbers of dead, in the tens of thousands, but such figures are rhetorical and cannot be accepted as statistics. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre and other churches suffered damage, the Patriarch Zacharias was taken to Persia, and the relic of the True Cross, which was venerated as the wood of Christ’s crucifixion, was carried off to Ctesiphon.

The role of Jewish communities in these events is one of the most contested questions in the sources. Some Christian accounts say that Jews from Galilee and elsewhere joined the Persians and took part in the violence, and Jewish apocalyptic writings of the period express messianic hopes connected with Persian victory. Later Jewish and Christian sources suggest that the Persians briefly granted Jews a degree of authority in Jerusalem, followed by a reversal. Modern historians treat these claims with caution. The Christian sources have strong reasons to blame Jews and to interpret the event as a divine chastisement, and their accounts of Jewish participation vary in detail. Archaeological work in Jerusalem and Palestine has found fewer clear destruction layers for 614 than the written sources might lead one to expect, which suggests that damage varied and that some literary descriptions exaggerate. The wise conclusion is that violence occurred, that some Jews likely cooperated with the Persians for their own reasons, that Persian policy shifted over time, and that the exact scale and responsibility remain debated.

The capture of Jerusalem mattered beyond Palestine. It struck at the religious center of Christendom at a moment when imperial ideology depended on the claim of divine favor. The loss of the True Cross was a shock, and its recovery would become a central theme of Byzantine propaganda. For Jews, the event posed questions about hope and risk under shifting rule. For Persia, it demonstrated the capacity to take the most symbolic city of the Roman East, and it allowed Khosrow to display a conquered patriarch and relic.

The next major target was Egypt, whose grain and tax revenue supported Constantinople. Persian forces entered Egypt in the later 610s and took Alexandria, with the conquest usually dated to about 618 or 619. The details of the campaign are less clear than those of Syria and Palestine, and the Coptic and Greek sources are partial. Persian rule in Egypt lasted roughly a decade, and it disrupted the grain shipments that had fed the capital, a fact that undermined the Byzantine fiscal and political position. A story preserved in later tradition reports that Heraclius considered moving the capital to Carthage, a tale that reflects how grave the crisis seemed even if its details are uncertain. By about 619, Persian forces held Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and much of eastern Anatolia, and raiding parties reached the Bosporus, with a Persian army visible at Chalcedon across from Constantinople. The Sasanian realm had reached its greatest territorial extent. That reach invited comparison with the Achaemenid Empire, but how consciously Khosrow II’s regime invoked Achaemenid precedents remains debated.

Section 34.17

Heraclius Strikes Back, 622–627

Heraclius spent the years after 610 on survival and reorganization. The state raised funds by drastic measures: the free bread distribution in Constantinople was ended after Egypt fell, silver coinage of a new heavier type (the hexagram) was introduced, and the Church, led by the patriarch Sergius, lent plate and treasure to the war effort. Military recruitment and organization were changed to produce a smaller but more mobile and better-motivated field army. Some historians stress the improvised nature of these measures, and others stress their effectiveness. In either case, the empire that had seemed near collapse in 619 was able to put an army in the field in 622.

Heraclius left Constantinople in the spring of 622 and spent the campaign season in Anatolia training troops and winning a series of limited victories against Persian forces. In the following years he shifted the war to the north and east, entering Armenia and Azerbaijan, where he attacked the Zoroastrian shrine at Ganzak (Takht-i Sulayman) in 624, an act presented by Byzantine writers as retaliation for Jerusalem. He wintered in the Caucasus region, where he found allies and supplies, and he fought a series of battles in which Persian armies were forced to withdraw or divide. This approach avoided direct confrontation with the strongest Persian armies in Syria and Egypt and instead threatened Persia’s heartland and its resource base.

The crisis came in 626. Khosrow II coordinated a plan in which a Persian army under Shahrbaraz moved to Chalcedon, while the Avars of the Danube, with Slavic and other allies, besieged Constantinople from the European side in July and August. The city, defended by the patrician Bonus and the patriarch Sergius with a naval force that blocked contact between the two besiegers, held. The siege collapsed in early August when the Avar fleet of small boats was destroyed and the besiegers withdrew. The siege was long remembered in Constantinople as a miracle attributed to the Virgin. Historians point to the coordination between Persians and Avars as evidence of diplomatic ambition and to the defense as evidence of the resilience of the capital’s institutions.

Heraclius, meanwhile, negotiated an alliance with the Western Turkic Khaganate, whose forces joined him in operations in the Caucasus in 627, including the siege of Tiflis. In the autumn of that year he led an army southward across the mountains into the Mesopotamian plain. The two armies met near the ruins of Nineveh on 12 December 627, in a hard-fought battle in which the Persian commander was killed and the Persian army was badly defeated. Heraclius then advanced to Khosrow’s palace complex at Dastagird, which he plundered, and threatened Ctesiphon. Khosrow fled. The older heroic narrative in which Heraclius alone saved Christian civilization is a creation of Byzantine panegyric and later tradition. A better explanation looks at the structures: the empire’s fiscal innovations, the loyalty of the capital’s institutions, the effectiveness of a mobile army operating deep in Persian territory, alliances with steppe powers, and the weakening of Persian morale and finances as the war dragged on.

Section 34.18

The Fall of Khosrow II and the Unstable Peace, 628–630

The defeat at Nineveh and the loss of Dastagird exposed deep fractures in the Sasanian elite. Khosrow II, in his final years, had alienated aristocrats and officials by heavy taxation, by the cost and failures of the war, by arbitrary treatment of commanders, and by uncertainty about the succession. In February 628, a coup led by nobles and military leaders deposed him and placed his son Kavad II (known as Shiroe) on the throne. Khosrow was imprisoned and killed within days, on or about 28 February. Kavad II promptly opened negotiations with Heraclius.

Khosrow’s overthrow in 628 opened the peace process, but negotiations continued for roughly two years through successive Persian governments. Those governments agreed to evacuate the occupied Roman territories of Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and Anatolia, to release prisoners, and to restore the pre-war frontier, and the True Cross was to be returned. Historians such as James Howard-Johnston place the final settlement in the time of Boran.

Kavad II died within months, perhaps of plague, and a child, Ardashir III, succeeded him, with power exercised by regents and generals. Shahrbaraz, the conqueror of Jerusalem and Egypt and commander of Persian forces in the west, made his own arrangements with Heraclius and then seized the throne after the child king’s murder, only to be killed within weeks. A series of short reigns followed, including those of Boran, a daughter of Khosrow II, and Azarmidokht, her sister, and several pretenders, until the accession of Yazdgerd III in 632. These years of dynastic chaos were the most serious political crisis in Sasanian history, and they occurred immediately after the empire’s military catastrophe.

In the Roman East, the end of the war brought a complicated restoration. Heraclius entered Constantinople in triumph and then traveled to the East to supervise the return of the Cross to Jerusalem, a ceremony most likely held in March 630. He sought to restore administration, to rebuild damaged cities and churches, and to heal the church divisions through doctrinal compromise, a project that would produce the controversy over Monotheletism in later years and is beyond the scope of this chapter. He also dealt with the aftermath of occupation: communities that had been under Persian rule, whether Christian, Jewish, or other, now faced the returning imperial administration, which in some places sought reprisal. Tradition says that Heraclius had promised Jews a general pardon and then, at the urging of churchmen, allowed violent acts against them. Historians debate the details and the reliability of the sources, and the scale of any retribution cannot be settled with confidence.

Both empires thus survived the war, and neither had been conquered. But the war had imposed enormous costs: lost cities and tax revenue, devastated provinces, displaced populations, ruined fortifications, and a generation that had lived through occupation, plague, and fiscal stress. The Sasanian political order was shaken at its center, and the Byzantine empire’s recovery depended on a fragile combination of military success, administrative improvisation, and religious reconciliation that had yet to be tested.

Section 34.19

The Middle East around 630: An End of One Late Antique Order

What did the region look like around 630? Yemen was in the Sasanian political sphere, governed by Persian-backed authorities, with a population that included Jews, Christians, and traditional worshippers, and with Persian settlers among them. The kingdom of Himyar no longer existed as an independent state, and Aksum, across the sea, had lost its foothold. In the north, the Lakhmid monarchy had been dissolved, the Ghassanid confederation fragmented, and the Arab frontier shaped by new arrangements that were not yet stable. Christian communities, Miaphysite, Chalcedonian, and Church of the East, were divided and deeply rooted. Jewish communities in Palestine, Babylonia, and Yemen had experienced the war and its aftermath in different ways. Byzantine Syria and Egypt were restored after years of occupation, and Sasanian politics were unstable. Commercial and religious networks, from the Red Sea to the Gulf and from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean, remained active, and pilgrims, merchants, and bishops continued to move.

It is crucial to end this chapter by emphasizing contingency. Nothing about the situation of 630 made Islam or the later Arab conquests inevitable. The empires had survived, and Heraclius had restored the Cross. The Sasanian crisis was grave, but Persian dynastic succession had survived earlier crises. The Arabian communities who would soon become politically significant in a new way were part of this connected world, but the sources of the sixth and early seventh centuries do not predict what would follow. Those developments belong to later chapters. Chapter 35 changes scale and asks what Arabia itself was like before Muhammad, and Chapters 36 through 39 follow the emergence of Islam and the conquests that reshaped the region.

Historical Debate

Were Byzantium and Sasanian Iran “Exhausted” by the War of 602–628?

The question: Older narratives explained the speed of the later Arab-Islamic conquests by arguing that decades of mutual destruction had left Byzantium and Iran too weak to resist. How far does the evidence support that model?

Emphasis A — Structural weakness and crisis: Some scholars, among them Walter Kaegi, have emphasized the serious military, administrative, and legitimacy crises confronting seventh-century Byzantium. Many historians also point to fiscal strain, damaged provinces, broken Arab frontier arrangements, and, for Iran, the collapse of central authority after 628. On this emphasis, both empires entered the 630s more vulnerable than before the war.
Emphasis B — Severe cost without helplessness: Other work, notably that of James Howard-Johnston, documents the enormous wartime costs, including especially heavy strain on Roman finances, yet stresses that neither empire was simply helpless prey in 630. Byzantium had won the war and recovered its provinces, and explanations of the conquests must also weigh the organization of the early Muslim movement, Iranian political fragmentation, the loss of frontier intermediaries, and the contingencies of particular battles. Some also question how far plague and economic damage can be shown for the 620s.

These are different emphases within overlapping scholarship, not opposed camps.

Evidence: Both emphases draw on the same Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and Persian sources, on coinage, and on archaeology that shows varied patterns of destruction and continuity. They differ in the weight placed on structural weakness versus political contingency and on the role of the new Muslim community, a subject for Chapters 36 through 39.

Why it matters: This textbook treats the war as a major disruption that shaped the conditions of the 630s without adopting a deterministic account. Students should be wary of any explanation that makes later outcomes appear inevitable.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through the Great War: Perspectives from Different Communities

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and each community contained a range of experiences.

From the perspective of Christian residents of Jerusalem and Palestine: The capture of the city in 614 brought killing, captivity, and the loss of the cross, relics, and clergy. Chronicle accounts interpret the disaster as divine punishment and emphasize grief and the burial of the dead, while the restoration of the Cross in about 630 was received as a sign of renewed favor. The surviving literature is largely clerical and does not capture the full range of experience.
From the perspective of Jewish communities in Palestine and elsewhere: The Persian advance raised hopes in some circles and fears in others. Some Jews may have cooperated with the Persians, and apocalyptic writing reflects expectations of change, but Christian authors’ accusations are polemical and the evidence of collective Jewish policy is weak. After 628 the return of Roman authority brought anxiety about reprisals, which different sources describe differently.
From the perspective of Arab frontier leaders and their followers: The dissolution of Lakhmid rule and the fragmentation of the Ghassanid system removed the structures through which Arab elites had gained status and income. Some groups found new opportunities in raiding and in service to different patrons, while others faced insecurity. Later tribal memory, preserved in Arabic tradition, records conflicts that the empires did not control.
From the perspective of Himyarite and Yemeni communities: Over a century, Yemenis had lived under Himyarite kings with monotheistic royal language, a persecuting ruler, an Aksumite occupation, a semi-independent Christian king, and a Persian-backed order. Inscriptions and later tradition show elites maneuvering among patrons, while the experience of ordinary farmers and traders remains largely invisible.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Late Antique Order, c. 500 to c. 630

What Changed

  • Himyar disappeared as an independent kingdom, and Yemen passed from Himyarite to Aksumite to Sasanian influence
  • The Lakhmid monarchy ended and the Ghassanid system fragmented, changing how empires dealt with Arab groups
  • The Byzantine-Sasanian relationship shifted from a recognized parity to total war and then to mutual crisis
  • Jerusalem, Egypt, and Syria were occupied and then restored, with lasting effects on administration, finances, and communal memory

What Stayed the Same

  • The Red Sea, Gulf, and desert networks continued to connect Arabia to Africa, Iran, and the Mediterranean
  • Christian, Jewish, and other communities remained deeply rooted across the region
  • Both empires survived, and their institutions, tax systems, and churches persisted
  • Arab communities continued to be political actors on the frontiers, whatever the fate of particular dynasties

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both rupture and persistence prevents two errors: reading the war as a complete collapse that made later change inevitable, or ignoring the disruption it caused.

Cause and Effect

The War of 602–628

Long-term causes

Centuries of Roman-Persian rivalry over Armenia, Mesopotamia, and the Arab frontier; competition for trade routes; religious politics; and the earlier war of 572–591.

Short-term causes

The mutiny and murder of Maurice in 602, Khosrow II’s claim to avenge him, the removal of the Lakhmid king, and Byzantine internal instability.

Immediate effects

Persian conquest of Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and parts of Anatolia; the fall of Jerusalem; Heraclius’s counteroffensive; the deposition and death of Khosrow II.

Long-term and regional consequences

Sasanian dynastic crisis; Byzantine fiscal and religious strain; fragmented Arab frontier arrangements; Yemen under Persian influence; and a region whose later transformation cannot be explained by exhaustion alone.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the war’s course or outcome. Leadership, finance, geography, alliances, religious legitimacy, and chance all interacted.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farmers, Sailors, Pilgrims, and Refugees

Behind kings and generals stood ordinary people whose lives are harder to recover. Highland Yemeni farmers depended on terrace agriculture and on the maintenance of water works such as the Ma’rib dam, and conscripted or hired labor for repairs was part of the rhythm of life. Sailors and merchants at Adulis, Aden, Aila, and Gulf ports worked within the seasonal pattern of the winds. Pastoral groups on the Syrian and Arabian steppe moved among wells and pastures while negotiating with imperial subsidies and raids. Pilgrims visited Sinai, Rusafa, Jerusalem, and martyr shrines, and the loss and return of the Cross shaped devotional life. Women appear in the sources largely through martyr narratives, royal marriages, and papyri, and the evidence for their daily work is thin.

War introduced dislocation. Cities under siege faced hunger and disease, refugees fled to Constantinople, Cyprus, and North Africa, and families were separated by deportations to Persia. Tax collectors, whether Persian or Byzantine, extracted resources, and administrators adjusted to new regimes. Because surviving texts come mostly from clergy, officials, and elites, historians use archaeology, papyri, coin finds, and inscriptions to approach popular experience, always aware of the gaps.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Arabia as part of the connected political, commercial, and religious system of late antiquity. Its central argument is that the peninsula was neither isolated from the great empires nor simply controlled by them. Red Sea, Indian Ocean, Persian Gulf, caravan, frontier, religious, and diplomatic networks linked Arabian communities to Africa, Byzantium, and Iran, and Arab rulers, Yemeni kings, and Aksumite commanders pursued their own interests within those networks.

Sections 34.1 through 34.3 established the framework: the evidence, which ranges from South Arabian inscriptions to Greek, Syriac, Ethiopic, and later Arabic sources, and the geography of the Red Sea, the Hijaz corridor, the Syrian steppe, and the Gulf. They showed that commerce in aromatics, textiles, spices, and silk, and the ports and caravan stations that handled it, tied Arabia to the wider world, while avoiding a Meccan-centered account that Chapter 35 will treat on its own terms.

Sections 34.4 through 34.9 followed the Yemeni sequence. Himyar unified the south by about 275 and in the late fourth century began to use monotheistic royal language, with Jewish and Christian communities and traditional practices coexisting. The persecution at Najran, conventionally dated 523, and the diplomatic exchanges that followed turned a local conflict into a regional one. Aksum crossed the Red Sea around 525 and installed a Christian order; Abraha then transformed Aksumite command into a semi-independent kingship, documented in inscriptions of 548 and 552; and a Sasanian expedition in the 570s ended Aksumite rule and brought Yemen into the Persian sphere.

Sections 34.10 through 34.13 turned north. Byzantium and Iran dealt with Arab leaders through subsidies, titles, and alliances, but the Jafnids (Ghassanids) and Lakhmids (Nasrids) were not passive buffers. They fought wars of their own, patronized churches and poets, and built centers such as Rusafa and al-Hira. Their religious worlds, Miaphysite in Syria and Church of the East in Mesopotamia, show that “Arab” did not imply one religion, and the Jewish communities of Yemen, Palestine, and Babylonia were also part of the networks.

Sections 34.14 through 34.18 narrated the great war. The peace of 591 created a temporary equilibrium, but the overthrow of Maurice in 602 and Khosrow II’s declared intent to avenge him opened a conflict of unprecedented scale. Persian armies conquered Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt and reached the Bosporus. The fall of Jerusalem in 614 and the capture of Alexandria around 619 marked the high tide. Heraclius reorganized the state, struck into Persian territory, withstood the siege of 626, and defeated the Persians near Nineveh in 627. Khosrow II was overthrown in 628, and over the following two years the peace process restored the earlier frontier.

The chapter emphasized the different classes of evidence throughout. Contemporary inscriptions, martyr narratives, imperial chronicles, and later Arabic traditions answer different questions and have different weaknesses. The Ma’rib inscription of 548 reveals diplomatic networks and Christian kingship, but it does not mention a campaign against Mecca remembered in later tradition. The Syriac letter about Najran shows rapid communication across the frontier, but it is a work of religious advocacy. Accounts of Jerusalem in 614 agree on violence but disagree on scale and on the role of Jewish communities.

Continuity and change were intertwined. Himyar, the Lakhmid monarchy, and the Ghassanid system disappeared or were transformed, and Yemen passed into the Sasanian sphere. Yet networks of trade, pilgrimage, religious scholarship, and diplomacy persisted, and both empires survived. Regional variation mattered: the experience of Najran’s Christians, Aksumite soldiers in Yemen, Arab confederations on the steppe, Jewish communities in Palestine, and Coptic Christians in Egypt differed from one another.

The Historical Debate considered whether the war “exhausted” Byzantium and Iran. The evidence suggests serious strains, especially for Iran, where the political order fractured, and for the Byzantine fiscal and religious systems, but it also shows Byzantine recovery and military capacity. The model of exhaustion explains too little by itself, and the later transformation of the region must be explained by reference to other factors, including the emergence of a new religious and political movement in Arabia.

The chapter concludes Volume II by showing the late antique Middle East at its most developed and most disrupted. Chapter 35 will change scale and ask what Arabia itself was like before Muhammad, and Volume III will follow the emergence of Islam, the early caliphate, and the conquests that transformed the region. Nothing in the world of 630 made those developments inevitable; they must be explained by the evidence of the chapters ahead.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 34 closes Volume II by showing the late antique Middle East as an interconnected system. From the highlands of Yemen to the palaces of Ctesiphon and the walls of Constantinople, kings, bishops, merchants, and soldiers participated in networks that crossed seas and deserts. The chapter’s central lesson is that the history of Arabia cannot be separated from the history of its neighbors, and that the histories of those neighbors cannot be understood without Arabia and the Red Sea.

The chapter also demonstrates the importance of method. Understanding Najran, Abraha, the Ghassanids, or Jerusalem in 614 requires comparing inscriptions, chronicles, martyr stories, poetry, and later tradition, and being honest about what each can and cannot show. That habit of mind will be essential in Volume III, where the sources for the emergence of Islam are in many respects even more complex.

Finally, the chapter has urged resistance to hindsight. The war of 602–628 transformed the region, but it did not make later events inevitable. Both empires survived, communities adapted, and the world of 630 was open to several futures. Chapter 35 now turns inward to Arabia itself, asking what its societies, economies, religions, and politics were like before Muhammad, and Volume III will follow the consequences. The late antique order described here, with its empires, frontier dynasties, churches, and trade routes, forms the setting within which that history began.
