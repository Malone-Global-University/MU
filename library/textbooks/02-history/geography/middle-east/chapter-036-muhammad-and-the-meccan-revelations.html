Part IX — Arabia and the Emergence of Islam

Muhammad and the Meccan Revelations

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

The Qur'an is addressed, again and again, to people who are present and who are answering back. It reports their objections, repeats their questions, and responds to their doubts. In the passages that Muslim tradition and modern scholarship alike classify as Meccan, the audience is a town of traders, herders, clan leaders, dependents, and slaves who are being told that a single God will judge them, that the sacred places they honor must be understood differently, and that their wealth and their obligations to the weak will be weighed. The text does not describe a world from outside. It speaks into one. This chapter asks how that speech began, who first heard it, why it was resisted, and how, over roughly a dozen years, it produced a community that left its home town.

Chapter 35 reconstructed the Arabia into which that speech came: a peninsula of oases, pastoral confederations, caravan towns, and South Arabian kingdoms, drawn into the orbit of two great rival empires, the Byzantine and the Sasanian, and penetrated by Jewish, Christian, and local polytheistic traditions. Mecca, in the Hijaz, was a modest but sacred and commercial settlement, centered on the Kaʿba and governed not by a king but by the negotiated authority of clans belonging to the tribe of Quraysh. The man whose preaching would divide that settlement was a member of one of those clans, the Banū Hāshim, which was respected for its custodial role at the sanctuary but was not among the most powerful.

For Muslims, what began around the year 610 was the direct communication of God's word to Muhammad through revelation, waḥy, and the Qur'an is that word. For a historian, the same events must be approached with the methods of any academic discipline: by asking what sources survive, how early they are, who produced them and why, and what they can and cannot support. This chapter respects the first understanding as the self-understanding of a living religious community and pursues the second as its method. The two are not required to agree on every question, and a textbook does not need to adjudicate between them in order to explain how the earliest Muslim movement emerged and what it meant to those who joined or opposed it.

The difficulty is that the sources for Muhammad's Meccan years are unevenly distributed in time. The Qur'an, whatever one concludes about its ultimate origin, is by far the earliest and most contemporary body of evidence for the preaching itself, though it contains almost no narrative biography and names Muhammad only rarely. The detailed life story, the sīra, together with hadith reports, was committed to writing in the eighth and ninth centuries, a hundred and fifty or more years after the events. Between these bodies of evidence stand important but fragmentary early traditions, a handful of non-Muslim references, and the physical evidence of early Qur'anic manuscripts. A chapter on Muhammad's Meccan life must therefore be at once a narrative and an exercise in source criticism, and it signals clearly which of the two it is doing at each point.

The central story is one of interaction. Revelation, social opposition, kinship politics, and diplomacy with outsiders each shaped the others. A message about one God and the coming judgment was not simply rejected by hostile merchants; it was heard differently by Muhammad's wife Khadija, by his young cousin ʿAlī, by wealthy and respected men such as Abū Bakr, by enslaved people such as Bilāl, and by elders such as Abū Ṭālib, who never accepted it but protected its proclaimer. Protection by kin, the central institution of Arabian social order, determined who could preach safely and who could be harassed with impunity. When that protection weakened, Muhammad and his followers looked beyond their clans, first across the Red Sea to Christian Aksum and then to the oasis of Yathrib, later called Medina.

Equally important is what this chapter does not assume. The Hijra of 622 was not the inevitable outcome of Meccan hostility. It required that a community elsewhere, divided by its own conflicts, be willing to take on obligations of protection toward a stranger, and that Muhammad's followers be willing to leave the only social world they knew. Tradition preserves the memory of how uncertain that process was. Nor was the later shape of Islamic institutions, its law, its caliphate, its sectarian divisions, present in the Meccan period. Those developments belong to later chapters. Chapter 37 takes up the Hijra and the formation of the community in Medina; Chapter 38 follows its leadership crisis after Muhammad's death in 632; Chapter 39 follows the conquests that transformed the Middle East.

The governing question of the chapter is therefore this: how did Muhammad's religious preaching develop into a distinct community within Meccan society, and why did the interaction between revelation, social opposition, kinship politics, and external alliances ultimately lead that community toward migration? The twenty-one sections that follow approach the answer in four stages: Muhammad's early life and the sources for it; the content and chronology of the Meccan revelations; the first believers and the conflict with Quraysh; and the escalating pressures that led to the Hijra.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Qur'an, sīra literature, hadith, later chronicles, and external sources as evidence for Muhammad's Meccan life, and explain the strengths and limits of each.
  2. Describe the social position of Muhammad's clan, the Banū Hāshim, within Quraysh and Meccan society, and explain the role of kinship protection in Arabian politics.
  3. Summarize the traditional accounts of Muhammad's birth, orphanhood, early commercial activity, and marriage to Khadija, while identifying where evidence is thin or legendary.
  4. Explain the traditional account of the first revelations and the approximate chronology of Muhammad's prophetic mission, noting scholarly uncertainty about dates and sequence.
  5. Analyze the Qur'an as a historical source for the Meccan period, including its form, language, rhetorical strategies, and the problems of dating its passages.
  6. Identify the principal themes of Meccan preaching: God's oneness, rejection of idolatry, resurrection and judgment, moral accountability, social ethics, and the narratives of earlier prophets.
  7. Evaluate why different Meccan groups joined, tolerated, or opposed Muhammad, avoiding single-cause explanations and the treatment of Quraysh as a unified bloc.
  8. Describe the forms of social pressure faced by early Muslims and explain how clan protection shaped who was vulnerable.
  9. Assess the significance of the migration to Aksum, the boycott of the Banū Hāshim, the deaths of Khadija and Abū Ṭālib, and the outreach to Ṭāʾif and the tribes.
  10. Explain how contacts with Yathrib and the pledges at al-ʿAqaba made the Hijra possible, and why the Hijra should be understood as a historical development rather than an inevitable result of persecution.
  11. Evaluate competing historiographical approaches to the sources for Muhammad's life.

Historical Context

The late sixth and early seventh centuries were turbulent across the Middle East. Chapter 34 traced how the Byzantine and Sasanian empires, exhausted by decades of rivalry, entered a great war in 602 that would last until 628. Sasanian armies took Antioch, Damascus, and, in 614, Jerusalem, and in the following years occupied Egypt. The Qur'an contains an allusion to a Byzantine defeat and a prediction of recovery, which many interpreters connect to these events, though the precise historical referent has been debated. Whatever the interpretation, the imperial contest was close enough to Arabia to be known there, and its disruption of caravan and maritime routes affected the commercial environment of the Hijaz. Arabian client kingdoms on the desert edges, the Ghassanids allied with Byzantium and the Lakhmids with the Sasanians, had already weakened by the time the war began.

Chapter 35 described the Hijaz itself: a landscape of volcanic fields, wadis, and mountain valleys where pastoralism and oasis agriculture coexisted with caravan commerce along the western route between South Arabia and the Mediterranean. Mecca lay in a dry valley without agriculture, dependent on the well of Zamzam, trade, and the sanctuary around the Kaʿba, where numerous deities and sacred objects were honored and to which pilgrims came at set seasons. Quraysh, the tribe whose clans controlled the town, had secured access to the sanctuary and a reputation for neutrality in a world of feuds. Around Mecca lay nearby markets, such as ʿUkāẓ, and the highland town of Ṭāʾif, associated with the tribe of Thaqīf. Some 400 kilometers to the north lay Yathrib, an agricultural oasis with Jewish and Arab inhabitants whose political order was fractured by recurrent feuding. Across the Red Sea lay the Christian kingdom of Aksum, a major regional power that had in the sixth century intervened in South Arabia.

Religious life in this environment was plural. Polytheistic cults, including those of al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt, coexisted with a recognition of a high god, Allāh, whose name appears in pre-Islamic contexts. Jewish and Christian communities lived in Arabian oases and in Najrān; Christians and Jews of the Roman and Persian worlds had developed rich traditions of scripture, ascetic practice, and apocalyptic expectation. Persons who sought a religion of one God without adopting Judaism or Christianity are sometimes called ḥanīfs in the later sources. The chapter that follows asks how a new prophetic voice emerged within this plural environment, and it is the task of this chapter to show that voice in context rather than as an explanation of everything that followed.

Division I · Section 36.1

Reconstructing Muhammad's Meccan Life: Sources and Historical Problems

No ancient figure is documented in the same manner as a modern one, and Muhammad is no exception, but the problem takes a distinctive form. The Qur'an, the foundation text of Islam, is closest in time to the preaching of Muhammad, yet it is not a biography. It addresses audiences, argues, warns, consoles, and narrates stories of earlier prophets; it names Muhammad only a handful of times and rarely identifies the circumstances to which it responds. Its relationship to the historical Muhammad is the subject of Section 36.6. For events in his life, historians must turn to the biographical and historical literature of Islam, most of which was composed after the Umayyad period had ended and the Abbasids had come to power, as described in Chapters 43 and 44.

The earliest surviving biography, in the form in which it is usually read, is the Sīrat Rasūl Allāh of Ibn Isḥāq (d. c. 767), preserved primarily in the recension of Ibn Hishām (d. 833), though Ibn Isḥāq's original text is itself lost and survives in part through other transmitters. Other major sources include al-Wāqidī (d. 823) on the Medinan campaigns, Ibn Saʿd (d. 845), whose biographical dictionary opens with a life of Muhammad, and al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), whose universal history incorporates earlier material. Alongside these narratives stand the hadith collections, assembled in the ninth century by compilers such as al-Bukhārī and Muslim, which record sayings and actions attributed to Muhammad, each accompanied by a chain of transmitters. These texts are not uniform. Some reports preserve circumstantial detail; others serve devotional, legal, or polemical purposes, including the explanation of Qur'anic verses, the defense of particular families or groups, or the demonstration of Muhammad's prophetic status through miracles.

Some earlier material survives in fragmentary form. A scholar of Medina, ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr (d. c. 713), is reported to have answered inquiries about the early period in letters to the Umayyad caliph, and portions of such material are preserved in later collections. Other early transmitters, such as al-Zuhrī (d. 742), passed on narratives that were eventually incorporated into the major works. Historians differ on how confidently these attributions can be accepted, a problem discussed in the Historical Debate below.

Non-Muslim sources add a limited but valuable perspective. Christian and other writers of the seventh century, including the author of a Greek Jewish-Christian dialogue known as the Doctrina Jacobi (composed c. 634), the Armenian historian Sebeos (writing in the later seventh century), and the Syriac Christian tradition, mention an Arab prophet and the early conquests, though they say little about Mecca. Their usefulness lies in confirming that a prophetic movement was associated with Muhammad's name in the generation after his death, and in providing external perspectives on its early character. Material evidence includes early Qur'an manuscripts, such as the Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest, and fragments on parchment whose radiocarbon dating places the writing within or close to the seventh century, a topic that matters to the assessment of the Qur'an's early transmission but does not resolve the questions about Muhammad's biography.

Historical Vocabulary
Sīra, Hadith, and Isnād

Sīra (plural siyar) means "way" or "conduct" and refers, in this context, to the genre of biographical writing about Muhammad. Hadith (ḥadīth) means a report, typically a short narrative or saying attributed to Muhammad or to a Companion. A hadith consists of a matn (the text) and an isnād, the chain of named transmitters who passed it down. Muslim hadith scholars developed rigorous methods for evaluating isnāds and classifying reports as sound, good, or weak, a development discussed in Chapter 47. Modern historians use these materials as evidence but apply additional critical methods, comparing variant versions, studying how accounts change over time, and testing reports against other kinds of evidence.

For these reasons, Muhammad's Meccan life is reconstructed with graduated confidence. Some matters are widely accepted: that Muhammad was a Meccan from Quraysh, that he preached monotheism and the coming judgment, that a group of followers formed, that opposition developed, and that in 622 he and many followers relocated to Yathrib. Others, such as the exact dates, the sequence of conversions, and the details of encounters with opponents, are less secure. Still others, such as miraculous childhood episodes, are recognized by many historians as devotional elaboration, though they are valuable as evidence for how later communities imagined Muhammad. This chapter indicates, where it matters, whether a statement rests on the Qur'an, on early traditions with strong claims to antiquity, or on later narrative.

Historical Debate

How Much of Muhammad's Early Biography Can Historians Establish Independently of Later Islamic Traditions?

The question: If nearly all narrative detail about Muhammad's life was written down generations after his death, how far can the biographical tradition be trusted as history? Scholars have answered the question in markedly different ways, and the disagreement is not between believers and skeptics only. It also divides non-Muslim historians of the Islamic world.

Interpretation A — The tradition contains substantial historical memory that can be sifted: Some historians, including W. Montgomery Watt in the twentieth century and, more recently, scholars working with the technique of comparing chains of transmission, such as Harald Motzki and Gregor Schoeler, argue that early transmission can often be traced back to the late seventh century and sometimes earlier. They hold that careful source criticism makes it possible to separate earlier layers from later elaboration and to reconstruct a broad outline of Muhammad's life, particularly where independent lines of transmission converge.
Interpretation B — The tradition is deeply shaped by later concerns and must be treated with deep suspicion: Other scholars, associated with the "skeptical" school represented by John Wansbrough, Patricia Crone, and Michael Cook, argued that the biographical tradition reflects the theological, legal, and political needs of the eighth- and ninth-century communities that produced it, and that its apparent detail may conceal literary invention. On this view, the Qur'an and external evidence must carry more weight, and a historical biography in the conventional sense may be impossible.
Interpretation C — A middle position stresses tradition as a source for early community memory: Fred Donner and others have argued that, while individual reports may be unreliable, the sources reflect a broad tradition of early believers whose remembered memories, even when shaped by later concerns, preserved a core understanding of the original movement, particularly its emphasis on monotheism and moral accountability.

Evidence: All approaches use the same material: the Qur'an, the sīra and hadith literature, the early non-Muslim sources, and the early manuscripts. They differ in how much independence and reliability they attribute to the biographical tradition and in the burden of proof they require.

Why it matters: This textbook uses the sources critically and does not treat any single position as definitively settled. Narrative detail from the sīra is presented as "tradition reports" when it cannot be independently confirmed, and the reader is invited to see how the evidence is built.

Student task: Choose one episode narrated in this chapter, such as the first revelation or the migration to Aksum. Identify what kinds of evidence support it, how early they are, and what a skeptical and a more confident historian would each say about its reliability.

Division I · Section 36.2

Mecca, Quraysh, and the Banū Hāshim

Chapter 35 examined Mecca's religious and commercial environment; this section considers it from the vantage point of one family. Quraysh, according to tradition, was a tribe whose ancestor Quṣayy had gathered its scattered groups in the Meccan valley and organized the offices of the sanctuary, such as providing water and food to pilgrims and holding the keys to the Kaʿba. Whatever the historical accuracy of this narrative, by Muhammad's lifetime Quraysh was divided into numerous clans, including Banū Hāshim, Banū ʿAbd Shams, Banū Makhzūm, Banū Zuhra, Banū Taym, Banū ʿAdī, and others. Mecca had no single ruler. Authority was exercised by the heads of clans, who met in an assembly house, the Dār al-Nadwa, and negotiated matters of common concern, including security for caravans and the sanctuary.

Muhammad belonged to the Banū Hāshim, named after Hāshim ibn ʿAbd Manāf, the great-grandfather. In the traditional account, Hāshim had been prominent in organizing trade agreements, and his son ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib held the honorable office of providing water to pilgrims and was credited with rediscovering the Zamzam well. By Muhammad's time, however, other clans, notably the Makhzūm and the ʿAbd Shams, had become wealthier and more influential in commerce and diplomacy. The Banū Hāshim possessed religious prestige and a reputation for honor but modest wealth and military strength relative to the strongest clans. Some modern historians have used this relative position to explain features of Muhammad's later career, though such reconstruction must be tentative.

Social organization in Mecca rested on kinship and on contractual relationships that extended it. A person's safety depended on the willingness of his or her clan to defend and avenge them. Individuals outside the clan could obtain protection through a formal grant of patronage, called jiwār, or become clients (mawālī, singular mawlā) of a patron. Alliances among clans, such as the pact known as the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl, in which members of several clans are said to have agreed to defend wronged persons in Mecca, were arranged to maintain order. Enslaved persons, many of them African or foreign-born, stood outside this network except through the protection of their owners. Women could own property and engage in trade, and their status varied by family and marriage arrangements. These institutions are crucial to understanding the events described later in the chapter, because they define what protection meant and how it could be withdrawn.

Economically, Mecca depended on commerce and on services connected to the sanctuary. Long-distance trade in the sixth century has been described in a classic scholarly account as a highly profitable caravan trade linking South Arabia with Syria. Patricia Crone's 1987 study, Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam, challenged this view, arguing that the evidence for Mecca as a hub of an extensive international spice trade is weak and that Meccan trade was probably more modest and regional, dealing in leather, textiles, and other local goods. Subsequent scholars have disagreed about how far to accept this challenge. For present purposes, it is sufficient to say that Mecca was commercially active, that its leading families engaged in trade, and that the economy of the town was sufficiently developed to produce visible inequalities between wealthy merchants and those dependent on them.

Division I · Section 36.3

Birth, Childhood, Orphanhood, and Early Formation

The sources agree that Muhammad was born in Mecca into the Banū Hāshim, the son of ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and Āmina bint Wahb of the Banū Zuhra. The traditional date is about 570, associated with the "Year of the Elephant," when an Abyssinian-supported ruler of Yemen, Abraha, is said to have marched against Mecca. The Qur'an alludes to a failed expedition of an army with elephants (sūra 105), and the identification of this event with the year of Muhammad's birth is a tradition whose chronology has been questioned. Modern scholars note that the date of the Abyssinian expedition is itself uncertain and that the connection with Muhammad's birth may be a later literary device. A birth in the 570s is, nonetheless, consistent with the traditional claim that he died in 632 at about the age of sixty-two or sixty-three.

Tradition reports that ʿAbd Allāh died before Muhammad's birth or shortly after it, leaving the child fatherless, and that his mother died when he was about six. The Qur'an itself appears to confirm that Muhammad was an orphan, in a passage addressing him as someone whom God found an orphan and sheltered (sūra 93). This is one of the few biographical details directly attested in the Qur'an. Guardianship passed to his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and, after the latter's death, to his uncle Abū Ṭālib, who would remain his protector for decades. Orphanhood in a society based on kin protection implied vulnerability, but the presence of a supportive uncle meant that Muhammad belonged to a respected household. The theme of orphanhood in the Qur'an, where the orphan appears as a person to be protected, may be connected to Muhammad's own experience, though historians caution against reading ethical themes as simple autobiography.

Later traditions add colorful episodes. Muhammad is said to have been entrusted to a wet-nurse, Ḥalīma, of the Banū Saʿd, a tribe of the open desert, a custom among Meccan families. He is said to have accompanied Abū Ṭālib on a trading journey toward Syria, during which a Christian monk, Baḥīrā, recognized signs of prophethood. Narratives of the "opening of the chest" by angels and similar episodes served to establish Muhammad's purity and prophetic destiny. Most modern historians treat such stories as devotional narrative rather than as reliable biography, while recognizing that they reflect the importance of Muhammad in the community's memory. The Baḥīrā story is also notable for the polemical and apologetic uses made of it, by Muslims and by Christian writers, over many centuries.

Other reported episodes of youth, such as participation in the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl and arbitration among clans in the rebuilding of the Kaʿba, when he is said to have settled a dispute over who would place the Black Stone, belong to the category of traditions that portray a trustworthy and mediating character. These reports are plausible in their social context but are difficult to verify. What can be said with greater confidence is that Muhammad grew up in a setting where kinship, honor, and trade were central, that he lacked the protection of a father, and that he came to maturity among a family of modest standing within a society conscious of rank. The next section considers how marriage changed his circumstances.

Division I · Section 36.4

Trade, Khadija, Marriage, and Muhammad's Household

Tradition reports that as a young man Muhammad engaged in commerce, including travel to Syria, and gained a reputation for honesty. It was in this context that he entered the service of Khadija bint Khuwaylid, a woman of the Banū Asad clan of Quraysh. The sources describe her as a wealthy and respected merchant who hired men to conduct caravans on her behalf. Her prior marriages and widowhood are mentioned in traditions, and she is said to have been older than Muhammad, commonly stated as about forty at the time of their marriage, which tradition places around 595 when he was about twenty-five. The precise ages and dates are uncertain and may reflect literary patterning, but the broad outline, a marriage between Muhammad and a woman of independent means and standing, is consistently reported.

The marriage matters historically for several reasons. First, it brought Muhammad economic security, allowing him to withdraw periodically for reflection, a practice associated in tradition with the period before the first revelations. Second, it connected him with a family, the Banū Asad, which included Khadija's cousin Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who is said to have been knowledgeable about Christian scripture. Third, it shows that Meccan women could hold substantial economic and social roles, a point relevant to later developments, including the prominence of women among early believers. Khadija's role as a trusted partner continues throughout the early mission until her death.

The couple's children, according to the sources, included several daughters, Zaynab, Ruqayya, Umm Kulthūm, and Fāṭima, and sons, al-Qāsim and ʿAbd Allāh, who died young. Muhammad's household also came to include Zayd ibn Ḥāritha, a young man who had been captured and enslaved, given to Muhammad by Khadija, and freed and adopted, and ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Muhammad's cousin, who is said to have been taken into his household as a child during a period of hardship for Abū Ṭālib. The household thus encompassed kinship, patronage, adoption, and emancipation. Such arrangements show how family structure in Mecca extended beyond biological descent and how a person like Zayd could become a member of a family through a formal act.

The household also shows the importance of women in the transmission and shaping of the early community. Khadija's support, and later the roles of Muhammad's daughters in marriage alliances, illustrate how family relations connected individuals to wider networks. The later sources celebrate Khadija as the first believer, a claim consistent with her central place in the narrative of the first revelations. Even if details of her biography are shaped by later devotion, the significance of an economically independent and respected woman in the earliest history of Islam is a distinctive feature of the sources, and later chapters will return to the role of women in the first Muslim community.

Division II · Section 36.5

The First Revelations and the Beginning of Muhammad's Prophetic Mission

According to the dominant tradition, when Muhammad was about forty, around 610, he experienced the first revelation while engaged in a practice of seclusion on Mount Ḥirāʾ, a hill a few kilometers from Mecca, during the month of Ramadan. The most widely cited account, preserved in the collection of al-Bukhārī and elsewhere and traced through ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr to Muhammad's wife ʿĀʾisha, describes a period in which he experienced true visions "like the break of dawn," followed by a preference for solitude. In the cave he was approached by an angel, identified in later tradition as Jibrīl (Gabriel), who commanded him to recite. Muhammad answered that he was not one who could recite, and after the command was repeated, the first words of revelation were delivered. The account in the Primary Source Window below compares this tradition with the Qur'anic text commonly identified as the first revelation.

The traditions emphasize the terrifying and overwhelming character of the experience. Muhammad is described as returning to Khadija trembling and asking to be covered. She is portrayed as reassuring him, citing his kindness to kin, support for the weak, hospitality, and truthfulness as reasons why God would not abandon him. She then took him to Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who, in the narrative, recognized the experience as comparable to that of Moses and predicted that Muhammad's people would reject him. These details have generated important discussion. Some scholars find in the account an authentic memory of fear and doubt that a later community would have been unlikely to invent; others note the literary and theological function of Khadija's and Waraqa's recognition scenes. The identification of Waraqa as a Christian authority has also been read as a means of connecting Muhammad's mission to earlier scripture.

Tradition further reports a pause in revelation, the fatra, after the first experience, followed by renewed communication. Another widely cited account, attributed to the Companion Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh, places the descent of sūra 74 after this pause and describes Muhammad seeing the angel suspended between heaven and earth, returning home, and asking to be wrapped in a cloak. The Qur'an's address in that sūra to a person who is covered, commanded to rise and warn, has led some interpreters to treat it as the first revelation. The existence of competing traditions about which passage came first is an example of the uncertainty in the transmitted accounts, and it illustrates how different communities later told the story.

The year 610 should be understood as a convention. It follows from the traditional claim that Muhammad was forty at the time and from reckoning back from his death in 632 and the duration of the Meccan and Medinan periods. The sources also disagree on the relative duration of secret and public preaching. Many accounts speak of about three years of private invitation to family and trusted associates, followed by a command to warn his nearest kin, an allusion associated with a verse in sūra 26, and then public proclamation. Others compress this chronology. What can be said confidently is that the origins of the preaching lay in a series of experiences of revelation, that the message began with the creator God and the duty to give thanks, and that the movement began small and personal.

Two Kinds of Evidence for the Beginning of Revelation: Qur'an 96:1–5 and a Hadith Account

Sources: (1) The Qur'an, sūra 96 (al-ʿAlaq), verses 1–5 · Date: traditionally identified as an early Meccan revelation, c. 610; dating of the text's earliest written form is a separate question · Type: scripture, understood by Muslims as the speech of God. (2) A report on the first revelation in the hadith collection of al-Bukhārī (d. 870), with an isnād through al-Zuhrī and ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr to ʿĀʾisha · Type: transmitted narrative, recorded roughly two centuries after the events.

Context: Sūra 96 is among the passages most often identified by tradition with the first revelation. The hadith account tells how the revelation was received. The two sources therefore do different things: one is the text, the other a story about how the text began.

Paraphrase of Qur'an 96:1–5: The text commands its addressee to recite in the name of his Lord, who created, who created the human being from a clinging substance. It repeats the command to recite and describes the Lord as most generous, the one who taught by the pen and taught the human being what he did not know.
Paraphrase of the hadith account: ʿĀʾisha is reported to have said that revelation began for Muhammad with true dream-visions that came like the dawn, and that he then came to love solitude, spending nights in devotion at Ḥirāʾ. There an angel came to him and told him to recite. He replied that he was not one who recites. The angel pressed him hard, repeated the command twice more, and then delivered the first words. Muhammad returned trembling to Khadija, who reassured him by recalling his goodness to others and took him to Waraqa ibn Nawfal.

Analysis: The Qur'anic passage emphasizes creation, divine generosity, and the pen as the means by which knowledge is transmitted, a notable theme in a society where literacy, though present in Mecca, was a specialized skill. The hadith places the passage within a dramatic personal narrative that includes fear, doubt, and the supportive role of Khadija. Historians note that the narrative is told in the first person through ʿĀʾisha, who was not yet born or was a child at the time, so the account must have originated in Muhammad's own later recollection or in a chain of reporters whose reliability is debated. Scholars also point to the narrative's connection with the story of earlier prophetic callings, which may have shaped its form.

Limitations: The Qur'an gives no biographical frame; the hadith gives a frame but is a later source. Neither can independently confirm the date. The identification of sūra 96 as "first" depends on the hadith itself, and an alternative tradition favors sūra 74. A historian can say that early Muslims remembered the beginning of revelation in these ways, not that either account is an unmediated record.

  2. Why might the narrator of a story about Muhammad's fear and doubt be considered an important point for historians? What counterarguments exist?

Division II · Section 36.6

The Qur'an as Historical Evidence for the Meccan Period

For Muslims, the Qur'an is the word of God, delivered to Muhammad by revelation and recited by him to his followers over about twenty-two years. The word qurʾān itself is connected to the verb meaning to recite, and the text understands itself as recitation: oral proclamation intended to be heard, memorized, and performed in prayer. It is divided into 114 chapters (sūras), each composed of verses (āyas, a word that also means "sign"). Sūras are ordered in the received text roughly from longest to shortest, not chronologically. Muslim tradition and modern scholarship classify sūras, or portions of sūras, as Meccan or Medinan according to when they were believed to have been revealed relative to the Hijra, and the standard Cairo edition of 1924 labels 86 sūras Meccan and 28 Medinan. These labels are themselves interpretations, a point developed in Section 36.7.

As historical evidence, the Qur'an has strengths and limitations. Its great strength is proximity: for those who accept that it originated in Muhammad's preaching, it is a contemporary voice, and even scholars who debate its compositional history generally agree that large parts of it reflect the religious and social environment of seventh-century western Arabia. Because it quotes and answers critics, it provides a window into how the preaching was received. It reveals that opponents asked why the messenger was a human who ate food and walked in markets, why there was no angel accompanying him, why the resurrection was necessary or possible, and whether he was a poet, a soothsayer, or a person possessed. The text also alludes to accusations that someone taught him, an allegation answered by the observation that the supposed teacher spoke a foreign tongue while the recitation was in clear Arabic.

Its limitations are equally important. The Qur'an rarely provides names, dates, or places, and when it alludes to events, such as the battle of Badr or the defeat of the Byzantines, it does so obliquely, relying on an audience that knew the referents. Understanding allusions often depends on exegetical tradition, tafsīr, composed later, which tends to explain verses by linking them to narratives from the sīra and hadith. That creates a circularity: the biography is used to interpret the text, and the text is used to confirm the biography. Historians try to reduce this circularity by reading the text for its internal evidence first and by checking the traditions against it.

The Qur'an's form also shapes how it can be used. Meccan passages are marked by short verses, strong rhythm and rhyme, oaths by natural phenomena, vivid depictions of the Last Day, and direct appeals. They are performative speech, comparable in some respects to late antique homily and hymn, a connection emphasized by scholars such as Angelika Neuwirth, who reads the Meccan sūras as a developing conversation with Jewish and Christian liturgical and exegetical traditions. Other scholars stress the text's engagement with Arabian poetic and oratorical forms. Both observations are compatible: the Qur'anic proclamation spoke in a register intelligible to a late antique Arabian audience, one that already knew poets, soothsayers, and the language of oaths and warnings.

The relationship between the original oral proclamation and the written text is a further question. Muslim tradition holds that Muhammad's followers memorized and wrote down revelations during his lifetime and that a complete written collection was made after his death, a story whose details differ among reports and which is discussed in connection with the caliphate of Abū Bakr and ʿUthmān in later chapters. Early manuscript evidence, including radiocarbon-dated fragments, indicates that a text closely resembling the received Qur'an existed in the seventh century, though the details of its early transmission remain debated. This chapter does not develop a history of the Qur'an's compilation. It treats the text as the earliest available evidence for the content of Meccan preaching, aware that the sequence in which it was delivered cannot be read directly from its present order.

Division II · Section 36.7

The Development and Chronology of the Meccan Revelations

Muslim scholars from early times were interested in the order of revelation and compiled lists of sūras in presumed chronological order. Such lists, which differ in detail, rest on reports about the occasions of revelation (asbāb al-nuzūl) and on internal indications. Nineteenth-century European scholars, notably Gustav Weil and Theodor Nöldeke, whose Geschichte des Qorāns (1860) became the foundation of subsequent work, attempted to systematize this evidence by dividing the Meccan sūras into three periods based on style, vocabulary, and subject matter, and they placed the Medinan sūras in a fourth group. Later scholars such as Régis Blachère, Richard Bell, and others proposed variations on this approach.

In the commonly described scheme, the earliest Meccan sūras are short, intense, and rhythmic. They feature oaths, vivid imagery of the Day of Judgment, warnings about wealth and neglect of the poor, and the praise of the creator. The middle Meccan sūras, in this scheme, are longer, with an increasing emphasis on the proclamation of divine oneness, on prophetic stories, and on the argument with those who rejected the message, and they use the divine name al-Raḥmān, "the Merciful," prominently. The later Meccan sūras are longer still, with extended narratives of earlier prophets, lengthy arguments, and more elaborated legal and ethical formulations, and they show a changing tone as opposition intensified.

This broad pattern remains the starting point for much scholarship, but it has well-known limits. Criteria such as verse length and rhyme do not map cleanly onto time, since a speaker may vary form according to subject. Many sūras appear to contain material of different dates, with verses added or placed in a context different from that of their initial delivery. The distinction between Meccan and Medinan can also mislead when treated as a strict dichotomy; the labels may reflect interpretative judgments rather than certain knowledge. Some recent scholars have therefore emphasized literary and structural analysis of sūras as compositions, examining how internal arrangement reflects a process of editing, while others continue to defend the validity of broad chronological classification.

For historical purposes, the most defensible statement is that the Qur'an preserves evidence of development in the preaching of Muhammad: from short, urgent warnings addressed to a local audience toward longer discourses that draw on narratives of earlier prophets and answer detailed objections. This development should not be pressed into a tidy story of a "simple" early message becoming "complex." Even the shortest passages presuppose a rich religious vocabulary. The classification helps students see the likely contours of change while recognizing that no consensus exists on a precise sequence, and no chronology of individual verses can be treated as certain.

Division II · Section 36.8

Monotheism, Idolatry, and the Challenge to Meccan Religious Authority

At the center of the Meccan message was the proclamation of the oneness of God, tawḥīd, and the rejection of associating other beings with God, shirk. The Qur'an presents God as the creator of the heavens and the earth, the one who sends rain, causes plants to grow, and sustains humankind, and it argues that such a being alone deserves worship. Short sūras such as the one conventionally numbered 112 state God's oneness and indivisibility in compact form, and sūra 109 expresses a formula of separation between the worship of the speaker and that of his hearers. The text insists that other deities, the objects of cult, have no power to benefit or harm, a theme developed through arguments from creation and through satire of beings that cannot hear or answer.

Historical Vocabulary
Tawḥīd, Shirk, and Jāhiliyya

Tawḥīd (literally "making one") is the doctrine of God's oneness and uniqueness. Shirk is "association": ascribing partners or intermediaries to God. The Qur'an and later Islamic thought treat it as the gravest error, though what counted as shirk, and who was accused of it, changed over time. Jāhiliyya is a term used in the Qur'an and later literature for a state of ignorance or heedless behavior, and in Islamic historical writing it came to designate pre-Islamic Arabia. Historians treat the word with caution because it is a retrospective religious judgment, not a neutral description of Arabian society, a point already noted in Chapter 35.

Historians are cautious about the picture of Meccan religion that the Qur'an and later tradition provide. As Chapter 35 showed, Arabian polytheism was not a single system. The Qur'an itself suggests that its audience believed in Allāh as creator and supreme god, but sought the intercession of other beings, whom they regarded as daughters of God or as intermediaries. It names al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt in sūra 53, and criticizes the notion that God would have daughters while men have sons. The target of the preaching was therefore not an absence of belief in God but the structure of intercession and worship around the sanctuary and its cults, which had economic and political dimensions through pilgrimage, offerings, and the privileges of guardians.

Its implications for authority were substantial. To tell the people of Mecca that their ancestors' practices were wrong was to challenge the principle of ancestral authority (sunna of the forefathers), by which communities legitimated custom. The Qur'an records the objection of opponents that they found their fathers following a certain way and would follow in their footsteps. It responds by appealing to reason and to the evidence of divine signs, and to the example of earlier prophets whose peoples had made the same objection. The challenge was also political in a society in which religious prestige and commercial interest were intertwined; a movement denying the efficacy of intermediary cults implied a revaluation of the sanctuary's role, even though the Qur'an does not call for abandoning the Kaʿba and, in later passages, associates it with Abraham.

One episode is relevant to the question of how the preaching treated the Meccan deities: the account, preserved in some early and later sources, of the so-called "Satanic verses," in which Muhammad is reported to have recited words allowing intercession by the three goddesses and then to have withdrawn them. The story appears in works such as Ibn Saʿd and al-Ṭabarī and in some reports connected with the migration to Aksum. Many Muslim scholars rejected its authenticity on theological and technical grounds; some modern historians consider its embarrassing content as a sign that it may preserve an early memory, while others regard it as a later interpretation of a Qur'anic passage. It is mentioned here to illustrate the sensitivity of the topic and the divergence of scholarly views, not as established fact.

Division II · Section 36.9

Resurrection, Judgment, Moral Responsibility, and Social Ethics

The proclamation of the one God was inseparable from a message about accountability. Many Meccan passages speak of a coming hour, the Day of Judgment, on which the cosmos will be transformed and all humans will be raised and judged. They describe the sun folded up, the stars falling, mountains moving, and the books of deeds opened, and they depict paradise and hell with sensory detail. The idea of resurrection was among the most strongly contested aspects of the message; the Qur'an reports skeptical questions about how decayed bones could be raised, and responds that the creator of the first creation can recreate it. This eschatological urgency is a defining characteristic of the early Meccan style.

The doctrine of judgment had direct implications for conduct. The Qur'an condemns those who hoard wealth, count it obsessively, and imagine that it will make them immortal (sūra 104), and those who do not urge the feeding of the needy or who neglect the orphan (sūras 89 and 107). It addresses prayer, charitable giving, and honesty in trade, and criticizes cheating in weights and measures. A passage concerning the burial of infant girls, evoked as a question on the Day of Judgment about the crime for which a girl was killed (sūra 81), has been taken as evidence for such practices in pre-Islamic Arabia, though scholars debate how widespread they were and how the verse should be read. These themes reflect a moral vision in which wealth and status were not signs of divine favor but trusts subject to obligation.

Historians have long debated how far such ethical teachings reflected a social crisis in Mecca. W. Montgomery Watt, in Muhammad at Mecca (1953), argued that the growth of commerce had loosened tribal solidarity, leaving orphans, the poor, and other vulnerable persons without protection, and that Muhammad's message responded to this dislocation. Later scholars have questioned this picture, noting that Mecca's commercial development may have been more modest than Watt assumed and that religious concerns, including the fear of judgment, can be understood in their own terms. A balanced view recognizes that the Qur'an's social ethics addressed real inequalities in a stratified society, without treating the message as merely a response to economic change.

The Meccan ethic is distinctive in combining individual responsibility with social obligation. Persons are accountable for their deeds, and no one will bear another's burden. At the same time, the text calls for generosity toward relatives, orphans, the needy, and travelers, redirecting the logic of honor and reciprocity that governed social life. Women appear in the Meccan text as moral agents, addressed along with men as believers who will be judged by their deeds. These themes provided a basis for solidarity among followers across clan lines, anticipating the formation of a community of believers whose primary loyalty was to a shared faith. That development created tension with the kin-based order and is part of the background to the conflict described in Division III.

Division II · Section 36.10

Earlier Prophets, Sacred Narratives, and Late Antique Religious Traditions

The Meccan Qur'an places Muhammad within a lineage of messengers sent to earlier peoples. It narrates, often in compact and allusive form, stories of Noah and the flood, Abraham, Lot, Joseph, Moses and Pharaoh, and Jesus, as well as figures with local or Arabian associations, such as Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and Shuʿayb, sent to peoples named ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Madyan. The structure of these narratives is often paradigmatic: a prophet warns his people to worship God alone, they reject him and call him a liar, and punishment follows. The stories function as warnings to the Meccan audience and as reassurance to Muhammad that earlier prophets had suffered similar rejection.

The Qur'an's relationship to Jewish and Christian traditions has been a subject of discussion since the earliest period. The text assumes familiarity with biblical figures and narratives, and sometimes retells them with variations or emphases that differ from the Hebrew Bible and the Gospels, in ways that parallel extra-biblical Jewish and Christian traditions known in Syriac and other late antique literature. Scholars debate the channels of transmission: oral exchange with Jews and Christians in Arabia, the influence of Syriac Christian homilies and liturgy, or the circulation of stories in Arabic. Muslim tradition holds that the Qur'an corrects distortions in earlier scriptures and restores the original message of the prophets. A historian can observe both that the Qur'an stands within the wider world of late antique monotheistic discourse and that it speaks with a distinctive voice.

Abraham plays a prominent role. In the Meccan passages he appears as a model of monotheism who rejects the idols of his people and submits to God, and in later passages he is associated with the foundation of the Kaʿba and the pilgrimage, connecting the Meccan sanctuary to a monotheistic past. Moses is the most frequently mentioned prophet, whose confrontation with Pharaoh provided a template for Muhammad's own experience. Jesus and Mary appear with reverence, in accounts that affirm the virgin birth and Jesus' status as messiah and messenger while rejecting his divinity, a position which places the Qur'anic message in implicit dialogue with the Christological debates discussed in Chapter 33.

The Meccan preaching also reflects the broader religious atmosphere of the late antique Near East: expectation of the end of time, concern with judgment, ascetic practice, vigil prayer, and the authority of scripture. The seventh-century Byzantine-Sasanian war, with its apocalyptic resonance among Jews and Christians, formed the backdrop of this atmosphere. Poets and soothsayers (kāhins) were known in Arabia, and Qur'anic rejections of the charge that Muhammad was one of them indicate the cultural categories available to hearers. The Qur'an describes Muhammad as ummī, a term interpreted by many Muslims as "unlettered" and by some modern scholars as referring to a person from a people without a previous scripture; the sense of the term remains debated. In any case, the Meccan text presented itself as an Arabic recitation (qurʾān ʿarabī) addressed to a people who had not previously had a messenger of their own.

Division III · Section 36.11

The First Followers and the Formation of the Meccan Community

According to tradition, the first person to accept Muhammad's message was Khadija, followed by his young cousin ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, his freedman and adopted son Zayd ibn Ḥāritha, and a respected merchant of the Banū Taym, Abū Bakr ibn Abī Quḥāfa. Different groups in later Islamic history gave priority to different figures as "first" believer, according to their theological and political commitments, so the order must be handled with caution. Through Abū Bakr's influence, according to tradition, several prominent young Qurayshīs joined, including ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān of the Banū Umayya, al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, and Ṭalḥa ibn ʿUbayd Allāh. The group thus included members of several clans, evidence that the movement did not originate within a single kin group.

The early community also included persons of low status, and sources name enslaved men and women and clients without strong clan protection: Bilāl, an Ethiopian slave of the Banū Jumaḥ; ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir and his parents Yāsir and Sumayya, clients of the Banū Makhzūm; Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt, a craftsman; Ṣuhayb; and others. Women appear among the early believers, including Fāṭima bint al-Khaṭṭāb and Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr. Later sources also describe the conversion of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, of the Banū ʿAdī, as a significant moment, after which the believers are said to have felt more confident in public worship; the accounts of his conversion are narratively elaborate and differ in details, so their historical core is uncertain.

This composition is historically revealing. The movement did not appeal only to the poor and powerless, as some older interpretations suggested, nor was it limited to the elite. It included young men from established families, merchants, a woman of independent means, freedmen, slaves, and clients. The mixture is consistent with a message that spoke to moral and religious concerns across social lines. The conversion sequence reported in the sources is shaped by later memory and by the status of individuals in subsequent Islamic history, so that those who became caliphs or prominent Companions (aṣḥāb) are given conspicuous places. Historians therefore treat the list of "early converts" as a valuable but interpreted source.

Numbers are uncertain. Traditions variously report about forty converts after several years, a figure that may reflect a conventional pattern, and a few dozen to over a hundred by the time of the Aksum migrations and the later Hijra. It is generally accepted that the movement remained small in Mecca, a minority among a population of several thousand. Its significance lay less in size than in its intensity and its capacity to form bonds that cut across clan and status lines.

Division III · Section 36.12

Worship, Religious Commitment, and Community Identity

What did it mean to belong to the community in Mecca? The Meccan Qur'an gives indications of practice without describing a fully developed ritual system. It commands prayer (ṣalāt), including nighttime vigil, prostration, and recitation of the Qur'an. A sūra addressed to a person wrapped in garments instructs him to rise for much of the night and recite with measured care, a passage often read as evidence for the importance of nocturnal prayer and recitation in the earliest community. Other passages call for charity (zakāt, in the sense of purifying giving), remembrance of God, patience, and steadfastness in the face of mockery. The believers are described as those who bow, prostrate, and give to the needy.

The elaborate system of five daily prayers, ablutions, fasting during Ramadan, the pilgrimage, and the legal rules of Islamic practice developed later, in part during the Medinan period and in part through the consolidation of juristic traditions discussed in Chapter 47. The tradition places the commanded five daily prayers in connection with the Night Journey and Ascension, near the end of the Meccan period, but earlier reports mention two daily prayers or other arrangements. The chapter therefore distinguishes the Meccan practices which the text attests from later institutional forms. This distinction is crucial for avoiding the projection of later Islam onto the earliest community.

Tradition recounts that the believers at first met discreetly, praying in the ravines around Mecca, and gathered at the house of al-Arqam ibn Abī al-Arqam near the hill of al-Ṣafā, where Muhammad taught and recited. The reality may have been more varied. Recitation was likely central: believers memorized and repeated passages, so that the Qur'an became a shared possession. The text also reinforced identity by describing believers as a distinct group with solidarity, the muʾminūn (those who have faith) and, in the Meccan texts, by an ethic that cultivated moral discipline. Such practices created a community defined by belief rather than by descent, an unusual form of social identity in the Arabian context.

At the same time, nothing in the Meccan evidence suggests an organized institution with offices, a treasury, or governance. Muhammad served as a preacher, and the believers remained dispersed in their own clans and households, with the practical authority of clan heads over their members intact. The community was a movement of conviction within a society whose structures it did not replace. Only in Medina, as the next chapter explains, would the community acquire the political and legal capacities of a polity.

Division III · Section 36.13

Why Quraysh Opposed Muhammad's Preaching

The Qur'an and the sīra both testify to opposition, but the explanation of its causes requires care. Four categories of motives are discernible in the sources, and they frequently overlapped. The first was religious: the message denied the efficacy of the deities to whom many Meccans offered worship and insisted on a resurrection that many found implausible. The second was the authority of tradition: to reject the practices of ancestors was to insult them and to destabilize custom. The third concerned status and leadership: acceptance of Muhammad's claim to prophetic authority implied acknowledgment of a man from a modest clan as a figure of unparalleled importance. The fourth was economic and political: the sanctuary and its cults were sources of prestige and income, and leaders feared that conflict would damage Mecca's reputation for neutrality and its relations with the tribes that came to its markets and pilgrimages.

The sources name individuals with distinctive stances. Abū Lahab, an uncle of Muhammad and a member of the Banū Hāshim, is portrayed as an implacable enemy, and the Qur'an, in sūra 111, condemns him and his wife. Abū Jahl (ʿAmr ibn Hishām) of the Banū Makhzūm is depicted as one of the fiercest opponents; his clan was powerful, wealthy, and rivalrous with the Hāshimites, and tradition stresses long-standing competition between the two. al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra, also of Makhzūm, was among the elders whose opinions carried weight. Abū Sufyān of the Banū ʿAbd Shams, whose family would later play a central role in the Umayyad caliphate, appears in the sources as a leader of opposition in later years, though his earlier role is less clear. Others, such as ʿUtba ibn Rabīʿa, are shown as less hostile at times, and some leaders are depicted as hesitant or as willing to negotiate.

Reports of negotiations illustrate that opposition was not monolithic. Sources describe offers by Qurayshī leaders to give Muhammad wealth, leadership, or medical treatment if he abandoned preaching, and counter-offers to worship each other's gods in alternation. Such stories are literary in form, but their presence suggests a memory that elites tried both inducement and compromise. They also reveal that many Meccans saw the matter as a dispute within the town that could be resolved by arrangement, and that the more radical position, a demand to abandon the message entirely, was rejected.

Historians have been wary of reducing the opposition to a single cause. It is tempting to read the conflict as a clash between a commercial elite and a social reformer, but the Qur'an addresses religious claims as much as economic ones, and among the believers were wealthy merchants. Conversely, some opponents were motivated by sincere religious conservatism, and others by rivalries among clans or by personal considerations. The Meccan response also depended on whether Muhammad's own clan, Banū Hāshim, was prepared to protect him, a matter to which the chapter now turns.

Division III · Section 36.14

Persecution, Social Pressure, and the Limits of Clan Protection

Opposition in the sources takes a range of forms. Muhammad is described as being mocked, called a poet or a madman, and subjected to harassment, including reports that refuse to be dismissed as mere propaganda. Yet the sources emphasize that he was not killed or expelled in the early period. The reason was kinship. As a member of Banū Hāshim, whose head Abū Ṭālib supported him, Muhammad had the protection of his clan, and an attack on him would have risked blood vengeance and a damaging feud. The tradition explicitly emphasizes that Abū Ṭālib, although he did not accept Muhammad's message, defended his kinsman out of loyalty and honor. This protective role is among the best illustrations of how Meccan social order shaped the course of the movement.

The experience of other believers differed according to their status. Those from strong clans might be pressured by their families, confined, or beaten by relatives, and some are said to have been compelled to recant. The most severe treatment fell on those without clan protection: slaves and clients. Bilāl is reported to have been tortured by his master Umayya ibn Khalaf, who is said to have exposed him to the heat of the desert with a stone on his chest until Abū Bakr purchased and freed him. ʿAmmār's mother Sumayya is traditionally regarded as one of the first martyrs of Islam, killed by Abū Jahl. Khabbāb is reported to have been burned. These narratives circulated widely, and while individual details may have been elaborated, the general pattern of violence against the unprotected is consistent with the structure of Meccan society and with the Qur'an's own references to believers being tried.

Social pressure took nonviolent forms as well: mockery, exclusion, economic coercion, and attempts to induce believers to leave. Believers faced difficulty in marriage and trade where relatives or business partners were hostile. Abū Bakr's reported practice of buying and freeing enslaved believers, if accurate, shows how wealthier members could redistribute protection within the community and reflects the use of wealth to defend the vulnerable. At the same time, the conflict also revealed the limits of the kin system: as a movement defined by belief, the community had members whose clan loyalties conflicted with their religious commitments, and clan heads had to decide whether to protect or discipline them.

Historians distinguish the well-attested pattern from the details of individual stories. The vulnerability of slaves and clients, the protection of Hāshimites by clan loyalty, and the rising pressure over time are broadly consistent across sources. Specific episodes, such as the reported attempts to harm Muhammad or the dramatic confrontations at the Kaʿba, appear in different versions and occasionally conflict. It is therefore most accurate to speak of a period of increasing harassment of uneven severity, tied to status and protection, rather than a uniform persecution of all believers.

Division III · Section 36.15

The Migration to Aksum and the Search for Refuge

According to tradition, in the fifth year of the mission, conventionally around 614 or 615, Muhammad advised a group of believers to seek refuge in Aksum, whose ruler, the Negus (Ethiopian negus, king), was described as just and not oppressing those under his protection. The first group is typically reported as about a dozen men and several women, including ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān and his wife Ruqayya, Muhammad's daughter. A second, larger migration followed, led by Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, bringing the total to perhaps eighty or more people, according to some accounts. The emigrants sailed from the Red Sea coast, probably through the port of Shuʿayba near Mecca, to the Aksumite territory.

Aksum was a natural destination. It was a Christian kingdom, converted in the fourth century, with a large navy, an extensive trade network, and a record of intervention in South Arabia, including the conquest of Himyar in the sixth century (Chapter 34). It had commercial ties with the Hijaz, and its reputation as a place where outsiders might find security was plausibly known to Meccans. The choice shows that the believers did not view themselves as separate from the wider world of monotheism, and that they knew where a powerful Christian ruler might extend protection. It also indicates that Muhammad's clan network did not extend protection over all the believers, so that departure was a practical solution for those who lacked protectors.

The traditional narrative continues with a Qurayshī delegation, including ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, who traveled to the Negus with gifts to demand that the emigrants be returned. The Negus is said to have questioned the Muslims, and Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib spoke on their behalf, describing their former way of life, the coming of the prophet, and their persecution, and recited a portion of the Qur'an relating to Mary. The Negus, moved, is reported to have refused to surrender them. This story has a strong literary shape and appears in several versions. Its general claim, that the Muslims were received and protected in Aksum, is widely accepted, but details of the audience, the role of ʿAmr (who converted later), and the relation of the Negus to the believers' doctrine of Jesus remain disputed. Some scholars have also asked whether the Aksum migration reflected economic and commercial motives as well as flight from persecution.

Tradition says that some of the emigrants returned to Mecca after hearing a report that Quraysh had accepted Muhammad's message, a report sometimes connected with the "Satanic verses" episode discussed in Section 36.8, and found conditions worse; some then went back to Aksum or to Medina later. Many remained in Aksum until after the Hijra, rejoining Muhammad in the 620s. The episode shows that the believers sought refuge outside the Arabian clan system and that connections extended across the Red Sea. It also demonstrates that the movement could not rely solely on Meccan structures of protection. The significance of Aksum for the later Muslim memory is also notable: the Negus is remembered with respect in Islamic tradition, and the episode is often cited as evidence of early positive relations between Muslims and Christians.

Multiple Perspectives

The Conflict in Mecca: Perspectives from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives drawing on patterns in the Qur'an and the later sources. They are not quotations from any named person and should not be read as certain accounts of what individuals thought.

From the perspective of a well-connected early follower such as a young Qurayshī merchant: Faith might bring conflict with his own family and with the clan elders on whom his trade depended, yet his kin ties meant he was seldom in physical danger. His challenge was to hold his commitment against the expectations of the family, and his resources allowed him to assist others.
From the perspective of an enslaved or client convert such as Bilāl or Sumayya: Acceptance of the message offered a dignity and moral equality not available in Meccan society, but it exposed the believer to direct coercion by an owner or patron who had no kin obligation to protect him or her. Freedom, if it came, depended on the generosity of others.
From the perspective of a leader of a powerful clan such as Makhzūm or ʿAbd Shams: The preaching threatened ancestral custom, the sanctuary's prestige, and the delicate balance among clans on which commerce and security depended. Some leaders saw a dangerous disturbance, others a tolerable dispute, and others a rival clan's attempt to gain advantage.
From the perspective of a non-Muslim protector such as Abū Ṭālib: Honor required defending a kinsman, even without sharing his beliefs, and clan solidarity limited his options. The cost was a growing tension with other clans and the possibility of a break within Quraysh.
From the perspective of a Christian authority in Aksum: The arrival of a small group of monotheists seeking refuge was an opportunity to assert royal protection and perhaps to engage a new Arabian religious movement from a position of strength. Sources preserve little of this viewpoint directly, and the accounts we have come mainly from Muslim tradition.
Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Work, and Belief in Meccan Society

Daily life in Mecca revolved around the household, the market, and the sanctuary. Water was drawn from wells, above all Zamzam; food relied on dates, grain, milk, and meat, much of it brought from surrounding oases and pastoral groups; houses were of stone and mud brick in a rocky valley. Men and women engaged in trade and crafts, and some worked as herders in the hills. Enslaved persons performed domestic and manual labor. Marriage alliances connected families, and the household of Khadija and Muhammad illustrates how kin, clients, and freed persons lived together.

For ordinary believers, joining the movement meant adding commitments to this routine: night prayer, recitation, giving to the needy, and the risk of social friction. The sources, written largely by male scholars of later centuries, give more detail about prominent men than about women, children, and the poor, whose experience must be inferred from scattered references. The Qur'an itself, addressed to both men and women, and the reports on women such as Khadija, Sumayya, and Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr, are valuable for reconstructing the participation of women, but the reconstruction remains partial.

Division IV · Section 36.16

The Boycott of Banū Hāshim and Meccan Clan Politics

The sources describe a collective measure taken by leading Qurayshī clans against the Banū Hāshim and their close allies, the Banū al-Muṭṭalib, in response to their continued protection of Muhammad. According to tradition, a written document, kept in the Kaʿba, bound the signatories not to marry with, trade with, or otherwise deal with the protected clans until they surrendered Muhammad. The Hāshimites and Muṭṭalibids, including both Muslims and non-Muslims, are reported to have withdrawn to a quarter or ravine associated with Abū Ṭālib (shiʿb Abī Ṭālib) and to have endured shortages and hardship for about two to three years, dated in the traditional chronology to roughly 616–619. Abū Lahab is said to have broken with his clan and sided with the opposing group.

The boycott is historically important for what it reveals about Meccan politics. It shows that kin protection was a collective commitment, since the clans who protected Muhammad were threatened with isolation, and that opponents sought to use social and economic pressure rather than direct violence, which would have caused blood feud. It also shows division within Quraysh: the story describes members of other clans, such as Hishām ibn ʿAmr and Muṭʿim ibn ʿAdī, who were uneasy with the severity of the measure and who are said to have worked to annul it. In the dramatic conclusion of the tradition, the document was found to have been eaten by termites except for the name of God, a miracle story that shows how the episode was embedded in devotional narrative.

Modern historians differ about the boycott. Some accept it as a real episode with exaggerated details; others suspect that the story reflects later elaboration, perhaps modeled on narratives of persecution of earlier prophets or on later political conflicts among clans. Evidence in the Qur'an for a boycott is indirect at most. Questions persist about its duration, the extent of the hardship, and whether trade restrictions could have been enforced in a town in which Hāshimites had many relatives among other clans. Without independent confirmation, the safest conclusion is that some form of organized pressure on the protective clans is plausible and consistent with the later fragility of Muhammad's position, while the specific narrative cannot be treated as settled.

Division IV · Section 36.17

The Deaths of Khadija and Abū Ṭālib and the Crisis of Protection

Within a short span, according to tradition around 619 and in the tenth year of the mission, Muhammad lost both his wife Khadija and his uncle Abū Ṭālib. Later writers called this period the "Year of Sorrow" (ʿām al-ḥuzn). The label is traditional rather than contemporary, but it expresses the perceived significance of the losses. Khadija had been his emotional and economic mainstay, and, in the sources, the first to believe him. Abū Ṭālib had been the political guarantor of his safety.

The death of Abū Ṭālib altered the balance of protection. The new head of the Banū Hāshim was Abū Lahab, who, according to the tradition, was hostile to Muhammad and withdrew or restricted his clan's protection. Reports describe an increase in harassment, including incidents in which Muhammad was insulted or physically attacked in the streets. After Khadija's death, he is said to have married Sawda bint Zamʿa, a widow among the believers, and to have been betrothed to ʿĀʾisha, the daughter of Abū Bakr, an arrangement that tied him more closely to his most important Qurayshī supporter. Marriage alliances in this period functioned as ways to strengthen bonds.

The episode demonstrates the dependence of the movement on kin-based protection, but it also shows how new forms of solidarity were beginning to matter. Muhammad's security no longer rested only on his birth clan but on relations of loyalty with individual believers and, eventually, with groups outside Mecca. Historians generally accept the sequence, that the loss of his protectors was followed by renewed efforts to find a secure base, while observing that the dating and the character of Abū Lahab's role rest on later narrative. In the surviving accounts, the pivot from clan protection to external alliance begins here.

Division IV · Section 36.18

Ṭāʾif, Tribal Diplomacy, and the Search for New Support

Tradition reports that Muhammad, with Zayd ibn Ḥāritha, traveled to Ṭāʾif, a cool, fertile highland town some seventy kilometers southeast of Mecca, dominated by the tribe of Thaqīf and linked to Mecca by commercial and social ties. His purpose, according to the sources, was to seek protection and a hearing among its leaders. The account says that the Thaqīf leaders rejected him, with some ridicule, and set the town's rabble and slaves on him, so that he was pelted with stones and sought refuge in an orchard. The story, often cited as an example of his endurance, shows him praying to God to take notice of his weakness. On his return, since he could not re-enter Mecca without a protector, he is said to have sought the guarantee of Muṭʿim ibn ʿAdī of the Banū Nawfal, who gave it, an instance of a non-Muslim leader honoring the institution of jiwār.

The Ṭāʾif visit reveals several features of the situation. Muhammad understood that his future might depend on finding a community outside Mecca willing to shelter him; he approached a town with ties to Mecca that might be sympathetic to a challenge to Meccan hegemony, and he failed. The episode also shows that protection could be sought from individuals outside one's kin, in a formal sense. Some scholars treat the details with caution, since the story has dramatic and devotional features, but the sources agree on the broad outlines of the journey and its rejection.

Tradition also reports that during the pilgrimage and market seasons, when tribes gathered around Mecca at fairs such as ʿUkāẓ, Majanna, and Dhū al-Majāz, Muhammad approached tribal leaders, presented his message, and asked for protection. Groups named in the sources include Banū ʿĀmir ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa, Kinda, Kalb, Banū Ḥanīfa, and Banū Shaybān, and the replies are portrayed as refusals or as conditional offers that Muhammad could not accept, such as a demand for succession to leadership after his death. Abū Lahab is said to have followed him and discouraged the tribes. Whether or not each encounter occurred as reported, the narrative describes a sustained effort at diplomacy, in which Muhammad treated the Arabian tribal world as a field of possible alliances.

Division IV · Section 36.19

The Night Journey and Ascension in Islamic Tradition

The Qur'an contains an allusion, at the beginning of sūra 17, to a journey in which God took his servant by night from the sacred place of worship to a farther place of worship, whose surroundings were blessed, to show him some of His signs. The text does not name the servant, the destination, or the date. In the tradition, the journey is called the isrāʾ, and it is closely associated with the miʿrāj, an ascent through the heavens. Sūra 53 includes a vision of a being near a lote tree at the farthest boundary, and some interpreters connect it with the Ascension, while others connect it with earlier visionary experiences.

Hadith and sīra narratives greatly elaborate on these allusions. They describe Muhammad being carried from Mecca to Jerusalem on a creature called al-Burāq, leading prophets in prayer, and then ascending through the seven heavens, meeting earlier prophets, and receiving the command to pray fifty times daily, reduced by negotiation to five. Different reports disagree on whether the journey was physical or spiritual, whether it occurred in one night, and when it happened, with suggestions ranging across the later Meccan period. The interpretation of the "farther place of worship" as the sanctuary in Jerusalem became firmly established, and it connected Muhammad's story to the city sacred to Jews and Christians and later to Islamic devotion.

For historical analysis, it is important to separate three levels. The Qur'anic text is brief and allusive. The narrative expansions are products of the following centuries, shaped by devotional, theological, and literary concerns, and they became a rich tradition of Islamic piety and, later, mystical literature. And questions about the original event, its date, and whether it was a physical or visionary experience are matters of faith and interpretation, which a historian can describe but not resolve. The episode is significant for this chapter because, in the dominant tradition, it comes late in the Meccan period and links Muhammad's prophetic experience to the wider prophetic history and the sacred geography of the Near East, though the chronology is uncertain.

Division IV · Section 36.20

The People of Yathrib and the Pledges at al-ʿAqaba

Yathrib, about 350 kilometers north of Mecca, was an agricultural oasis made up of dispersed settlements, fortified compounds, and date palm groves. Its population, according to the sources, included Arab groups, notably the Aws and Khazraj, and several Jewish clans, among them Qaynuqāʿ, al-Naḍīr, and Qurayẓa. The Aws and Khazraj, who by tradition had come to the oasis after earlier inhabitants, were locked in cycles of feuding. The sources describe a major conflict, the battle of Buʿāth, around 617 or 618, after which both groups were exhausted and without a unifying leader. Some modern scholars consider the picture of a society seeking a neutral arbitrator to be plausible, while recognizing that it may be partly retrospective and that details about the Jewish clans' relationships are contested. The oasis was thus open to a new form of arbitration and leadership, and its inhabitants had some familiarity with monotheism through their Jewish neighbors.

The Meccan market and pilgrimage gave Yathribīs contact with Muhammad. Tradition reports that around 620, a small group of Khazrajīs met him and accepted his message, and in the following year, a larger delegation, twelve men according to the sources, met him at al-ʿAqaba, near Minā, and pledged allegiance. The account says that this first pledge involved a commitment to worship God alone, avoid theft, adultery, killing children, and slander, and obey in what is right, a text resembling the pledge Qur'an 60:12 prescribes for women. Muhammad then sent Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr to teach the Qur'an and the faith in Yathrib, where, with local supporters such as Asʿad ibn Zurāra, he is reported to have won many converts across clans.

Historical Vocabulary
Bayʿa, Hijra, and Naqīb

A bayʿa is a pledge of allegiance, literally linked to the clasping of hands in agreement; in later Islamic history it became the formal act by which a ruler was recognized. The pledges at al-ʿAqaba are the earliest prominent examples in the tradition. Hijra means "migration" or "breaking of ties"; it refers not only to the movement from Mecca to Yathrib but, in Arabian social terms, to the severing of kin attachments, a significant act in a kin-based society. A naqīb is a representative or chief of a group; the sources report that twelve naqībs were appointed at al-ʿAqaba to represent the Yathribī clans.

In the following pilgrimage season, according to tradition around 622, a larger group, reported as seventy-three men and two women, came from Yathrib and met Muhammad secretly at night at al-ʿAqaba. His uncle al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, who had not yet become a Muslim in the traditional account, is said to have attended and ensured that the Yathribīs understood the risk they were taking. The sources report that the Yathribīs pledged to protect Muhammad as they protected their own families, and the exchange raised the question of what would happen if their commitment led to war. They agreed, and twelve representatives were chosen from the Aws and Khazraj. Some traditions call this the "pledge of war," though that name may reflect later understanding of its consequences, and the text of the pledge differs among reports.

The pledges were significant because they transformed the question of refuge. The Yathribīs were not simply accepting a preacher; they were assuming obligations of protection comparable to kin ties, a new form of political alliance founded on shared belief and a mutual pledge. This is why the Hijra should be seen as the result of negotiation. It depended on the willingness of the Yathribīs to take a risk and on Muhammad's reading of their situation, rather than merely on Meccan hostility. The terms of the pledge also reveal that its participants anticipated the possibility of conflict with Mecca, which Chapter 37 will examine.

Division IV · Section 36.21

The Decision to Migrate and the End of the Meccan Period

Following the second pledge, according to the sources, Muhammad instructed his followers to begin migrating to Yathrib, a few at a time, so as to avoid attracting attention. Many of them left their homes and property, and some are said to have been prevented by family members or to have lost wealth in the process. The believers who traveled included the emigrants (muhājirūn) who would be joined in Medina by the local supporters (anṣār), a distinction fundamental to the next chapter. Muhammad himself remained in Mecca with Abū Bakr and ʿAlī, with a few others, until close to the end.

The traditions describe a Qurayshī council at the Dār al-Nadwa in which leaders, alarmed by the emigration and the alliance with Yathrib, considered how to deal with Muhammad, including imprisonment, expulsion, or killing him by a collective act involving members of all clans, so that the Banū Hāshim could not take revenge against a single clan. The story is told in dramatic form and includes a Qur'anic reference to plots against him, and it is typical of narrative that gives a unified conspiratorial agency to Quraysh. Historians view the story with caution while accepting that Meccan leaders had reason to fear the movement's alliance with Yathrib, a town on their northern trade routes.

Muhammad and Abū Bakr are then reported to have left Mecca secretly and taken shelter in a cave on Mount Thawr to the south, avoiding pursuit before heading toward Yathrib by an indirect route, with ʿAlī said to have remained in Muhammad's bed to mislead observers. The Qur'an refers to two men in a cave, understood in tradition as an allusion to this episode. The account of arrival and events in Yathrib is reserved for Chapter 37. In 638, according to Islamic tradition, the caliph ʿUmar established the Hijra as the starting point of the Islamic calendar; its first day, 1 Muharram, corresponds to 16 July 622 CE.

Why did migration occur? A summary of the chapter's argument is appropriate. Religious claims challenged the cult and authority of Mecca. The message created a community defined by belief, which cut across clan lines and exposed members to unequal protection. The opposition of powerful clans, the boycott, and the loss of Abū Ṭālib weakened Muhammad's protection. Attempts to find a base in Ṭāʾif and among the tribes failed until Yathrib, torn by conflict and in need of arbitration, offered a political opening. Even then, the outcome was not inevitable: it depended on pledges, on individual choices, and on the willingness of two communities to risk a dangerous change. The Hijra marks the end of the Meccan period and the beginning of a new kind of community, but it was made possible by what had come before.

Cause and Effect

Toward the Hijra: How Revelation, Opposition, Protection, and Diplomacy Interacted

Long-term causes

A plural religious world of Arabian cults and Jewish and Christian monotheism; a kin-based order in which protection depended on clan loyalty; a Mecca whose sanctuary, commerce, and prestige were intertwined; a Yathrib weakened by feud.

Short-term causes

The proclamation of God's oneness and the judgment; the formation of a community of belief across clans; opposition by powerful clans; the boycott; the loss of Khadija and Abū Ṭālib; failed approaches to Ṭāʾif and the tribes.

Immediate effects

Refuge in Aksum for some believers; the pledges at al-ʿAqaba; the departure of most Meccan Muslims to Yathrib and the end of the Meccan period.

Long-term consequences

A community defined by belief acquires a political base; the later Islamic calendar begins with the Hijra; the new alliance leads to the conflicts and institutions examined in Chapter 37 and beyond.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the Hijra. Hostility alone would not have produced it without a place willing to receive the movement; Yathrib's conflicts alone would not have produced it without a message and a leader around whom people could unite.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Message and Its World

What Continued

  • Belief in Allāh as creator, already known in Arabia
  • The importance of the Kaʿba and pilgrimage to Meccan life, though reinterpreted
  • Kin protection and clan politics as organizing institutions
  • Participation in late antique themes: prophets, scripture, judgment, vigil prayer, and apocalyptic expectation

What Changed or Was Challenged

  • Exclusive worship of one God and rejection of intercessory cults
  • A community defined by belief that cut across kin and status lines
  • Moral accountability for wealth and treatment of the vulnerable
  • The beginning of a break from ancestral authority as a source of legitimacy

Why the pattern matters: The Meccan movement was neither a simple break with its environment nor a mere continuation of it. Reading it as both rooted in and transformative of its world helps avoid treating Islam as either inevitable or unintelligible.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the emergence of Muhammad's preaching in Mecca and the pressures that led his followers to leave it. It began with the sources: the Qur'an as the earliest and most contemporary evidence, the sīra and hadith as later narrative traditions, and a small body of external and material evidence. It argued that the biography of Muhammad must be reconstructed with graduated confidence and that scholars differ about how much of the tradition can be trusted.

Muhammad was born into the Banū Hāshim, a respected but not dominant clan of Quraysh, in a Mecca governed by negotiated clan authority and organized by kinship, patronage, and contract. Traditions of his orphanhood, upbringing under Abū Ṭālib, commercial activity, and marriage to Khadija provide the biographical background, though many details are shaped by later devotion.

The traditional date of the first revelations, around 610, begins his prophetic mission. The Qur'an preserves evidence of a developing preaching, from short, urgent warnings to longer arguments and narratives, though scholars disagree about the precision with which its passages can be dated. Its central themes were God's oneness, the rejection of intermediary cults, resurrection and judgment, moral and social responsibility, and the stories of earlier prophets, placing it within the wider world of late antique religious discourse.

The first followers included a woman of means, young men from several Qurayshī clans, and enslaved persons and clients, creating a community of belief that crossed lines of kinship and status. Its practices in Mecca centered on prayer, recitation, and ethical commitments, not on the institutions that developed later.

Opposition arose from religious disagreement, loyalty to ancestral custom, rivalries of status and leadership, and concern for the sanctuary's prestige and Mecca's commerce. Quraysh was not a united bloc; its leaders took different positions. Clan protection shielded Muhammad but left unprotected believers vulnerable to coercion and violence.

The migration of some believers to Christian Aksum, the boycott of the Banū Hāshim and Banū al-Muṭṭalib, and the deaths of Khadija and Abū Ṭālib show the changing balance of protection. The failure at Ṭāʾif and among the tribes made the search for a new base urgent.

The Night Journey and Ascension, in Islamic tradition, connect Muhammad's experience to a wider prophetic history and sacred geography, though the Qur'anic text is brief and its later elaborations are products of subsequent centuries.

Yathrib, divided by feuding, offered an opening. Through the pledges at al-ʿAqaba its representatives assumed obligations of protection toward Muhammad, and his followers began to leave Mecca. The Hijra of 622 was thus the result of religious preaching, social opposition, the weakening of kin protection, and political negotiation, not a simple outcome of persecution.

The chapter's significance lies in showing how a religious message produced a new kind of community within a kin-based society, and how that community came to need a political home. Chapter 37 follows it to Medina, where the movement became a polity, and the volume's later chapters trace the consequences across the Middle East.

Chapter Reflection

The Meccan period matters because it shows how a religious movement forms before it has power. Muhammad's followers had no army, no state, and no institutions beyond the bonds of belief, family, and mutual obligation. What they possessed was a message and a capacity to build solidarity across lines of kinship and status. Understanding that stage is essential to understanding what came later, because the later institutions of Islam were built by people who remembered the Meccan years as a time of testing and conviction.

The chapter also shows how much depended on social structure. Kin protection allowed Muhammad to preach and made his poorer followers vulnerable. The weakening of that protection pushed the movement toward outside alliances, and Yathrib's divisions made one such alliance possible. These dynamics warn against explanations that treat religious change as the product of belief alone or of material interest alone.

Finally, the chapter illustrates the care required in writing about foundational religious figures. The evidence is uneven, the traditions are rich and contested, and honest history acknowledges where knowledge ends. That caution does not diminish the importance of the subject; it allows students to see how a community's memory and scholarly reconstruction can illuminate one another.

Chapter 37 turns to Medina, where the emigrants and the Yathribī supporters, together with other communities of the oasis, formed a new political and religious order. From there, Chapter 38 examines the succession after Muhammad's death, and Chapter 39 turns to the conquests that remade the Middle East. The Meccan years provide the foundation, though not the inevitability, of what followed.
