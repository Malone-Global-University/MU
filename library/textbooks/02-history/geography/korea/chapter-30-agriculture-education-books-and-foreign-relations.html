Agriculture, Education, Books, and Foreign Relations

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

A harvest measured in a county, a boy reciting classics in a provincial school, a printed calendar sent to a magistrate, an envoy carrying a memorial toward Beijing, and a licensed ship entering a southern port: these scenes seem unrelated, yet each belonged to one system that kept the early Joseon dynasty running.

Chapters 25 through 29 described the founding of the dynasty, its Neo-Confucian institutions, the creation of Hangeul, the court's technical knowledge, and its status hierarchy. This chapter asks how all of that was sustained. A bureaucracy, a court, schools, printing offices, garrisons, and embassies all required resources, trained people, reliable communication, and managed relations with neighbors.

The chapter's controlling question is how the Joseon state converted resources, knowledge, and relationships into a functioning political order. Its answer emphasizes flows: grain, cloth, and labor moved from countryside to government; books and instructions moved from the capital toward officials and local institutions; students moved through schools toward office; envoys, goods, texts, and information crossed borders.

The chapter also corrects two misconceptions. Joseon agriculture was not simply irrigated rice farming; households combined many crops according to region and environment. And early Joseon was not isolated. Its rulers sought not the absence of foreign contact but the channeling of contact into institutions they could regulate: ritualized missions to Ming China, titles, markets, and garrisons on the Jurchen frontier, and authorized ports and intermediaries for Japan and Tsushima.

Evidence comes from the Veritable Records (Sillok) of successive reigns, law codes, agricultural manuals, land and household registers where they survive, printed books themselves, school regulations, diplomatic records, and archaeology of fortifications and kilns. Most of these were produced by officials, so the experience of farmers, women, frontier residents, and sailors must often be inferred. This chapter closes Part V and stops before the crises of the late sixteenth century treated in Chapter 31.

Two features of the evidence shape everything below. First, the state's own records dominate: the Veritable Records, statutes, and compilations describe what officials intended and often what they complained about, not what a household experienced. Second, the period's major achievements, from land measurement to printed books, were products of a small literate elite. The chapter therefore pairs official evidence with attention to what it cannot show.

The chapter moves from the land to the school, the book, and the border. Sections 30.2–30.5 follow grain and labor from field to treasury; Sections 30.6–30.9 follow people and texts from classroom to office; Sections 30.10–30.13 follow envoys, frontier households, and ships across Joseon's edges; Section 30.14 draws these together.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Explain how land tax, tribute, labor service, and military duty converted rural production into state capacity.
  3. Analyze the Sejong-era land-tax reform as an exercise in measurement and political consultation.
  4. Distinguish Seonggyungwan, hyanggyo, and seowon and their changing roles.
  5. Assess literacy in Classical Chinese and Hangeul without assuming mass literacy.
  6. Explain the social functions of state printing and book distribution.
  7. Interpret the Ming–Joseon relationship as a negotiated hierarchy.
  8. Trace Jurchen frontier policy, including trade, titles, settlement, and northern expansion.
  9. Explain Tsushima's role and the regulated exchange of the Gyehae Treaty system.
  10. Evaluate why "Hermit Kingdom" misdescribes early Joseon.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did the state consult many people about land-tax reform rather than simply decree it?

Historical Context

Joseon inherited a largely agrarian society from Goryeo, along with Buddhist monasteries, hereditary elites, and a long habit of diplomacy with continental dynasties. The founders of 1392 altered land arrangements and Confucianized government (Chapters 25–26), but they depended on the same rural households and on existing local power. In the Ming dynasty, founded in 1368, Joseon faced a new continental power whose recognition mattered for royal legitimacy. To the north, Jurchen communities lived on both sides of the Amnok (Yalu) and Duman (Tumen) rivers; to the south, raiders commonly called wokou (wakō) operated from islands and coasts, and Tsushima's ruling Sō family lived between Korean and Japanese worlds.

Earlier chapters assigned the examination system (26), Hangeul's creation (27), printing and agricultural technology (28), and the status order (29). This chapter examines how those pieces worked together in practice.

Several earlier developments bear directly on this chapter. Goryeo had already maintained schools, printed books, and dealt with continental dynasties, steppe neighbors, and Japanese traders, so early Joseon often reworked existing practice. Its founders recast land arrangements, promoted Neo-Confucian learning, and sought Ming recognition, but they governed a countryside in which landholding, local influence, and habits of service changed only gradually. Readers should therefore expect continuity as well as innovation in every system discussed below.

Section 30.1

The Material Foundations of the Joseon Order

Every institution described in Part V drew on agricultural surplus. The court, bureaucracy, schools, military establishments, and printing offices could not sustain themselves solely through their own production; they depended on agricultural surplus and labor drawn from rural households. This chapter treats agriculture, revenue, education, printing, and diplomacy as interlocking systems rather than separate topics. Households produced; officials measured and collected; schools trained the people who did the measuring; books standardized what those people knew; diplomacy protected the conditions under which the system operated.

The chapters of Part V examined the dynasty's founders, its Neo-Confucian institutions, its script, its court sciences, and its status order. Each of these was an aspiration until households, magistrates, teachers, printers, and envoys made it routine. The sections that follow therefore move between scales: the field and the storehouse, the county school and the printing office, the capital's diplomatic halls and the ports and river crossings at the edges of the kingdom. Evidence differs at each scale, and readers should keep track of whose voice the sources preserve.

Section 30.2

Farming a Diverse Peninsula

Wet-rice cultivation was politically and culturally prized, especially in the warmer south and in irrigable valleys, but rural households lived on varied combinations of crops. Dry fields produced barley, wheat, millet, sorghum, and beans; gardens yielded vegetables; hemp and, increasingly, cotton supplied cloth, which also served as a form of payment. The northern and eastern highlands, with shorter growing seasons, differed sharply from the southern plains, while island and coastal communities combined farming with fishing and salt-making. The agricultural calendar, organized around monsoon rains and cold winters, shaped labor, festivals, and tax timing. Agricultural Korea was regional Korea.

The peninsula's geography set the terms. The western and southern plains and lower river valleys, with warmer summers and abundant monsoon rain, favored irrigated rice where water could be controlled. The interior basins and the northern and eastern highlands, with shorter growing seasons and colder winters, leaned on dry-field crops such as barley, millet, sorghum, and beans. Rainfall was concentrated in summer, so a late or weak monsoon could bring drought at transplanting time, while floods threatened lowland fields; both made harvests uncertain.

Most households combined crops rather than specializing. Barley and wheat were harvested before the summer rains in many areas, vegetables and beans filled gardens and field margins, and hemp and, increasingly through the fifteenth century, cotton supplied cloth. Coastal and island communities added fishing, seaweed gathering, and salt-making, exchanging these products for grain. Labor was intensely seasonal, peaking at transplanting and harvest, and the gap before the harvest left poorer households vulnerable to spring shortage. Rice, though prestigious and heavily taxed, described only part of the rural economy, and a policy suited to one region could misfit another.

Section 30.3

Rural Production, Labor, and Agricultural Change

Production depended on household labor, with men, women, children, and, on many estates, nobi (Chapter 29) performing distinct tasks. Water control through embankments and reservoirs expanded gradually, and crop rotation and multiple cropping in dry fields could make land use more intensive. Scholars disagree about how rapid improvement was in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and surviving evidence rarely allows precise productivity figures. The Nongsa jikseol, discussed as a compilation in Chapter 28, matters here as applied knowledge: it gathered experienced farmers' practices and circulated them to officials, who were expected to encourage local cultivation. How far it reached actual farmers is uncertain, since most could not read it and depended on local practice.

Household labor was the basis of production. Men commonly handled plowing where oxen were available, while women's work included weeding, textile production, and food processing; the evidence does not support a rigid universal division, and labor shifted with household size, status, and season. Children contributed from an early age. On landed estates, nobi (Chapter 29) worked the owner's fields or paid dues, so arrangements differed sharply between a small owner-cultivator, a tenant, and a yangban landlord's dependents.

Water control was a decisive investment. Embankments, small reservoirs, and channels extended irrigation, and the central government repeatedly ordered local officials to repair and build them, though many works depended on local labor rather than central funding. Multiple cropping was better established in dry fields; farmers and officials explored more intensive use of paddy land, but rice–barley double cropping was not yet a broadly established fifteenth-century system. Manure, ash, and green matter helped maintain fields, though the extent and pace of these practices varied and is hard to quantify. Cotton spread during the fifteenth century and became increasingly important for clothing and as a medium of exchange.

The Nongsa jikseol of 1429, compiled under Sejong, is best read as an attempt to collect the experience of farmers in the kingdom's own provinces rather than simply transmit Chinese prescriptions unsuited to local climate. Yet the book reached officials and literate households, not most cultivators, who learned from neighbors and their own trial and error. Historians therefore doubt that written manuals alone transformed productivity, and no surviving source permits reliable national yield figures.

Section 30.4

From Harvest to Revenue: Taxation and State Obligations

The state extracted resources in several forms. A land tax, paid mainly in grain, was assessed on cultivated land. Local tribute (gong) required households to supply products, such as textiles or specialty goods, for court and government use. Labor service demanded periodic work on roads, fortifications, and buildings. Military obligations fell on many commoner men. In early Joseon, households were grouped so that supporting households (boin) supplied cloth and other aid to men in active service; the broad substitution of cloth payment for personal service developed further in later periods and should not be read back into this system. These burdens fell unevenly: yangban enjoyed privileges, while commoners carried most service. Collection passed through magistrates, clerks, and local intermediaries, whose discretion and corruption could make nominally fixed obligations heavier in practice.

Historical Vocabulary

Tribute, Labor Service, and Land Tax

Definition: Three overlapping obligations: a grain tax on land, locally assigned goods (gong), and unpaid labor or military duty.

Why it matters: Together they financed government without a modern cash tax system.

Potential misunderstanding: "Tribute" here means domestic goods owed to the state, not only gifts sent to China.

Understood as a system, these obligations required information. The state needed to know who held which land, which households could supply labor or goods, and where grain could be stored and moved. Household and land registers, compiled under local magistrates, were imperfect instruments: the clerks who kept them were often hereditary local functionaries, and households could be omitted, undercounted, or shielded by influential patrons. A government dependent on agricultural revenue had strong reasons to seek better records.

Logistics added burdens. Grain collected in the provinces had to be carried to local storehouses and in part to the capital by river and coastal shipping, exposing it to spoilage, theft, and shipwreck, and the labor of hauling fell on commoners. Tribute goods were assigned by locality, sometimes requiring households to buy items not produced nearby. Because exemptions favored elites and obligations were assigned through local intermediaries, incidence was unequal, and opportunities for evasion, padding, or extortion existed at every stage. The fiscal crises of the nineteenth century belong to later chapters; the point here is that the early system already depended heavily on local discretion.

Everyday Life and Social History

What the State Meant to a Household

For most households the state appeared at harvest, when grain was assessed and collected, and in calls for labor or military support. A few families sent sons to county schools or tutors; many households encountered books only indirectly, through a calendar's dates for planting or festivals. Coastal residents lived with the risk of raids, and frontier settlers bore the costs of relocation. Sources are mostly official, so such experiences must be inferred.

Section 30.5

Measuring the Harvest: Sejong and the Land-Tax Reforms

Earlier assessments depended heavily on officials' annual inspections of crops, which invited arbitrariness. Sejong's government sought a more regular system, gongbeop, in which fields were classified into grades by quality and tax varied with annual harvest grades. In 1430 the government polled officials and villagers, reportedly some 170,000 people, a notable effort to gauge opinion, though the responses reflected regional and status divisions. Disagreement concerned whether fixed rates would burden poor regions and how to handle variations in harvest. The 1444 framework classified land into six grades of quality (jeonbun yukdeung) and harvests into nine annual grades (yeonbun gudeung), producing rates from roughly 4 to 20 du per gyeol. It fixed the system's structure after years of debate and trial, but implementation spread unevenly by province and was not instantly uniform across the kingdom. It illustrates the same governing mentality seen in Chapter 28: to measure, compare, and standardize, making a diverse countryside administratively legible.

Before the reform, tax on a field often depended on an official's inspection of the year's crop, which invited inconsistency, favoritism, and dispute, and officials could not always inspect in time. Sejong and his advisers sought a rule that would limit arbitrary assessment while still responding to good and bad harvests. The 1430 consultation was unusual for a premodern government: it solicited views widely, yet it gathered opinion rather than counting votes, and the king and officials decided. Responses differed by region, reflecting local farming conditions and fears that fixed rates would burden poorer districts.

The government then experimented in selected areas and adjusted the rules before the 1444 framework. A gyeol was not a fixed modern area: it represented land of equivalent productive capacity, so poorer grades occupied larger physical areas for the same assessment. With six land grades and nine harvest grades, rates ranged from roughly 4 to 20 du per gyeol. The system offered administrative advantages, since officials applied published standards, but it still relied on local judgment to grade land and judge harvests, and rollout was uneven across provinces. Like the water clocks and rainfall gauges of Chapter 28, it expressed a belief that careful measurement could make a diverse countryside governable, and the gap between formal scheme and daily practice is a recurring theme of this chapter.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Converting Production into State Capacity

Long-term causes: An agrarian economy and inherited bureaucratic traditions. Short-term causes: A new dynasty's need for legitimacy and revenue. Trigger: Dissatisfaction with arbitrary harvest assessment. Transformation: Land grading, schools, printing, and regulated diplomacy. Immediate effects: More predictable revenue and standardized knowledge. Long-term consequences: A durable administrative order dependent on peasant labor and local officials. Complexity: Outcomes varied by region and depended on local implementation.

Section 30.6

Schools and the Education of an Official Elite

Chapter 26 described examinations; this section concerns the education that preceded them. The Seonggyungwan, reestablished in the new capital in 1398 on Goryeo foundations, gathered students to study the Confucian classics and participate in rituals honoring Confucius. Government-supported hyanggyo were intended for each county, with teachers, classroom space, and ritual facilities, though quality varied with local resources and official attention. Curriculum emphasized classics, history, and moral texts, learned largely through memorization and commentary. Much early education occurred in households and village tutors' schools, favoring families with books, leisure, and teachers. Access therefore was unequal by status, region, and gender, since women were excluded from the formal school system.

Historical Vocabulary

Seonggyungwan, Hyanggyo, Seowon

Definition: The capital academy, county state schools, and private academies, respectively.

Why it matters: They formed layers of institutions reproducing a Confucian governing culture.

Potential misunderstanding: Seowon are not "early Joseon schools" in general; they appear only in the sixteenth century.

Seonggyungwan sat at the apex of state education. It had Goryeo antecedents and was reestablished in Hanyang in 1398, and it combined teaching with rituals honoring Confucius and other sages, so that study and ceremony were joined. Its students were drawn mainly from elite and scholar families and lived under institutional rules. Below it, the hyanggyo were local state institutions, in principle in every county, with a shrine, a lecture hall, and lodging; in practice their teachers, funding, and attendance varied with local resources and official attention; their educational function weakened more substantially in later periods.

Curriculum centered on the Confucian classics, histories, and moral primers, learned through memorization, recitation, and commentary, with composition as preparation for the examinations explained in Chapter 26. Much early learning happened at home or with private tutors and village teachers, which favored families that could spare a boy's labor and afford instruction. Access therefore depended on status, wealth, and region, with the capital and southern towns better served, and women were excluded from formal schools, though some learned at home.

Evidence for schooling comes largely from regulations, royal orders, and officials' memorials that complain about empty classrooms, unpaid teachers, or students who avoided residence. Such complaints show both the ideal and its frequent failure, but they cannot by themselves measure enrollment. The contrast between an ideal network reaching every county and a patchwork dependent on local funds, land grants, and official attention is one of the chapter's clearest examples of the gap between design and practice.

For a family, schooling was a strategic investment. A boy's years of study meant years without his labor, and the return depended on passing examinations or gaining standing among local scholars. This is why education reproduced existing advantage: households with land, books, and a scholarly tradition could sustain study, while most farming families could not. At the same time, some families of modest means sent a promising son to a village teacher, and the examination route, though weighted toward elites, was not entirely closed.

Section 30.7

From State Schools to Private Academies

In 1543 the local official Ju Se-bung founded a shrine and school associated with the Goryeo scholar An Hyang, later known as Baegundong Seowon. Local scholars wanted institutions combining teaching, study, commemoration of exemplary scholars, and local elite networks, partly because state schools were seen as weakened by examination-oriented study. These early academies were few. Their large expansion and their political and factional significance belong to later centuries, and should not be read back into this period.

Private education long preceded the academies. Families hired tutors, lineages taught their sons, and scholars gathered disciples. The seowon added something distinct. In 1543 Ju Se-bung, a local official, established a shrine honoring the Goryeo scholar An Hyang together with a school, and the institution later known as Baegundong Seowon is conventionally regarded as the first seowon. It combined teaching, commemoration, and ritual, giving local scholars a place of their own.

Its founders wanted what they felt state schools could not supply: a setting for serious study detached from examination pressure, and a means of honoring exemplary scholars locally. Scholar networks supported it. But this was a single beginning, not a general pattern of the early Joseon period. The later proliferation of seowon and their role in factional politics belong to later chapters and should not be projected back onto the fifteenth century.

The seowon also reflected a wider concern among some scholars that state schools had become routes to office rather than places of moral cultivation. Commemorating a revered scholar locally tied study to ethical example, and the shrine gave the institution a ritual identity that a county school lacked. These ideas should be treated as the motives of particular scholars, not as a program of the whole elite, and sources on the earliest academy largely preserve the views of its sponsors.

Section 30.8

Literacy in Classical Chinese and Hangeul

Classical Chinese remained the language of examinations, official documents, and elite scholarship. Hangeul, discussed in Chapter 27, created the possibility of reading and writing the spoken language, and government used it in some translations and instructional texts. Evidence suggests use among women, among people outside the highest scholarly elite, and in private correspondence, but adoption was gradual, and no reliable literacy rates survive. The better formulation is that literacy was layered: many people could not read at all; some could read Hangeul; a small elite commanded Classical Chinese. Oral transmission, images, and ritual continued to carry knowledge for the unlettered.

Literacy was not a binary. Some people could read only a few characters or recognize seals and account entries; some could read Hangeul but not Classical Chinese; a small elite commanded the classical language of examinations and scholarship. No reliable percentages survive, and estimates should be treated with suspicion. Clerks and merchants often needed practical partial literacy without the classical education of a scholar.

Hangeul (Chapter 27) opened new possibilities, used in translations of moral and Buddhist texts, in instructional works, and in private letters, including letters by women. But adoption was gradual, and elite men continued to prize Classical Chinese. Knowledge also moved through oral reading and recitation, images, and mediation by literate relatives or neighbors. Creating a script did not produce immediate mass literacy, since access to teachers, books, and leisure mattered as much as the alphabet.

Regional and social setting shaped literacy. A capital clerk, a provincial merchant, a village elder, a yangban woman who wrote letters in Hangeul, and a farmer who could not read at all lived in different textual worlds, and the same household might include several of these. The sources, mostly official or elite, overrepresent the literate, so what they show about reading is necessarily a view from above.

This is also why government notices were often read aloud and posted with explanation, and why seals, tallies, and oral testimony remained central to administration. A state that printed extensively still relied on the spoken word to reach most of its subjects.

Section 30.9

Books, Printing, and the Circulation of Knowledge

Chapter 28 treated printing technology. The social question here is what was printed and why. The government published Confucian classics, law codes, histories, agricultural and medical works, moral instruction, calendars, and dictionaries, and distributed them to offices and schools. Printing served standardization: officials across the country could consult the same texts and calendars. Woodblock and movable metal type coexisted, with movable type suited to smaller runs of varied texts and woodblocks to works reprinted repeatedly. Distribution was limited, and books remained costly, so the circulation of knowledge was concentrated among officials, schools, and wealthier families, with gradual diffusion outward.

The state printed to govern. Offices produced Confucian classics, histories, law codes, calendars, medical and agricultural works, moral instruction, and reference books, and sent them to offices and schools, so that officials in distant counties worked from common texts. Standardization was the aim: the same calendar, the same code, the same commentary. Chapter 28 treated the technology; here the point is function.

Woodblocks and movable metal type coexisted. Type suited limited runs of varied texts, while blocks could be stored and reprinted when demand continued. Books were costly, distribution was limited, and storage and copying by hand remained important, so access concentrated among officials, schools, and wealthy families. The relation of print to government was close, and a teleological story of a printing revolution misdescribes it.

What was not printed also matters. Many works circulated in manuscript, copied by hand by scholars, monks, and private families, and some texts were deliberately limited to officials. Printing offices worked on official orders, so the choice of titles reflected governmental priorities more than market demand, and the public for books, though growing among the educated, was small. Because surviving copies are concentrated in institutional and family collections, historians must be cautious about inferring wide readership from the titles that happen to survive.

Print thus worked with schools and registers as one of the state's tools of standardization, and the combination of common texts, trained officials, and measured land is what makes Sejong-era government appear systematic. Its reach, however, stopped where literacy and local cooperation stopped.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Goryeo to Early Joseon

What changed? Tax measurement became more systematic, Confucian schooling was promoted, and Hangeul appeared. What remained continuous? Agrarian dependence, printing, and tributary diplomacy with continental powers. When? Mostly gradually across the fifteenth century. Who experienced change most directly? Officials and scholars; farmers saw greater continuity. Why it matters: Early Joseon was a transformation of inherited institutions, not a clean break.

Section 30.10

Joseon and Ming China: Hierarchy, Legitimacy, and Diplomacy

Joseon's founders sought Ming investiture, in which the emperor recognized the Korean king. The Hongwu emperor accepted the dynastic change and approved the name Joseon in 1393, but relations then deteriorated and Taejo never received formal investiture, patent, and seal. Taejong obtained the Ming patent and seal in 1401 and, after the Yongle emperor's accession, a renewed investiture in 1403. The stable relationship described below was thus earned through difficult early negotiation. Missions traveled regularly to the Ming court, bearing memorials, gifts, and requests, and returned with imperial responses and goods. The relationship was deliberately hierarchical in ritual, with Joseon accepting the position of a subordinate king. But it was not colonial rule: Joseon governed itself, chose its own officials, and used the relationship to secure legitimacy, security on its northern flank, and access to books and goods.

Historical Debate

Dependency, Alliance, or Negotiated Hierarchy?

The question: How should historians characterize the Ming–Joseon relationship?

Dependency view: Emphasizes ritual subordination and Joseon's need for recognition.
Negotiated hierarchy view: Emphasizes that Joseon rulers actively used the framework for their own purposes and retained domestic autonomy.

Evidence: Diplomatic protocols, records of disputes, and court debate.

Points of disagreement: Chiefly interpretation and terminology, since "sovereignty" is a modern concept.

Why it matters: It determines whether Joseon is seen as peripheral or as an active diplomatic agent.

Student task: Identify evidence that could distinguish a ritual from a practical subordination.

Early relations were tense. Ming authorities accepted the change of dynasty and approved the name Joseon in 1393. Relations were then strained by disputes over diplomatic documents, frontier issues, and Jurchen populations, and Taejo never received formal Ming investiture. Taejong's acquisition of the patent and seal in 1401, and the renewal under the Yongle emperor in 1403, settled the question of legitimacy. Investiture meant recognition of a ruler within a ritual hierarchy; it did not give the emperor power to appoint Joseon officials or collect its taxes. Terms such as colony, alliance, or sovereign equality each mislead, because they import modern categories onto a system with its own protocol and language.

Because the Ming relationship was conducted in writing and ritual, the surviving evidence is unusually rich on protocol and unusually thin on private opinion. Memorials, imperial responses, and court debates in Joseon record how rulers phrased requests, how they justified accepting hierarchy, and how officials argued over costs and wording. Reading these documents, historians find that Joseon leaders treated the framework as useful: it supported the king's legitimacy at home and reduced the risk of conflict with a far larger neighbor, even when specific demands were resented.

Section 30.11

Diplomacy as Exchange: What Moved between Joseon and Ming

Missions carried more than ceremony. Envoys brought gifts, horses, cloth, and other goods, and returned with silks, books, and medicines; members of missions also conducted private trade, observed Ming society, and gathered information. Calendars and scholarly texts entered Joseon through these channels, and Korean travelers wrote accounts of what they saw. Tribute was thus an institutional channel of exchange as well as a ritual of hierarchy. Requests and disputes, for example over the horses or goods demanded by the Ming, show that negotiation was constant.

The mission system brought regular contact. Embassies included senior envoys, interpreters, clerks, physicians, and attendants, bringing memorials, tribute goods, and gifts and receiving return gifts in turn. After the Ming capital shifted to Beijing in 1421, Joseon embassies normally traveled overland through Liaodong toward Beijing; earlier missions went to the Ming court at Nanjing. Members also carried goods for private trade, and Joseon sought books, medicines, textiles, and the Ming calendar, while Ming authorities sometimes pressed for horses and other items, prompting negotiation. Beijing was also a place to gather information about Ming politics and the Jurchen and Mongol borderlands. Hierarchy and mutual utility coexisted: the ritual placed Joseon beneath the emperor, yet the exchange served Joseon's needs, and neither humiliation nor disguised free trade describes it adequately.

Embassy journeys also shaped Joseon's knowledge of the world. Members recorded what they observed on the road and in Beijing, and such observations fed court discussion of Ming practice, from ritual to technology. Because missions were costly for the communities along their routes, which supplied food, transport, and labor, their benefits and burdens were unevenly shared, a reminder that diplomacy, like taxation, rested on local support.

Section 30.12

The Jurchen Frontier: Trade, Conflict, Settlement, and Expansion

The Amnok and Duman basins were populated by Jurchen communities, some allied with or receiving titles from Joseon, some trading at frontier markets, some raiding, and some also in contact with the Ming. The frontier was negotiated and politically fluid, not a fixed modern border. Joseon offered titles, gifts, and trade to win cooperation while also fortifying the border.

The Jurchen were not a single political actor. Communities along the Amnok, in the Duman valley, and farther north differed in leadership, economy, and alignments, and some acknowledged Ming titles, some Joseon gifts and trade, and some both. Joseon used titles, grain, cloth, and market access to attract cooperation, answered raids with campaigns, and accepted the movement of some households. Ming and Joseon claims overlapped, so frontier policy had to watch both.

Under Sejong, commanders such as Choe Yun-deok on the Amnok and Kim Jong-seo on the Duman built forts and relocated southern households to the new settlements. Settlers faced cold, isolation, and raids, and the state's demands on them were heavy. The Four Counties and Six Garrisons followed different courses: three of the Four Counties were abolished in 1455 and Jaseong in 1459, yet Joseon continued to regard the Amnok as its boundary, whereas the Six Garrisons had a separate institutional history. The modern North Korea–China border should not be read back onto this frontier, which was made through negotiation, settlement, and withdrawal.

Evidence for the frontier includes royal orders, campaign reports, records of resettlement, fortification remains, and Ming and Joseon correspondence, almost all produced by outside officials. Jurchen views must be inferred from their actions, such as accepting titles, trading, relocating, or raiding, and reading those actions as simple loyalty or hostility would flatten them. Frontier residents, whether relocated Korean households or local Jurchen families, appear mainly as objects of policy, though their decisions often shaped its outcome.

Section 30.13

Japan, Piracy, Tsushima, and Regulated Exchange

Raiders called wokou afflicted the coasts in late Goryeo and early Joseon. The early wokou were principally Japanese maritime raiders, many associated with western Japan and Tsushima, though membership was not completely homogeneous; they were not a national Japanese military force. The heavily multinational wokou belong especially to the sixteenth century. Joseon combined defense, offers of trade, and diplomacy. Tsushima's limited arable land left its people dependent on exchange and access to Korean markets, which made the island both a base for raiders and a potential partner in restraining them. In 1419 a fleet attacked Tsushima, which the court viewed as a base for raiding; the expedition damaged the island but did not end contact, and negotiation resumed, with the Sō rulers accepting Korean-issued titles and permits in exchange for trading privileges and responsibility for controlling their ships.

Busanpo and Naeipo, opened earlier and closed after 1419, reopened in 1423, and Yeompo was added in 1426, completing the Three Ports and allowing regulated residence and trade under Korean supervision. The 1443 Gyehae Agreement fixed Tsushima's annual ships at fifty and an annual grant at 200 seok of rice and beans. Japanese residents in the ports grew in number, and pressure over quotas and regulation culminated in the armed disturbance of 1510, after which Joseon tightened restrictions. Tsushima's interests were not identical to those of Japanese central authorities, which shows why premodern relations cannot be mapped onto modern national boundaries. The road to 1592 belongs to Chapter 31.

The regulated system depended on the cooperation of people at the ports as well as officials. Residents, interpreters, local officials, and merchants all negotiated how quotas and permits worked in practice, and the tension between rules and daily exchange is what produced the pressures that erupted in 1510. Japanese settlers' growing numbers and Korean efforts to limit them illustrate a border society in which both sides depended on and distrusted each other.

For historians, this section's main value is as a corrective. A picture of early Joseon as closed misses a world of licensed ships, intermediaries, and negotiated privilege, and a picture of Tsushima as simply Japanese overlooks an island whose survival depended on Korea. These arrangements were stable for decades and then strained, which is why they can be described accurately without hindsight about what followed.

Primary Source Window

The 1443 Gyehae Agreement with Tsushima

Context: Concluded between Joseon officials and the Sō lord of Tsushima and recorded in the Veritable Records and later regulatory compilations.

Source type: Diplomatic and trade regulation, known through official compilation.

Paraphrase (not a quotation):

The Tsushima lord was permitted to send fifty ships a year and received an annual grant of 200 seok of rice and beans, with extra ships allowed for urgent reports. These are the terms recorded in the agreement, not measures of actual annual traffic.

Analysis: The quota and grant tied Tsushima's interests to Korean rules, making the Sō responsible for restraining raiders and unauthorized traders.

Limitations: The text shows intended rules, not how often they were obeyed or evaded, and preserves the Korean side's view.

Perspective: Joseon officialdom; Tsushima merchants and ordinary sailors are largely absent.

Section 30.14

Early Joseon in the East Asian Order

Early Joseon was agriculturally based yet administratively ambitious, Confucian yet not instantly transformed, highly literate at the elite level yet far from a mass-literacy society, and embedded in regional networks rather than isolated. Its rulers practiced managed connectivity. The system described here was relatively stable, but the geopolitical world around it would change dramatically, as Chapter 31 will show.

The early Joseon order was durable because its parts reinforced each other. Taxation supported schools and offices; schools and printed books produced officials who could measure, assess, and write; diplomacy secured recognition and a manageable frontier and coast. But each part depended on local implementation: magistrates and clerks determined how taxes were collected, local officials decided how schools fared, and frontier and port arrangements were repeatedly renegotiated. State capacity was real but limited, and it rested on peasant labor and elite cooperation.

That durability does not imply that later wars were inevitable. The system worked in a particular setting of a stable Ming, a fragmented Jurchen world, and a regulated Japanese trade. Changes in that setting, examined in Chapter 31, would test it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter argued that the early Joseon order rested on interconnected systems. Agricultural households, working diverse regional environments, produced the resources on which government depended.

Taxation, tribute, labor service, and military duty converted production into state capacity, and the gongbeop reform extended the measuring mentality of Chapter 28 to land and harvests.

Schools, from Seonggyungwan to hyanggyo, reproduced a governing culture, while early academies foreshadowed later developments. Literacy was layered, and printing standardized knowledge for officials and institutions.

Abroad, Joseon managed its position through ritualized Ming missions, negotiated Jurchen frontiers, and regulated exchange with Tsushima. Together these show managed connectivity, not isolation.

The system was durable yet dependent on peasant labor, local administration, and diplomatic balance, setting the stage for the pressures addressed in Chapter 31.

The chapter also showed that education and print formed the human and textual side of the same system. Seonggyungwan and the hyanggyo trained an official elite unevenly; the first seowon of 1543 pointed toward later developments without defining the early period; literacy was layered; and printed books standardized what officials knew.

In foreign relations, early Ming tensions over the dynasty's legitimacy gave way to a stable ritual hierarchy after 1401–1403, while the Jurchen frontier and the Tsushima ports showed Joseon managing contact through titles, garrisons, quotas, and permits. The Gyehae Agreement illustrates how regulation tied a neighbor's interests to Korean rules.

Across all these arenas the pattern was managed connectivity resting on local implementation, and the chapter's evidence, mostly official, leaves farmers, frontier settlers, and sailors largely indirect.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the early Joseon state was sustained by ordinary flows of grain, labor, books, and envoys, not only by edicts and ideals.

It reveals a society that was agrarian yet administratively ambitious and externally connected. Its methods of measurement, schooling, and regulated diplomacy shaped later centuries.

The same systems also exposed vulnerabilities: dependence on peasant labor, uneven local administration, and delicate frontier and maritime arrangements. These continuities carry into the pressures of the late sixteenth century.

The chapter leaves one lesson for the wider textbook: a state is sustained less by its proclamations than by the routine flows that connect field, school, and border, and those flows depended on the cooperation of people whose voices the sources rarely preserve.
