The Daewongun, Reform, and the Defense of the Old Order

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 1868 King Gojong, then about fifteen years old, took up residence in Gyeongbokgung, the principal palace of the dynasty's founders, which had stood in ruins for roughly two and a half centuries since the Imjin War. Behind the rebuilt halls lay a government that had tried, in only a few years, to rebuild far more than a palace: its ministries, its laws, its tax rolls, its armies, and its claim to rule over the elites who served it. Whether that effort rescued the Joseon order or merely loaded it with new burdens is the question of this chapter.

The man most responsible for that effort was not the king. He was Yi Haeung, known by the title Heungseon Daewongun, the king's biological father. Yi Haeung had never expected to rule, nor to be the father of a ruler. A distant member of the royal Yi house with little political standing, he was drawn into the center of the state in January 1864 when the childless King Cheoljong died and the senior royal woman at court selected his young son as heir. For about a decade, Yi Haeung's influence over court appointments, institutional change, finance, and foreign policy was greater than that of any other individual in the kingdom, though he never held the throne, and, as this chapter explains, he never held an uncomplicated formal regency either.

Popular memory, in Korea and abroad, has often compressed this decade into a single image: the stubborn isolationist who ordered the persecution of Catholics, repelled French and American warships, and erected stone monuments forbidding accommodation with the West. That image contains real evidence, but it is incomplete in ways that distort the period. The same government compiled new law codes, curtailed the political role of powerful in-law families, closed hundreds of privately endowed Confucian academies, extended military taxation to households that had long escaped it, rebuilt a great palace, and issued an overvalued coin that damaged the commercial economy. Its program resisted coercive diplomacy with Western states, yet Joseon at the time already maintained established diplomatic and trading relationships with Qing China and with Japan. "Isolationism" therefore describes, at best, one part of a broader project.

That project can be called a defense of the old order by means of reform. The Daewongun's aim was not to replace Neo-Confucian monarchy, as later reformers would attempt, but to restore its authority, revenue, and defensive capacity after decades of weakness described in Chapters 43 and 44. His methods, however, were forceful and often costly. They challenged the privileges of in-law lineages, provincial scholar-gentry, and tax-exempt households, and they imposed extraordinary charges on farmers, merchants, laborers, and officials to pay for the monarchy's renewed grandeur. The reforms strengthened the center in some respects and generated resentment in others, and by 1873 opposition from within the Confucian establishment, and from King Gojong himself, ended the Daewongun's effective control.

Historians reconstruct this decade from a distinctive body of sources. The annals of the reign, the Gojong Sillok, and the daily record of the royal secretariat, the Seungjeongwon Ilgi, preserve edicts, memorials, and appointments. Law codes such as the Daejeon Hoetong record the legal framework of the new government. Contemporary memorials, local records, diaries, and the reports of foreign missionaries, diplomats, and naval officers supply other perspectives. Each has limitations. The reign annals of this period were compiled in the colonial era, long after the events, and the voices of ordinary taxpayers appear mostly through official complaints and later memory.

This chapter concentrates on the years 1863 to 1873. It draws on Chapters 43 through 46 for the inherited political system, the fiscal and social crisis, the Catholic persecution and Western naval approaches, and the Donghak movement, without retelling their narratives. It examines how the Daewongun's government used the Joseon state's own institutions, the State Council, the examination-based civil service, the legal codes, and the palace itself, in an effort to restore it, and why that effort produced both achievements and growing resistance. Chapter 48 then takes up the diplomatic order that would change after the Daewongun's departure.

Understanding this decade matters beyond the biography of one powerful figure. It shows what reform from within the Neo-Confucian order could and could not accomplish on the eve of the opening of Korea. It also illustrates a broader problem in Korean history: the difficulty of separating principled resistance to foreign coercion from the defense of entrenched interests, and of recognizing that the same policy may be experienced as strengthening the state by one group and as a burden by another.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Yi Haeung came to hold decisive political influence in 1863–1864 and why historians distinguish his practical authority from Gojong's constitutional position and the regency of Queen Dowager Sinjeong.
  2. Trace the Daewongun's efforts to reduce the dominance of the Andong Kim and other in-law families and to broaden recruitment into government.
  3. Describe the reorganization of the Bibyeonsa, Uijeongbu, and Samgunbu and the legal consolidation represented by the Daejeon Hoetong and Yukjeon Jorye.
  4. Analyze the suppression of the seowon as a challenge to local elite privilege, and explain why it provoked Confucian resistance.
  5. Evaluate the fiscal measures of the period, including land-tax investigation, the sachang granaries, and the hopo household tax, as attempts to restore state revenue while reducing exemptions.
  6. Assess the political purposes, financing, and economic consequences of rebuilding Gyeongbokgung, including contributions, labor, tolls, and the issuance of the dangbaekjeon.
  7. Distinguish resistance to coercive Western demands from a supposed absence of foreign relations, situating the Daewongun's policies within Joseon's existing diplomacy with Qing China and Japan.
  8. Interpret the 1866 persecution, the French and American expeditions, and the cheokhwabi monuments as policy choices with political consequences rather than as isolated episodes.
  9. Explain the causes of the political crisis of 1873, including the memorial of Choe Ik-hyeon, Gojong's growing authority, and shifting court alliances.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Daewongun as reformer, defender of tradition, or both, using primary and secondary evidence responsibly.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What was the relationship between the Daewongun's informal power and the formal authority of King Gojong and Queen Dowager Sinjeong, and why does that distinction matter for how we describe the period?
  3. Why did a government seeking to reduce the privileges of elites simultaneously impose extraordinary costs to rebuild royal grandeur?
  4. Which reforms addressed problems identified in the crisis of the early 1860s, and which created new ones?

  8. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct the events of 1873, and what are the limits of that evidence?

Historical Context

By the early 1860s Joseon was a dynastic state more than four and a half centuries old, governed by a monarchy that ruled through a civil bureaucracy recruited by examination, a Confucian ideology of moral government, and a society ranked by status. Chapter 26 described the design of that state, and Chapters 37 through 42 showed how seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Joseon adapted to recovery, commercial growth, and intellectual change. The nineteenth century, as Chapter 43 explained, brought a different pattern. The kings who followed Jeongjo's death in 1800 were mostly young or inexperienced at accession, and political power gravitated to a few lineages related to the royal house by marriage, above all the Andong Kim. This pattern, called sedo jeongchi, did not abolish the monarchy, but it hollowed out the king's capacity to act independently of his wife's relatives.

The consequences, examined in Chapter 44, were fiscal and social. The three principal state revenue systems, the land tax, the military cloth tax, and the grain-loan system known as hwanjeong, had been corrupted by local officials, clerks, and powerful households, so that the burden fell most heavily on cultivators and commoners while many privileged holders paid little. The great rebellions of 1862, which began at Jinju under Yu Gye-chun and spread across several provinces, demonstrated how far this resentment had spread, though the dynasty's brief attempt at tax reform in that year did not survive. Chapter 46 added a different strand: the rise of the Donghak religion in the southeast, which the government regarded with suspicion.

Overlying domestic strain was a changing international environment, discussed in Chapter 45. Qing China, Joseon's principal diplomatic partner, was weakened by the Opium Wars and the Taiping Rebellion. Russia had acquired territory along the Tumen River in 1860, bringing a European empire to Joseon's northern border. Western ships appeared with increasing frequency along the Korean coast, and the Catholic Church, whose Korean adherents, by commonly cited estimates numbering roughly twenty thousand, faced periodic persecution, linked Joseon to missionary networks that the government regarded as foreign and politically dangerous.

Historians therefore approach the Daewongun's decade as a response to a specific accumulation of problems: a weak monarchy, an unreliable fiscal system, social unrest, and foreign pressure. They do not agree on how to weigh his achievements against his costs, and the debate, treated in Section 47.22, turns partly on whether one measures his program against Joseon's own inherited standards or against the later transformations of the Gabo Reforms and the Korean Empire, examined in Chapters 52 and 53.

Division I — Political Crisis and the Daewongun's Rise · Section 47.1

Joseon in Crisis at the End of King Cheoljong's Reign

King Cheoljong (r. 1849–1863) had come to the throne under circumstances that already signaled the monarchy's dependence. A distant royal relative living in obscurity on Ganghwa Island, he was chosen in 1849 after the death of the childless Heonjong, and his accession owed much to the Andong Kim lineage, which dominated court appointments through the royal marriage connections described in Chapter 43. Cheoljong's reign saw no reversal of that pattern. High offices, military commands, and provincial governorships were distributed through a network of related families and their clients, and the monarch's own political agency was limited by the fact that the officials around him often owed their positions to others.

The consequences for governance were cumulative rather than sudden. Local magistrates and clerks, whose appointments were influenced by well-placed patrons and who received little regular salary, financed themselves through irregular exactions. The land tax, the military cloth levy, and the grain-loan system, which together made up much of the state's revenue, were administered in ways that allowed influential households to reduce their liability and pushed the shortfall onto others. Chapter 44 traced how this produced the uprisings of 1862, and the dynasty's hasty creation of a reform bureau that same year, which the court abandoned once the immediate emergency passed, left the underlying problems unresolved.

The court also faced the early stages of external pressure. Reports reaching Seoul through the annual Beijing embassies described the defeat of the Qing in the Second Opium War and the destruction of the imperial summer palace in 1860. The Russian presence on the Tumen River, and the arrival of Western vessels in Korean waters, raised questions that the government was poorly placed to answer. Meanwhile, as Chapter 45 explained, Catholic communities had grown despite earlier persecutions and were again drawing official attention.

It is important not to read this situation as an inevitable decline. Many officials and scholars recognized the problems and proposed remedies, and the dynasty's institutions retained considerable capacity for repair, as the following decade would demonstrate. The crisis of the early 1860s created an opening for a determined political figure, and what that figure chose to do was not predetermined by the circumstances he inherited. Historians nevertheless generally agree that the weakness of the monarchy, the unreliability of the fiscal system, and the accumulated social grievance were the problems any new government would have to confront.

Section 47.2

Yi Haeung, King Gojong, and the Succession of 1863

Yi Haeung was born in 1820 into a branch of the royal Yi house that stood far from the throne. His family held royal rank and a measure of honor, but it lacked substantial political influence, and in the middle decades of the nineteenth century, with a monarchy dominated by in-law lineages, a collateral royal relative could be a risk to the powerful and a potential candidate for the throne. Later accounts, repeated in popular tradition, describe him as deliberately cultivating an appearance of dissipation and insignificance to avoid suspicion while he built relationships. Historians treat these stories cautiously: they appeared in later retellings and fit the tradition's picture of a patient, calculating figure, but the contemporary evidence for the specific anecdotes is limited.

What is better documented is the political position that his family occupied by the early 1860s. His second son, Yi Myeongbok, born in 1852, was a young boy of the royal line, and Cheoljong had no surviving son. When Cheoljong died in January 1864, the twelfth lunar month of 1863, the question of succession fell to the senior royal woman, Queen Dowager Sinjeong, the widow of the late Crown Prince Hyomyeong and a member of the Pungyang Jo family. In Joseon practice, a senior dowager could designate the heir when the king died without a successor, and Sinjeong used that authority to select the eleven-year-old boy, who became King Gojong. Because the succession was arranged through adoption into the line of Crown Prince Hyomyeong, the legal and ritual position of Gojong's biological father was unusual: as the king's birth father who was not himself a king, Yi Haeung received the title Daewongun and the honorific Heungseon.

Historians ask why Sinjeong chose him. Several explanations are offered, and they are not mutually exclusive. The dowager and her kin, the Pungyang Jo, had their own reasons to prefer an arrangement that would counterbalance the Andong Kim, whose power had long overshadowed hers. Yi Haeung had reportedly maintained connections with members of that family. A young king with a politically inexperienced but ambitious father also offered a way to restore royal authority without installing another in-law lineage as the dominant faction. These explanations rest partly on later reconstruction, but they agree on the main point: the selection of Gojong was a political decision made within a field of competing interests, not simply a response to dynastic necessity.

Historical Vocabulary
Daewongun, Regency, and Personal Rule

Definition. Daewongun was a title for the birth father of a king who had himself never reigned, which arose when a king was adopted into the line of his predecessor. Suryeom cheongjeong ("listening to government from behind a screen") referred to a regency exercised by a senior royal woman on behalf of a young king. Chinjeong means the monarch's personal rule.

Historical context. When Gojong was enthroned, Queen Dowager Sinjeong held the formal regency, and Yi Haeung held the title of Daewongun. No law gave a Daewongun a right to govern. His authority came from his relationship to the young king, from the dowager's willingness to defer to him, and from his control over appointments and relationships at court.

Why it matters. Describing the Daewongun as "regent" can obscure this arrangement. The distinction explains why his authority was powerful but always vulnerable: it depended on the consent of the dowager and, increasingly, on the goodwill of a king who would reach adulthood.

Potential misunderstanding. Many accounts say he "ruled as regent from 1863 to 1873." More precisely, the formal regency belonged to the Queen Dowager, ended in 1866, and was followed by a period in which Gojong was the formal ruler while the Daewongun's influence remained dominant until 1873.

Section 47.3

The Daewongun and the Exercise of Royal Authority

The formal constitution of Joseon authority in the first years of Gojong's reign had three components. The king was the source of legitimate rule, but a boy of eleven could not govern. The Queen Dowager held the regency and issued directives in the royal name. And Yi Haeung, without a formal office in the civil bureaucracy, held the title of Daewongun and a recognized place of honor and influence. The question for historians is how power actually passed through these arrangements.

The mechanism was largely informal. The Daewongun's residence at Unhyeongung in Seoul became a center to which officials came, and decisions on appointments, memorials, and policy were shaped in consultation with him. The regency's directives, issued in the Queen Dowager's name, conveyed his preferences and gave them legal form. Officials who cooperated with him gained office; those who did not could be removed. This system depended on trust between him and Sinjeong, on a circle of loyal officials, and on a continuing perception that his authority reflected the wishes of the royal family. It did not depend on a formal constitutional entitlement, and it could be challenged if those conditions changed.

A significant moment came in 1866. Gojong, by then about thirteen by Western reckoning, was married that year to the daughter of the Min family, and the Queen Dowager formally ended her regency. This transferred the formal exercise of royal authority to the king, but the political reality did not change immediately. The Daewongun continued to exert powerful influence, and many officials continued to treat his wishes as decisive. The change nevertheless mattered in principle: from 1866 the Daewongun's authority rested even more completely on the willingness of a growing king to defer to his father.

This explains a central feature of the period's politics. The Daewongun's power was real, but it had no independent legal base, and so he needed to build institutions and alliances that could sustain it. That helps explain the vigor with which he restructured government, rewrote laws, and rebuilt the palace. It also explains why his position became precarious once other actors, Queen Min and her kin, Confucian scholars, and the king himself, began to see their interests as different from his. Chapters later in this volume show how these tensions shaped the court after 1873.

Section 47.4

Breaking the Monopoly of the In-Law Families

The Daewongun's first political priority was to reduce the control of the Andong Kim and similar lineages over high office. This did not require destroying them. Some Andong Kim officials continued in service, and some accommodated themselves to the new arrangement. But the Daewongun reshuffled appointments so that the kin network that had controlled the State Council, the military commands, and key provincial posts lost its monopoly. Close allies, including members of the royal clan, the Pungyang Jo connection, and officials who had previously been marginal, moved into important positions. Jo Du-sun, an experienced official, became a leading figure in the new government and in its legal and administrative projects.

A second element was broader recruitment. Joseon politics had been shaped by factions, whose long contest over court office was described in Chapters 37 and 42, and by regional and lineage disadvantages that excluded some groups from high posts. The Daewongun's government promoted a policy often described as tangpyeong, a balanced recruitment that drew on members of different factions, including the Namin and Bukin, and favored able officials regardless of earlier affiliation. Some officials with local or regional backgrounds, previously excluded from the highest positions, entered government. The aim was to widen the pool of loyal talent and reduce the capacity of any single lineage to dominate.

The government also tried to ensure that the monarchy would not be captured by a new in-law family. The choice of Gojong's consort illustrates this concern. The young woman selected in 1866 came from a branch of the Yeoheung Min family that lacked prominent senior male relatives, which, according to many accounts, made her an attractive choice for a Daewongun who wished to avoid creating another powerful in-law network. Historians note the irony that this calculation failed: as Section 47.19 explains, the Min family and its allies became a significant political force and ultimately played a role in the end of his influence.

These measures produced a government answerable more directly to the monarchy and to the Daewongun's own circle, which marked a real change from the 1850s. They did not, however, constitute a social revolution. The officials promoted were drawn from the same yangban class, educated in the same Confucian curriculum, and committed to the same political order. The reform aimed to reallocate power within the elite and to restore the center's capacity to direct it, not to alter the social basis of government.

Division II — Rebuilding Royal Government and Challenging Elite Privilege · Section 47.5

The Bibyeonsa, Uijeongbu, and Samgunbu Reorganization

The Bibyeonsa, or Border Defense Council, had been created in the sixteenth century as a body to coordinate military matters, and by the nineteenth century it had acquired authority far beyond its original purpose. Its senior members, drawn from the highest officials, increasingly handled general policy. The Uijeongbu, the State Council, which Chapter 26 described as the formal apex of the civil bureaucracy, had in practice lost much of its role. To the Daewongun, who wanted to rebuild a structure of government that could be coordinated from the center, this shift was a problem: it concentrated power in a body that had been shaped by the dominance of in-law families.

His government restored the State Council's functions and reduced the Bibyeonsa's political role in the middle of the 1860s, returning it nominally to military and border matters. Military authority was concentrated in the Samgunbu, the Three Armies Directorate, which was revived in 1868 as a body to coordinate military affairs. Secondary sources date the stages of this process somewhat differently, and some describe the Bibyeonsa as abolished, while others emphasize that it was reduced and then absorbed. For students, the key point is the direction of change: policy-making authority was returned to formal organs of government, and military matters were organized under a body that could be supervised by the Daewongun's circle.

The reorganization has a double significance. It was an attempt to restore the constitutional design of the dynasty by reviving institutions that had been marginalized, and it also served the Daewongun's political position by dismantling a structure that had supported rival interests. Historians sometimes debate which purpose predominated. Most agree that the two were intertwined, and that the reforms represented a conscious attempt to recover an older ideal of how Joseon should be governed.

Section 47.6

Law, Administration, and the Reconstruction of Government

Joseon's legal system was built around the Gyeongguk Daejeon, the foundational administrative code completed in the fifteenth century, and later supplements. By the nineteenth century, successive laws, royal edicts, and precedents had accumulated, and officials worked with a layered body of regulation that was difficult to consult and sometimes contradictory. Chapters 26 and 37 described earlier efforts to update the code. The Daewongun's government undertook a further consolidation.

In 1865 the Daejeon Hoetong was compiled, bringing together the earlier codes and later regulations into a single comprehensive work. Two years later, in 1867, the Yukjeon Jorye, an administrative compendium organized around the Six Ministries, set out the duties and procedures of the central bureaucracy. Senior officials, including Jo Du-sun, supervised these projects. They did not create a new legal order, and their content was largely drawn from existing law, but the act of compilation had political significance. It declared that the dynasty's laws were being brought into order, gave officials a common reference, and reinforced the claim that the monarchy governed through a stable, rule-bound system.

Legal consolidation also served practical administration. A clear code made it easier for the center to evaluate local conduct and to insist on common standards. Historians note, however, that publishing a code did not ensure compliance. The problem, examined in Section 47.8, was often not ignorance of the rules but the incentives and power relationships that led officials and local elites to disregard them.

Section 47.7

The Suppression of Seowon and Local Aristocratic Privilege

Seowon were private academies, founded from the sixteenth century onward to honor Confucian worthies and to educate local scholars. By the nineteenth century, hundreds were scattered across the country. They combined education, ritual, and local social organization, and many held land, received royal recognition, and enjoyed exemption from certain taxes and labor service. Their communities of students and sponsors formed networks that could exert influence on local government and, through memorials to the throne, on national politics. Many were linked to lineages and scholarly factions, and some had become centers of local power and economic privilege.

To the Daewongun's government, these institutions posed both a fiscal and a political problem. Land and people attached to seowon were often exempt from taxation, so the burden on other households was greater. The academies' influence also limited the central government's ability to enforce its will, and some collected levies from nearby villagers with little oversight. The government began with a symbolic strike. In 1865 it ordered the removal of Mandongmyo, a shrine at Hwayang in Chungcheong province established by followers of the scholar Song Si-yeol to honor the Ming emperors, a place of great significance to conservative Confucians and a symbol of loyalty to the vanished Ming dynasty. The move provoked protests, including collective appeals by scholars, and the government responded firmly.

In 1868 the government restricted academies that had not received official recognition, and in 1871 it ordered the most sweeping measure: the abolition of the great majority of seowon, with only forty-seven retained, those honoring the most eminent figures under the principle that each person should be honored at no more than one site. Many academies were closed, their land and privileges reviewed, and their scholars dispersed. The government justified the measure as ending abuse of the people and restoring proper Confucian learning, and it presented itself as acting in the name of the monarchy's authority over local elites. The action was a major challenge to the social foundations of the yangban's local standing.

The resistance was strong. Scholars gathered at the capital to protest, submitted memorials, and in some cases refused to leave. The Daewongun, according to a tradition recorded in later accounts, declared that he would not tolerate institutions that harmed the people even if Confucius himself returned to life. Historians cannot verify the exact wording and treat the remark as traditional rather than documented, though it captures how supporters and critics understood his position. The suppression weakened a major pillar of local elite authority, and it generated bitterness that later contributed to his political isolation, as Section 47.20 explains.

Historical Vocabulary
Seowon

Definition. A private Confucian academy, typically including a lecture hall, a shrine honoring one or more Confucian scholars, and residential facilities for students.

Historical context. The first seowon appeared in the sixteenth century, and the institution spread widely in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, supported by local scholar-gentry and often given royal recognition. Many acquired land, labor, and tax exemptions.

Why it matters. The seowon was both an educational institution and an instrument of local power. Suppressing it meant confronting that power.

Potential misunderstanding. The seowon is not equivalent to a modern school. It also had ritual, social, and political functions, and its closure should not be read simply as a measure against education.

Government Directives and Arguments on the Suppression of Seowon, 1865–1871

Creator: the royal government acting through edicts issued in King Gojong's name, with the Daewongun's policy behind them · Date: 1865–1871 · Type: royal directives and court discussion preserved in the Seungjeongwon Ilgi and the Gojong Sillok; counter-memorials by Confucian scholars

Context: The measures against Mandongmyo and the seowon were announced as royal directives, and the surrounding court discussion and scholars' protests were recorded in the administrative diaries and annals of the reign. The Seungjeongwon Ilgi was kept by the royal secretariat as the contemporary daily record. The Gojong Sillok, in contrast, was compiled in the twentieth century under the colonial government, and historians use it with that fact in mind.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of the content of the government's position, not a quotation.

The directives argued that academies established to honor worthy scholars had become places of private exploitation: they sheltered people and land from public obligations, imposed demands on neighboring villagers, and served as bases for factional pressure on government. The remedy was to preserve a limited number of academies honoring truly eminent figures, one site for each, and close the rest. Scholars' counter-memorials argued that the academies were central to Confucian education and ritual, that closing them damaged moral life, and that the Ming-loyalist shrine at Hwayang expressed fidelity to the Confucian order.

Analysis: The source shows that the government framed the closures in Confucian terms, as restoring the proper purpose of learning, rather than as an attack on Confucian institutions. The scholars' replies show that they saw the matter as an assault on moral and ritual order. Both sides appealed to the same ideological tradition.

Limitations: The records mostly preserve the viewpoints of the court and of scholars able to write memorials. They say little about villagers who bore the exactions of some academies, or who may have welcomed or ignored the closures. The Gojong Sillok, compiled later, may reflect compilers' choices.

Perspective: The perspectives represented are those of the royal government and the scholar-gentry. Farmers and local officials appear indirectly.

Section 47.8

Provincial Officials, Local Government, and the Limits of Central Reform

Joseon governed its provinces through governors and magistrates appointed by the center, assisted by local clerks, and checked in principle by secret royal inspectors and by the Confucian scholar-gentry whose opinions shaped local politics. The reforms of the 1860s depended on this machinery. If magistrates and clerks continued to collect irregular levies, the central government's tax reforms would not reach the farmers.

The Daewongun's government pressed for stricter discipline. It sent inspectors to review local administration, punished officials for abuses, and issued directives against unauthorized collections. Contemporary and later accounts credit it with periods of greater vigilance, and some local officials were disciplined. The consolidation of laws gave the center clearer standards, and the removal of tax-exempt privileges aimed to broaden the base from which revenue could be drawn.

The limits, however, were considerable. Clerks and magistrates who benefited from irregular collection had incentives to evade supervision. Local scholar-gentry, offended by the seowon closures and the new taxes on households, were not reliable partners and could obstruct implementation. The center's information about local conditions remained imperfect, and the effectiveness of inspection depended on the honesty of the inspectors themselves. Historians distinguish between reforming legal institutions, which the center could do by decree, and securing compliance across the countryside, which required cooperation that the Daewongun's methods often alienated.

This gap between law and enforcement is a central theme of the chapter. It helps explain why the measures described in the following sections, some of them well conceived in principle, had mixed results in practice, and why a program that increased the center's formal power could still leave rural communities facing heavy and sometimes arbitrary demands.

Division III — Fiscal, Economic, Social, and Palace Reforms · Section 47.9

Land Taxation, Exemptions, and State Revenue

The land tax was the foundation of Joseon state revenue. It was assessed on land measured in units called gyeol, registered in cadastral records, and collected in grain, cloth, or cash by local officials. In principle the system rested on accurate registers. In practice, as Chapter 44 explained, land could be omitted from the registers, underassessed, or granted exemptions, and clerks could add unauthorized surcharges. The result was that the burden varied unpredictably and fell heavily on those least able to resist.

The Daewongun's government sought to restore central control over this revenue. It ordered investigations to uncover land hidden from the registers, scrutinized holdings that claimed exemptions, and tried to curb unauthorized levies by local officials. Where it could enforce these measures, the aim was to bring more land into the tax base and to reduce the pressure on cultivators who had borne extra charges. The closing of seowon, which removed exemptions on academy land and people, was part of the same effort, as were measures aimed at privileged palace and institutional holdings.

The effects are difficult to measure. The surviving records do not allow a precise estimate of how much additional revenue the investigations produced, and historians caution against assuming that decrees translated into collections. The central government clearly sought to increase revenue, and the scale of its spending on the palace and on military preparations, discussed below, made that goal pressing. But the structural problem, the dependence of tax collection on local officials and elites who had reasons to resist, persisted. The government's response to these limits was often to supplement the regular tax with extraordinary levies, which, as later sections show, created new burdens.

Section 47.10

Reforming Grain Loans and Rural Finance

The grain-loan system, hwanjeong, had begun as a form of relief. State granaries lent grain to farmers in the lean spring months, to be repaid after the harvest with a modest interest charge, and the interest helped finance the granaries and local administration. Over time the system changed. Officials turned lending into compulsion, forcing households to take loans they did not need and repay with interest, and in many places recorded loans that never existed. Chapter 44 identified this as one of the three great sources of rural grievance, and it had been a central target of the 1862 uprisings.

The Daewongun's government responded by promoting the sachang, community granaries managed at the village level. The idea, drawn from Confucian precedent, was that villagers would contribute grain to a local store, administered by respected local people, and lend from it to members of the community at limited interest. This promised to reduce the opportunities for corruption by officials and clerks, since the granary would be locally supervised. The government encouraged its spread in the later 1860s, and it was presented as a way to restore the original purpose of grain relief.

Implementation was uneven. A system that relied on local management could work where there were trustworthy local leaders, but it also depended on the same local elites whose cooperation the government had strained. Contributions to the sachang were themselves a burden for villagers, and officials could subvert the new arrangement as they had the old. Historians disagree about how widely and how durably it succeeded. It is best understood as a sincere attempt to address a recognized abuse that met the persistent difficulty of making reform effective at the village level.

Section 47.11

The Hopo System and the Burden of Military Taxation

The military cloth tax, gunpo, had originated as payment in lieu of military service, and since the eighteenth century it had been reduced to a fixed amount of cloth per adult male. In theory it applied to commoner men, while yangban families, who regarded themselves as exempt from such obligations, did not pay. Over time the system suffered from the same abuses as the others: clerks could collect for dead men and infants, and families that could not evade the levy paid for those who disappeared from the rolls. Reformers had proposed a household-based tax, the hopo, that would include privileged households, and the idea had been debated in earlier reigns without being adopted over yangban opposition.

In 1871 the Daewongun's government instituted the hopo. The tax was assessed on households rather than on individual men, at a rate commonly reported as two nyang per household, and it applied to yangban households as well as commoner ones. Accounts report that yangban households could pay under the name of a household slave, a face-saving device that avoided the appearance of a gentleman being taxed directly. The measure represented a real redistribution of burden: households that had long escaped military taxation now contributed, and the weight on commoners who had borne the levy alone was reduced.

Yangban resistance was predictable, and the government stood firm. The reform illustrates the Daewongun's willingness to press against elite privilege when the fiscal and political reasons aligned, and many historians view it as one of his most significant achievements, since it moved toward the principle that tax obligations should not depend on social status. Its significance should not be overstated, however. It left other inequalities untouched, did not resolve the problem of local collection, and coincided with heavy new levies that fell on the same households. The Gabo reformers of Chapter 52 would later go further in dismantling status distinctions.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Taxpaying Household in the 1860s

For a commoner household in the countryside, the decade brought overlapping changes that central policy treated separately. The household faced the land tax, which was assessed on the fields it cultivated or rented. It faced obligations connected to the military cloth tax, which after 1871 became a household charge. It might borrow or be compelled to borrow from the granary system, whether the older hwanjeong or the new sachang. In addition, it could be called on for labor, for example to haul timber or stone for the palace, or to contribute to the extraordinary levies described in Section 47.13. A yangban household, by contrast, might for the first time face a military tax, while the closing of a local seowon altered the social life of the district's gentry and the livelihoods of those associated with it.

Urban residents in Seoul experienced the period differently. They lived beside the palace construction, saw the stockpiling of materials, and encountered new tolls at the city's gates. Merchants and artisans handled the new coin, whose inflated nominal value disrupted prices. For households that depended on daily purchases, a shift in the value of currency could affect food costs immediately. The surviving sources are written overwhelmingly by officials and scholars, so these experiences must be reconstructed from official complaints, memorials, and later accounts. They show that reform, as lived, was a combination of changed obligations, uncertain prices, and shifting local authority, rather than a set of discrete decrees.

Section 47.12

Rebuilding Gyeongbokgung Palace, 1865–1868

Gyeongbokgung had been built at the founding of the dynasty in the 1390s, as Chapter 25 described, as the principal palace of the new capital. It was burned during the Imjin War of 1592, discussed in Chapters 32 through 35, and was not rebuilt after the war. The kings of the later dynasty resided mainly at Changdeokgung and other palaces, and the site of the old principal palace lay largely empty for roughly two and a half centuries. Earlier rulers had considered rebuilding it, but the cost and the demands of recovery from war and invasion prevented action.

In 1865, with the Queen Dowager's authorization, the government announced reconstruction. The decision had a clear political logic. A great palace, rebuilt at the dynasty's original site, would symbolize the restoration of royal authority at a moment when the monarchy had been weakened by decades of in-law dominance. It would also display the Daewongun's capacity to command resources and labor on a large scale. The project was organized through a special office and drew on officials, artisans, laborers, and materials from across the country. Work was substantially completed in 1868, and the king moved into the new palace that year. Accounts commonly report that the rebuilt complex included well over three hundred buildings, though the figures vary with how structures are counted.

The palace's architectural significance is real. The reconstruction recovered the formal plan of the dynasty's ceremonial and administrative core, and it preserved design traditions of Joseon court architecture. Gwanghwamun, the principal gate, the throne hall, and the surrounding complex embodied the ideals of orderly Confucian kingship. The decision to rebuild was therefore not frivolous. It was a calculated political statement. Its costs, however, were heavy, and the methods used to meet them are the subject of the next two sections.

Section 47.13

Financing Reconstruction: Donations, Labor, Materials, and New Taxes

The government did not possess the surplus to pay for a palace of this scale from regular revenue, and it devised a combination of measures. The first was wonnapjeon, a system of contributions described as voluntary donations. Officials, yangban, and wealthy households were invited, and in practice often pressed, to give money, and donors could receive honors or favor in return. Because the donations were nominally voluntary, they avoided the formal appearance of a new tax, but critics at the time and later historians observed that refusal was difficult, particularly for those who depended on official goodwill.

The second element was labor. The construction required large numbers of workers, and the government drew on corvée obligations, on hired labor, and on artisans mobilized from many regions. Materials, above all timber and stone, were procured from across the country, and the demand for timber led to the felling of trees on private and protected lands, provoking complaints from owners. The third element was new taxation. The government imposed gate tolls on goods entering Seoul and additional levies on land, and it drew on other extraordinary charges. Sources date and name these measures with some variation, but they agree that the sum of the levies was heavy and that they fell unevenly.

The unequal burden is crucial to understanding the political consequences. Wealthier households could often absorb or avoid the charges, while ordinary taxpayers and laborers bore demands for money and work that they could not decline. Merchants faced tolls that raised the cost of trade into the capital. A government that had come to power promising to reduce abuse and restore equity was now making extraordinary demands for a purpose, the palace, that many in the countryside regarded as remote from their concerns. This created the central tension of the Daewongun's program: the effort to reduce entrenched fiscal privilege was financed in part by new burdens that undermined the support the reforms might otherwise have won.

Historical Vocabulary
Wonnapjeon and Dangbaekjeon

Definition. Wonnapjeon means "money willingly submitted," a donation system used to finance the palace. Dangbaekjeon means "coin worth one hundred," a high-denomination coin issued in the later 1860s.

Historical context. Both were extraordinary measures adopted to raise funds beyond the regular revenue system. Wonnapjeon relied on nominal voluntarism, and dangbaekjeon on the state's ability to set the face value of coin.

Why it matters. Together they show how a government with limited regular income turned to devices that shifted costs onto the population indirectly.

Potential misunderstanding. "Voluntary" donations were not necessarily voluntary in practice, and a coin's nominal value is not its economic value.

Section 47.14

Dangbaekjeon, Currency Inflation, and Economic Disruption

By the mid-1860s, copper coinage had become central to Joseon commerce, as Chapter 39 described, and the standard coin, the sangpyeong tongbo, circulated widely. The government turned to coinage as a revenue source. The dangbaekjeon, authorized in late 1866 and in circulation by 1867, was assigned a nominal value of one hundred times the standard coin, while its metal content was worth only a small multiple of it, commonly estimated at about five or six times. The difference between nominal and intrinsic value was the state's profit. Official Korean historical research confirms that it circulated from 1867 and that its excessive nominal value contributed to serious inflation, and that the revenue supported general government expenses, not only the palace.

The market responded predictably. Merchants and the public did not accept the coin at its stated value, prices of goods rose, counterfeiting became attractive because the profit margin was large, and trade was disrupted in Seoul and other commercial centers. The government attempted to supplement the supply with imported Qing cash coins, and by 1868 it had abandoned the dangbaekjeon, though the monetary confusion persisted. The currency experiment thus brought short-term revenue at the price of price instability, damage to commercial confidence, and hardship for households dependent on market purchases.

Historians treat the episode as a significant failure and as an example of the limits of a state seeking resources through monetary means in a commercializing economy. It also illustrates a recurrent problem: when regular taxation could not be reformed effectively, the government turned to improvisation. Later Joseon governments, including that of the 1880s, would again resort to debased coinage, and the experience of the 1860s forms part of that history.

Division IV — Foreign Threats, Military Defense, and Diplomatic Resistance · Section 47.15

Joseon's Strategic Environment and Military Preparedness

The Daewongun's foreign policy must be set within Joseon's established diplomatic framework. For centuries Joseon had maintained a tributary relationship with the Qing and, before it, the Ming, which included regular embassies to Beijing and a recognition of Chinese primacy, while governing its own internal affairs. It also had a managed relationship with Japan, conducted through the Tsushima domain and the Japanese trading enclave at Busan, as Chapter 30 explained. Joseon was therefore not a closed state. It was a state with defined diplomatic and commercial channels, to which it restricted foreign contact.

What changed in the 1860s was the character of external pressure. Russia, which now shared a border on the Tumen River, sought trade and contact, and Korean farmers in the northern provinces, driven by hardship, began crossing into the Russian Far East, creating early Korean communities beyond the peninsula. Western warships and merchant vessels appeared with growing frequency, and news from China showed what could follow. Qing weakness reduced the reliability of its protection.

Joseon's military capacity was limited. Its capital garrisons and provincial forces were underfunded, and its weapons, including matchlocks and traditional cannon, were outdated compared with steam warships and modern artillery. The Daewongun's government sought to improve coastal defenses, especially around Ganghwa Island, the gateway to the Han River and the capital, to strengthen fortifications, and to improve weaponry. The Samgunbu coordinated these efforts, and sharpshooters were recruited from hunting populations in the north. Reports of experimental ships and improved artillery suggest a willingness to try new technology, but results were limited. His understanding of foreign threats was shaped by information from Beijing and from the Catholic and Russian questions, and it led him to conclude that accommodation would invite further demands.

Section 47.16

Catholicism, Foreign Diplomacy, and the Crisis of 1866

Chapter 45 narrates the Byeongin persecution of 1866, in which French missionaries and many Korean Catholics were put to death. This section addresses the policy decisions behind it. Early in the Daewongun's rule, the government was not uniformly hostile to Catholicism. Contemporary and later accounts report that some members of the court's circle had connections to Catholics, and in 1865–1866 some Korean Catholics proposed that Joseon seek an understanding with France and Britain as a counterweight to Russia. The proposal, which the Daewongun reportedly considered but did not carry through, failed to produce an agreement.

Historians offer several explanations for the shift to persecution in early 1866. One emphasizes the political risk: conservative Confucian scholars, such as Yi Hang-ro and his followers, condemned Catholicism as a threat to ancestral ritual and social order, and a government that appeared tolerant could be accused of abandoning orthodoxy at a moment when its own legitimacy rested on a defense of the Confucian tradition. A second emphasizes international anxiety: news of Western expansion in China and the perceived link between missionaries and foreign powers made the Church appear as a potential channel of foreign interference. A third stresses the failure of the diplomatic approach. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the exact weight of each remains debated.

The persecution was severe, and it cost the lives of nine of the twelve French missionaries then in Korea and, by commonly cited estimates, thousands of Korean Catholics, though figures vary. Its consequences extended beyond religion. It provided the pretext for the French expedition of the same year, and it tied the Daewongun's government to a policy that would be remembered as intolerant. Historians note that it was also a political choice, shaped by the balance of forces at court, rather than the automatic expression of a fixed ideology.

Section 47.17

The French and American Expeditions as Tests of Government Policy

The naval confrontations of 1866 and 1871 are narrated in Chapter 45. Here they are considered as tests of the government's policy. In 1866 the American armed merchant ship General Sherman went up the Taedong River toward Pyongyang and was destroyed after a confrontation with local forces. A French naval force that autumn occupied part of Ganghwa Island, in response to the killing of missionaries, and withdrew after encountering resistance. In 1871 an American squadron, accompanied by a diplomat seeking a treaty and an explanation of the General Sherman affair, engaged Korean forts at Ganghwa, inflicting heavy Korean casualties, commonly cited at roughly two hundred and forty killed, before withdrawing without a treaty.

For the government, these events carried several implications. Militarily, they showed the technological gap: Korean defenders fought bravely but were outgunned. Politically, however, the departure of both expeditions without achieving their aims was interpreted at court as vindication. Foreign forces had come, been resisted, and left, which seemed to confirm the policy of refusing accommodation. The Daewongun's prestige as defender of the kingdom rose accordingly, and officials could argue that firmness worked.

Historians caution against accepting that interpretation uncritically. The foreign withdrawals reflected the limited aims and resources of the expeditions as much as Korean success, and the French occupation had included the removal of royal documents from Ganghwa, a loss that stung. The episodes also revealed vulnerability, and some officials drew different lessons. In 1868 an attempt by a German merchant, Ernst Oppert, with others, to open the tomb of the Daewongun's father to gain leverage showed how far foreign adventurers might go, and reinforced anti-foreign sentiment. The overall effect was to strengthen the government's confidence at a moment when the underlying military imbalance had not changed.

Section 47.18

Cheokhwa, Anti-Foreign Monuments, and the Defense of Sovereignty

Cheokhwa, "rejecting accommodation," was a position articulated by conservative scholars such as Yi Hang-ro. Its roots lay in the Neo-Confucian distinction between civilized order and barbarism and in the conviction that Western contact, commerce, and religion would erode the moral foundation of society. Its advocates argued that to treat with the West was to invite subordination, and that resistance, even at great cost, was necessary. The position drew strength from the Qing experience, which seemed to show what treaties could bring.

After the American expedition of 1871 the government erected stone monuments, the cheokhwabi, at Jongno in Seoul and at other sites around the country. Their inscriptions, in Classical Chinese, conveyed in brief form that Western incursion should be fought, that accommodation amounted to betraying the country, and that this commitment should be passed to descendants. They were an unmistakable public declaration of policy. Many were taken down in the following decades as policy changed.

It would be a mistake to treat the Daewongun's attitude as simple isolationism. The government maintained its relationship with Qing China and continued the managed relationship with Japan, but it resisted Western demands that came with armed pressure and sought to impose treaty terms. Its refusal in the same years to accept Japanese notifications of the Meiji government in customary form illustrates the point: the dispute turned on titles and diplomatic form, and it was the beginning of tensions Chapter 48 examines. Contemporary opinion was also not unified. Some officials, such as the scholar-official Pak Gyu-su, later argued for engagement, and the debate between accommodation and resistance continued within the court. The policy of cheokhwa represented one position among those available, one that the Daewongun championed with unusual force and that the government could for a time sustain.

Division V — Political Opposition, Loss of Power, and Historical Assessment · Section 47.19

Queen Min, Court Alliances, and the Changing Balance of Power

In 1866 Gojong married a young woman of the Yeoheung Min family, later known as Queen Min and, posthumously, Empress Myeongseong. As Section 47.4 noted, the Daewongun's household is reported to have favored her partly because her natal family lacked prominent senior male relatives, and the marriage was connected to his own family through kin relationships. She was intelligent, politically capable, and, as the king matured, increasingly influential. Historians caution that accounts of her are heavily shaped by later political and national narratives, hostile and sympathetic alike, and that her early years at court are poorly documented.

The balance of power in the palace shifted through several developments. Gojong grew from boy to young man and began to form his own judgments. The Queen's relatives, the Min, gained positions, and a political network gathered around her. Tensions over the royal succession deepened the divide. In 1868 a concubine of the king bore a son, whom the Daewongun is reported to have favored, while the Queen's own first child, born in 1871, lived only a few days. Later accounts connected this loss to mutual suspicion between the Queen and the Daewongun, but the details are contested and rest partly on rumor. What is clear is that by the early 1870s the court contained two centers of influence, and that the old alliances, including those that had brought the Daewongun to power, were shifting.

Kin relationships complicated the picture. The Pungyang Jo, who had supported the Daewongun's rise, did not necessarily remain aligned, and members of the Daewongun's own extended family took different sides. The dynamics resembled the politics of earlier reigns: marriage ties could generate new dominant lineages. The Daewongun had tried to prevent a repeat of in-law dominance by choosing a consort from a weakly connected family, but the Min network showed how quickly a royal marriage could create a new faction. This is a more accurate framework than reducing the period's politics to a personal rivalry.

Section 47.20

Growing Opposition to the Daewongun's Policies

By the early 1870s opposition came from several directions. Confucian scholars who had protested the closing of Mandongmyo and the seowon regarded his domestic measures as violations of the order he claimed to defend. Local gentry resented the loss of privileges and the new household tax. Merchants and urban residents suffered from tolls, inflation, and disrupted trade. Rural taxpayers, whose burdens had not been clearly reduced, felt little reason for gratitude. Some officials who had lost positions or influence also harbored grievances.

Historians distinguish among kinds of opposition. Some objections were principled: scholars sincerely believed that the suppression of academies and the extraordinary levies violated Confucian norms of benevolent government, and that the concentration of power in the king's father was improper while the king was of age to rule. Other opposition arose from lost interests, such as the tax exemptions of seowon and yangban households. The two overlapped, and the same person could hold both motives. The record usually presents arguments in the language of principle, and historians must infer the role of interest from context.

A notable feature of the opposition is its Confucian character. The Daewongun's domestic critics largely shared his anti-foreign stance. Many of them, including Yi Hang-ro's followers, supported the exclusion of Western contact. Their quarrel was with how he governed at home. This shaped the nature of the crisis: it did not turn on whether to accept foreign relations but on the legitimacy and burden of his domestic rule.

Section 47.21

Choe Ik-hyeon, King Gojong, and the Political Crisis of 1873

The crisis came in late 1873. Choe Ik-hyeon, a Confucian scholar of the school of Yi Hang-ro who held office in the government, submitted a memorial to the throne. In form it was advice to the king, but its content was a sharp criticism of government policy. It addressed the burdens placed on the population, the new taxes and currency measures, the closing of the academies and the removal of Mandongmyo, and the manner in which authority was exercised. Though framed with the conventional deference, it was widely understood as an attack on the Daewongun's administration and a call for the adult king to govern personally.

Choe Ik-hyeon's Memorial of 1873

Creator: Choe Ik-hyeon, Confucian scholar and official · Date: late 1873 (tenth lunar month, Gojong 10) · Type: memorial to the throne, preserved in court records and the Gojong Sillok

Context: Memorials were a standard channel by which officials and scholars advised or criticized the monarch, and criticism was traditionally expected to be framed as loyal counsel. Choe wrote at a time when Gojong was in his early twenties and the Daewongun's influence remained dominant. The text survives in reign records compiled with later editorial involvement, and the accuracy of specific wording should be checked against accessible editions before it is quoted.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of the memorial's main themes, not a quotation.

The memorial argued that government had drifted from the standards of Confucian rule: that heavy and novel exactions had burdened the people, that policies concerning academies and the Ming-loyalist shrine had harmed proper ritual and learning, and that those who held power had overreached. It urged the king, now of an age to rule, to take direct charge of government and to correct these policies.

Analysis: The memorial shows how critics used Confucian norms to challenge a leader who also claimed Confucian legitimacy. By addressing the king rather than the Daewongun, it used the proper form while carrying an unmistakable meaning.

Limitations: It represents one scholar's perspective, though one shared by many. It does not tell us how much the king had already decided, how far other court actors coordinated with Choe, or how ordinary taxpayers regarded the policies. The exact wording and context require careful verification.

Perspective: Conservative Confucian scholar-officialdom. Perspectives of the Daewongun, the Queen, merchants, and farmers are not directly represented.

Questions for students:

  2. Who wrote it, and in what circumstances?
  3. Why might a critique be framed as advice to the king?

The court's response is revealing. Officials aligned with the Daewongun denounced the memorial and called for punishment. Gojong, however, did not rebuke Choe and instead signaled approval, promoting him to office. This indicated that the king was prepared to challenge his father's position. Soon afterward Choe was sent into exile on Jeju, a measure interpreted by some historians as a concession to the Daewongun's supporters even as the political result favored the king. In the same weeks, Gojong declared that he would exercise personal rule, officials associated with the Daewongun were removed, and the Daewongun withdrew from the capital to a residence in Yangju.

Why did this happen? Historians identify several converging factors: the king's maturity and desire for authority, the realignment of court factions around Queen Min and her kin, the accumulated resentment of scholars and the economic burdens of the 1860s, and the absence of any formal legal basis for the Daewongun's power. Explaining the outcome as a personal rivalry with the Queen alone is insufficient. The Queen's faction was a participant, but Choe's initiative came from the Confucian scholarly world, and the king's support reflected his own interests. The Daewongun's displacement was achieved without violence, but his supporters remained a force, and he would return to political prominence later, as subsequent chapters show.

Multiple Perspectives

The Same Reforms, Experienced Differently

Note: these are analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians, not quotations from named individuals.

Royal government and the Daewongun's circle: The measures restored institutions, curbed in-law dominance, broadened the tax base, and rebuilt the symbols of dynastic authority. Costs were regrettable but necessary to renew a weakened state, and resistance reflected privilege defending itself.
Yangban and local scholar-gentry: The closing of academies, the loss of exemptions, and the household tax attacked their standing, and the extraordinary levies violated Confucian ideals of benevolent rule. Their objections mixed principle and interest.
Rural taxpayers and laborers: Some welcomed relief from the military cloth burden and from abuses of the grain loans. But corvée labor, extra levies, and uncertain prices meant that the state's demands often remained heavy, and the sources rarely preserve their voices directly.
Merchants and urban residents: Gate tolls, inflation, and a coin of doubtful value disrupted trade and household budgets, though palace construction also created work and demand for some.
Section 47.22

The Results and Contradictions of the Daewongun's Reform Program

Assessment must begin with achievements. The Daewongun's government curtailed the political monopoly of in-law families, revived formal institutions of government, consolidated the legal codes, extended military taxation to privileged households, and curtailed the economic and political power of the seowon. It rebuilt the principal palace and projected an image of renewed royal authority. These were substantial accomplishments, and some, notably the principle that elite households should share fiscal burdens, anticipated later reforms.

The contradictions are equally important. The program sought to reduce privilege while imposing extraordinary costs that fell on ordinary people. It defended a Confucian order while antagonizing Confucian scholars. It increased the center's formal power but left the problem of local compliance unsolved. It sought to strengthen the kingdom's defenses while the underlying military gap remained, and its currency policy damaged the commercial economy. Its authority rested on informal power that could not be passed on or made permanent.

Historical Debate

Was the Daewongun a Reformer, a Defender of Tradition, or Both?

The question: Historians differ on how to characterize the Daewongun's program, and on what standard to apply.

Interpretation A — Conservative obstacle to modernization: Older accounts, influential in the twentieth century, emphasized his anti-Western policies and described him as a conservative who delayed Korea's adaptation to the modern world, contrasting him with later reformers.
Interpretation B — Reformer within the old order: Other scholarship stresses his administrative, legal, and fiscal reforms, particularly the hopo and the curtailing of in-law and seowon privilege, as serious attempts at state strengthening that drew on Joseon's own reform tradition.
Interpretation C — Both, with costs: A third approach holds that the two descriptions are not exclusive: he reformed in order to defend, and the methods he used undermined the order he sought to preserve.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same records, the Sillok, the Seungjeongwon Ilgi, legal compilations, and memorials, but weigh differently the domestic reforms, the foreign policy, and the economic costs.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and standards rather than basic chronology: whether to judge him by Joseon's own reform traditions or by the later modernizing agenda, and how to weigh intentions against results.

Why it matters: The choice shapes how the decade is placed in the story of Korea's opening, either as an obstacle or as the last major attempt at internal renewal.

Student task: Evaluate the three interpretations using evidence from Sections 47.4 through 47.18, and identify what additional evidence would help to decide among them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Institutional Change and the Surviving Joseon Order, 1863–1873

What Changed

  • The in-law monopoly over high office was broken, and recruitment was broadened
  • The State Council and Samgunbu were revived as organs of policy and military authority
  • The legal codes were consolidated
  • The seowon network was drastically reduced, and privileged households were drawn into military taxation
  • The principal palace was rebuilt, and an extraordinary mix of levies and currency came into use

What Stayed the Same

  • Government remained monarchy ruling through a Confucian examination-based yangban bureaucracy
  • The status system and slavery persisted, with only the military tax touching privilege
  • The tributary relationship with Qing China and the managed relationship with Japan continued
  • Local administration still depended on clerks and local elites whose cooperation was uncertain

Pattern. Change was concentrated at the top, in institutions and law, and was deliberately restorative in aim. It was most strongly felt by in-law lineages, scholar-gentry, and taxpayers, and least by the structure of status and the basis of the state. Its significance lies in showing both what reform from within could achieve and where it stopped. Chapters 52 and 53 show later reformers going further.

Section 47.23

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The Daewongun's decade ended with the monarchy more assertive in form but fiscally strained, diplomatically committed to resisting Western treaty demands, and politically divided. Gojong now ruled in his own name, and the court was shaped by new alignments. The diplomatic questions left unresolved, including Japan's relations with Korea and the pressure for treaties, did not wait. Chapter 48 examines how they were resolved and how the old diplomatic order ended in 1876. The review materials that follow ask students to synthesize the chapter and to apply the habits of historical thinking it has used: distinguishing formal authority from practical power, weighing intention against result, and reading sources with attention to their makers and limits.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the decade from 1863 to 1873, when Yi Haeung, the Heungseon Daewongun, dominated Joseon politics. His rise followed the death of Cheoljong, the selection of his young son Gojong by Queen Dowager Sinjeong, and a political setting shaped by in-law dominance, fiscal disorder, and social unrest. The chapter argued that his authority was powerful but informal: distinct from the formal regency of the Queen Dowager, which ended in 1866, and from Gojong's constitutional position.

The first reforms concerned the structure of government. The Daewongun reduced the dominance of the Andong Kim and other in-law families, broadened recruitment, restored the State Council's functions, curtailed the Bibyeonsa, and revived the Samgunbu. The compilation of the Daejeon Hoetong in 1865 and the Yukjeon Jorye in 1867 consolidated the legal and administrative framework and expressed the claim that the dynasty governed through ordered institutions.

A second group of measures challenged local elite privilege. The removal of Mandongmyo in 1865, restrictions in 1868, and the major suppression of 1871, which retained forty-seven academies, attacked the economic and political position of the seowon and provoked strong Confucian protest. The hopo of 1871 extended military taxation to yangban households, advancing the principle that tax obligation should not depend on status.

Fiscal and economic policy was the most contradictory area. The government investigated land and exemptions, promoted the sachang to reform grain loans, and rebuilt Gyeongbokgung between 1865 and 1868. It financed the palace through nominally voluntary contributions, labor, tolls, and additional levies, and through the dangbaekjeon, whose inflated nominal value brought inflation and commercial disruption. The reforms sought to reduce privilege while imposing extraordinary burdens.

Foreign policy must be understood within Joseon's established diplomacy with Qing China and Japan. The Daewongun resisted Western demands backed by force. His government carried out the Byeongin persecution in 1866 for reasons historians still debate, faced the French and American expeditions of 1866 and 1871, and erected the cheokhwabi in 1871. The withdrawal of the foreign forces strengthened the government's confidence even though the military gap remained.

Opposition grew from scholars offended by the academy closures and levies, from gentry who lost privileges, from merchants and taxpayers burdened by costs, and from a court whose alignments shifted as Gojong matured and Queen Min's connections gained influence. In late 1873 Choe Ik-hyeon's memorial, Gojong's support for the challenge, and the withdrawal of the Daewongun marked the end of his effective control.

The evidence for this decade comes from the Seungjeongwon Ilgi, the Gojong Sillok, legal codes, memorials, and foreign accounts, each with limits. The Sillok was compiled in the colonial era, and the experiences of ordinary people appear mostly indirectly. Historians therefore reason cautiously about the effects of reforms and about motives.

Interpretations differ. Older views stressed an obstacle to modernization, other scholarship stresses an ambitious reformer within the Joseon tradition, and many historians now hold that he was both. The chapter argued that the Daewongun tried to save the monarchy by reorganizing and strengthening existing institutions, but that the methods used also created burdens, opposition, and tensions within the court.

The decade matters for what comes next. It showed the achievements and limits of reform within the Neo-Confucian order, left the fiscal system strained, and bequeathed unresolved diplomatic questions, above all the relationship with Japan, to the government that followed. Chapter 48 turns to the Treaty of Ganghwa and the end of the old diplomatic order.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the last sustained attempt to renew Joseon from within before the crises of the opening. The Daewongun worked with the dynasty's own tools: its codes, its institutions, its ideology of Confucian kingship, and its palace. That choice reveals both the resilience and the limits of the old order. It could be reorganized and strengthened in important respects, but the methods that restored royal authority also produced burdens and resentments that the system could not easily absorb.

The chapter also illustrates a recurring problem in historical judgment: the difference between formal authority and effective power, and between intention and result. The Daewongun held no clear legal basis for his rule, yet shaped the state profoundly. He defended Confucian order while angering Confucian scholars. He reduced privilege while imposing costs that fell on the unprivileged. Understanding such contradictions does not require choosing between praise and blame but demands attention to evidence, context, and the experience of different groups.

The decade shaped what followed. The fiscal strain, the unresolved diplomatic questions, and the realignment of the court set the conditions for the events of the later 1870s and 1880s, and the reforms of the Daewongun later served as a reference point, positive and negative, for those who sought other paths. Chapter 48 turns to the Treaty of Ganghwa and the end of the old diplomatic order, and the chapters of Volume III take the story of reform, crisis, and sovereignty forward.
