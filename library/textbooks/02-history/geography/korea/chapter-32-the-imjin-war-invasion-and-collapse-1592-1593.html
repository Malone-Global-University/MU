The Imjin War: Invasion and Collapse, 1592–1593

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In roughly two months, an army that landed on the southeastern coast reached the northwestern city of Pyongyang, and the king of Joseon was heading for the Amnok River. Yet the dynasty did not fall.

Few events in Korean history seem as abrupt as the first months of the Imjin War. Between the landing at Busan in the fourth lunar month of 1592 and the fall of Pyongyang in the sixth, Japanese armies defeated Joseon's conventional defenses, occupied Hanyang, and spread across much of the peninsula. Contemporaries described the speed with shock, and later memory has often reduced it to a single image: a king fleeing north before an unstoppable army.

That image contains truth and distortion. Joseon did lose its major field armies, its capital, Pyongyang, and extensive territory within about two months. But it did not lose its monarchy, its government, or its capacity to command resources in the provinces. The chapter therefore asks two linked questions: how did Japanese forces overrun Joseon's conventional defenses so quickly, and how did the Joseon state survive the collapse of its armies and the loss of its capital?

The answer proposed here is multi-causal. Japanese success rested on experienced armies, operational speed, effective command, firearms, and the use of several columns. Those advantages interacted with Joseon's emergency mobilization system, dispersed forces, delayed information, uneven training, and inability to concentrate troops before the advance overtook them. Neither firearms alone, nor factionalism, nor cowardice explains the outcome, and the chapter resists explanations that assume the result was inevitable.

The evidence is uneven. The principal Korean source is the court annals, in two compilations made decades later, supplemented by official chronologies, memoirs, local records, and later commemorative paintings. Japanese campaign records offer another view. Almost all were produced by elites, so the experience of commoners must often be inferred.

The chapter follows Chapter 31, which explained why Joseon entered the crisis. It stops before the naval war of Chapter 33 and the righteous armies and Ming intervention of Chapter 34, previewing them only to explain why rapid advance did not become complete control. The renewed invasion of 1597 belongs to Chapter 35.

Historical Context

Chapter 31 traced the road to war: factional politics at court, Hideyoshi's unification of Japan, failed diplomacy, contested intelligence, and uneven military preparation. This chapter begins where that account ends, with the fleet crossing the strait. A few background points are needed to read what follows.

Joseon had gone roughly two centuries without a full-scale foreign invasion of the peninsula, though it had faced raids such as the Eulmyo raid of 1555 and recurrent fighting on its northern frontier. Its defense relied on regional garrisons, walls, and a system of emergency mobilization: when danger appeared, the court sent a general to command troops raised in each district. The system saved the cost of large standing armies but depended on time.

Japan, by contrast, had emerged from a long period of warfare under Hideyoshi, with veteran commanders and many firearms. Joseon also had a strong tradition of gunpowder weapons, so the contrast cannot be reduced to one side having firearms. What mattered was how weapons were combined with training, formation, command, and speed.

Finally, Joseon was a Confucian monarchy whose legitimacy rested on the king's presence and care for his subjects, with a bureaucracy that extended into the provinces. That structure explains both why the loss of the capital was politically severe and why the state could continue to function elsewhere.

Section 32.1

The Japanese Landings at Busan

Chapter 31 ended with Joseon's court divided, its intelligence uncertain, and its defenses unprepared for a campaign of the scale Toyotomi Hideyoshi had planned. The invasion began on the thirteenth day of the fourth lunar month of 1592 (late May in the Gregorian calendar), when the first division under Konishi Yukinaga, accompanied by troops associated with Tsushima, crossed the strait and landed near Busan. Dates in this chapter follow the lunar calendar of the Joseon court records. The first division is commonly estimated at about eighteen thousand men, though figures in Japanese and Korean sources differ and should be treated as approximations.

The setting mattered. Busan was already a point of contact: Japanese traders and officials had long been present in the port area, and Tsushima's ties to Joseon gave local commanders some familiarity with Japanese ships and people. That familiarity cut two ways. It meant the coast was not unknown territory, but it also made the first sightings of a large fleet ambiguous at the moment when clear recognition mattered most.

What, then, did surprise mean? Joseon officials had received warnings, and the court had taken some measures, including fortification work in the south. What was missing was an assembled field army at the point of attack. Joseon's system raised troops after danger appeared, and regional garrisons were small. The Japanese, by contrast, arrived as a concentrated seaborne force already organized into divisions, able to choose the point of landing and to attack within hours. That timing gave them an immediate operational advantage without implying that Joseon had no defenses. The first defenders fought, and their fate is the subject of the next section.

The invaders' stated aim, as Chapter 31 explains, was to pass through Joseon toward Ming China, and Joseon had refused to grant passage. For the commanders at the coast this meant that the landing was not a raid but the opening move of a campaign meant to continue across the peninsula. Local officials, however, had no means of confirming the scale until the fleet and its troops were already ashore, and the evidence suggests that many first reports were incomplete and reached the capital only after a delay.

Section 32.2

Busanjin and Dongnae: The First Defenses

The Japanese fleet appeared off Busan on the thirteenth. The main assault on Busanjin, the coastal garrison, began early on the fourteenth, and the fortress fell that day. Its commander, Jeong Bal, died in the fighting. The Japanese used massed firearms to suppress the walls and then assaulted with infantry, a method for which a garrison designed against raids was ill prepared. The interval between landing and fall was extremely short.

Dongnae, a walled administrative town a short distance inland, fell on the fifteenth. Its magistrate, Song Sang-hyeon, refused to yield and was killed. Later tradition attributes to him a message, written on a board, rejecting passage for the invaders. This story is a powerful part of later memory, but its exact wording and circumstances rest on later accounts and should be distinguished from the contemporary record, which establishes the fall of the town and his death. Garrison soldiers and townspeople were both caught inside the walls, and accounts record heavy killing of inhabitants; casualty figures vary widely and are treated cautiously here.

These engagements show that resistance began at once. They also show a structural mismatch: a defense built from local garrisons and walls met an army organized for campaigning. Their commemoration is a separate historical subject. In the late Joseon period, the state and local elites honored Jeong Bal and Song Sang-hyeon as loyal martyrs and commissioned commemorative paintings, discussed in the Primary Source Window. Students should treat such images as evidence of how later generations remembered sacrifice, not as eyewitness records of the assaults.

The evidence for these first fights is thinner than their fame suggests. The principal accounts were compiled by officials and memoirists after the fact, drawing on survivors' reports and on the later effort to honor the dead, so details such as the number of defenders, the course of each assault, and the final words attributed to the commanders vary between sources. The coastal naval forces of the region did not effectively contest the landing, a subject for Chapter 33, which left the garrisons to face the invaders alone. Historians therefore treat the secure core, the dates, the fall of the two positions, and the deaths of Jeong Bal and Song Sang-hyeon, separately from the later elaborations.

Section 32.3

Three Columns North

The Japanese army did not march as a single mass. The invading force was organized into numbered divisions, with Konishi Yukinaga's first division followed by Katō Kiyomasa's second and Kuroda Nagamasa's third, and further divisions behind them. Overall figures in the first wave are often given in the range of well over one hundred thousand, but estimates vary and should not be treated as exact.

The divisions took different roads. Konishi's route ran roughly through Miryang and Daegu toward Sangju and Chungju. Katō's followed an eastern line through the interior toward the same central region. Kuroda moved by a western approach through the Gimhae area and across the Chupungnyeong pass toward the Han River region. Sources differ on exact routes and timing, and the divisions sometimes converged and overlapped rather than moving in neat parallel lines.

Why split? Several roads let armies find food and fodder from a wider area, kept many districts under pressure at once, and made it impossible for Joseon commanders to know where a single stand would matter. The mountains channel movement into river valleys and passes, so the roads themselves defined the options. Speed depended on seizing towns and junctions and on not besieging every fortified place.

This should not be read as perfectly coordinated modern planning. Communication among columns was slow, commanders competed for position and credit, and supply remained a standing risk. In the early weeks, requisitioning from a lightly defended countryside kept the divisions fed, but the further they moved from Busan the thinner their link to the coast became, a problem Sections 32.10 and 32.13 take up.

The weapons the divisions carried help explain how they fought. The Japanese army fielded large numbers of matchlock arquebuses alongside spearmen, swordsmen, and archers, and its infantry had experience from the wars of Japan's preceding decades. Joseon had a long tradition of gunpowder weapons, but these were mostly larger cannon and older hand firearms, and its field forces relied heavily on archers and cavalry. The difference was less a simple gap in technology than a difference in how firearms were integrated into trained infantry formations, which is why the chapter treats weapons as one factor among several.

Section 32.4

Sangju and the Failure to Concentrate

Joseon's response relied on a system known as jeseung bangnyak, in which, when an invasion occurred, the court sent a general from the capital to take command of troops raised in each district. The system avoided the expense of large standing armies in peacetime and tied command to central appointment. Its weakness was timing: it assumed that officials could gather men, supplies, and commanders before the enemy arrived.

Yi Il was sent south to take command and tried to assemble forces at Sangju. By the time he reached the district, local administration was already disrupted, and the troops he could collect were few, hastily gathered, and poorly trained. Information was also late: the court's picture of Japanese movements lagged behind the actual advance. The Japanese reached the area before a defense could be organized, and Yi Il's force was defeated around the twenty-fourth to twenty-fifth day of the fourth month; sources differ slightly on the date. He withdrew toward Chungju to join the larger force under Sin Rip.

The episode shows how jeseung bangnyak failed under invasion conditions. The weakness lay in the system's dependence on time, not in the courage of individual commanders or soldiers. The Japanese tempo compressed the weeks Joseon required into days, and the central plan was overtaken before it could be carried out.

The quality of the troops raised at Sangju also reflected deeper institutional problems. By the sixteenth century, many of the military obligations of commoners had been commuted to payments of cloth, and historians commonly note that registers listing large numbers of eligible men did not correspond to trained soldiers available on short notice. Commanders sent from the capital therefore arrived in districts where the paper strength of the levy far exceeded the number who could actually be assembled and drilled. This is a structural explanation of Yi Il's predicament, one that does not depend on any failure of personal courage.

Section 32.5

Chungju and Tangeumdae

Sin Rip had a reputation as a capable commander with experience on the northern frontier, where he had fought Jurchen raiders, and the court placed unusual confidence in him. He was given authority to gather the largest force then available. After Yi Il's defeat at Sangju, this force was the main remaining field army between the Japanese advance and Hanyang. Its size is reported differently in surviving sources, and later numbers should be treated as estimates. It included cavalry and levied infantry, many of them hastily raised and untested.

Sin Rip took position at Chungju, where roads from the south converged. Surviving accounts say his advisers recommended holding the Joryeong pass, a mountain route where a smaller force might have exploited the terrain against columns strung out along the road. Sin Rip, according to the same tradition, rejected this partly because he believed his cavalry could operate more effectively on open ground, and perhaps also because he doubted the reliability of his infantry in a defile. He therefore fought near Tangeumdae, where the Dal and Han rivers bound a flat but wet and constricted area. The terrain proved poorly suited to the cavalry charges he intended.

On the twenty-eighth day of the fourth month (some modern reference chronologies differ by a couple of days) the Japanese attacked, using massed firearms against the charging horsemen and then closing with infantry. Joseon cavalry attacks failed, the army was pushed back against the river, and many soldiers were killed or drowned. Sin Rip died, according to accounts by plunging into the river. Reports of the scale of the slaughter vary, but all agree the army was destroyed as a field force.

Historians caution against calling his choice an obvious blunder. Hindsight makes Joryeong look attractive, but a defense there would have depended on inexperienced troops holding a pass against veteran infantry, and the army might have been bypassed. The more secure judgment is that Joseon had one concentrated army and one decisive chance, and that the loss left no comparable force before the capital. Its strategic consequence was immediate: when news reached Hanyang, the court faced an empty defensive space.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary: Bunjo (Branch Court)

Definition: a secondary court headed by the heir, able to act for the crown in the provinces. In 1592 it was led by Gwanghaegun, and it operated from about June 1592 until about October 1593.

Historical context: the idea of dividing the court was an emergency measure adopted when the king moved north. It was distinct from Gwanghaegun's appointment as crown prince on the twenty-ninth day of the fourth month, which came first.

Why it matters: it let the central government keep a second center of authority, helped gather troops and supplies, and projected royal legitimacy into the provinces.

Potential misunderstanding: it was not a rival court, and it should not be read as a preview of Gwanghaegun's later reign.

Section 32.6

The Decision to Abandon Hanyang

News of Chungju reached the court within days, probably carried by survivors and officials, and its force was strengthened by the fact that the court had little other information. Seonjo's advisers debated three options: remain in Hanyang and defend it, withdraw to a nearby fortified position, or move north while preserving the royal government. The record shows officials arguing about all three. No organized army remained to defend the capital, and the fear of encirclement was real.

Several considerations shaped the choice. The royal house itself had to be preserved, since capture of the king would have gravely threatened the dynasty's ability to govern, negotiate, and maintain legitimacy. The palace and shrine buildings themselves could not be carried away, but the ancestral spirit tablets of Jongmyo and the ritual tablets of Sajik could be, and were, taken north with the fleeing government. Sajik represented the deities of soil and grain, and Jongmyo housed the royal ancestors. Preserving those ritual objects was itself part of preserving dynastic legitimacy. Succession needed to be secured, and on the twenty-ninth day of the fourth month Gwanghaegun was named crown prince. Seonjo departed on the thirtieth.

The evacuation was both strategically intelligible and politically damaging. A Confucian monarchy's legitimacy rested heavily on the king's presence and on his care for his subjects, and leaving the capital to the enemy carried obvious costs. Officials and later writers argued over who was responsible. Students should separate the contemporary crisis, in which the options were poor, from later moral judgments, which often assume that the outcome was foreseeable.

Section 32.7

Royal Flight and Crisis of Legitimacy

The royal party left Hanyang on the thirtieth day of the fourth month and traveled north through Gaeseong and Pyongyang toward Uiju. The departure was hurried and the weather poor. Contemporary records describe a court moving without adequate provisions, with attendants, officials, and guards dropping away, and with food and shelter short. Communication was slow: orders had to be carried by messengers over roads that the Japanese were approaching, so the court often acted on out-of-date information.

The flight also produced anger. Accounts record that residents of Hanyang and people along the route blamed officials for the collapse, and senior ministers were attacked and in some cases removed from office amid public and court blame. Rumors circulated about who had failed and who had fled. These accounts come mainly from elite sources and reflect their own concerns about order and rank, so the exact extent of popular anger is uncertain.

A further question, which became acute as the court approached Uiju in the sixth month, was whether the king should seek refuge in Ming territory beyond the Amnok River. The court was still debating and preparing for entry into Liaodong in the later part of the sixth month. Some officials argued for it as a way to preserve the dynasty; others opposed it, fearing that crossing the frontier would amount to abandoning the realm and would invite its loss. The court's arrival near the frontier kept the option open.

A monarch retreating from his own capital was a political crisis in a state whose legitimacy depended on the king's presence and care for his subjects. Later moral judgments of Seonjo, including both harsh condemnation and sympathetic readings, were written in different political circumstances. Students should distinguish what the contemporary sources record, a court in danger making poor choices among poor options, from later verdicts about his character.

Section 32.8

The Fall and Occupation of Hanyang

Japanese forces entered Hanyang on the second day of the fifth month. The city they found was already in disorder. The king and court had left, and the palaces and some government offices had been damaged. Accounts differ on responsibility. Some sources attribute early burning and looting to Hanyang residents angry at the court, including attacks on offices that held records of bonded people, while others attribute destruction to Japanese troops. The evidence is mixed, and the responsibility for particular fires cannot be assigned with certainty.

The occupiers sought stores, records, and officials. Government granaries and archives had been disrupted, and much of the population had fled. The records and stores that remained were of limited use without people to administer them. Not all state records were lost: the Jeonju archive's set of the earlier dynastic annals survived the war and later provided the basis for restoring archival sets destroyed elsewhere.

The Japanese attempted to establish order in the city and to use any officials who remained, but administration depended on cooperation that was mostly absent. Possession of the capital did not compel surrender. The king had not been captured, the court continued to operate elsewhere, and provincial authorities and the branch court continued to function. Hanyang was a major military prize, but the occupiers held a large city without secure supply, with a hostile or absent population, and without a Joseon government to deal with. Occupation did not automatically mean governance.

Section 32.9

Gwanghaegun and the Branch Court

Seonjo appointed Gwanghaegun crown prince on the twenty-ninth day of the fourth month, the day before leaving Hanyang. The appointment settled the succession at the moment the king's own survival was in doubt. It was not yet the branch court. The bunjo was formed about a month later, around June 1592, and operated until about October 1593.

Its purpose was to divide the risks and tasks of government. While Seonjo's court moved toward the frontier, Gwanghaegun traveled through Pyeongan, Hwanghae, and Gangwon provinces, working to rally local populations, recruit soldiers, collect grain and supplies, confirm or appoint officials, and show that royal authority had not vanished from the countryside. Official Korean chronologies emphasize these functions of morale, manpower, and supply.

The work depended on communication. The branch court sent orders and appeals to local magistrates and commanders, received reports, and tried to coordinate recruitment and provisioning in areas the Japanese had not reached or were only beginning to threaten. Surviving sources show that it kept contact with the main court, but they vary on the details of specific orders, and they were compiled by officials with their own views of who deserved credit.

Why does this matter for the chapter's central question? A state is not defeated simply because its capital falls. It must also lose the capacity to command resources and obedience. The branch court preserved part of that capacity at a moment when the main court was in flight. It also reduced the danger that a single capture would end the dynasty, since the heir and the king were in different places, and it gave provincial officials a visible royal figure to answer to.

The institution had limits. Its authority depended on the willingness of local officials and communities to respond, it operated under constant threat from Japanese movements, and it had to move frequently. It also raised tensions between the two centers, since a second royal figure exercising authority in the provinces raised questions about the relationship between king and heir. Later developments, including Gwanghaegun's rise to the throne in 1608, make it tempting to read the bunjo backward as a preview of his reign. Students should resist that teleological move and treat it first as a wartime improvisation whose significance for 1592 lies in the survival of the state.

Section 32.10

Beyond Hanyang: The Advance to Pyongyang

After occupying Hanyang, the Japanese commanders moved north along the main road toward Pyongyang, but not at once. They reorganized, consolidated the capital, and prepared the next stage. The advance met an obstacle at the Imjin River, where Joseon forces tried to hold the crossing. According to the surviving tradition, the Japanese feigned withdrawal, the defenders crossed in pursuit, and the pursuing force was defeated, opening the river line. Details differ among sources.

Meanwhile the court continued north. Seonjo had reached Pyongyang, where officials debated holding the city or moving farther toward Uiju and the Amnok River, which marked the frontier with Ming China. The court eventually left, and Pyongyang fell on the thirteenth day of the sixth month. Its defenders had been few, and the city was reportedly largely abandoned by its population, though accounts of the condition of its stores and the extent of the flight vary.

The Japanese reached Pyongyang roughly two months after the landings, an extraordinary distance given the roads, rivers, and seasonal conditions. It was also a point of strategic overextension. Pyongyang lay far from Busan and from the sea route by which reinforcements and supplies were meant to arrive. The army now needed grain, garrisons, and communication across a vast and hostile countryside. Reaching the city was operational success; holding it was a far harder problem, examined in Section 32.13.

The Japanese halted at Pyongyang for a time. Their commanders had to consolidate the position, and the plan behind the invasion had assumed that supplies and reinforcements would reach the northern armies by sea along the western coast. That assumption was already under strain, a subject taken up in Chapter 33. Even before Pyongyang fell, Japanese representatives opened negotiations: on the ninth day of the sixth month, Yi Deokhyeong met them on the Daedong River to hear their demands. Sources differ on what each side hoped to achieve.

The pause is a reminder that the geographic reach of the advance and the strength of the occupation were different things. A force that could move rapidly along a road could not, by the same effort, secure the grain, labor, and communications needed to move farther. It also gave Joseon's surviving authorities time, in the north and elsewhere, which the next sections show they used.

Section 32.11

War Reaches Hamgyong

Katō Kiyomasa's forces moved northeast into Hamgyong province, the most remote theater of the first campaign. Their operations extended the reach of the invasion to a region far from the main axis toward Hanyang and Pyongyang, and showed how widely the Japanese campaign was spread.

Seonjo sent Prince Imhae toward Hamgyong and Prince Sunhwa toward Gangwon to gather troops; Sunhwa later moved north and joined Imhae before both were seized at Hoeryong. The missions were an example of the court's effort to extend its reach beyond the main road of the invasion. Their mission collided with local conditions. In Hamgyong, officials, garrison commanders, local elites, and commoners held differing loyalties and grievances, and the princes' arrival did not automatically produce effective mobilization. Provincial authority had already weakened under the pressure of the advance.

At Hoeryong the princes were captured by Katō's forces, and local figures were involved in the capture. Official Korean accounts confirm the capture, but the exact roles of individuals and their motives are less certain and were later disputed. Later blame narratives, which often assigned responsibility to particular local officials or to the northern population as a whole, were shaped by postwar politics and should be read critically.

The episode shows that wartime collapse could expose preexisting relationships among court authority, provincial officials, local elites, commoners, and military communities. It does not mean that northern populations welcomed invasion; other districts resisted, and Chapter 34 treats organized local resistance. It does show that provincial authority was not uniform and that the state's claim on obedience could weaken quickly in distant regions, which is part of why the branch court's work elsewhere mattered.

Section 32.12

Occupation and Civilian Disruption

For ordinary Koreans, invasion meant first of all a decision: flee or stay. Many households took what they could carry and went into mountains, up valleys, or to islands. Others, bound by age, illness, or tied labor, stayed. Villages emptied ahead of the armies, and in occupied areas fields were abandoned during the critical planting season of the fourth to sixth months, creating the conditions for later shortages. Evidence for the scale of such losses is uneven, and this chapter does not offer population figures.

Armies fed themselves partly by requisition. Soldiers took grain, livestock, and draft animals, and the occupiers sought porters and laborers. Official granaries and records were captured, burned, or abandoned, which damaged the state's capacity to tax and to identify people and property. Behavior varied by place, time, and unit: sources record killings, burning, and seizure of people, and also cases in which Japanese forces tried to induce local cooperation with promises of protection. Neither universal massacre nor benign occupation fits the evidence.

Experiences differed by status. Elite families often had resources to flee but lost property and written records. Commoners faced conscription by both sides, requisition, and hunger. Nobi sometimes escaped bondage in the chaos or were left behind. Women, children, and the elderly were especially vulnerable to separation and captivity; some captives were later taken to Japan. Monks, artisans, and merchants had varied roles and are poorly documented in this phase.

People also made accommodations. Some officials fled, some stayed and tried to preserve order, some cooperated under compulsion, and others sheltered with local networks. These choices should be read cautiously. Postwar accounts often assigned blame or praise according to later loyalty standards. Organized resistance by righteous armies and fighting monks belongs to Chapter 34. Source limits are important: most surviving texts were written by officials and literati, so commoners' experience is mostly inferred from official reports, later petitions, and diaries.

Section 32.13

Why Rapid Advance Did Not Mean Complete Conquest

The difference between movement and control is central to the chapter. Japanese armies had moved quickly along roads and occupied major cities. That is not the same as controlling countryside, population, tax collection, supply, communications, and political legitimacy. Each of these required more than armies marching through.

Distance mattered. By the time the army reached Pyongyang, it was far from Busan, the point of entry. Garrisons held cities and key positions, but every garrison reduced the force available to advance, and the further the army went the more men it needed to guard its line. Requisition kept armies fed in the first weeks, but it depended on finding grain and animals, and as populations fled or hid supplies, this became harder. The seasonal cycle added to the problem, since disrupted planting reduced the food that occupiers and civilians alike could obtain.

Politically, the Joseon administrative network, though damaged, continued to function in many places. Provincial officials, the branch court, and surviving garrisons kept lines of authority alive. Communications were vulnerable: long roads and river crossings could be disrupted, and messages and supplies depended on them. Joseon's survival also meant that the Japanese had no governing partner who could turn military victory into political settlement.

Several factors increasingly constrained the occupiers. Naval resistance threatened the sea route that was meant to bring reinforcements and supplies, a development treated in Chapter 33. Organized local resistance, covered in Chapter 34, began to attack lines of communication and isolate garrisons. And the arrival of Ming forces, also discussed in Chapter 34, eventually changed the strategic balance. These are noted here only as high-level reasons why rapid conquest did not become complete control.

Section 32.14

From Collapse to a Longer War, 1592–1593

By the summer of 1592 the initial Joseon field-defense system had collapsed and much of the peninsula was under Japanese control or contested. Yet the first phase of rapid conquest had reached its limit. The armies had moved from Busan to Pyongyang and Hamgyong in about two months, but their territorial reach had outrun their ability to control what they had crossed.

Several developments explain the change. First, the Joseon state survived. The king and court remained beyond Japanese reach, the branch court kept parts of the provinces connected to the crown, and surviving officials retained administrative capacity. Second, occupation proved thin. Garrisons held roads and cities but depended on long supply lines running through a countryside whose inhabitants were fleeing or hostile. Third, the sea route that tied the invaders to Japan came under increasing pressure from Joseon naval forces, a development narrated in Chapter 33. Fourth, local resistance expanded, and Ming forces entered the war, subjects of Chapter 34. The first Ming contingents entered Korea after Pyongyang fell, while the major Ming-led counteroffensive belongs to that later chapter.

The effect was a change in the character of the war. In the first weeks it had been a race to the northern frontier, decided by speed and by the failure of Joseon's conventional defense. By early 1593 it had become a longer struggle over supply, communications, and political legitimacy. Japanese commanders found it difficult to turn battlefield success into durable control, and Joseon forces and their allies had time to reorganize.

The collapse examined in this chapter was therefore real, but it was a collapse of Joseon's first conventional defensive system, not of Joseon as a political order. That distinction is the chapter's central finding and the bridge to Chapters 33 and 34.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window 1: The Seonjo Annals, Fourth Month, 28th–30th Days, 1592

Creator and date: there are two compilations. The original Seonjo Sillok was completed in 1616, during Gwanghaegun's reign. The Seonjo Sujeong Sillok, a revision, was completed in 1657, during Hyojong's reign. Type: official dynastic chronicle, compiled from daily court records.

Context: the entries for these three days cover the report of the defeat at Chungju, the debate over whether the king should leave, the appointment of Gwanghaegun as crown prince on the 29th, and Seonjo's departure on the 30th. Historians compare the two compilations because the revision was made in a later court after factional changes, and because differences between them show what each compiler chose to include, omit, or judge. The original was compiled when the reign's principal figures were still politically relevant, and the revision responded to later disputes.

Paraphrase (not a quotation):

The entries record news of the defeat, officials arguing over where the king should go, the naming of an heir, and a departure under difficult conditions.

Analysis: the annals let students reconstruct the decisions as officials faced them, without hindsight, and show how the court understood its options. Limitations: both texts were compiled decades after the events, and each reflects the politics of its compilers. They cannot tell us what refugees, soldiers, or the Japanese experienced. Perspective: the court elite; commoners and the invaders are missing.

Primary Source Window 2: Busanjin Sunjeoldo and Dongnaebu Sunjeoldo

Creator and date: late-Joseon commemorative record paintings. Both subjects were first painted in 1709, and the surviving versions were recopied by Byeon Bak in 1760, more than 160 years after the battles. Type: visual source.

Composition and meaning: Busanjin Sunjeoldo uses a high bird's-eye viewpoint, placing the fortress against masses of surrounding Japanese troops and showing Jeong Bal among its defenders. Dongnaebu Sunjeoldo places the rounded fortress centrally, surrounds it with attacking troops, and gives visual prominence to Song Sang-hyeon's martyrdom. Their compositions reveal a commemorative purpose centered on loyal resistance and sacrifice rather than eyewitness documentation of the sequence of fighting. They belong to a wider late-Joseon culture of commemoration in which the state and local elites honored such defenders.

Analysis and limits: they are not eyewitness images. They show how later generations remembered the battles and what virtues they wished to honor, and they can help identify the layout of the fortifications as later artists understood them. They cannot establish the course of the fighting, casualty numbers, or the experience of civilians. Perspective: the late-Joseon state and local elites; the Japanese attackers and ordinary inhabitants are largely absent.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did Joseon's Conventional Defenses Collapse So Quickly?

The question: what best explains the speed of collapse?

Institutional and mobilization weakness: this view emphasizes the emergency system, dispersed forces, unfit levies, and the long peace, arguing that Joseon could not assemble forces in time. It draws on court records of the commanders' difficulties.
Japanese operational capability: this view stresses veteran infantry, massed firearms, multiple columns, and tempo, arguing that the invaders outperformed what any defense could have achieved on the same schedule. It draws on Japanese and Korean accounts of the battles.
Multi-causal interaction: most historians combine the two, adding geography, information delays, and command decisions, and treat the outcome as the product of their interaction.

Points of disagreement: the weight of each factor, and whether better decisions, such as at Joryeong, could have changed the outcome. Source problems: Korean sources reflect factional blame; Japanese sources emphasize success. Why it matters: the explanation shapes how Joseon is judged, and whether the collapse is read as inevitable. Student task: identify evidence that could separate these explanations.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Invasion, 1592

These are reconstructed analytical perspectives, not quotations.

A Busanjin defenderA garrison soldier faced assault by a much larger force with little prospect of relief. Resistance was real, but the odds were severe, and the defender's fate was later folded into a tradition of loyal sacrifice.
A refugee householdA farming family had to decide quickly whether to flee. Leaving meant abandoning fields and stores in the planting season, while staying meant exposure to requisition and violence. The decision depended on information that was often rumor.
A court official with SeonjoAn official saw departure as necessary to preserve the dynasty, yet knew that it would draw blame. He also had to manage a moving government with little information and limited food.
A Japanese commanderA commander saw speed and success, but also worried about supply, distance, and the absence of a governing partner among the Joseon elite.
A provincial magistrateA local official had to choose between staying, fleeing, or trying to maintain administration. His choices were later judged by standards of loyalty that did not account for the circumstances.

These groups did not hold equal power or freedom of action; refugees and commoners had the fewest options.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Government in Crisis

What changed: the capital was lost, the field armies were destroyed, territory came under Japanese control, and the king was in flight. The balance of power in the provinces shifted.

What continued: the monarchy, the heir, the bureaucratic ideal, local administration in unoccupied areas, and the court's claim to legitimacy.

Why it matters: the pattern was abrupt military change with gradual institutional adaptation. Those in the path of the armies felt change most directly, while officials in unoccupied provinces experienced greater continuity. Recognizing both prevents the error of equating military defeat with state collapse.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Invasion Success versus Settled Conquest

Long-term conditions

A long period without full-scale invasion, a levy-based emergency mobilization system, and Japan's long experience of war.

Short-term causes

Late and uneven mobilization, scattered forces, weak information flow, and Japanese use of several columns.

Immediate triggers

The landing at Busan, the fall of the first strongholds, and the defeats at Sangju and Chungju.

Initial effects

Loss of Hanyang and Pyongyang, royal flight, and occupation of large areas.

Transition factors

Survival of the state, thin occupation, naval pressure, local resistance, and Ming entry.

Longer consequences

A prolonged war in which occupation did not become political control.

Complexity: sequence is not causation. The defeats followed one another, but each also depended on the structural conditions listed here, and no single cause explains the outcome.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households under Invasion

Section 32.12 describes the civilian experience in detail. In brief, farming households faced the decision to flee, requisition of food and animals, abandonment of fields in the planting season, and the loss of records and property claims. Women, children, and the elderly faced particular risks of separation and captivity. Nobi sometimes escaped bondage in the disorder or were left behind. Artisans, merchants, and monks had varied roles. Evidence is thin and mostly indirect, so some of this must be inferred cautiously from official reports, petitions, and diaries.

Chapter Summary

The Imjin War began on the thirteenth day of the fourth lunar month of 1592, when Konishi Yukinaga's first division landed near Busan. The question this chapter has pursued is how Japanese forces overran Joseon's conventional defenses so quickly, and how the Joseon state survived the collapse of its armies and the loss of its capital.

The first defenders resisted. Busanjin fell on the fourteenth and Dongnae on the fifteenth, with commanders Jeong Bal and Song Sang-hyeon killed. The speed of these falls reflected a mismatch between garrisons built against raids and an army organized for campaigning, and the later commemoration of the defenders is a separate story of memory.

The Japanese then advanced in several columns along different roads. The structure spread the burden of supply, pressed many districts at once, and denied Joseon commanders a clear point of defense. It was not perfectly coordinated, and its logistical risks grew with distance.

Joseon's response depended on jeseung bangnyak, an emergency system that assumed time to raise troops. At Sangju Yi Il could gather only hastily assembled men and was defeated around the twenty-fourth to twenty-fifth day of the fourth month. The Japanese tempo compressed the weeks the system needed into days.

At Chungju on the twenty-eighth day Sin Rip's concentrated army was destroyed at Tangeumdae. The choice of ground, which surviving accounts say he preferred for his cavalry over the Joryeong pass, is better understood as a gamble made under severe constraints than as an obvious blunder. Its strategic consequence was that no comparable force stood before the capital.

The court then decided to leave Hanyang. Gwanghaegun was named crown prince on the twenty-ninth day and Seonjo departed on the thirtieth. The decision was strategically intelligible and politically damaging, and the journey north through Gaeseong and Pyongyang toward Uiju exposed the monarchy to blame.

Japanese forces entered Hanyang on the second day of the fifth month and found a disordered city. Responsibility for the fires is uncertain. Possession of the capital did not compel surrender, because the king had not been captured, the court continued elsewhere, and the occupiers lacked a cooperating administration.

The state survived in part through the branch court, formed around June 1592 and active until about October 1593, which kept a second center of authority and helped gather troops and supplies. Pyongyang fell in the sixth month, roughly two months after the initial landings, and Katō's forces reached Hamgyong, where the capture of Princes Imhae and Sunhwa revealed tensions among the court, local officials, and communities without showing that the north welcomed the invasion.

Civilians bore much of the cost. They fled, lost fields and records, faced requisition and violence, and experienced separation and captivity. Their experience varied by place, status, and time, and evidence for it is uneven.

By the summer of 1592 the first phase of rapid conquest had reached its limit. Thin occupation, long supply lines, the survival of Joseon's government, and the later effects of naval pressure, local resistance, and Ming entry, treated in Chapters 33 and 34, changed the character of the war. The collapse was real, but it was the collapse of Joseon's first conventional defensive system, not of Joseon as a political order.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that a state can lose its armies and its capital without ceasing to exist. The first months of the Imjin War revealed the limits of Joseon's emergency system, but they also revealed the resilience of its institutions: the monarchy, the heir, the bureaucracy, and a network of provincial officials.

It also shows how different the problems of moving and holding are. Japanese forces achieved an extraordinary advance, but their reach outran their control, and the state they sought to subdue continued to function elsewhere. That gap between occupation and political control is a pattern that recurs wherever armies meet societies with durable institutions.

The chapter prepared the later course of the war. Chapter 33 turns to the naval campaign that challenged the Japanese supply line, and Chapter 34 to the righteous armies, fighting monks, and Ming intervention that transformed the land war. Chapter 35 follows the renewed invasion of 1597.

Finally, the chapter illustrates the care needed in handling memory. Later generations honored the defenders and judged the king and officials, often in ways that reflected their own concerns. Distinguishing the contemporary record from later memory is the historian's task, and it is one this textbook continues to ask of its readers.
