Unified Silla and the Consolidation of Royal Power

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Victory in war is not the same thing as government. When the fighting described in Chapter 13 finally subsided in the 670s, Silla's rulers possessed something no earlier Silla king had ever possessed: effective authority over most of the Korean peninsula, including cities, farmland, aristocratic families, and administrative traditions that had belonged, until recently, to two rival kingdoms. Turning that battlefield outcome into a working political order was a separate, unfinished, and often contested project.

In the closing years of the seventh century, officials in the Silla capital of Gyeongju confronted a set of problems that no previous Silla government had faced at this scale. The kingdom's territory and population had increased dramatically. Its territory now included the fertile lowlands of the former Baekje kingdom in the southwest and, in the north and center of the peninsula, communities that had until recently identified with Goguryeo. Local elites in these regions possessed their own administrative traditions, their own networks of landholding and labor, and, in many cases, their own memories of resistance against Silla armies. Meanwhile, at court, the very aristocratic families whose soldiers had won the wars now expected to be rewarded, consulted, and deferred to, placing real limits on how far any king could concentrate power in his own hands.

This chapter examines how Silla's monarchy responded to these problems between the 670s and the early eighth century, a period historians often treat as the formative decades of what is conventionally called "Unified Silla." The response took many forms: a reorganized provincial system built around nine administrative circuits; a network of five secondary capitals designed to project royal authority and manage newly incorporated elites away from Gyeongju; expanded central offices and ranks; a reformed military establishment built partly around units drawn from formerly hostile populations; and new or renewed mechanisms for taxing land and labor. Two kings dominate this story—Munmu, who oversaw the transition from war to peace, and his son Sinmun, whose reign is associated with the most concentrated burst of administrative reorganization and with a serious aristocratic challenge to royal authority.

Yet this chapter resists any temptation to describe the outcome as a smoothly centralized, uniformly administered kingdom. The label "Unified Silla," used throughout this textbook and by most historians for convenience, describes a real and significant political achievement, but it should not be mistaken for the modern idea of a unified nation-state governing a fully integrated population under uniform law. Silla's rule never extended over all the territory once held by Goguryeo; within a generation, a new state, Balhae, would govern much of the north, a development examined fully in Chapter 16. Even within territory Silla did control, the depth of royal authority varied by region, by social class, and by the particular interests of powerful aristocratic lineages who continued to dominate the highest offices of the state.

The evidence available for this period is uneven. Later court chronicles, compiled centuries afterward, provide a narrative skeleton of reigns, revolts, and administrative measures, but they were written to serve later political and dynastic purposes and require careful, critical reading. Archaeological evidence from Gyeongju and the secondary capitals illuminates the material scale of the consolidated kingdom. A remarkable set of household registers, generally known as the Silla Village Documents and preserved by chance in Japan, offers a rare and concrete glimpse of how the state's demands for labor, grain, and other resources reached down into ordinary villages—though, as this chapter's Primary Source Window explains, historians disagree even about which century the documents belong to, and the evidence must be handled with corresponding caution.

This chapter also introduces a distinction that will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook, wherever a state expands its authority over newly conquered or newly incorporated territory: territorial conquest, political incorporation, and administrative uniformity are three different things, achieved, if at all, at different speeds and to different degrees. Silla's rulers conquered Baekje and incorporated portions of former Goguryeo territory in the southern and central peninsula by 676. They incorporated many of the conquered population's elites and communities into Silla's political system over the following decades. They never achieved, and probably never sought, a uniform administrative reach into every village and every social class across the entire kingdom. Holding these three processes apart is essential to understanding what "Unified Silla" actually meant.

Finally, this chapter looks ahead without trespassing on later chapters' territory. The flourishing of Buddhist institutions, monastic scholarship, and royal religious architecture associated with eighth-century Silla, including the famous temple and grotto complexes examined in Chapter 15, depended directly on the wealth, stability, and royal patronage that the political consolidation described here made possible. Understanding how Silla's kings built a functioning postwar state is therefore a necessary foundation for understanding the cultural achievements that followed, and for understanding why, a century and more later, the political arrangements described in this chapter would begin to strain and eventually unravel.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the term "Unified Silla" is useful for organizing this period while also identifying its limitations as a description of the kingdom's actual territorial and political reach.
  2. Distinguish territorial conquest, political incorporation, and administrative uniformity as separate historical processes operating at different speeds after 676.
  3. Describe how Silla incorporated former Baekje territory, elites, and communities into its expanded kingdom.
  4. Describe how Silla incorporated populations and elites associated with former Goguryeo, including the Silla-sponsored restoration polity associated with Anseung and Bodeok, while distinguishing incorporated populations from those who remained outside Silla's control.
  5. Explain the administrative purposes of the nine-province system and the five secondary capitals, and evaluate what each institution could and could not accomplish.
  6. Analyze the relationship between the Silla monarchy and the true-bone aristocracy, including the continuing significance of the bone-rank system after 676.
  7. Trace King Sinmun's administrative measures, including his response to the revolt associated with Kim Heumdol, and assess their significance for royal authority.
  8. Describe the basic mechanisms through which the Silla state extracted land-based revenue, agricultural produce, textiles, and labor from its population.
  9. Distinguish the 687 grant of gwallyojeon from the 689 abolition of nok-eup, and explain why conflating these two measures misrepresents Sinmun's land and compensation policy.
  10. Describe the reorganization of Silla's military establishment, including the Nine Seodang, and explain its role in both defense and political integration.
  11. Evaluate competing historical assessments of how centralized Unified Silla actually was, using specific administrative, military, and social evidence.
  12. Assess the everyday consequences of postwar consolidation for farmers, soldiers, local elites, and resettled communities.

Historical Context

Chapter 13 traced the wars of 598 to 676: Goguryeo's earlier conflicts with Sui and Tang China, the Silla-Tang alliance that destroyed Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668, the restoration movements that briefly kept both defeated kingdoms' causes alive, and the subsequent conflict between Silla and its former Tang ally, which ended with Tang forces withdrawing from most of the peninsula by 676. That chapter emphasized that Silla's victory established rule over much of, but not all of, the Korean peninsula, and that Balhae, a new state drawing together former Goguryeo and Mohe populations, would soon rise in the north to govern much of the remaining former Goguryeo territory, a "North and South States" arrangement examined in Chapter 16.

Before the wars, Silla had been a kingdom centered tightly on the Gyeongju basin in the southeastern peninsula, governed through a hereditary aristocracy organized by the bone-rank system, with political and military institutions built around defending and slowly expanding a comparatively modest core territory. The wars against Baekje and Goguryeo, and then against Tang forces, required Silla to mobilize larger armies, manage new alliances, and administer territory far beyond its earlier borders even before the fighting had fully ended. By the time hostilities subsided in the mid-670s, Silla's kings governed a kingdom substantially larger and more populous than anything earlier Silla rulers had known, filled with communities whose political loyalties, administrative habits, and elite structures had been shaped by decades or centuries under different governments.

This chapter picks up directly from that settlement. It does not retell the campaigns already covered in Chapter 13, and it does not anticipate the Buddhist cultural flourishing covered in Chapter 15 or Balhae's formation covered in Chapter 16. Its task is narrower and more specific: to explain what the Silla monarchy did, administratively and politically, with the victory it had won, and how successfully it did so.

Section 14.1

What Was "Unified Silla"?

Historians commonly refer to the period following 676 as "Unified Silla," and this textbook uses the term throughout Part III and beyond for the same reason most scholarship does: it is a convenient shorthand for a real and significant transformation in Silla's territorial reach and political stature. Before the wars described in Chapter 13, Silla was one of three competing kingdoms, alongside Baekje and Goguryeo, and the several Gaya polities of the southern peninsula, which Silla had absorbed by the sixth century. After 676, Silla governed the former territory of Baekje in the southwest and much of the southern and central portions of former Goguryeo territory, making it, by a wide margin, the dominant political power on the peninsula.

Yet the term carries real risks of overstatement, and this chapter asks students to hold it at arm's length even while using it. Silla's "unification" did not extend to all territory once associated with Goguryeo. Substantial northern and Manchurian regions remained outside Silla's administrative reach, and within roughly two decades of 676, these regions would coalesce into Balhae, a new state drawing together former Goguryeo and Mohe populations, examined fully in Chapter 16. Historians describing this era sometimes prefer the phrase "North and South States" precisely to avoid implying that Silla's victory produced a single unified Korean government over the whole peninsula.

Even within the territory Silla did control, "unification" describes a legal and military claim to sovereignty more than a finished social or administrative reality. Former Baekje and Goguryeo communities did not instantly become indistinguishable from Silla's core population. Local elites in newly incorporated regions retained distinct family histories, religious traditions, and, in some cases, resentments toward the kingdom that had conquered them. The task of this chapter is to describe how Silla's monarchy worked, over the following decades, to convert a military claim into a functioning system of administration—and to assess how far that effort actually succeeded.

This textbook's use of "Unified Silla" should therefore be read throughout this chapter and the chapters that follow as a period label describing a transformed and dominant kingdom, not as a claim that the entire peninsula was governed by one uniform state, or that former political and cultural identities disappeared the moment the wars ended.

Section 14.2

A Kingdom Transformed by Victory

The Silla that emerged from the wars of the seventh century differed from earlier Silla in scale, composition, and administrative burden. Territorially, the kingdom's effective boundaries moved far beyond the Gyeongju basin and the southeastern peninsula that had anchored Silla's earlier history, incorporating the rich agricultural lowlands of the former Baekje kingdom around the Geum and Yeongsan river systems, along with much of the Han River basin and territory extending into the central and, more unevenly, northern peninsula that had previously belonged to Goguryeo.

This territorial expansion brought a correspondingly large increase in population, much of it unfamiliar with Silla law, taxation, or court ritual. It also brought new elites: former Baekje aristocratic families with their own landholding traditions and local followings, and former Goguryeo military and administrative figures accustomed to a different governing structure. Some of these elites had fought against Silla for years or decades; others had defected, surrendered, or found accommodation with the new order well before the fighting fully ended.

The kingdom's frontiers also changed in character. Where Silla's earlier borders had faced Baekje and Goguryeo directly, the postwar kingdom's northern frontier faced an unstable and only partially controlled zone of former Goguryeo territory, soon to be contested with, and eventually bordered by, Balhae. Managing this frontier required different military and administrative arrangements than defending a compact southeastern kingdom had required.

Finally, victory itself created new political pressures at court. The aristocratic lineages whose soldiers, generals, and resources had made victory possible expected recognition, office, and reward. A king who tried to concentrate too much authority in the throne risked alienating the very families whose cooperation had won the war and whose continued cooperation would be needed to govern the expanded kingdom. This tension between rewarding the aristocracy and strengthening royal authority runs through nearly every development examined in this chapter.

Section 14.3

King Munmu and the Immediate Postwar Settlement

King Munmu reigned through the final and most difficult years of the wars described in Chapter 13, including the conflict with Tang forces that followed the destruction of Baekje and Goguryeo, and he remained on the throne as large-scale fighting subsided by the mid-670s. Later chronicles credit him with a range of measures associated with the transition from war to peacetime administration, including efforts to organize newly incorporated territory, reward military leaders, and manage the delicate diplomatic relationship with Tang China after the withdrawal of Tang forces from most of the peninsula.

Munmu's reign should not be treated, as later nationalist retellings have sometimes treated it, as the moment when a single continuous Korean nation-state came into being under a founding hero. The administrative structures most closely associated with postwar consolidation, including the nine-province system and the completed five-secondary-capital network, are more securely associated with the reign of his son and successor, Sinmun, discussed later in this chapter. Munmu's more immediate task was narrower: ending active warfare, stabilizing relations with Tang China, beginning the incorporation of former Baekje and Goguryeo territory and elites, and managing the material and human costs of decades of conflict.

Later Korean tradition remembers Munmu partly through legendary elements, including accounts describing his wish, after death, to become a guardian dragon of the sea protecting the kingdom from foreign threats. Such traditions illustrate the cultural memory later generations attached to the ruler who presided over the end of the wars, but they belong to the realm of commemorative legend rather than administrative history, and this chapter treats them accordingly: as evidence of how Munmu was remembered, not as a description of what he actually did as an administrator.

Section 14.4

Former Baekje Lands after Conquest

Baekje's defeat in 660 was followed by years of restoration warfare, discussed in Chapter 13, before Silla's authority over the former kingdom's territory became reasonably secure. Once secured, the southwestern lowlands that had formed Baekje's core presented Silla with both an opportunity and a challenge: rich agricultural land and established administrative traditions on one hand, and a population and elite class with their own political memory and, in some cases, continuing resentment on the other.

Silla's approach combined administrative absorption with selective accommodation of local elites. Former Baekje administrative centers were incorporated into Silla's expanding provincial framework, discussed below, rather than being abandoned or left under separate governance. Surviving evidence indicates that members of the former Baekje ruling stratum were granted Silla ranks and incorporated into the new political order, generally below the standing of native Silla true-bone aristocrats. The precise fate of individual families' property and local influence varied and is not documented well enough to reconstruct as a uniform policy. Political incorporation therefore opened meaningful paths into Silla service without erasing the hierarchy that privileged native Silla aristocratic birth.

Political conquest, in short, did not erase Baekje identity or social structure overnight. Local administrative habits, family networks, and cultural and religious traditions associated with the former kingdom persisted for generations, even as the region became administratively integrated into Silla's provincial system. Archaeological evidence from the former Baekje region, including continued production of distinctive local ceramic and architectural styles well into the Unified Silla period, supports this picture of gradual rather than instantaneous cultural absorption.

Section 14.5

Former Goguryeo People within Silla

Incorporating former Goguryeo populations presented a related but distinct challenge. Goguryeo's territory had extended far to the north, into Manchuria, well beyond any area Silla ever controlled, so Silla's incorporation of "former Goguryeo" people and territory in fact concerned only the southern portion of that much larger former kingdom, chiefly areas south of the frontier that would soon separate Silla from Balhae.

In the years immediately following Goguryeo's fall in 668, Silla supported a Silla-sponsored Goguryeo restoration polity led by Anseung, a Goguryeo royal claimant whose precise relationship to the former Goguryeo royal house is reported differently across the surviving sources, commonly associated with the place-name Bodeok. This arrangement served several purposes at once: it offered a focus of loyalty for former Goguryeo restoration sentiment that Silla could influence rather than oppose outright, it provided Silla with a political and military instrument during the final years of conflict with Tang forces, and it allowed for a more gradual transition of former Goguryeo territory and population into direct Silla administration than an abrupt annexation might have permitted. Silla recognized Anseung as a Goguryeo restoration ruler in 670 and styled him king of Bodeok in 674. The arrangement did not end there, however: in 683 Sinmun summoned Anseung to Gyeongju and incorporated him into the Silla aristocracy, granting him rank and a royal-family surname, and in 684 a revolt at the former Bodeok center was suppressed, after which the polity's territory and population were absorbed directly into Silla's county administration. The transition from sponsored restoration polity to full incorporation therefore unfolded across roughly a decade and a half, not in a single stroke.

It is essential to distinguish, as this chapter's introduction emphasized, between former Goguryeo populations who came under Silla administration through this process and the much larger former Goguryeo territory and population that remained outside Silla's control entirely and would, within a generation, form the core of Balhae. Silla's "unification" incorporated only a portion of Goguryeo's former reach. Describing Silla's kingdom after 676 as having absorbed "all of Goguryeo" would misrepresent both the geography and the political outcome of the wars.

Former Goguryeo elites incorporated into Silla could receive official rank, military command, land, or other rewards, but surviving evidence indicates that the highest Silla aristocratic standing remained dominated by native Silla lineages. This structural limitation on advancement represents one of the clearest illustrations of the distinction between incorporation and full political equality.

Section 14.6

The Nine Provinces

Traditional accounts associate the reorganization of Silla's expanded territory into nine provinces, or nine-ju, with the reign of King Sinmun, its structure generally described as complete by 685, though the process of defining and adjusting these provincial boundaries likely developed gradually over several decades rather than through a single administrative act. Whatever its precise timeline, the nine-province system represented a deliberate attempt to organize Silla's core territory, former Baekje territory, and former Goguryeo territory under a single, common administrative framework rather than governing each region through separate or improvised arrangements.

Each province was governed by an appointed official, typically drawn from Silla's aristocracy, responsible for administration, taxation oversight, and coordination with central authorities. The provinces combined regions of quite different historical background: some corresponded to Silla's own earlier core territory, others to former Baekje lands, and others to former Goguryeo territory in the southern and central peninsula. By placing all of these regions within the same administrative category, the nine-province system worked to normalize Silla's expanded rule as a single ongoing kingdom rather than as a wartime occupation of conquered territory.

Students should resist imagining the nine provinces as equivalent to modern provincial administration, with uniform bureaucratic reach into every village and consistent enforcement across the entire territory. The degree of actual administrative penetration almost certainly varied by province, with areas closer to Gyeongju and to the secondary capitals discussed below more thoroughly integrated than more distant or more recently pacified regions. The nine-province system provided a framework for governance; it did not by itself guarantee uniform administrative reality on the ground.

Section 14.7

The Five Secondary Capitals

Alongside the nine provinces, Silla's postwar government completed a longer-running strategy of secondary capitals, or sogyeong, that had begun before the unification wars and was extended in stages afterward. Gukwon Sogyeong predated the wars, Bukwon Sogyeong was established in 678, Geumgwan Sogyeong in 680, and Seowon and Namwon Sogyeong followed in 685, bringing the network to its full complement of five. Positioned in strategically and symbolically significant locations across the expanded kingdom, including territory formerly associated with Baekje and Goguryeo, these centers should not be understood as capitals in the modern political sense, nor simply as ordinary provincial administrative seats; they served a distinctive set of purposes tied specifically to the challenges of postwar consolidation.

First, the secondary capitals functioned as instruments for relocating and managing elites from newly incorporated regions. By encouraging or requiring prominent local families, including former Baekje and Goguryeo aristocrats, to maintain residences or official connections in these centers, Silla's court could keep a closer watch on regional elites while offering them a degree of status and integration into the wider kingdom's political life. Second, the secondary capitals helped balance the overwhelming political and economic dominance of Gyeongju itself, discussed in the next section, by providing alternative centers of administrative and cultural activity across the kingdom's expanded geography. Third, they served practical administrative and military functions, including tax collection, communication with the central court, and coordination of regional defense.

The precise administrative powers and populations of each secondary capital are not uniformly well documented, and historians continue to debate exactly how these centers functioned in practice and how they related to the nine-province structure described above. What is reasonably clear is that the secondary capitals represented a deliberate royal strategy, developed over several decades, for managing an expanded and diverse kingdom without either abandoning regional administration to local elites entirely or attempting to govern every region directly from Gyeongju alone.

Section 14.8

Gyeongju and the Royal Center

Despite the development of provincial administration and secondary capitals, Gyeongju remained overwhelmingly the political, social, and religious center of the consolidated kingdom. Archaeological and textual evidence describes a capital of substantial scale and wealth during this period, with extensive aristocratic residences, palace complexes, and religious institutions concentrated within and around the city. Court ritual, the highest offices of government, and the great majority of Silla's true-bone aristocratic families remained rooted in Gyeongju even as the kingdom's territory and administrative structures extended far beyond it.

This concentration of wealth, status, and decision-making authority in a single city had significant consequences for how the consolidated kingdom actually functioned. Provincial governors, secondary-capital administrators, and military commanders operating at a distance from Gyeongju depended on connections to the capital's aristocratic networks for advancement, and major decisions of state remained firmly centered on the royal court and its surrounding aristocracy. At the same time, this concentration meant that Silla's practical administrative reach into distant provinces depended heavily on the effectiveness of officials sent out from Gyeongju and on the cooperation of local elites, rather than on any dense, uniform bureaucratic apparatus extending evenly across the kingdom.

The scale of Gyeongju itself, including later development of urban infrastructure and extensive Buddhist architecture discussed further in Chapter 15, testifies to the substantial resources that postwar consolidation, expanded taxation, and continued aristocratic wealth made available to the royal center. This concentration of resources should be understood as both a symptom and a cause of the kingdom's continuing capital-centered political structure.

Section 14.9

Monarchy and the Bone-Rank Aristocracy

No feature of Silla's political structure did more to limit royal centralization than the bone-rank (golpum) system, which continued to govern eligibility for high office, marriage, dress, housing, and other markers of status throughout the Unified Silla period. By the mid-seventh century, the highest status category, generally described as "sacred-bone" (seonggol), reserved in earlier generations for a narrow royal lineage, had become extinct, and the throne itself had passed to members of the "true-bone" (jingol) rank, the highest surviving aristocratic category. This meant that, from the mid-seventh century onward, the king was drawn from the same broad aristocratic status category as the powerful noble families who dominated the highest offices of state, rather than from a separate and untouchable royal caste above them.

This arrangement had a double effect on royal power. On one hand, it meant that Silla's kings after this transition were, in a formal sense, aristocrats among aristocrats, dependent on cooperation, marriage alliances, and political management within the true-bone elite to maintain their position. On the other hand, powerful true-bone lineages could challenge royal authority, influence succession, and, particularly in later Silla, participate in succession struggles among branches of the royal and aristocratic Kim elite. The aristocratic revolt discussed in the next section demonstrates the capacity of elite lineages to challenge a king, but it should not be treated as proof that every true-bone family possessed an equivalent dynastic claim.

Below the true-bone rank, the bone-rank system maintained further status categories, generally described using "head-rank" (dupum) numbers, that governed the eligibility of lower aristocratic and administrative families for office and advancement. Officials from head-rank backgrounds staffed much of the expanding central and provincial bureaucracy described in this chapter, but they typically faced firm ceilings on how high they could rise regardless of talent or service, a structural limitation that some later Silla intellectuals would criticize, a development explored further in Chapter 15's treatment of Silla's cultural and intellectual life.

Students should avoid describing the bone-rank system as a simple modern caste system or as directly comparable to social hierarchies in unrelated societies. It was a specifically Silla institution, rooted in that kingdom's own political history, governing an interlocking set of privileges and restrictions that shaped nearly every dimension of elite life, including access to the offices and instruments of the expanded postwar state described throughout this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Bone-Rank: Seonggol and Jingol

Definition: The bone-rank (golpum) system was Silla's hereditary status hierarchy, determining eligibility for office, permissible dress and housing, and marriage practice according to birth. Its two highest categories were "sacred-bone" (seonggol), reserved for the narrowest royal lineage in earlier Silla history, and "true-bone" (jingol), a broader high aristocratic category from which, after the extinction of the sacred-bone line by the mid-seventh century, Silla's monarchs themselves were drawn.

Historical context: The bone-rank system predates the wars discussed in Chapter 13 and long outlasted them, continuing to shape Silla politics and society throughout the Unified Silla period examined in this chapter and the two that follow it.

Why it matters: Because the throne itself belonged to the true-bone rank after the mid-seventh century, Silla's kings shared formal status with the very aristocratic families whose cooperation, wealth, and military resources they depended on to govern the expanded postwar kingdom. This shared status helps explain both the possibilities and the limits of royal consolidation examined throughout this chapter.

Potential misunderstanding: The bone-rank system should not be treated as equivalent to unrelated caste or class systems from other societies, nor should "true-bone" be casually translated as simply "nobility." It was a specific Silla institution combining descent, ritual status, and administrative eligibility in ways that had no exact parallel in neighboring Baekje, Goguryeo, or Tang China.

Section 14.10

Royal Government and Offices

Alongside provincial reorganization, Silla's postwar government expanded and refined a set of central offices responsible for administration, finance, military affairs, and court ritual. These offices, staffed by officials holding recognized bone-rank and head-rank status, oversaw functions including taxation, the management of state and royal lands, military recruitment and supply, and the conduct of diplomatic relations, particularly with Tang China, which remained Silla's most important external relationship throughout this period despite the recent conflict between the two.

This administrative expansion reflected genuine bureaucratic development: officials operated within a more elaborate structure of ranks, responsibilities, and reporting relationships than earlier, smaller Silla had required. At the same time, this bureaucratic expansion coexisted with, rather than replaced, the hereditary privileges of the bone-rank system. Access to the most senior central offices remained effectively restricted to true-bone families, meaning that administrative sophistication and aristocratic privilege developed together rather than in tension with one another during this period.

The hwabaek, a council of high aristocrats with roots extending back into earlier Silla history, continued to play a role in major state decisions during this period, including matters of succession and other high political questions. Students should be cautious about describing the hwabaek in terms borrowed from modern representative institutions; it was a body of leading true-bone aristocrats exercising influence appropriate to a hereditary aristocracy, not an elected or broadly representative assembly, and its precise powers relative to the king varied according to political circumstance rather than fixed constitutional rule.

Section 14.11

King Sinmun and Royal Consolidation

King Sinmun, who succeeded his father Munmu in 681, is associated with the most concentrated period of administrative reorganization examined in this chapter, but his reign began with a direct challenge to royal authority rather than an uncontested consolidation of power. Shortly after his accession, a powerful aristocrat, Kim Heumdol, whose daughter had married into the royal family, led a revolt against the new king, an episode later chronicles connect to broader aristocratic anxiety about the direction of royal policy. Sinmun suppressed the revolt and used the occasion to remove or weaken a number of aristocratic rivals, an outcome later tradition sometimes presents as a decisive turning point toward stronger royal authority.

Following this episode, Sinmun's reign is associated with several of the major administrative measures already discussed in this chapter: completion of the nine-province structure and the five-secondary-capital network by 685, the final incorporation of the Bodeok polity by 684, and reform of central military organization discussed below. Sinmun is also associated with efforts to expand education and recruitment for government service, including the establishment of an institution for Confucian-influenced learning intended to train officials, a development connecting this chapter's administrative focus to the intellectual and educational history of Silla explored further in Chapter 15.

It would be a mistake, however, to read Sinmun's reign as producing anything approaching an absolute monarchy free from aristocratic constraint. The very fact that a revolt reached the point of open conflict at the start of his reign demonstrates the continuing capacity of true-bone families to challenge royal policy directly. Sinmun's measures should be understood as a serious and partly successful attempt to strengthen royal administrative capacity and reduce the most immediate threats to his own authority, not as the permanent resolution of the underlying tension between monarchy and aristocracy that had shaped Silla politics before the wars and would continue to shape it long after Sinmun's death, a tension that resurfaces repeatedly in later Silla history and features prominently in the political crises examined in Chapter 18.

Section 14.12

Land, Taxation, and Labor

Governing an expanded kingdom required Silla's administration to extract resources from a much larger and more varied population than before, chiefly in the form of agricultural produce, textiles, and labor service. The details of Silla's fiscal system during this period are known mostly through fragmentary later references and through the household registers discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, rather than through a single comprehensive legal code, so historians must reconstruct the system's basic outlines with appropriate caution rather than assuming a fully documented, uniform national system.

What the evidence does support is a general picture in which households were registered by officials according to size, labor capacity, landholding, and other resources, including livestock and, in some cases, specific trees valued for their produce. This registration served as the basis for assessing obligations owed to the state, which appear to have included grain and other agricultural tribute, textile production, and corvée labor service on public works, military support tasks, or other state projects. Local officials, often drawn from regional elite families rather than centrally appointed strangers, played an important role in administering these obligations at the village level, illustrating once again how the postwar state depended on cooperation with local society rather than replacing it entirely with centrally controlled administration.

Land itself existed under a range of different arrangements during this period, including land associated with the state, land granted or assigned in connection with office-holding, land held by religious institutions, and land held by ordinary cultivating households, though the precise legal character of these categories, and how sharply they were distinguished in practice, remains a subject of scholarly reconstruction rather than settled certainty. Students should resist importing later or foreign landholding concepts, such as fully private freehold property in the modern sense or a rigidly feudal system of vassalage, onto this evidence without qualification.

Section 14.13

Nok-eup and Official Land Support

One specific and much-discussed institution associated with this period is nok-eup, generally translated as "stipend villages" or similar terms, referring to an arrangement by which officials received support connected to specified villages or land districts rather than, or alongside, direct salary payments. Traditional chronicles distinguish several related but separate measures, and students should take care not to collapse them into a single event. In 687, Sinmun granted gwallyojeon (官僚田), or official land allotments, to civil and military officials according to rank. Two years later, in 689, he abolished nok-eup and substituted graded annual grain payments to officials. These were two different policy instruments addressing two different aspects of official compensation, and later, in 757, well beyond this chapter's chronological focus, monthly official stipends were abolished and nok-eup was restored.

Historians have long debated exactly what rights nok-eup actually conferred on officials who held it. At minimum, it likely involved a right to a defined share of the agricultural produce, and possibly labor service, generated within the specified village or district. Some historians have argued that nok-eup approached a form of local administrative and economic control over the specified population that could rival or complicate central authority, while others caution that the evidence does not clearly establish anything resembling private ownership of land or population, or a fully feudal grant of local sovereignty, in the medieval European sense. Gwallyojeon, by contrast, is generally understood as a more narrowly defined land allotment tied to office and rank rather than a grant of authority over a village's population, though the exact boundary between the two kinds of arrangement is itself a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.

This chapter treats Sinmun's 689 abolition of nok-eup as a meaningful attempt to reduce direct aristocratic control over land-based revenue and to tie official compensation more closely to royal administration and evaluation, consistent with the broader pattern of royal consolidation discussed throughout this chapter. At the same time, the later restoration of nok-eup in 757 suggests that whatever royal gains Sinmun's reform achieved were not permanent, and that aristocratic interests retained enough long-term influence to see the older arrangement eventually revived. Students should resist treating either the 689 reform or the 757 restoration as a simple, one-directional story of either royal victory or aristocratic victory; the underlying tension between royal administrative preference and aristocratic economic interest continued to shape Silla politics well beyond the period examined in this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Nok-eup and Gwallyojeon

Definition: Nok-eup refers to villages or land districts assigned to officials as a source of income, generally understood to involve rights to a share of agricultural produce and possibly labor from the specified area. Gwallyojeon (官僚田) refers to a distinct measure: official land allotments granted in 687 to civil and military officials according to rank, two years before the separate 689 abolition of nok-eup and its replacement with graded annual grain payments.

Historical context: Traditional chronicles record gwallyojeon grants in 687, the abolition of nok-eup and substitution of grain payments in 689, and the restoration of nok-eup in 757 after monthly stipends were abolished. These three dates mark three separate policy moments, not a single continuous reform.

Why it matters: The nok-eup and gwallyojeon measures connect directly to this chapter's central theme of the balance between royal authority and aristocratic economic power. Treating 687 and 689 as the same event obscures the fact that Sinmun pursued more than one distinct instrument for restructuring official compensation.

Potential misunderstanding: Nok-eup should not be casually equated with full private ownership of land, nor with a fully feudal grant of local sovereign authority, and it should not be confused with gwallyojeon, which addressed land allotment rather than village-level revenue rights. The exact bundle of rights involved in each institution remains a subject of scholarly reconstruction, and students should present claims about them with appropriate caution.

Section 14.14

Military Organization and the Nine Seodang

Silla's postwar military establishment served two closely related purposes: defending an expanded and, in the north, uncertain frontier, and providing a mechanism for incorporating diverse populations into structures loyal to the Silla throne. Traditional accounts describe a central military formation known as the Nine Seodang. Later institutional records categorize its nine units by population of origin, including units associated with Silla, former Baekje, former Goguryeo, the Bodeok polity, and Mohe (Malgal) peoples, indicating the deliberate incorporation of manpower from several political and regional backgrounds into centrally organized royal forces.

Historians treat the specific population labels attached to individual Seodang units with appropriate caution, since later textual traditions, compiled well after the events they describe, may simplify or standardize what were likely more complicated and locally variable recruitment patterns. What can be said with more confidence is that incorporating former Baekje and Goguryeo military manpower, along with Bodeok and Mohe recruits, into centrally organized units serving the Silla throne served both practical military needs, given the scale of territory now requiring defense, and political purposes, offering formerly separate populations a path toward integration and advancement through loyal service rather than leaving them as a permanently excluded or potentially rebellious population.

Beyond the central Seodang units, Silla maintained regional military forces responsible for provincial defense and internal order, generally coordinated with the provincial administrative structure discussed earlier in this chapter. Frontier defense, particularly along the uncertain and shifting northern boundary that would eventually separate Silla from Balhae, required sustained military investment and attention throughout this period, reflecting the genuinely contested and incomplete character of Silla's northern consolidation.

Section 14.15

How Integrated Was Silla?

Having surveyed the major administrative, military, and social institutions of postwar Silla, this section steps back to ask directly how far these institutions actually succeeded in creating an integrated kingdom, a question this chapter's Historical Debate feature, below, examines in greater depth.

The evidence for substantial administrative achievement is considerable. Silla established a provincial system covering territory formerly divided among three rival kingdoms; it built a network of secondary capitals, completed by 685, to manage regional elites and balance Gyeongju's dominance; it expanded central offices and, at least for a time, attempted to reform official compensation through both the 687 gwallyojeon grant and the 689 abolition of nok-eup; and it organized a military establishment capable of incorporating diverse populations and defending an extended frontier. Household registration, illustrated by the Silla Village Documents discussed below, demonstrates that state administration reached down to the level of individual villages, recording population, land, and resources with real specificity.

At the same time, the evidence for persistent limits on integration is equally considerable. The bone-rank system preserved hereditary aristocratic privilege throughout this period and structurally limited how far former Baekje and Goguryeo elites, or lower-ranking Silla families, could rise regardless of service or talent. The revolt associated with Kim Heumdol demonstrates that aristocratic families retained the capacity to challenge royal authority by force even at the height of postwar consolidation. The later restoration of nok-eup in 757 suggests that royal efforts to redirect land-based revenue away from aristocratic control did not achieve a permanent or complete victory. And Silla's rule never extended to all former Goguryeo territory, leaving a substantial portion of the peninsula's earlier political geography outside the "unified" kingdom altogether.

The most defensible conclusion, and the one this chapter and its accompanying Historical Debate feature ask students to reach, is a qualified one: Silla's monarchy achieved a genuine and historically significant expansion of administrative reach and royal authority after 676, while simultaneously operating within limits imposed by aristocratic privilege, regional variation, and incomplete territorial control that prevented anything resembling full centralization in the modern sense.

Section 14.16

A Consolidated Kingdom—but Not a Finished Political Order

By the early decades of the eighth century, Silla's monarchy had built a substantially more elaborate and far-reaching system of government than any earlier Silla ruler had commanded: a provincial structure spanning former Silla, Baekje, and Goguryeo territory; a completed network of secondary capitals managing regional elites; expanded central offices; a reorganized military incorporating diverse populations; and administrative mechanisms capable of registering villages, assessing taxation, and mobilizing labor. This was a genuine political achievement, built directly on the military outcome described in Chapter 13, and it created the stability and resources that would support the remarkable Buddhist institutional and cultural flourishing examined in Chapter 15.

Yet this chapter has also shown that consolidation remained incomplete in several fundamental ways. Powerful true-bone aristocratic families continued to dominate the highest offices of state and retained the capacity, demonstrated at the very start of Sinmun's reign, to challenge royal authority directly. Local and regional identities associated with former Baekje and Goguryeo persisted well beyond the period examined in this chapter, and even the fifteen-year process by which the Bodeok polity was finally absorbed shows how gradual full incorporation could be. Substantial northern territory once associated with Goguryeo remained entirely outside Silla's control, soon to be organized under Balhae. And the kingdom's capacity to project uniform administrative authority varied considerably depending on distance from Gyeongju, the cooperation of local elites, and the particular history of the region in question.

The kingdom Silla's rulers had built, in other words, had solved the immediate problem of surviving and organizing the aftermath of decades of warfare, but it had not resolved the underlying tension between a monarchy seeking to expand its authority and an aristocracy determined to preserve its own privileges, wealth, and influence. That tension, along with the religious, cultural, and intellectual life that flourished within the stability this chapter has described, forms the subject of the next chapter, which turns from the machinery of government examined here to the religious institutions, scholarship, and artistic achievement of Silla's Buddhist civilization.

Primary Source Window

The Silla Village Documents — A Disputed Date

Creator: unnamed Silla local officials responsible for household and land registration · Date: genuinely disputed among specialists; proposed dates range from the later seventh century to the later ninth century, and no single dating commands unanimous scholarly agreement · Type: Administrative household and land registers, preserved by chance as part of a later manuscript in Japan

Context: A set of household registers, commonly referred to as the Silla Village Documents, survived not in Korea but as reused paper within a later manuscript held in Japan, where they were rediscovered by modern scholars. The documents record, for a small number of villages, detailed information including household counts by size category, population figures, landholding by category, numbers of livestock, and counts of certain valuable trees such as mulberry and walnut, apparently for purposes connected to taxation and labor assessment.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the kind of information the documents record.

For each village, the registers list the number of households, grouped into several size or wealth categories, along with total population figures broken down by age and sex categories, total landholding by type, and counts of livestock and certain economically valuable trees, with figures updated periodically to reflect deaths, births, migration, and other changes.

Analysis: These documents provide historians with rare, concrete evidence of how the Silla state's administrative reach extended into ordinary rural communities, confirming that the provincial and taxation structures discussed throughout this chapter were not merely aspirational frameworks described in later chronicles but were connected to genuine, detailed record-keeping at the local level. The level of demographic and agricultural detail recorded suggests a meaningfully sophisticated administrative capacity for whichever period the documents belong to.

Limitations: Only a small number of villages are represented in the surviving documents, and historians disagree, sometimes sharply, about their date, about how representative these villages were of Silla's rural population as a whole, and about the exact administrative purpose the registers served. Because the proposed dates span more than a century, from the later seventh century to the later ninth century, the documents cannot be used to characterize any single, precisely dated moment in Silla administration without acknowledging this uncertainty. The documents also cannot, by themselves, reveal how consistently such registration was maintained across the kingdom's full territory, particularly in more distant or more recently incorporated provinces. Historians must therefore treat the documents as valuable but geographically limited and chronologically uncertain evidence rather than as a complete picture of Silla's rural administration at one fixed point in time.

Perspective: The documents represent an official, state-centered perspective, recording population and resources as objects of administrative interest rather than describing village life from the perspective of the villagers themselves. The internal experience, attitudes, and possible resistance of registered households do not appear directly in this kind of record.

Historical Debate

How Centralized Was Unified Silla?

The question: Did the administrative measures examined in this chapter—provincial reorganization, secondary capitals, expanded central offices, land and taxation reform, and military reorganization—produce a genuinely centralized kingdom capable of governing its expanded territory uniformly, or did aristocratic privilege, regional variation, and incomplete territorial control mean that centralization remained partial and uneven?

Interpretation A — Silla achieved substantial centralization: Some historians emphasize the scale of administrative achievement: a provincial system spanning three formerly rival kingdoms' territory, a completed network of secondary capitals managing regional elites, expanded central bureaucracy, two distinct land and compensation measures under Sinmun (the 687 gwallyojeon grant and the 689 abolition of nok-eup), a reorganized military incorporating diverse populations, and detailed household registration reaching to the village level. Taken together, these measures represent one of the more ambitious administrative projects in early Korean history.
Interpretation B — Centralization remained partial and uneven: Other historians emphasize the persistence of hereditary aristocratic privilege through the bone-rank system, the demonstrated capacity of true-bone families to challenge royal authority by force, the later restoration of nok-eup after its supposed abolition, continuing regional identities associated with former Baekje and Goguryeo, the fifteen-year process required to fully absorb even the comparatively small Bodeok polity, and the incomplete territorial reach of Silla's government, which never extended over all former Goguryeo territory. On this view, "centralization" describes a royal aspiration and a partial achievement rather than a completed political transformation.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: later court chronicles describing administrative measures and the Kim Heumdol revolt, archaeological evidence from Gyeongju and the secondary capitals, and the Silla Village Documents. They differ chiefly in how much weight to give evidence of administrative ambition versus evidence of continuing aristocratic and regional limits.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and emphasis rather than a dispute over basic facts: both interpretations generally agree on the existence of the nine provinces, the five secondary capitals, the Kim Heumdol revolt, and the 687 and 689 land and compensation measures. They disagree about how to weigh this evidence in reaching an overall assessment of the kingdom's political character.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they understand the eventual political crises of later Silla history, examined in Chapter 18, since a view of Silla as largely successfully centralized would treat later aristocratic conflict as a later reversal, while a view of Silla as only partially centralized would treat later conflict as the continuation of tensions present from the very beginning of the postwar period.

Student task: Using the specific evidence presented in this chapter, construct a qualified answer to the debate's question rather than choosing one interpretation exclusively. Identify which kinds of evidence you find most persuasive and explain why a fully one-sided answer would be difficult to defend.

Evidence Traditions for Understanding Postwar Consolidation

The perspectives below are defined by surviving bodies of evidence rather than invented voices. Each source type illuminates different aspects of consolidation and carries its own limitations.

Court and aristocratic evidence preserved in the Samguk Sagi: Later chronicle traditions preserve Sinmun's accession, the Kim Heumdol revolt, the 687 gwallyojeon grant, the 689 abolition of nok-eup, and the fate of the Bodeok polity. They are indispensable for political chronology but were compiled centuries later and reflect court-centered priorities.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Silla before 660 and Silla after 676

What Changed

  • Territorial scale expanded dramatically, incorporating former Baekje and much of southern former Goguryeo territory
  • Population grew substantially, including communities with no prior history of loyalty to the Silla throne
  • Provincial and secondary-capital administration developed to manage an expanded and more diverse kingdom
  • Central military organization incorporated diverse populations, including the Nine Seodang, rather than relying solely on Silla's traditional core forces
  • Land and compensation policy, including the 687 gwallyojeon grant and the 689 nok-eup reform associated with Sinmun, became an active area of royal intervention

What Stayed the Same

  • Gyeongju remained the political, aristocratic, and religious center of the kingdom
  • The bone-rank system continued to govern eligibility for office, status, and royal succession
  • True-bone aristocratic families continued to dominate the highest offices of state and retained the capacity to challenge royal authority
  • The underlying tension between monarchy and aristocracy, present in earlier Silla history, persisted rather than disappearing after the wars

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both dramatic territorial and administrative change alongside deep social and institutional continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating "Unified Silla" as an entirely new political order created from nothing after 676, or treating the postwar period as simply "more of the same" despite the genuinely transformed scale and complexity of the kingdom's governing challenges.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Silla Reorganize Provincial Government after 676?

Long-term causes

Silla's traditional bone-rank aristocratic structure and Gyeongju-centered political geography had developed to govern a comparatively modest southeastern kingdom, not a territory spanning the former holdings of three rival states.

Short-term causes

The sudden postwar acquisition of former Baekje territory and southern former Goguryeo territory, along with unfamiliar populations, elites, and administrative traditions, created an immediate need for a governing framework capable of incorporating this new territory.

Effect

The development of the nine-province system and the five secondary capitals, completed by 685, provided a common administrative structure spanning the kingdom's diverse territory, while expanded central offices and military reorganization extended royal reach into taxation, labor, and defense.

Historical complexity

These measures achieved real administrative reach without fully resolving aristocratic privilege, regional variation, or incomplete territorial control, meaning the reorganization should be understood as a significant but partial response to the challenges of postwar consolidation.

Historical complexity: Provincial reorganization was not a single cause producing a single, complete outcome; it interacted with military reform, land policy, aristocratic politics, and the differing histories of former Baekje and Goguryeo territory to produce a kingdom that was more administratively developed than before but still short of uniform centralized control.

Everyday Life and Social History

Consolidation Below the Court

The administrative measures examined in this chapter were not experienced by most people primarily as abstract questions of centralization; they were experienced as concrete changes in daily obligations and community life. A farming household in a newly registered village encountered the postwar state chiefly through officials recording household size, landholding, and livestock, and through the resulting demands for grain, textiles, or labor service, evidence for which survives concretely in the Silla Village Documents discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window. A former Baekje local headman might experience consolidation as an uneasy balance between retained local standing and new obligations to Silla provincial administrators. A soldier recruited into the Nine Seodang from a former Goguryeo or Bodeok background might experience integration through military service and the possibility, however limited, of advancement within a hierarchy that still reserved its highest ranks for native Silla true-bone families.

Women's experience of this period is only partially visible in surviving evidence, though household registers such as the Silla Village Documents recorded population by age and sex categories, suggesting that women's labor and household roles factored directly into the state's assessment of village resources and obligations. Resettled populations, including communities relocated in connection with the five secondary capitals as they were established in stages between the pre-war period and 685, experienced consolidation as a combination of new administrative demands and, for some elite families, new opportunities for status and integration into the wider kingdom's political and cultural life. Because most surviving textual sources were produced by or for the court and aristocracy, historians must read such sources carefully alongside archaeological and administrative evidence to reconstruct the experience of populations who left few records of their own.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined what "Unified Silla" actually meant politically in the decades following the wars described in Chapter 13, and how successfully Silla's monarchy converted a battlefield victory into a functioning system of government. It began by cautioning against overstating the term "Unified Silla" itself: Silla's postwar kingdom governed most of the Korean peninsula but not all of the territory once associated with Goguryeo, and its rule over the territory it did control involved political incorporation and administrative development rather than instantaneous uniform integration.

The chapter traced how Silla absorbed former Baekje territory and incorporated members of the former ruling stratum into Silla ranks below the highest native aristocratic standing, and how a similar, though geographically more limited, process unfolded with former Goguryeo populations south of the eventual frontier with Balhae. The Silla-sponsored restoration polity associated with Anseung and Bodeok illustrates this process concretely: recognized in 670, elevated to a kingship in 674, and only fully absorbed after Anseung's incorporation into the Silla aristocracy in 683 and the suppression of a revolt at the former Bodeok center in 684, a transition spanning roughly a decade and a half rather than a single moment.

It examined the major administrative instruments of postwar consolidation: the nine-province system, which organized former Silla, Baekje, and Goguryeo territory under a common framework; the five secondary capitals, established in stages from before the unification wars through 685, which managed regional elites and balanced Gyeongju's dominance; and the continuing centrality of Gyeongju itself as the kingdom's political, aristocratic, and religious core.

The chapter gave particular attention to the relationship between monarchy and the true-bone aristocracy, explaining the bone-rank system's continuing role in governing eligibility for office and status even as the throne itself, following the extinction of the sacred-bone lineage, belonged to the same broad aristocratic category as the powerful families the king depended on to govern. King Sinmun's reign received focused treatment, including the aristocratic revolt associated with Kim Heumdol at the very start of his reign, his administrative and educational initiatives, and two distinct land and compensation measures: the 687 grant of gwallyojeon to officials according to rank, and the separate 689 abolition of nok-eup in favor of graded grain payments. The precise character of nok-eup remains debated among historians, and its restoration in 757 after a period of official stipends shows that compensation arrangements continued to change.

The chapter also examined the fiscal and military dimensions of consolidation: household registration and taxation in kind, illustrated concretely by the Silla Village Documents, whose date remains genuinely disputed among specialists across a range spanning more than a century; and the reorganization of central military forces, including the Nine Seodang, which combined defense needs with the political integration of diverse populations drawn from Silla, Baekje, Goguryeo, Bodeok, and Mohe backgrounds.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized a central analytical distinction: territorial conquest, political incorporation, and administrative uniformity are three different processes, and Silla achieved the first fully, the second substantially though unevenly, and the third only partially. This chapter's Historical Debate feature asked students to weigh evidence for and against describing Unified Silla as a genuinely centralized kingdom, concluding that a qualified answer, recognizing both real administrative achievement and persistent aristocratic and regional limits, is more defensible than an answer favoring either extreme.

The chapter closed by connecting its findings to the chapters that follow: the stability and resources that postwar consolidation provided created the conditions for the Buddhist institutional and cultural flourishing examined in Chapter 15, while the underlying tension between royal authority and aristocratic privilege, never fully resolved during this period, would resurface in the political crises of later Silla history examined in Chapter 18.

These themes, conquest, incorporation, administration, consolidation, and persistent limits, provide students with a framework for understanding not only Silla's own postwar kingdom but also the general historical problem, recurring throughout world history, of how expanding states attempt to govern newly acquired territory and populations.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Unified Silla
The conventional historical label for Silla's kingdom after 676, describing its dominant position on the peninsula while not implying rule over all former Goguryeo territory or full administrative uniformity.
Political incorporation
The process by which conquered populations, elites, and territory were drawn into a conquering state's political and administrative system, distinct from mere territorial conquest.
Administrative uniformity
The degree to which a state's laws, taxation, and governance are applied consistently across its entire territory; a condition Silla approached unevenly rather than achieving completely.
Bodeok polity
A Silla-sponsored Goguryeo restoration polity associated with Anseung, recognized in 670, elevated to a kingship in 674, and fully absorbed into Silla administration only after events in 683–684.
Nine provinces (nine-ju)
The provincial administrative structure organizing Silla's expanded postwar territory, combining former Silla, Baekje, and Goguryeo lands, generally described as complete by 685.
Five secondary capitals (sogyeong)
Regional administrative centers used to manage relocated elites and balance the political dominance of Gyeongju, established in stages from before the unification wars through 685.
Bone-rank system (golpum)
Silla's hereditary status hierarchy governing eligibility for office, marriage, and status markers, including the seonggol and jingol ranks.
Seonggol
"Sacred-bone," the highest bone-rank category, restricted to a narrow royal lineage that became extinct by the mid-seventh century.
Jingol
"True-bone," the highest surviving bone-rank category after the extinction of the sacred-bone lineage, from which Silla's monarchs and highest officials were drawn after the mid-seventh century.
Head-rank (dupum)
Bone-rank categories below true-bone status, governing the eligibility of lower aristocratic and administrative families for office, generally with firm ceilings on advancement.
Hwabaek
A council of high true-bone aristocrats exercising influence over major state decisions, not equivalent to a modern representative assembly.
Kim Heumdol revolt
An aristocratic uprising at the start of King Sinmun's reign in 681, illustrating continuing aristocratic capacity to challenge royal authority.
Nok-eup
Villages or land districts assigned to officials as a source of income, abolished by Sinmun in 689 in favor of graded grain payments, and restored in 757.
Gwallyojeon
Official land allotments granted in 687 to civil and military officials according to rank, a distinct measure from, and two years prior to, the 689 abolition of nok-eup.
Nine Seodang
A central military formation whose units later institutional records associate with Silla, Baekje, Goguryeo, Bodeok, and Mohe populations.
Corvée labor
Compulsory labor service owed to the state, assessed as part of household obligations alongside agricultural and textile tribute.
Household registration
The administrative practice of recording population, landholding, and resources by household, illustrated concretely by the Silla Village Documents.
Silla Village Documents
A set of household registers of genuinely disputed date, preserved by chance in Japan, providing rare direct evidence of village-level Silla administration.
Restoration movement
Political and military efforts by former Baekje or Goguryeo loyalists to revive their fallen kingdoms, discussed in Chapter 13 and referenced here in connection with the Bodeok polity.
Sponsored polity
A modern analytical term for a nominally autonomous polity whose actions are substantially directed by a more powerful sponsoring state; used here for the Bodeok/Anseung arrangement, which was not itself a fixed Silla-period institutional category.
Regional elite
Locally powerful families, often with pre-conquest standing, whose cooperation local administration depended upon even after territorial incorporation.
Frontier administration
Governance arrangements adapted to contested or unstable border regions, particularly relevant to Silla's uncertain northern boundary with territory that became Balhae.
Royal consolidation
The process by which a monarchy expands and secures its administrative, military, and fiscal authority, examined throughout this chapter as a partial rather than complete achievement.
Central offices
Government bodies and positions based at the royal capital responsible for administration, finance, military affairs, and diplomacy.
Taxation in kind
Tax obligations paid through agricultural produce, textiles, or labor rather than money, characteristic of Silla's fiscal system in this period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look past the drama of conquest, examined in Chapter 13, toward the quieter but equally consequential work of governing what had been won. Silla's rulers faced a problem common to expanding states throughout world history: how to convert military success into durable political authority over unfamiliar territory and populations, without either provoking the aristocracy whose cooperation victory itself had depended upon, or leaving newly conquered regions effectively outside the reach of royal government.

The answers Silla's monarchy developed, examined here through the nine provinces, the five secondary capitals, bone-rank politics, land and taxation policy, and military reorganization, represent a genuine and historically significant achievement. They also represent a partial and, in important respects, unfinished one. The persistence of aristocratic privilege, the fifteen years it took to fully absorb even the comparatively small Bodeok polity, the incomplete reach of royal administration into every region and every social class, and the northern territory that remained permanently outside Silla's control all demonstrate that "Unified Silla" describes a real transformation without describing a finished or uniform political order.

This chapter's central distinction, between conquering territory, incorporating it politically, and administering it uniformly, is not a narrow technical point relevant only to seventh-century Korea. It is a general historical pattern that recurs whenever a state expands beyond its original core, a pattern students will encounter again in later volumes of this textbook, from Goryeo's incorporation of the Later Three Kingdoms' successor states, to Joseon's administration of its provinces, to the modern Korean states' own, very different, twentieth-century challenges of building governing capacity.

Understanding how Silla's kings built, and only partially completed, a postwar political order equips students to appreciate both the remarkable cultural and religious achievements the following chapter will examine, made possible by the stability this consolidation provided, and the political tensions, rooted in the very institutions examined here, that would eventually reshape Silla's history in the centuries that followed.
