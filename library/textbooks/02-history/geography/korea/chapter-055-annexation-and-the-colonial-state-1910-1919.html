Annexation and the Colonial State, 1910–1919

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On 29 August 1910 the name "Korean Empire" disappeared from the world's list of states. Within weeks, the capital that Koreans called Hanseong or Seoul was being administered as Keijō (Gyeongseong), a district of a province, and the old structures of the Korean court, the cabinet, the Residency-General, and the protectorate were being replaced by a single institution: the Government-General of Chōsen. The question this chapter asks is what that institution actually was, and what it did to the people who lived under it.

Chapter 54 traced the diplomatic and military steps by which Japan extinguished Korean sovereignty: the war with Russia, the protectorate treaty of 1905, the forced abdication of Gojong in 1907, the disbanding of the Korean army, the suppression of the righteous armies, and the annexation treaty concluded on 22 August 1910 and promulgated on 29 August. Chapter 55 begins on the far side of that event. Its subject is not the act of annexation but the system that followed it: how a state that had formally ceased to exist was replaced by a colonial government with extraordinary concentrated powers, how that government reached into provinces, counties, townships, villages, households, schools, temples, and markets, and how Koreans experienced, adapted to, evaded, and resisted it between 1910 and the eve of 1919.

The central historical problem is one of institutional transformation. Annexation did not merely substitute Japanese rulers for Korean ones. It created a new constitutional arrangement in which the executive, legislative, judicial, police, fiscal, and educational functions of government were gathered under a governor-general appointed from the senior ranks of the Japanese army or navy, answerable to the emperor in Tokyo and not to any elected body in either Korea or Japan. Koreans were declared imperial subjects but were not given the political rights that the Japanese constitution attached to subjecthood in the home islands. The colonial regime of the 1910s is often called "military-police rule" (budan seiji) because of the prominent place of the gendarmerie, but that label describes only part of the system, which also included a cadastral survey, a new fiscal and banking order, a restructured legal system, regulated schools and temples, and new roads, railways, and city plans.

This chapter treats that system as a functioning apparatus rather than as a list of repressive decrees. It examines how legal authority was concentrated; how the police acted as administrators as well as enforcers; how land registration, taxation, and property law reinforced one another; how financial and company regulation shaped economic opportunity; how education, religion, and cultural knowledge became arenas of political contest; and how railways, ports, and city streets both connected Korea to Japan's economy and extended the reach of the state. It also asks what Koreans did: how officials, intermediaries, landlords, tenants, teachers, students, pastors, Buddhist abbots, Cheondogyo members, and conspirators responded, and how a decade of accumulating pressure prepared the ground for the events of 1919.

The historian's evidence for these questions is unusually rich but unevenly distributed. The colonial state generated enormous quantities of its own records: the Government-General Gazette (Chōsen Sōtokufu Kanpō), ordinances and regulations, statistical yearbooks, annual administrative reports, cadastral maps and registers, court judgments, police files, and publicity materials such as the commemorative volumes for the 1915 exhibition. These are indispensable, but they are the records of the governing power and reflect its categories and purposes. Korean voices survive in diaries, memoirs, petitions, trial testimony, the surviving Korean-language press, religious periodicals, oral histories, and the later writings of nationalist historians, but they are scattered and in many cases were produced under conditions of censorship or recorded long afterward. Wherever possible this chapter distinguishes what the colonial government claimed, what it ordered, what it measured, and what Koreans reported experiencing.

The chapter also takes care with terminology. "Colonial" is used here for the system of rule that Japan established over Korea after 1910; it was not identical to the colonial systems of European empires, nor to Japan's earlier colony of Taiwan, and the Japanese state itself usually spoke of Korea as part of the "outer territories" (gaichi) of the empire or, in rhetoric, as a land to be unified with Japan under the principle of a single sovereign. "Modernization" is used cautiously, as a description of institutional and technological change and not as a verdict on its human consequences. "Resistance," "accommodation," and "collaboration" are treated as overlapping and shifting conditions of people placed in constrained circumstances, not as fixed moral categories.

Finally, the chapter observes the boundaries established by the textbook's architecture. Developments that continue past 1919, including the changes in colonial administration after the March First Movement, the rise of mass culture and cities treated in Chapter 57, the long-term economic transformations of Chapter 58, the independence organizations of Chapter 59, and the later assimilation campaigns of Chapter 60, are noted only where necessary to explain their origins. The chapter ends at the threshold of 1919, with the political, economic, religious, and social grievances that had accumulated by then and with the death of the former emperor Gojong in January 1919, which opens the way to Chapter 56.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the immediate legal and administrative consequences of annexation in August 1910 and describe how the Government-General of Chōsen replaced the institutions of the Korean Empire and the protectorate.
  2. Analyze why the governor-generalship concentrated executive, legislative, military, and judicial-administrative powers to an unusual degree, and how its ordinances functioned as law.
  3. Trace the organization of provincial, county, township, and municipal administration, including the consolidations of 1914, and explain how it differed between Japanese-populated cities and Korean rural areas.
  4. Describe how the integrated gendarmerie-police system, summary punishment, the Flogging Ordinance of 1912, publishing controls, and association controls operated as an interlocking system of coercion and administration.
  5. Evaluate the land survey of 1910–1918 as a measurement program, a property-law transformation, and a fiscal project, and distinguish registered ownership, customary rights, state land, and tenancy.
  6. Explain how the colonial budget, Bank of Chōsen, Oriental Development Company, Company Ordinance, and agricultural policy shaped unequal economic opportunity without assuming that all Korean enterprise ceased.
  7. Compare the 1911 education ordinance, private-school regulations of 1911 and 1915, Temple Ordinance, and cultural-knowledge policies as efforts to shape loyalty, identity, and future leadership.
  8. Assess railways, roads, ports, urban reorganization, the 1915 exhibition, and public-health measures by asking who built, controlled, paid for, and benefited from them.
  9. Interpret rural, urban, gendered, and legal experiences of colonial rule, including the Sinminhoe and the 105-Person Incident, using sources that present different perspectives.
  10. Distinguish official colonial statistics and development claims from evidence about living standards, and evaluate competing historical interpretations of the first colonial decade.
  11. Contextualize the First World War, international discourse on self-determination, and the death of Gojong as elements in the crisis of colonial legitimacy by early 1919.

Historical Context

By August 1910 the Korean state had been dismantled in stages. The protectorate treaty of 1905 had given Japan control over Korea's foreign relations and installed a Resident-General in Seoul, Itō Hirobumi, whose office extended its authority into domestic administration. The agreement of 1907, following Gojong's forced abdication, allowed Japanese officials to take up posts within the Korean government and led to the dissolution of the Korean army, an event that sent soldiers into the righteous armies (uibyeong) and intensified rural guerrilla warfare. By 1909 Japanese forces had broken the main concentrations of armed resistance, and in July 1909 the Korean judiciary and prisons were transferred to Japanese control. In June 1910 police affairs were also transferred. Annexation in August 1910 therefore formalized an authority that Japan already exercised in most fields.

The colonial regime inherited structures as well as grievances. The Korean Empire, proclaimed in 1897, had attempted to build its own modern institutions: a land survey under the Gwangmu reforms that issued deeds in part of the country, a modernized army and police, new schools, railways, telegraphs, and a monetary system that had come under Japanese influence after 1905. Its governing elite included reformers, conservatives, and officials who collaborated with Japan, and many Koreans had come to believe that some form of modernization was necessary, even as they disagreed fiercely about who should lead it and on whose terms. The colonial state built on earlier surveys, banks, police, and legal reforms, and many of its personnel and legal drafts dated from the protectorate years. At the same time it ended the political and diplomatic existence of the state that had initiated some of them.

The Korea of 1910 was predominantly agrarian. Most of its population, roughly thirteen million by contemporary official estimates that were themselves imperfect, lived in villages and depended on rice, barley, and other crops grown under a mix of owner-cultivation, tenancy, and customary arrangements. Landholding was uneven, rights to land were often layered rather than singular, and documentary proof of ownership varied widely across regions and over time. A small number of cities, including Seoul, Pyongyang, Busan, Incheon, Wonsan, and Daegu, were centers of commerce and administration, and treaty ports such as Busan, Incheon, Wonsan, Mokpo, Gunsan, and Jinnampo had Japanese settlements that predated annexation. The Japanese population in Korea was small in 1910, on the order of 170,000, but it was concentrated in ports and capitals and enjoyed legal and commercial advantages from the treaty era onward.

The wider international environment was permissive. Britain, the United States, Russia, France, and other powers with interests in East Asia had accepted Japan's position in Korea through agreements concluded between 1905 and 1910, and none contested the annexation. The Japanese empire had also accumulated colonial experience in Taiwan, acquired in 1895, and in Karafuto and the Kwantung Leased Territory, but Korea was larger, more densely populated, and culturally closer to Japan in some respects and more resistant to it in others. Japanese officials, journalists, and scholars debated whether Korea should be governed as a distinct colony or progressively assimilated into the home islands, and that debate shaped policy throughout the decade. The decisions made in 1910 were therefore not the unfolding of a single plan, but a series of choices by officials who drew on precedent, military priorities, financial constraints, and their own ideas about Korean society, while Koreans responded in ways that the planners did not control.

Section 55.1

From Annexation to Colonial Rule: The Abolition of the Korean State

The annexation treaty, signed on 22 August 1910 by Prime Minister Yi Wan-yong for the Korean side and Resident-General Terauchi Masatake for Japan, and made public on 29 August, declared in its first article that the Korean emperor ceded all rights of sovereignty over Korea completely and permanently to the emperor of Japan, and in its second that the Japanese emperor accepted that cession and agreed to the complete annexation of Korea to the Japanese empire. The circumstances of its conclusion, including the pressure under which it was signed and the long-running scholarly and political dispute over its legal validity, belong to Chapter 54. For the purposes of this chapter, what matters is its practical effect. On 29 August the Korean Empire, which had called itself Daehan Jeguk since 1897, ceased to exist as a state. The old name Chōsen (Joseon) was restored as a geographic and administrative designation, and the Residency-General, the Korean cabinet, and the former ministries were dissolved or reorganized under a new Government-General.

The Korean ruling house was neither abolished nor left in place. The former emperor Sunjong was given the title "King Yi" (Yi Wang) and his father Gojong the title "Retired King Yi" (Yi Taiō), and both, together with other members of the family, were incorporated into the Japanese imperial family's orbit of rank and privilege. An Office of the Yi Household (Ri-ōke Jimukyoku) was created under the Japanese Imperial Household Ministry to manage the family's affairs and finances, and the family received an annual grant from the Japanese treasury. The arrangement kept the former monarchs in Seoul, in the palaces at Changdeok and Deoksu, as figures whose presence both lent the new order a measure of continuity and removed any independent political function from them. Gojong's continued presence at Deoksugung until 1919 would later acquire large symbolic importance.

The new order also created a titled Korean elite. Japan established a system of peerage for selected Koreans, granting a total of seventy-six titles of marquis, count, viscount, and baron to officials and members of the royal family, many of whom had held high office under the Korean Empire and some of whom had been prominent in the annexation negotiations. A few recipients are recorded as refusing or returning their titles, although the exact details are less secure than the main fact. Separate payments, known as imperial bounties, were distributed to officials, elderly people, and local notables as a gesture of the new regime's benevolence. At the same time, political societies were dissolved, and a group of scholars and former officials, including the writer Hwang Hyeon, ended their lives in protest in the weeks after annexation. Their suicides are documented in Korean sources and in Hwang's own writings, and became an enduring symbol of refusal.

Annexation also changed the legal character of Koreans. They became subjects of the Japanese emperor, but the principle of subjecthood was applied differently in Korea than in the home islands. The Meiji constitution and the Diet's ordinary legislative process were not extended in the same form to Korea. Instead, Korea was treated as a territory governed by imperial ordinance and by the ordinances of the governor-general, and the Japanese household registration system (koseki) distinguished people legally registered in Korea from those registered in the home islands. These distinctions, which would persist through 1945, made possible a system in which Koreans and Japanese lived under the same sovereign yet under different laws, rights, and obligations. The next sections examine the institution that exercised that sovereignty in practice.

Section 55.2

Terauchi Masatake and the Creation of the Government-General

The Government-General of Chōsen was established by imperial edict at annexation and began operating on 1 October 1910. Its first head, Terauchi Masatake (1852–1919), was a general from the Chōshū domain who had risen through the Meiji army, served as war minister from 1902, and had been appointed Resident-General in May 1910 with the specific task of preparing and carrying out annexation. He initially continued to hold his position as war minister while serving in Korea. His appointment signaled that Korea was to be governed first as a strategic military possession and only second as a field of civil administration. Terauchi remained governor-general until October 1916, after which he became prime minister of Japan.

The governor-generalship was an unusual office. Governors-general were appointed by the emperor from among active or retired army generals and navy admirals, a rule that remained in force until 1919 and, although formally relaxed then, was never in practice applied to a civilian appointment. The governor-general stood directly under the emperor rather than within the ordinary line of cabinet ministers, held command over the Japanese forces stationed in Korea, and supervised the entire administrative apparatus. Within the empire this combination was extraordinary. In Japan proper, military command, legislation, and administration were separated among the emperor, the Diet, the cabinet, and the services. In Korea, the governor-general exercised significant authority in all of these domains, answerable in theory to the emperor and in practice to the political leaders who had chosen him. A colonial affairs bureau in Tokyo coordinated some matters, but the Government-General retained broad autonomy.

Several reasons explain this concentration. Japanese leaders regarded Korea as strategically vital and politically unstable, and they believed that the swift suppression of resistance and the creation of a reliable administrative base required unified command. The army had a long record of involvement in Korean affairs from the Sino-Japanese War through the Russo-Japanese War and the campaigns against the righteous armies. The army and navy ministries insisted on military appointments to the governor-generalship, and political opponents of that arrangement in Japan had limited influence over it. Finally, the model of Taiwan, where a similar concentration of authority existed, offered precedent. The combination meant that the rules governing Koreans could change rapidly by governor-general ordinance, without parliamentary debate in either Korea or Japan.

The institution itself was initially housed on Namsan in Seoul, in buildings inherited from the Residency-General. It was a large organization from the outset, built on Japanese officials who had served the protectorate and on additional personnel brought from Japan. A new headquarters on the grounds of Gyeongbokgung, begun in 1916 and completed in 1926, would later become a symbol of the colonial order, but it did not exist in the 1910s and should not be imagined as the setting of the early regime. The first years of the Government-General were years of improvisation and consolidation, in which officials built the legal and administrative machinery whose operation the rest of this chapter describes.

Historical Vocabulary
Government-General, Military-Police Rule, and Colonial Ordinance

Definition. The Government-General (Chōsen Sōtokufu) was the colonial administration of Korea from 1910 to 1945, headed by a governor-general. Military-police rule (budan seiji, literally "rule by force of arms") is the label commonly applied to the regime of 1910–1919, in which gendarmes held police powers and the governor-general was a senior military officer. A colonial ordinance (seirei) was a rule issued by the governor-general with the force of law.

Historical context. These terms were produced by the colonial system itself and by later historians. "Military-police rule" was a retrospective label widely used after the reforms that followed 1919, when the regime itself began to speak of "cultural rule" (bunka seiji). Neither phrase should be read as a complete account of how colonial government operated.

Why it matters. The terms point to the distinctive features of the regime: concentration of authority, direct military leadership, and law made by executive decree.

Potential misunderstanding. "Military rule" does not mean that the army governed every neighborhood directly. Most daily administration was carried out by civilian officials, police, and Korean intermediaries, within a system whose top leadership and coercive backbone were military.

Section 55.3

Colonial Executive Power, Legislation, and the Bureaucratic State

The legal foundation of the Government-General rested on a transfer of legislative power. Rather than require Korean law to be enacted by the Japanese Diet, imperial instruments and the March 25, 1911 Law No. 30 on Laws and Ordinances to Be Enforced in Korea authorized the governor-general to issue ordinances (seirei) having the force of law within Korea, subject to imperial sanction and to the supervision of the Japanese government. Imperial edicts at annexation continued most existing Korean laws and regulations in force until replaced, which provided a transitional legal base but also allowed the Government-General to revise them selectively. In practice, ordinances regulated land, taxation, companies, education, religion, publishing, criminal punishment, and much else, and the regime issued them rapidly, often with little advance public notice.

Below the governor-general stood the civil administrator (seimu sōkan), who supervised the departments and handled much of the routine direction of the bureaucracy. For most of the decade this post was held by Yamagata Isaburō. The central secretariat and departments (initially General Affairs, Home Affairs, Finance, Agriculture, Commerce and Industry, and Justice) drafted ordinances, prepared budgets, supervised provincial governors, and managed the flow of instructions and reports. A separate police affairs bureau, directed by the commander of the gendarmerie, linked the coercive apparatus to the civil bureaucracy. A Central Advisory Council (Chūsūin), staffed by Korean notables with a Korean leadership, was intended to give the colonial order a Korean advisory face, but it had no legislative power and was consulted only on questions the Government-General chose to put to it.

Budget administration illustrates the system's dependence on Tokyo and also its autonomy. Korea's finances were organized in a special account, reviewed in Japan, and the Japanese treasury provided subsidies in the early years to cover the gap between colonial revenue and expenditure. These subsidies diminished and ended toward the end of the decade as revenues from land tax and other sources rose. The governor-general nevertheless had wide discretion over how funds were spent, and a large share of early expenditure went to administration, police, and infrastructure rather than to schools or welfare.

The effect of this structure was to concentrate legal authority while distributing practical decision-making through a bureaucratic chain that reached the villages. Because ordinances could be issued and changed by the same officials who enforced them, the system was flexible and responsive to administrators' priorities, but it was also unaccountable to those it governed. Koreans had no elected body, no independent press, and no way to challenge ordinances through legislative process, and the courts, as Section 55.8 shows, were part of the same administrative structure. The bureaucratic state of the 1910s was therefore both more regular and more arbitrary than its predecessor: more regular in its records, budgets, and procedures, and more arbitrary in the absence of any political check on its rules.

The distinction between seirei and ordinary administrative regulations is particularly important. Law No. 30 authorized the governor-general to issue measures with statutory effect, usually after imperial approval, while ordinary Government-General regulations (furei) handled a broad range of subordinate administrative matters. In urgent circumstances the procedures could be accelerated. These were not laws debated in a Korean elected assembly, nor did they require that Korean taxpayers, teachers, merchants, or villagers consent to them. Yet the governor-general was not acting outside every legal constraint: legislation took shape within the Japanese imperial hierarchy, and some matters had to be referred to Tokyo. The crucial imbalance was therefore not an absence of written rules but the location of rule-making authority. For those governed, a carefully documented ordinance could be as coercive as a discretionary police order when no representative institution existed to challenge its policy. This institutional arrangement helps explain why measures regulating companies, schools, and property could be formally regular while profoundly unequal in political accountability.

Section 55.4

Provincial Administration and the Reorganization of Local Government

The colonial government kept the thirteen-province structure that the Korean state had established in 1896: Gyeonggi, North and South Chungcheong, North and South Jeolla, North and South Gyeongsang, Hwanghae, Gangwon, North and South Pyeongan, and North and South Hamgyeong. Each province had a governor and a provincial office organized into departments. A number of governors, especially in the early years, were Koreans, often men who had served the Korean government, but the practical weight of provincial government lay with the Japanese officials in the departments, especially the head of the province's internal affairs, and with the police chief. A Korean governor could be a figurehead presiding over Japanese subordinates, although individual cases differed and some governors had real influence.

Beneath the province lay the county (gun), headed by a county magistrate, and beneath the county the township (myeon), headed by a township chief. Cities were organized differently. Japanese settlements in the ports and in Seoul had long been governed by residents' associations under Japanese consuls and later under the Residency-General. In 1914, a major reform replaced these associations with municipalities (bu) in about a dozen cities, each headed by an official appointed by the government and advised by a council whose membership was weighted toward the Japanese population. Special arrangements applied to islands such as Jeju, which was placed under an island office within South Jeolla Province, and Ulleungdo, and to remote areas.

The reorganization of 1914 was the most important structural change to local government in the decade. The Government-General reduced the number of counties from roughly three hundred twenty to around two hundred twenty and consolidated townships from over four thousand three hundred to about two thousand five hundred. Officials justified the consolidation as a rationalization that would produce units large enough to support salaried staff, tax collection, and public works. It also reduced the number of local offices to be supervised and created administrative units that cut across older kinship, market, and community ties. The change displaced village communities from administrative boundaries they had known and made the township office, with its clerks, tax registers, and police liaison, a more significant presence in local life.

The difference between urban and rural administration shaped the colonial experience. In Japanese-populated cities, services such as roads, water, sanitation, and schools for Japanese children were funded and organized in ways that favored the settler community, and its representatives had influence in municipal councils. In predominantly Korean rural areas, the state appeared chiefly as the township office, the police station, the tax collector, and the survey party. This contrast between a relatively well-served settler urban world and an extractive, regulated rural world was among the structural inequalities of the colonial order.

Section 55.5

Japanese Officials, Korean Intermediaries, and the Politics of Colonial Administration

Colonial administration depended on personnel, and the composition of the civil service reveals the structure of power. Officials were classified by rank: senior appointees (chokunin) named by the emperor, mid-level appointees (sōnin) and lower-ranking officials (hannin). Japanese officials held nearly all senior posts, and the great majority of mid-level ones. Koreans were more numerous in the lowest ranks, as clerks, assistants, interpreters, and township officials, and were seldom promoted to positions of broad authority. Japanese officials serving in Korea received supplementary allowances on top of salaries, which increased the gap in compensation between Japanese and Korean officials of comparable rank.

The regime relied on Korean intermediaries at every level. Interpreters, police assistants, township chiefs, village headmen, tax-collection agents, and members of the Central Advisory Council helped to translate orders, identify residents, collect revenue, and keep order. Their reasons for service varied. Some had been officials under the Korean Empire and saw administrative service as continuity of a career, or as a means of protecting local interests. Some needed an income in a society where the abolition of the Korean state and army eliminated many careers. Some used the position for advantage, to accumulate land, influence, or protection. A smaller number supported colonial rule because they believed that Japanese leadership offered the only route to modern reform. Coercion also played a part, since refusal of an official summons could bring pressure on a family.

Historians therefore avoid collapsing intermediaries into a single category. The category "collaborator," which became politically charged after 1945, mixes the minister who signed the annexation treaty with the village clerk who recorded land declarations. This textbook examines the broader questions of collaboration, accommodation, and identity in Chapter 60. Here the important point is that colonial administration functioned through Korean participants, whose varied motives and circumstances shaped how rules were applied locally. A township chief might delay enforcement, warn neighbors of an inspection, or apply a rule strictly to enrich himself, and the same person might do all three at different times.

The politics of intermediaries also created internal Korean divisions. Landlords and local notables who cooperated gained favor and, often, enhanced social position, while tenants and poorer villagers experienced the same officials as agents of the state. The Central Advisory Council and the titled peerage offered prestige without power. The result was a layered system in which a small number of Japanese officials controlled policy, a larger group of Korean officials and notables implemented it, and the mass of the population experienced it as a series of obligations and prohibitions delivered through familiar neighbors and unfamiliar officers.

Section 55.6

The Gendarmerie-Police System and the Militarization of Colonial Government

The distinctive institution of the first colonial decade was the gendarmerie-police system (kenpei keisatsu seido), in which the Japanese military police (kenpeitai, here usually translated "gendarmerie") performed ordinary civil policing alongside civilian police. Japanese military police had been present in Korea since the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 and had been greatly expanded during the campaigns against the righteous armies in 1907–1909. Korean police affairs were transferred to Japanese control in June 1910, and after annexation the Government-General merged the structures. The commander of the gendarmerie, an army general, served at the same time as head of the Government-General's police affairs bureau, so that military command and civil police administration were joined in one person. The first holder of this combined post was General Akashi Motojirō.

The system distinguished functions that were separated in Japan. In the home islands, military police dealt mainly with discipline inside the army and had limited authority over civilians, while ordinary policing was in civilian hands under the Home Ministry. In Korea, gendarmes stationed in the countryside and in areas of past resistance were responsible for public order, criminal investigation, and political surveillance, while civilian police were concentrated in cities and ports. In practice the jurisdictions overlapped and the line shifted over time. In 1910, gendarmes and police were distinct but coordinated by the same chief, and in following years gendarmerie posts expanded in the countryside, while police stations spread to towns. Korean auxiliaries, recruited into the gendarmerie after 1908, and Korean policemen served at lower levels, usually under Japanese supervision.

Why did the regime choose this model? Official explanations stressed the need to complete the pacification of a country that had known recent insurrection, and to maintain order in a territory with few trained civilian police and difficult roads. Historians generally add that the army wanted to keep control of Korea's security, that the gendarmerie offered a ready-made organization with experience in the countryside, and that the system fitted a conception of Korea as a society requiring close supervision. The effect was to give the police an unusually extensive role in everyday life, as later sections show, and to give the colonial state an organization that could carry out surveillance and enforcement in villages that the civil bureaucracy could not yet reach.

The numbers of gendarmes and police rose through the decade, although the figures reported in sources vary with the categories counted and the dates. What is clear is that the density of police presence in Korea was higher than in the home islands and that the combined force was large relative to the government's other civil staff. The system should be understood both as an instrument of repression and as an organizational backbone. It created, on the ground, the network of posts through which a young colonial state could receive information, deliver orders, and compel compliance, which is why historians stress that the system was not an aberration of the army but the foundation of the regime.

Section 55.7

Police Stations, Rural Surveillance, and the Administration of Everyday Conduct

The police station was the point at which the colonial state met most Koreans. Stations and substations were scattered across towns and rural districts, each with a handful of officers who patrolled, inspected, and recorded. They investigated crimes, but they also issued permits for travel, trade, and public gatherings, supervised markets, checked household registers, enforced health and sanitation regulations, assisted with tax collection and land surveys, enforced forestry rules, and reported on local opinion. Informants, often recruited from within villages, supplemented direct observation. Searches of houses, inspections of schools and temples, and checks on strangers were routine methods of control.

Several features of this system worked together. Regular household investigations allowed police to compile lists of residents and to identify absent people. Passes and permits regulated movement across provincial and national borders, which was especially significant for northern provinces from which many Koreans migrated to Manchuria. Meetings for any purpose, including weddings, funerals, school ceremonies, and religious services, could come under police observation, and large gatherings required permission. The police also enforced a growing body of regulations on matters as intimate as burial, sanitation, and use of public paths. Because many rules were new, and because the police had discretion to interpret them, Koreans were often uncertain about which conduct was permitted.

The police functioned as agents of administration as much as instruments of political repression. Officers helped the township office collect taxes, supervised compulsory labor for road repair, enforced vaccination, checked on school attendance, and supported the work of cadastral survey teams. This dual role made them indispensable to the bureaucracy and intensified the sense of Koreans that the state was omnipresent. It also meant that the political repression of nationalists and the routine enforcement of fiscal and sanitary rules were carried out by the same officials, which made it hard to distinguish ordinary administration from political control. Reports from the period, including mission reports and memoirs, describe fear of the police as a general feature of village life, although the evidence is uneven and varies by region and by the personalities of local officers.

Resistance and avoidance were also present. Villagers hid unregistered land or unlicensed activities, warned neighbors of inspections, and appealed to sympathetic Korean officials. The police themselves were often Korean, and their behavior varied from harsh enforcement to quiet accommodation. Nevertheless the structural fact remained that a state with few other points of contact had created in the police station a mechanism by which its authority was projected into the countryside.

Section 55.8

Colonial Courts, Legal Codes, and the Unequal Administration of Justice

The judicial system was reorganized after annexation on a model already established during the protectorate. A court structure introduced in 1909 and expanded after annexation consisted of a high court in Seoul, three courts of appeal (at Seoul, Pyongyang, and Daegu), district courts, and branch courts, with prosecutors attached to each level. Japanese judges and prosecutors held the senior positions, and Koreans served mainly as junior judges, clerks, interpreters, and, later, as lawyers. The courts were formally independent of the police but operated within the Government-General, whose governor-general appointed judges and whose Justice Department supervised them. They were not a separate branch of government in the sense understood in the Japanese or American constitutional systems.

A set of ordinances issued in 1912 reorganized civil and criminal law. The colonial civil ordinance applied Japanese civil law in most matters, but excluded Koreans' family and inheritance relations, which were to be governed by "Korean custom," a category that the colonial authorities themselves defined and recorded (see Section 55.35). The criminal ordinance applied the Japanese penal code with modifications, replacing the older Korean penal practices, and criminal procedure ordinances governed investigation and trial. These reforms did introduce procedures resembling those of modern legal systems: codified rules, written judgments, lawyers, appeals. Some Koreans used the new courts to resolve property disputes and to challenge unfavorable decisions, and some historians emphasize that the arrival of a codified, procedural legal order was a significant change in Korean legal life.

But the legal system was unequal in design and practice. Japanese and Korean defendants did not stand before the law in identical conditions. Many Japanese settlers appeared in cases governed by Japanese law and handled by Japanese lawyers, while Koreans faced difficulties of language, cost, and unfamiliar procedure. Police and prosecutors had broad powers of detention and interrogation, as the 105-Person Incident (Section 55.36) illustrates. Summary adjudication rules, issued in 1910, empowered police chiefs to impose short sentences or fines for minor offenses without trial, which meant that ordinary people could be punished by the same officers who had arrested them. The Flogging Ordinance (Section 55.9) applied only to Koreans.

The result was a system with real legal modernization and real discrimination. A colonial court could issue a reasoned judgment and a police chief could impose a penalty without hearing, both under the same government. Historians of colonial law therefore resist both the view that colonial law was simply a facade for repression and the view that it was a neutral transfer of modern legal institutions. It brought new legal forms, and it did so within a structure that withheld political rights, concentrated authority, and treated different populations differently.

Section 55.9

The 1912 Flogging Ordinance, Summary Punishment, and Colonial Violence

The Chōsen Flogging Ordinance (Chōsen Taikei Rei) was promulgated on 18 March 1912 and took effect on 1 April. It allowed flogging, with a bamboo or wooden rod, as a substitute for short imprisonment or small fines in cases where the offender was a Korean man within specified age limits, commonly stated as between sixteen and sixty. Offenses included gambling, theft, assault, and similar petty crimes, and the maximum number of strokes was thirty. The implementing regulations specified the apparatus, the procedure, the presence of an official, and the medical examination of the offender. The punishment was applied by police and gendarmes under summary procedures, and it applied only to Koreans.

Officials defended the ordinance as adapted to Korean conditions. They argued that short prison terms were inadequate where prisons were few, that fines could not be collected from poor offenders, and that flogging was familiar from the older Korean penal tradition. Critics, including foreign missionaries and some Japanese commentators, noted that the Joseon state had in the late nineteenth century moved to restrict physical punishments, that the ordinance reintroduced corporal punishment in a new institutional setting, and that its racial application, limited to Koreans, contradicted claims of equal subjecthood. The Government-General maintained it until 1920, when it was abolished in the changed political circumstances following the March First Movement.

The ordinance has to be read with the rest of the coercive system. Its application depended on police discretion, which determined who was charged, how offenders were processed, and whether the case went to a court at all. Detainees faced conditions in police cells and prisons that were widely reported as harsh, and coercive interrogation, including beatings and other forms of torture, was documented in political trials. Records of how often flogging was applied, in what categories of cases, and with what physical consequences exist in official statistics and in trial and mission records, though their reliability is uneven. Memoirs and testimonies describe the humiliation and fear that the practice inspired, and nationalist writers later used it as a leading example of colonial violence.

Interpretation requires caution about scale and about motive. The ordinance did not end after a short period, did not apply to women or to Japanese, and was not the only means of punishment. Historians debate how far it reflected deliberate racial hierarchy and how far it reflected administrative convenience, but there is little dispute that it entrenched an unequal punishment regime and that it affected the daily experience of fear for Korean men in the countryside. It is best understood as one element in an interlocking system that also included summary adjudication, police discretion, and unequal access to courts.

Section 55.10

Newspaper Suppression, Publishing Controls, and the Restriction of Public Speech

Control of publishing rested on laws that the protectorate had already put in place: the Newspaper Law of 1907 and the Publication Law of 1909, which required licensing, allowed authorities to review material before publication, and gave the government power to confiscate issues and suspend or close publications. After annexation these laws were retained, and the Government-General applied them to Korean-language publishing with a severity that effectively ended the private Korean daily press. By the autumn of 1910, nearly all privately owned Korean newspapers had closed, been suppressed, or been absorbed, among them papers that had been prominent in the patriotic enlightenment movement of the preceding years.

The most important surviving Korean-language daily was the Maeil Sinbo, the successor of the Daehan Maeil Sinbo, which had been founded in 1904 under the editorship of the British journalist Ernest Bethell and had been an outspoken critic of Japanese policy. After Bethell's death in 1909 the paper passed to Korean and then to Japanese-controlled ownership, and following annexation it became the Government-General's Korean-language organ, with its content reflecting official positions. Japanese-language newspapers, such as the Keijō Nippō, and English-language publications aimed at foreign readers, served Japanese settlers and the foreign community. Korean readers therefore had access to a daily Korean-language press, but not to one independent of the government.

Not all publishing disappeared. Religious periodicals, particularly those of Christian denominations and of Cheondogyo, continued to appear under licensing restrictions, and specialist, academic, and literary publications, as well as books, circulated with varying degrees of difficulty. Korean publishers brought out classics and reprints, and the Society for Korean Classics (Joseon Gwangmunhoe), founded in 1910 by the writer Choe Nam-seon and others, worked on republishing historical and literary texts. Censorship focused above all on political news, patriotic history, and material seen as encouraging resistance, and it operated through prior review, confiscation of copies, and fines or imprisonment. Textbooks and books on Korean history and national heroes were among the materials removed from circulation.

The effects of these controls were political and cultural. Without a free Korean daily press, public debate on colonial policy was restricted, and information circulated through word of mouth, letters, religious networks, and publications produced abroad and smuggled in. The controls also encouraged forms of expression, such as literature, religious preaching, and educational activity, in which nationalist sentiment could be expressed indirectly. Historians therefore find evidence of public opinion in the 1910s less in newspapers than in religious sources, memoirs, police reports, and later recollections, all of which must be used with attention to their origins.

Section 55.11

Political Surveillance, Association Controls, and the Prevention of Organized Opposition

The regime sought not merely to punish opposition but to prevent organization. Political associations were dissolved at annexation, including the pro-Japanese Ilchinhoe as well as patriotic societies, and meetings and associations of a political character were prohibited or subject to approval. Regulations required notification and permission for assemblies, and police were entitled to attend, record, and dissolve them. Even the formation of societies for nonpolitical purposes, such as scholarly, religious, athletic, or philanthropic groups, required official approval, and approval could be withheld or withdrawn.

Preventive surveillance combined several tools. Police and gendarmes maintained lists of suspected nationalists, former righteous-army members, officials of the former government, and activists in the patriotic enlightenment movement. They monitored mail, travel, and associations, recruited informants, and used the household investigation system to track movements. When arrests occurred, investigation was conducted by police and prosecutors with extensive powers of detention, and cases passed to the courts, where judges typically accepted the prosecution's framework. The administrative restrictions, the investigative apparatus, and the judicial process thus operated as parts of a single system whose purpose was to limit the capacity of Koreans to coordinate.

The system had consequences that were visible in the structure of Korean opposition. Open political organization inside Korea became very difficult, which pushed nationalist activity into three forms: secret societies, such as the Sinminhoe and later groups; religious and educational networks, which enjoyed limited legal protection; and exile organizations in Manchuria, the Russian Far East, China, Japan, Hawaii, and the continental United States. Exile communities, often composed of former righteous-army fighters, intellectuals, and migrants, built schools, newspapers, and associations beyond the reach of the Government-General, though they too were watched by Japanese consular police. The case study of the Sinminhoe and the 105-Person Incident in Section 55.36 shows how these tools were used against a particular network.

The framework was not perfectly effective. Colonial police did not know everything, informants were of uneven reliability, and Koreans found ways to meet, travel, and communicate. Religious gatherings, school ceremonies, and family occasions provided cover for organization, and some officials turned a blind eye. But the system raised the costs and risks of political activity, ensured that most activity was clandestine, and meant that when a mass movement did emerge in 1919 it drew on networks that had been preserved in religious and educational institutions and abroad rather than on open political parties.

Section 55.12

From Gwangmu Land Reform to the Colonial Land Survey

Land measurement was not a Japanese novelty in Korea. The Joseon state had for centuries maintained land registers and conducted periodic surveys to assess taxes, although by the nineteenth century these records were widely regarded as outdated, unevenly maintained, and open to manipulation by local officials and landholders. In the late nineteenth century, officials of the Korean Empire, as part of the Gwangmu reforms, launched a survey (yangjeon) beginning in 1898 and issued certificates of ownership (jigye) to landholders in the districts surveyed. The survey covered a substantial portion of the country, including the Seoul region and many counties, but was interrupted by the Russo-Japanese War in 1904 and never completed. Its records and certificates remained partial and uneven.

Japanese officials in the protectorate era were attentive to this unfinished work and to the confusion of Korean land records. They believed that accurate registration of land and owners was the indispensable foundation of a modern tax system, of reliable credit, and of secure transactions, including those by Japanese buyers whose acquisitions after the treaty ports opened and especially after 1905 had often been disputed. Land was by far the main source of tax revenue, and the Government-General's ability to fund its administration without large permanent subsidies from Tokyo depended on obtaining a dependable fiscal base. Preparatory work began in the protectorate years, and a survey bureau was established in 1910 within the Korean government's finance administration under Japanese direction.

The colonial survey differed from the Gwangmu survey in aims, scale, and institutional basis. The earlier survey had been conducted by a Korean government that was trying to strengthen its own finances and to issue ownership documents under its own authority. The colonial survey was carried out by a foreign government with unified command over administration, courts, and police, applied modern technical methods such as triangulation and systematic mapping, and was designed to produce a uniform national record that would serve tax assessment and legal title. Historians continue to debate the relationship between the two efforts, with some emphasizing continuity of purpose and others emphasizing the transformation produced by the change in political authority.

Understanding the survey therefore requires holding together several things. It built on earlier Korean efforts and responded to real deficiencies in Joseon records. It served the interests of a colonial state seeking revenue and legal certainty. It aimed at, and in many respects achieved, a new standard of documentation. And it took place in a political setting in which Koreans had no voice in designing the rules, so that the legal categories imposed on a complex agrarian society were determined entirely by the officials who would use them.

Section 55.13

The Land Survey Program, 1910–1918: Organization, Measurement, and Records

A Temporary Land Survey Bureau, established in 1910, directed the program. Legal authority for the survey rested first on a land survey law enacted at the end of the protectorate and then on the Land Survey Ordinance issued by the Government-General in August 1912, which prescribed the procedures of declaration, investigation, adjudication of disputes, and registration. The bureau trained surveyors, employed Japanese engineers and large numbers of Korean assistants, and worked with provincial and local officials, police, and township and village leaders. Regional survey offices carried out the work in sequence across the peninsula, with fieldwork proceeding region by region and the project declared substantially complete in 1918.

The procedure involved several stages. First, a triangulation network established control points across the country. Second, owners were required to declare their land, parcel by parcel, to local authorities within specified periods, identifying the owner, the type of land, and the location. Third, survey teams measured each parcel, determined boundaries, and classified land by use, such as paddy, dry field, residential lot, forest, and other categories. Fourth, committees examined disputes over ownership and boundaries. Finally, the results were recorded in ownership registers and cadastral maps and made the basis of the land registration system. The project produced detailed cadastral maps of the surveyed land and registers covering 19,107,520 surveyed parcels (principally agricultural and residential plots, distinct from separately surveyed forestland), according to National Archives figures, at a reported project cost of approximately 20.4 million yen.

The survey was a massive organizational undertaking and generated detailed records that remain important sources for historians. It also required huge cooperation from rural society. Villagers had to understand and comply with the declaration requirements, guide surveyors, and attend investigations, while village leaders and township officials assisted with identification and notification. The police supported the process, and the declaration system placed responsibility on owners to claim their land within strict time limits. Those who failed to declare, or who declared incorrectly, faced legal consequences, while those who understood the system and had documents or influence were better placed.

The results transformed the documentary landscape. A uniform, precise, and centralized record of land replaced a patchwork of local and customary documentation. For the colonial state this was a major administrative achievement, making it possible to identify taxable land and owners across the territory. For Korean landholders it created formal title with state backing, but also subjected them to a legal framework in which what mattered was what was registered, not what had been customarily understood.

Section 55.14

Ownership Claims, Customary Rights, and the Legal Consequences of Registration

The central legal issue of the survey was the translation of an older and more layered system of land rights into a modern property regime. In many parts of Korea, multiple parties had recognized claims on the same land. The owner of the right to the land itself and the cultivator who held a long-standing right to farm it, sometimes inherited and transferable, could be different people, and in places the cultivator's claim, known by various local terms, was substantial. Palace, government, and institutional lands might be cultivated by families who had paid dues for generations. Communal lands were used by villages for grazing, fuel, and gathering without individual title. These arrangements rested on custom, local recognition, and sometimes documents, but not on a single register of owners.

The survey required each parcel to have one legally recognized owner. Where a single owner declared and no one disputed, the result was straightforward and gave that owner a title protected by the state. Where multiple claims existed, officials had to decide who was the owner, and in many cases the decision favored the person who held the documentary title or who declared first, and treated the cultivator's customary rights as tenancy or as no legal right at all. The cultivator, who might have regarded the right to farm as secure, could thus be reclassified as a tenant with weaker protection, or lose recognition entirely.

Registration therefore increased certainty for some and decreased security for others. Owners with clear documents and the knowledge to use the system gained more secure and more easily transferable property, which could be mortgaged and sold. Holders of customary rights, poorly documented claims, or rights that the new categories did not recognize found themselves weakened. Whether this was an intended effect, a consequence of the requirement of single ownership, or a mixture, is debated by historians. What the evidence does support is that the survey changed rights through law, that it did so according to the categories and procedures of the administering state, and that the consequences differed across regions, social groups, and types of land.

Disputes were not rare. Contested claims were heard by committees, and many cases involved boundaries, inheritance, former official lands, and temple or lineage property. Some owners succeeded in getting recognition after appeals, others lost claims. Resistance occasionally took the form of non-declaration, hiding of land, or petitioning, but the consequences of non-declaration could be severe. The result of the process was a formal register in which ownership appeared as a clear fact, although the clarity had been produced by a legal process that excluded some rights from consideration.

Section 55.15

State Lands, Former Royal Property, Forests, and Communal Resources

The survey also classified land that belonged to no private individual, and these decisions had major consequences. Land held by the Korean government, by the royal household, by palaces and offices, and by institutions such as post stations, military units, and schools had been administered through multiple channels and was often cultivated by tenants paying rent or dues. Imperial properties had been consolidated and enlarged during the last years of the Korean Empire. Under the colonial regime many of these properties were reclassified as state land (kokuyūchi), and some were transferred to the Yi royal household's account, some retained by the Government-General, and some sold or leased, including to the Oriental Development Company and to Japanese individuals and companies. Estimates of the share of land classified as state land vary and are debated, although it clearly constituted a substantial portion of the country.

Uncultivated land, forests, and wasteland were an important category. In the protectorate years the Forest Law of 1908 required owners to declare their forest holdings within a set period, and a Forest Ordinance followed in 1911, with a survey of forest and wilderness land later in the decade. Many villagers, whose use of forests was customary and communal, had no document of ownership and did not declare, so that substantial areas passed into state hands. Government forestry policy aimed at timber production, afforestation, and control of erosion, but it also limited access to wood, fodder, and gathering that rural households used for fuel and daily needs.

Village commons, including grazing grounds, tidal flats, and shared woodland, posed a conceptual challenge to a system of individual title. Where a community had used land collectively under local rules, the survey's insistence on an owner required either assigning the land to a village head, a lineage, a temple, or an institution, or classifying it as state land. The consequences depended on how the land was classified and who controlled the decision. Communities that were well organized, or that had influential local leaders, could sometimes secure title. Those that were not might lose access.

These changes were gradual and uneven, and historians emphasize that their effect cannot be inferred from the legal categories alone. Access to forest or common land might continue by tolerance, evasion, or negotiated arrangements, and enforcement varied. But the legal basis of access changed, and with it the leverage of villagers against officials, landlords, and companies. The reclassification of former royal and state lands also created a reservoir of property that the colonial government could use to support its own finances, lease to settlers and companies, and distribute as patronage, a function that historians identify as an important by-product of the survey.

Section 55.16

Land Taxes, Assessment, Revenue Collection, and Fiscal Capacity

The survey's fiscal purpose was explicit. Once land had been measured, classified, and attached to an owner, it could be assessed for tax on a regular basis. The Land Tax Ordinance of 1914 established a system in which tax was levied as a percentage of the assessed value of land, and rates and assessments were fixed on the basis of survey results. The rate was set at a little over one percent of the assessed value, with distinctions among types of land. This replaced a patchwork of older assessments, which had varied by region, been paid in kind or cash, and been subject to local manipulation, with a uniform, cash-based system administered by the state through local revenue offices.

The effect on revenue was substantial. Land tax receipts rose over the decade as surveys were completed, tax-evading land was brought onto the rolls, and collection became more regular. This increase was a significant part of how the Government-General reduced its reliance on subsidies from Japan, and official reports presented it as evidence of administrative success. From the administrative viewpoint the improvement lay in regularity and predictability: the state knew what land existed, who owned it, and what it was worth, and could compel payment through its police and local offices.

Regularity, however, was not the same as fairness. A uniform system could impose a heavier burden on some than the old one had. Land previously unreported became taxable, and owners who had benefited from irregular assessment saw their obligations rise. Taxes had to be paid in cash, which exposed farmers to market prices and to credit, and which required converting harvests into money at times when prices might be low. The legal liability for the tax fell on the registered owner, who often passed the cost onto tenants through rents, so that the impact depended on the relationship between landlord and cultivator. Local officials who collected the tax, and the police who enforced it, were part of the system through which burdens were applied.

The fiscal system must be distinguished from the broader question of whether Koreans benefited from the state's greater revenue. The colonial government used its revenue to fund an administration, a police system, and infrastructure that served its priorities. Whether this constituted a form of public investment from which Koreans broadly benefited, or an extraction to support a colonial apparatus, is one of the central questions of evaluation discussed in Section 55.37. The administrative fact established in this section is that the survey and tax system gave the colonial state a financial foundation that its predecessors had lacked.

Section 55.17

Landlords, Tenants, Farmers, and the Social Consequences of Colonial Property Reform

The social consequences of the survey cannot be summarized as the confiscation of Korean land. Historians who have studied the registers and the process caution against the claim, sometimes found in popular and older nationalist accounts, that the survey seized most Korean land. Most land was registered to the Korean owners who declared it. The state did take over a considerable amount of land as state property, and Japanese individuals, companies, and the Oriental Development Company acquired land through purchase, foreclosure, and leasing, so that Japanese ownership grew, though it remained a minority of total land and was concentrated in fertile areas, especially in the southwest. Nevertheless the survey did not by itself dispossess the rural population in a single blow.

Its social effects were mediated by the structure of rural society. Where landlords held large estates and tenants cultivated them, the registration of the landlord as sole owner strengthened his legal position and weakened any residual claims of cultivators. The ability to sell, mortgage, and reallocate land made it easier for landlords to adjust tenancy terms, and for landowners to convert to cash-based relations. Contemporary and later statistics show that by the late 1910s a large share of rural households were wholly or partly tenants: official data from 1918, with the caveats appropriate to such sources, show roughly four in ten households cultivating as owner-tenants, a similar share as full tenants, and only about a fifth as full owners. These patterns reflected long-term processes of inequality that predated colonial rule, but the survey, by clarifying and securing title, made them legally firmer.

Regional variation was large. In the fertile southwestern provinces, tenancy and large holdings were more common and Japanese acquisition of land was concentrated. In the mountainous northeast and in parts of the north, small owner-cultivation and slash-and-burn farming were more significant, and forest and common land mattered more. Households that depended on customary rights, wage labor, or forest products experienced the changes differently from owner-cultivators. Some farmers prospered by taking advantage of commercial opportunities, while others lost land through debts, taxes, and failure to document claims. Many migrated to cities, to Manchuria, or to the Russian Far East.

The survey was therefore neither a politically neutral technical exercise nor a simple act of plunder. It was a legal and administrative reform carried out by a colonial state for reasons of governance, fiscal capacity, and property security, whose effects depended on prior rural structures and on who could navigate the new system. It strengthened registered owners, reduced the claims of cultivators and communities, and gave the colonial state both revenue and a mass of land to dispose of. These consequences contributed to the agrarian tensions of the following decades, which Chapter 58 examines.

Historical Vocabulary
Cadastre, Customary Rights, Title Registration, Tenancy, and Land Taxation

Definition. A cadastre is an official register and map of land parcels, owners, and values, used for taxation and legal title. Customary cultivation rights were claims to farm particular land based on long-standing local practice, which could be inherited or sold but were not necessarily recorded in a state register. Title registration is the legal recording of a single owner for each parcel. Tenancy is the cultivation of land owned by another in return for rent. Land taxation is the levying of tax on land by area, assessed value, or yield.

Historical context. The colonial survey converted a system of overlapping rights into one of single ownership and cash taxation, and the meaning of each term changed in the process: a cultivator who had held a customary right might become, in law, a tenant.

Why it matters. The terms show that a technical process (measurement) also was a legal transformation (rights) and a fiscal one (taxation).

Potential misunderstanding. "Registration" does not mean "confiscation." Most registered land was assigned to Korean owners, though the process affected rights unequally.

Historical Debate

Did the Colonial Land Survey Primarily Modernize Land Administration or Facilitate Dispossession?

The question: Historians disagree about the survey's character and consequences. One central dispute is whether it should be understood mainly as a modernizing reform that established secure property rights and an efficient tax system, or as a mechanism by which the colonial state and Japanese interests acquired land and weakened Korean cultivators.

Interpretation A — The survey facilitated dispossession: Many Korean nationalist and later historians argued that the survey enabled the colonial state to seize large amounts of land, particularly by classifying land as state property when owners failed to declare or lacked documents, and that it weakened the customary rights of cultivators. They emphasize that the process was designed and administered by the colonial government and that Japanese acquisition of land followed.
Interpretation B — The survey largely formalized existing ownership: Other historians, using the registers and statistics, argue that most land was registered to the Koreans who claimed it, that the share taken as state property was limited and largely consisted of former royal and government lands, and that the legal formalization of private property resembled developments in other states. They stress that the survey's principal effect was to secure and standardize title.
Interpretation C — Both processes occurred, unevenly: Many scholars hold that formalization and dispossession were not exclusive. The survey could secure the title of documented owners while weakening cultivators and communities, and its effects varied by region, land type, and social position.

Evidence: Historians draw on cadastral maps and registers, ordinances, official reports, local records of disputes, landholding statistics, memoirs, and later oral testimony.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the scale of land transferred to state or Japanese ownership, the interpretation of categories such as state land, the treatment of customary rights, and the significance of the survey in the later growth of tenancy and rural inequality. Part of the disagreement is also a matter of definition: what counts as "dispossession" when legal rights are redefined.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand colonial legitimacy, the relation between legal reform and political power, and the origins of the agrarian structure examined in Chapter 58.

Division IV — Colonial Finance, Enterprise, and Economic Restructuring (Sections 55.18–55.22). The fiscal needs of colonial government and the economic welfare of Koreans were related but distinct questions.
Section 55.18

Colonial Budgets, Revenue Sources, Monopolies, and Government Expenditure

The Government-General's budget reveals what the regime valued. It was organized as a special account for Korea, with expenditure and revenue estimates reviewed in Tokyo, and with financial supplements from the Japanese treasury during the first years. The main sources of revenue were the land tax, taxes on liquor, tobacco, and other goods, customs duties, fees, receipts from state enterprises such as railways and communications, income from government monopolies, and earnings from state-owned land. The subsidy from Japan, large at first, declined as domestic revenue rose and ended toward the end of the decade, a result that officials cited as proof of administrative success.

Monopolies and state-controlled commodities contributed to revenue. The government maintained a monopoly on red ginseng, inherited from the protectorate period, regulated the production and sale of salt, and exercised strong control over liquor brewing through licensing and taxation. Customs policy was shaped by the annexation arrangements: the existing Korean tariff was maintained for a transitional period of about ten years, with the result that trade with Japan and with other countries was governed by different conditions and that revenue from customs was modest compared with the land tax. In the longer run, tariff arrangements were to be assimilated to Japan's, a process that belongs to the following decade.

Expenditure was weighted toward the instruments of rule and the infrastructure that served the colonial economy. Large shares went to administration, police and gendarmerie, the military establishment, railways, roads, ports, and communication. Education, public health, and social welfare received far smaller shares. The official reasoning held that security and communication were the preconditions of development, and that schools and hospitals would follow. Critics, then and now, observe that the balance reflected the regime's priorities and that Koreans, who paid taxes but had no voice in the budget, received less in services than the revenue they provided might have supported.

It is important to distinguish the fiscal needs of the government from the economic welfare of the population. A government can be fiscally healthy, with balanced budgets and rising revenue, while its population experiences stagnant incomes, rising inequality, or heavy burdens. Conversely, revenue can support investments that raise productivity. The budgets of the 1910s allow historians to see what the state chose to fund, but they do not by themselves show how households fared, a question that requires other kinds of evidence.

Section 55.19

Currency, Banks, Credit, and the Reorganization of Financial Institutions

The monetary and banking framework had been reshaped under Japanese influence before annexation. A currency reform in 1905, associated with the Japanese financial adviser Megata Tanetarō, had put Korea on a standard linked to Japan's, and a central bank, the Bank of Korea, was established in 1909. After annexation, it was reorganized in 1911 as the Bank of Chōsen, which became the colonial central bank, issuing notes denominated in yen, serving as the government's banker, and later extending its operations across the Japanese continental empire. The unification of the currency with Japan's simplified trade and capital movement between Korea and Japan and tied Korea's monetary system to Japanese policy.

The Oriental Development Company (Tōyō Takushoku Kaisha), founded in 1908 as a semi-official company under Japanese law, played a distinct role. It acquired and managed agricultural land, promoted Japanese agricultural settlement, and made loans, and over the following years became one of the largest landowners in Korea, building its holdings through purchases, state-transferred land, and foreclosures. It was intended in part to encourage Japanese emigration to Korea, a goal it achieved only modestly. Its operations, together with those of other Japanese landowners, made it a symbol of Japanese economic penetration in the countryside.

Agricultural and commercial credit developed through several channels. Agricultural and industrial banks, established in the protectorate years, were consolidated in 1918 into the Chōsen Industrial Bank. Financial associations (kinyū kumiai) provided small loans to farmers, often under local leadership. Japanese-owned commercial banks operated in the ports and cities, and a small number of Korean-owned private banks continued to function under government supervision. Access to capital differed greatly. Japanese borrowers, with ties to Japanese banks and with collateral recognized in the new registration system, could generally borrow on better terms than Korean borrowers, many of whom relied on informal moneylenders at high interest. After the survey, registered land could be mortgaged, which expanded credit but also made it easier for indebted owners to lose land through foreclosure.

The effect was to integrate Korea into a financial system whose center was in Japan. This integration brought money, standardization, and new financial institutions, and it made credit more available for some purposes. It also made Korea's economy more dependent on Japanese capital and policy, and it channeled the benefits of financial modernization disproportionately toward Japanese and a minority of wealthy Koreans, while leaving many ordinary farmers and small traders with limited and costly access to credit.

Section 55.20

The Company Ordinance of 1910 and the Regulation of Business Enterprise

The Company Ordinance (Kaisha Rei) was promulgated on 29 December 1910 and took effect on 1 January 1911. It required that companies be established only with the permission of the governor-general, authorized the government to impose conditions on those companies, and gave the governor-general power to order the dissolution of companies, including when their activities were judged contrary to public order or the public interest. It applied to companies in Korea, including Japanese companies seeking to operate there as well as Korean ones, although the ordinance's effect depended on who sought permission and on how officials exercised discretion. It remained in force until 1920.

Interpretations of its purpose and effect differ. Some historians have argued that it was designed to restrain the formation of Korean-owned enterprises, giving the government a means to block or delay Korean businesses and to protect Japanese interests, and they point to the broad discretion it conferred and to the small number of large Korean-owned companies in the 1910s. Other historians emphasize that the ordinance applied a general system of permission that was similar to rules in other contexts, that it was aimed in part at bringing order to a field where speculative and fraudulent ventures had proliferated, and that Korean companies continued to be established, including in trade, rice milling, brewing, brickmaking, banking, and manufacturing. A third view stresses the discretionary character of the system, in which the outcome depended on administrative attitude as much as text.

The available evidence supports caution. Statistical series show company formation continuing through the decade, with a large Japanese share of capital and Korean participation concentrated in smaller enterprises. Korean entrepreneurs also formed partnerships and unincorporated businesses that were not subject to the same permission requirement, which complicates arguments from the number of incorporated companies alone. Memoirs and business histories document both obstruction and successful ventures. The ordinance did not end Korean business, but it gave officials a powerful tool and shaped the conditions in which enterprise grew.

The ordinance's importance lies in what it shows about the colonial economic order. The state did not leave enterprise to markets alone. It used permission, licensing, and discretion to supervise the entry of businesses, and it did so in a context where the officials making decisions were Japanese and the principal capital investors were increasingly Japanese. Its repeal in 1920, as part of a policy shift after 1919, belongs to later chapters.

Section 55.21

Agriculture, Commercial Production, Rice Markets, and Colonial Economic Priorities

Agriculture was the economic base of colonial Korea, and policy focused on increasing and commercializing production. The Government-General expanded agricultural experiment stations, which had begun in the protectorate era, introduced improved rice varieties and cotton, promoted fertilizer and seed selection, and encouraged irrigation, including through irrigation associations organized under regulations of 1917. Agricultural advisers and extension officials worked with township and village leaders to spread new methods, often with police support. Cotton cultivation, silk production, and tobacco also expanded, and the government sought to adapt farming to the needs of Japanese industry.

Rice was the central commodity. Japan, with a growing urban population and a limited ability to expand domestic production, increasingly turned to Korean rice. Improved transport, the opening of ports, and the registration of land made it easier to buy and ship rice, and exports to Japan rose through the decade. The Japanese rice riots of 1918, provoked by high prices, intensified official interest in Korean rice as a solution to Japan's food supply, which led to the large-scale Rice Production Increase Plan of 1920, treated in Chapter 58. The 1910s thus laid foundations for that later program without yet involving its full scale and methods.

The effects on Korean farmers were mixed. Higher yields and market access could raise income for owner-cultivators and landlords who sold rice. Landlords, who received rent in kind and could sell it, often benefited most. Tenants paying fixed rents might gain from increased output or lose if rents rose with productivity. Cash taxes, fertilizer costs, irrigation fees, and debt made farmers more dependent on markets and credit, and exposed them to price swings. Regional differences were considerable: commercial rice cultivation was more developed in the south and southwest, while the north relied more on dry-field crops and other activities. Many Korean farmers continued to consume more barley, millet, and other grains than rice, as the region's best rice went to market.

Distinguishing the period before 1920 from the later one is essential. The commercialization and integration of Korean agriculture into the Japanese economy were real by 1919, but the systematic production campaigns, large-scale irrigation projects, and the sharp growth of rice exports belong mainly to the 1920s. The 1910s should be understood as the preparation of the legal, financial, and administrative groundwork on which later policies built.

Section 55.22

Industrial Enterprise, Trade, Employment, and Unequal Economic Opportunity

Industrial development in the 1910s was modest in scale compared with later decades. Government policy treated Korea primarily as a supplier of food and raw materials and a market for Japanese goods, and most manufacturing consisted of food processing, rice milling, brewing, textiles in small workshops, brickmaking, printing, and similar light industries. Mining expanded, encouraged by the Mining Ordinance of 1915, and included gold, iron, and coal, with Japanese and foreign companies prominent. An American-owned concession at Unsan, in the northwest, continued to operate in these years and illustrated the continued presence of foreign investment alongside Japanese. Japanese capital, through large corporations and small entrepreneurs, dominated large-scale enterprise.

Korean enterprise nonetheless existed and in some sectors grew. Korean merchants continued to operate in domestic trade, in the markets of Seoul and other cities, and in the rice and grain trade. Landlord families invested in rice mills, brewing, and small factories, and some founded banks and companies. Korean artisans and small manufacturers continued in traditional crafts and adapted to new products. The end of the decade saw the founding of new Korean-led ventures that grew in later years, but the range and scale of Korean business were constrained by limited capital, regulatory discretion, competition from Japanese firms with better access to credit, and the preferential position of Japanese in government contracts and licensing.

Trade patterns tied Korea more closely to Japan. A large majority of Korea's exports, principally rice, beans, and raw materials, went to Japan, and a large majority of imports, including cotton cloth and manufactured goods, came from Japan. The First World War increased demand for Korean exports and brought a period of commercial expansion, but gains were unevenly distributed, and wartime inflation raised prices of necessities.

Employment shifted in limited ways. New occupations appeared in railways, ports, mining, factories, offices, schools, and police, and wage labor expanded. Wages for Koreans were typically lower than for Japanese doing similar work, and skilled and supervisory positions were more often held by Japanese. Women worked in agriculture, in small workshops, and increasingly in some factories. These developments gave some Koreans access to new forms of income, but under conditions of unequal opportunity. Chapter 58 returns to labor, industrialization, and migration over the longer term.

Division V — Education, Religion, Language, and Cultural Regulation (Sections 55.23–55.27). Education and religion were not peripheral to colonial government. They were major arenas in which the state sought to shape political loyalty, cultural identity, and the future leadership of Korean society.
Section 55.23

The First Chōsen Education Ordinance of 1911 and Colonial Educational Objectives

The Chōsen Education Ordinance (Chōsen Kyōiku Rei), promulgated on 23 August 1911, set out the objectives and structure of schooling for Korean children. Its stated aim was to educate Koreans as loyal subjects of the Japanese empire according to the principles of the Imperial Rescript on Education of 1890, to give them knowledge and skills suited to their circumstances, and to emphasize moral instruction and the Japanese language. Officials described the policy as "adapted to the conditions" of Korea, which they characterized as less developed than Japan, and they favored practical and vocational education over advanced academic training for Koreans.

The ordinance divided education into three branches: ordinary education, vocational education, and specialized education. Ordinary education consisted of common schools (futsū gakkō) of four years for children, higher common schools for boys of four years, and higher common schools for girls of three years. Vocational schools provided training in agriculture, commerce, and technical occupations, and specialized schools offered higher professional training, later including medicine, law, and technical subjects. Japanese children in Korea attended a parallel system modeled on Japan's, with longer courses, so that the school system was segregated by ethnicity, though not entirely by law, because some Koreans attended Japanese-system schools and vice versa in limited numbers.

Education's ideological purpose has to be distinguished from its actual provision. The state's goals were to build loyalty and to prepare a population useful to the colonial order, but the schools that existed were few, and many Korean families did not send children to them, either because they could not afford fees and the loss of child labor, because the nearest school was far away, or because they distrusted a system run by the colonial state and taught in Japanese. Many continued to rely on village schools (seodang), where children learned Chinese characters and Confucian texts. The official school system therefore reached only a minority of Korean children, while the state's expectations of what schools should do were much more extensive than its capacity to deliver.

Section 55.24

Primary Schools, Secondary Schools, Gender, and Educational Inequality

The common school curriculum emphasized moral instruction (shūshin), Japanese language, arithmetic, singing, physical exercise, and practical subjects, with Korean language and Chinese characters taught as a subject alongside Japanese. Japanese was allotted a large part of weekly hours, and the classroom language of instruction was Japanese or a mixture, depending on teachers. History and geography were not offered as separate subjects in the common school course, but were folded into language readers and moral instruction. Textbooks were compiled or approved by the Government-General, and teachers were drawn from Japan and from Koreans trained in normal courses. Some Japanese teachers, including primary teachers, were reported to wear uniforms and swords, a practice that symbolized the militarized character of government and was recalled in many Korean memoirs.

Enrollment grew through the decade but remained low relative to the school-age population. Official statistics show Korean enrollment in common schools rising from a small base to roughly ninety thousand or so by the end of the decade, which was well below one in ten Korean children, while Japanese children in Korea attended school at near-universal rates. Fees, distance, the cost of books and uniforms, and the need for children's labor in farm households all limited attendance. Common schools in towns were better attended than in the countryside, and schools in the south were more numerous than in remote northern districts.

Gender sharpened the inequality. Girls' enrollment was much lower than boys', in keeping with prevailing family attitudes and with the limited provision of girls' schools. A small number of girls attended girls' higher common schools, mission schools, and a few private institutions, where they could receive education that prepared them to be teachers, nurses, and, increasingly, a new category of educated women. The expansion of girls' education was a significant development in the long run, but in the 1910s it affected only a small minority.

Beyond the common school, opportunities narrowed sharply. Higher common schools for Koreans offered four years of secondary-level instruction, while Japanese students had access to a five-year middle school curriculum that led more directly to higher education. Specialized schools for medicine, law, and technical subjects were established from 1915 onward and enrolled a small number of Koreans, but there was no university in Korea in the 1910s, and Koreans seeking higher education often went to Japan, the United States, or China. School expansion therefore coexisted with a design that limited Koreans' access to advanced education and to the professional and administrative careers that depended on it.

Section 55.25

Private Schools, Missionary Institutions, and the Struggle over Educational Autonomy

Before annexation, a large number of private schools, founded by Korean reformers, local communities, and Christian missions, had been established, many as part of the patriotic enlightenment movement that sought to strengthen Korea through modern education. Regulations introduced by the Residency-General in 1908 had begun to control them, and in October 1911 new private-school regulations required that they conform to the standards of the education ordinance, obtain permission, use approved textbooks, and employ qualified teachers. Schools that did not meet these requirements were closed or required to restructure. The number of private schools fell sharply, from more than two thousand around 1910 to under a thousand by the end of the decade, as some were closed, some merged, and some converted to public status.

In March 1915 the government issued revised private-school regulations that were stricter. They required that instruction in all private schools use Japanese in a larger share of the curriculum, that teachers hold qualifications recognized by the government, and that schools offering a general curriculum exclude religious instruction and religious ceremonies. Existing schools were given a period, often cited as ten years, to meet the new requirements. The rule affected missionary schools directly, since religious instruction and worship were central to their mission, and it triggered protracted negotiations between missionaries and the Government-General.

Missionary institutions were an important part of the educational landscape. Protestant and Catholic schools, especially in Seoul, Pyongyang, and the northwestern provinces, educated many Koreans in modern subjects, and several grew into higher-level institutions: Ewha for girls in Seoul, Pai Chai for boys, Soongsil in Pyongyang, and Chosen Christian College, founded in 1915 in Seoul and recognized as a specialized school in 1917, among others. Foreign missionaries had some protection from their governments, and the Government-General was cautious about confrontation with the United States and Britain, but it also viewed mission schools as places where Korean nationalism and independent organization could develop. Some missions decided to comply with the new regulations, others considered closing schools or withdrawing from the registered system.

Korean educators and reformers who had led private schools, such as those who had founded schools in Pyongyang and in other provinces, were often among the nationalists the police watched most closely. Some schools were closed because of suspected political activity, and teachers were arrested or left for exile. At the same time, many Korean communities continued to support private schools, and village schools (seodang) increased in number in official counts, suggesting a family preference for education outside the state system. The struggle over educational autonomy shows that schooling was a field where colonial control, Korean aspiration, and foreign missionary interests intersected.

Section 55.26

Buddhism, Christianity, Cheondogyo, and Colonial Religious Supervision

The Temple Ordinance (Sachal Rei), promulgated on 3 June 1911, placed the Buddhist order under government supervision. It designated thirty head temples (bonsan) with authority over subordinate temples, required that temple property be registered and that its disposal be approved, and made the appointment of head abbots subject to the governor-general's approval. For temples, this meant that a state that had earlier restricted Buddhism under Joseon now supervised and, in some respects, protected it, while also controlling its leadership and property. Japanese Buddhist sects, which had established missions in Korea, sought to influence Korean Buddhism, and some Korean monks welcomed links with Japanese Buddhism while others resisted. The debate over clerical marriage, which Japanese sects permitted, divided the Korean order.

Christianity occupied a different position. The Protestant churches, especially Presbyterian and Methodist, were a minority of the population but were concentrated in the northwest and Seoul and had an influence disproportionate to their size through schools, hospitals, publications, and Korean-led congregations. Missionaries tended to avoid direct political involvement, and the Government-General treated the churches with caution because of their foreign ties, but it also suspected Korean Christian leaders of nationalist sympathies, a suspicion strengthened by the 105-Person Incident. In 1915 regulations on the propagation of religion classified Shinto, Buddhism, and Christianity as recognized religions and required that places of worship and clergy be registered with the authorities. Catholic missions also continued, with their own schools and institutions.

Cheondogyo, the successor to the Donghak movement (Chapter 46), had been renamed in 1905 under its third leader, Son Byeong-hui. It had a large membership, with estimates varying widely, and an organized structure of local congregations that made it one of the best-organized Korean institutions of the period. The colonial authorities regarded it with suspicion because of the memory of the Donghak peasant uprising, but it was allowed to operate under supervision. It raised funds for a central church in Seoul, published periodicals, and sponsored educational activity. Other movements emerged as well: the Dangun-centered Daejonggyo, founded in 1909, moved its headquarters to Manchuria in 1914, and Won Buddhism's founder had his enlightenment in 1916, though the organization took institutional form later.

The religious organizations should not be treated as a single unified resistance movement. Their relations to colonial authorities differed, their leaders held different political views, and within each organization there were people who cooperated, people who kept apart from politics, and people who joined nationalist networks. What the colonial regime did was to create a framework of registration and supervision that gave religious institutions limited legal protection and subjected them to surveillance. That framework made religious organizations one of the few forms of organized life that survived under military-police rule, and it helps explain why religious leaders were prominent in 1919.

Section 55.27

Korean Language, History, Textbooks, and the Regulation of Cultural Knowledge

Control of cultural knowledge worked through textbooks, curricula, publishing permissions, and the state's own research. Korean-language instruction continued in the common school as a subject alongside Chinese characters, though with far fewer hours than Japanese, and Korean language was not suppressed in the manner of the policies of the late 1930s and early 1940s examined in Chapter 60. The Korean language remained the language of daily life, of religious services, of much of the surviving press, and of family and community. What changed was its status relative to Japanese, which became the language of government, higher education, and advancement.

Korean history was treated with particular care. Patriotic histories and biographies of national heroes, produced in the enlightenment movement, were removed from circulation under the publishing laws, and the school curriculum did not teach Korean history as a distinct subject. Official textbooks presented Korea in the context of Japan. At the same time, the colonial government built its own apparatus for studying Korea. Officials and scholars conducted surveys of customs, folklore, anthropology, and antiquities, produced regulations in 1916 for the preservation of historic remains and relics, and began archaeological excavations of ancient sites, including tombs of the Han-era commanderies in the Pyongyang region. The Chūsūin (Central Advisory Council) took over the investigation of customary institutions in May 1915, amid a separate history-compilation effort initiated in 1915. These projects preceded, but should not be confused with, the formal history-compilation bodies established in 1922 and 1925.

This effort was both scholarly and political. The research produced valuable data, lasting archives, and in some cases high-quality excavation and documentation. It also embodied assumptions about Korean history, including portrayals of Korea as dependent on outside powers and stagnant, that served the justification of colonial rule. Korean scholars and amateurs responded by preserving and republishing historical texts, as the Society for Korean Classics did, and by writing alternative histories, some in exile. The contest over history thus began early.

The early colonial approach should be distinguished from the more intensive assimilation policies of the later period. In the 1910s, the regime emphasized loyalty, order, and economic utility, and the state demanded obedience more than cultural conversion. The campaigns to change Korean names, to require shrine worship, and to suppress the Korean language were products of the 1930s and 1940s, as Chapters 60 and 62 explain. The 1910s' regulation of cultural knowledge laid institutional and conceptual groundwork for later policies without being identical to them.

Colonial citizenship refers here to the status of Koreans as imperial subjects without equivalent political rights. Discrimination means legal or administrative differences in treatment by ethnicity. Censorship is the official control of publications and speech through licensing, prior review, and confiscation.

Historical context. Japanese officials in the 1910s spoke of assimilation as a long-term goal but practiced distinct and unequal institutions. The distinction between rhetoric and practice is one of the key analytical problems of the period.

Why it matters. The terms help explain how the regime claimed to incorporate Koreans while denying them equal rights, a contradiction that fueled grievances.

Potential misunderstanding. "Assimilation" in the 1910s did not mean the wartime campaigns of the late 1930s and 1940s, which were far more intensive and are covered in later chapters.

Section 55.28

Railways, Roads, Telegraphs, and the Geography of Colonial Control

The main trunk lines of Korea's railways were built before annexation: the Gyeongbu line between Seoul and Busan, completed in 1905, and the Gyeongui line between Seoul and Sinuiju, opened in 1906, both constructed with Japanese capital and military priorities during and after the Russo-Japanese War. After annexation, a Railway Bureau of the Government-General took over management, and construction continued. The Yalu River bridge between Sinuiju and Andong (Dandong) in Manchuria, completed in 1911, connected the Korean network to the South Manchuria Railway. The Honam line to Mokpo and the Gyeongwon line to Wonsan opened in 1914, linking the southwestern rice plains and the northeastern port with Seoul. In 1917 the management of Korean railways was entrusted to the South Manchuria Railway Company, an arrangement that lasted until 1925 and integrated them further into Japan's continental transport system.

Roads were less developed, and the Government-General began a program of classification and construction around 1911, building and improving main roads connecting county seats, ports, and rail stations. Much of the labor, particularly on local roads, was drawn from villagers through compulsory service, often organized by township officials and police, an obligation that burdened rural households at times when they were needed in the fields. Telegraph and telephone lines, which Japan had taken over in the protectorate period, expanded along railways and roads, and the postal system extended into the countryside.

These networks had several functions at once. They moved goods, especially rice and other agricultural exports, from interior to ports and onward to Japan. They moved people, including Japanese officials, settlers, and merchants, and, increasingly, Koreans. They enabled the rapid movement of troops and police, the transmission of orders, and the collection of intelligence, which gave the colonial government a capacity for control that the Joseon state had never had. The same railway that carried a farmer's rice to port could carry gendarmes to a disturbed district.

The geography of the network reflected colonial priorities. Lines ran from the interior to ports and toward the Manchurian border, linking Korea to Japan and to the Japanese position in Manchuria, and were not designed first to connect regional Korean markets with each other. Fares and freight rates, ownership, and employment favored Japanese interests, and Koreans were hired in large numbers as laborers but rarely in senior positions. Nonetheless Koreans used the railways and roads, and the new mobility they offered changed the experience of space, contributing to the circulation of people and ideas that fed Korean social life and, eventually, political mobilization.

Section 55.29

Ports, Commercial Corridors, and the Integration of Korea into Japan's Economy

Korea's ports, opened to Japanese trade from 1876, became the gateways of colonial commerce. Busan, linked by ferry to Shimonoseki, was the principal passenger and freight link between Korea and Japan and had the largest Japanese population among port cities. Incheon, the port of Seoul, handled rice, imports, and passengers, and in 1911 began construction of a tidal lock dock that was completed in 1918. Gunsan and Mokpo in the southwest served as rice-export ports, Wonsan in the northeast as an outlet for the northern region, Jinnampo and Sinuiju in the northwest, and Cheongjin in the northeast, whose later development as a major port belongs to the 1920s and 1930s.

The ports hosted Japanese shipping lines, trading firms, banks, customs offices, warehouses, and railway terminals. Japanese merchants dominated much of the import and export trade, and Japanese-controlled companies handled shipping and insurance. Customs administration was run by Japanese officials. Koreans took part as dock laborers, small merchants, brokers, and some as established trading houses, but the structure of capital and credit favored Japanese. Regional trading networks that had flowed through local markets and itinerant merchants continued but were increasingly drawn into a system oriented toward the ports.

Trade statistics show strong growth in volume through the decade, boosted by the First World War, with Japan taking the overwhelming share of exports and supplying most imports. The commodity composition shows a pattern in which Korea exported rice, soybeans, and raw materials and imported manufactured goods, notably cotton textiles. Korea was thereby integrated into an imperial division of labor in which Japan processed and sold manufactures while Korea supplied food and materials, though this division was not absolute and later industrial development altered it.

Increased trade did not mean equal control over its benefits. Profits flowed disproportionately to Japanese firms and to Korean landlords who sold rice, while rural cultivators and urban workers saw more modest gains and in some cases, as in wartime inflation, losses. Port cities also became zones of cultural and social contact, with Japanese quarters, Korean quarters, and mixed commercial streets, and they were the places where the colonial order was most visibly built into the urban landscape.

Section 55.30

Urban Administration, Roads, Sanitation, and the Reorganization of Seoul

On annexation the old capital Hanseong was renamed Gyeongseong (in Japanese, Keijō) and placed under the administration of Gyeonggi Province, with an urban government (fu, later in 1914 formally a municipality) led by an appointed official and advised by a council. Seoul was both the colonial capital and a city with a large Korean population. Its Japanese residents, who numbered in the tens of thousands, formed a significant minority, concentrated in the southern district around Namsan and the commercial street the Japanese called Honmachi (now Chungmu-ro), while Korean residents lived in the northern districts around the palaces and in the commercial center along Jongno.

Urban improvements included the widening and straightening of roads, drainage and sewer works, and the extension of water supply and electric services, some of which were begun in the Korean Empire and the protectorate. An urban improvement plan from 1912 and following years prioritized main thoroughfares linking the government offices, stations, and the southern commercial district. The Bank of Chōsen's new headquarters, completed in 1912, and other substantial buildings in a European-influenced style, signaled the new institutions. Parts of the old city wall, gates, and other historic structures were altered or removed to allow for traffic, and the royal palaces, no longer seats of government, were gradually repurposed.

Improvements were distributed unequally. Japanese districts received better paving, drainage, and services earlier than Korean neighborhoods, and municipal funds and decision-making favored the settler community, whose representatives had more influence in councils. Korean areas, although they also benefited from some road and sanitation work, were more often treated as objects of regulation, with demolition of houses and the enforcement of sanitation rules. Land values and ownership shifted as the new road plan and registration system favored those with documents and capital.

The spatial division of Seoul into a Japanese south and a Korean north has been described by historians as one of the clearest expressions of colonial urban hierarchy, though the boundary was not a wall and contacts across it were many. Chapter 57 develops the later history of Keijō as a site of mass culture and modern urban life. For the 1910s the important points are that the colonial regime remade the capital's administration and streets to serve its purposes, and that the city's new infrastructure bore the marks of the inequality it was built to manage.

Section 55.31

The 1915 Chōsen Industrial Exhibition and the Transformation of Gyeongbokgung

From 11 September to 31 October 1915, the Government-General held an exhibition at Gyeongbokgung Palace to commemorate five years of colonial administration. Officially titled the Exhibition of Chōsen Products commemorating the fifth year of administration, and often called the Chōsen Industrial Exhibition, it displayed agricultural, mining, forestry, fishery, industrial, and artistic goods from across Korea, together with exhibits from Japan, in a series of purpose-built halls constructed on the palace grounds. Official reports counted more than a million visitors over the course of the exhibition, a figure that officials presented as evidence of its success.

The choice of Gyeongbokgung was deliberate. The palace, built in 1395 as the main royal residence of the Joseon dynasty and rebuilt in the 1860s under the Daewongun (Chapter 47), was the central symbol of the old regime. To stage the exhibition there, authorities cleared large areas of the palace grounds, demolishing or selling numerous buildings, altering the layout, and constructing exhibition halls. Historians estimate that a large share of the palace's buildings was lost, in several stages from the 1910s onward, though exact figures are uncertain. The central ceremonial halls survived, though their surroundings were transformed. The use of the royal palace to present the achievements of colonial rule turned a site of Korean sovereignty into a stage for the new order, and the very physical environment suggested that the old state had been superseded.

The exhibition's contents presented Japanese rule as modernization. Displays of agricultural improvements, railway and port development, and industrial products, along with models and maps, emphasized progress under the Government-General. The authorities encouraged attendance with discounted railway fares and organized group visits, including delegations of local notables, officials, and students from the provinces. Some visitors came out of curiosity, to see new goods and technologies. Others came under pressure from local officials. Korean reactions are difficult to reconstruct because the sources are mainly official, but memoirs and later recollections suggest a mixture of fascination, resentment, and unease at seeing the palace transformed. The exhibition's end was followed by the creation of a Government-General museum, using one of the buildings on the grounds.

One point of chronology requires emphasis. The large Government-General headquarters building, designed in a neoclassical style and placed in front of the palace's main hall, was started in 1916 and completed in 1926. It did not exist at the time of the 1915 exhibition and should not be pictured in descriptions of it. The 1915 exhibition belongs to the early stage of the process by which the palace was repurposed, in which the transformation was initially more modest and the headquarters, which more completely blocked the palace's central axis, came later.

Section 55.32

Hospitals, Public Health, Hygiene, and the Uneven Distribution of Modern Services

Modern medicine in Korea predated annexation, through mission hospitals, the Korean government's own institutions, and Japanese medical establishments in the treaty ports. After annexation the Government-General expanded the system. It maintained a central government hospital in Seoul and provincial charity hospitals, trained physicians and nurses, and, in 1916, established a medical specialized school. Vaccination against smallpox was promoted and, through regulation, made compulsory, and campaigns against cholera, plague, and other infectious diseases were run by police and health officials, with quarantine, disinfection, and reporting requirements. Water supply and sewage works in cities, and slaughterhouse and market regulations, also had public-health aims.

Some of these measures had real effects. Vaccination campaigns and quarantine contributed to the control of some epidemic diseases, and the introduction of modern hospitals and trained medical personnel expanded the available care, especially in cities. Mission hospitals, particularly those run by American Protestant missions, continued to play an important role, and some Korean physicians trained in these institutions and in Japan became prominent. The data on mortality in the 1910s is incomplete, and historians are cautious about drawing firm conclusions about overall health trends.

Access was unequal and enforcement coercive. Hospitals and clinics were concentrated in cities and towns, with Japanese populations better served than Koreans, and rural populations often had little access to modern care. Public-health measures were enforced by police and often experienced as intrusive: inspections of houses, quarantine, isolation of the sick, and fines for violations. Resistance, rumor, and distrust marked some campaigns, and many Koreans continued to rely on traditional medicine, healers, and religious practice. Fees and distance limited use of modern services. The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 struck Korea heavily, and official reports of the death toll, which vary and are likely undercounts, showed the limits of the system's capacity.

The evidence therefore supports neither a simple story of benevolent modernization nor one of pure neglect. Institutions of modern medicine and public health did expand, and some Koreans benefited, but their distribution reflected the colonial hierarchy and their enforcement relied on the police. As with railways and courts, the question is not whether these institutions existed but who controlled them and who had access.

Historical Debate

Can Infrastructure Development Be Evaluated Separately from Coercive Political Rule?

The question: Railways, roads, ports, courts, hospitals, and schools were built or expanded under a regime that denied Koreans political rights and relied on police coercion. Historians disagree about whether the development of these institutions can be assessed on its own terms or must always be assessed as part of the colonial system.

Interpretation A — Development can be evaluated separately: Some historians argue that the physical and institutional changes of the period created capacities, such as transport, legal systems, and medical services, that persisted after 1945 and contributed to later growth, and that analysis should measure these effects on their own merits, as a distinct question from political legitimacy.
Interpretation B — Development was inseparable from coercion: Others argue that the infrastructure was designed to serve colonial extraction and control, paid for by Korean taxes and compulsory labor, and distributed according to a racial hierarchy, so that its benefits cannot be evaluated without reference to the political order that shaped them.
Interpretation C — Evaluate both, in specific cases: A third approach examines each institution to ask who controlled it, who paid for it, who used it, and what it enabled, avoiding both blanket praise and blanket dismissal.

Evidence: Budgets, ownership and employment records, freight and passenger statistics, hospital and school statistics, labor-service records, memoirs, and later economic studies.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation (what would have happened without Japanese rule), counterfactuals, the weighting of long-term legacies against immediate costs, and the proper standards of evaluation, which involve both empirical and moral judgments.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes popular memory and public debate in Korea and Japan about colonial rule, and affects how the origins of postwar institutions are understood.

Student task: Choose one institution (railways, courts, or hospitals) and list the evidence that supports Interpretation A, B, and C for it.

Section 55.33

Rural Households, Village Administration, and Everyday Colonial Rule

For most Koreans, colonial rule arrived as a series of encounters with documents, officials, and obligations. A rural household in the 1910s might have been visited by surveyors who required the family to declare its land, by a police officer who checked the household register, by a township clerk who collected tax, by a school official who urged enrollment, and by a headman who allocated road labor. Each encounter involved a form, a permit, an inspection, or a demand, and each depended on local intermediaries who spoke both the language of the state and the language of the village.

The household's economic position shaped its experience. A landowning family with documents could register its land, pay the new tax in cash, and, if it had resources, benefit from improved seed and markets. A tenant family faced the landlord's rent demands, which could rise as the landlord's own tax burden and opportunities changed, and had little protection under law. A household dependent on forest products, gleaning, or common land found access narrowed by classification of land as state property or under new forest regulations. Compulsory labor for roads and other works, and fines for violating regulations on anything from sanitation to burial, added to burdens and uncertainty.

Village leaders held an ambiguous position. Headmen and township officials were expected to implement orders, but they also had to maintain relationships within the community. Some were pressured by the police and, depending on character and circumstance, either protected neighbors or exploited the position. Villagers developed practices of avoidance, negotiation, and quiet noncompliance, such as underreporting land, delaying payment, and sending children to the village seodang rather than the common school. At moments of tension, communities also joined in collective action, though in the 1910s such action was risky.

Authority therefore operated not mainly through spectacular violence but through paper, inspection, classification, and intermediaries, backed by the possibility of coercion. That is why many historians describe colonial rule as a bureaucratic regime of control in which the state became present in the details of daily life. It also helps explain why the grievances that burst into view in 1919 were so widespread: they had been built up through countless small encounters.

Section 55.34

Japanese Settlers, Korean Workers, Women, and the Social Hierarchy of Colonial Society

Colonial society was stratified by ethnicity, class, gender, and location. Japanese settlers, whose numbers roughly doubled between 1910 and the early 1920s, held advantages in occupations, wages, housing, and legal protection. They worked as officials, police, merchants, shopkeepers, craftsmen, professionals, and farmers, and lived mostly in cities and ports in neighborhoods with better services. The community was itself diverse, including officials who stayed briefly, small entrepreneurs seeking opportunity, and families who made lives in Korea, and some were poor. But as a group they were privileged by the legal and administrative order.

Korean society was also diverse. The traditional elite of landowners, former officials, and scholars adapted differently: some collaborated, some withdrew, and some joined nationalist networks. A small but growing group of educated Koreans, trained in mission schools, specialized schools, and Japanese or American institutions, took up work as teachers, clerks, physicians, journalists, pastors, and lawyers. Merchants and landlords benefited in some cases from commercial expansion. Most Koreans were farmers, laborers, artisans, and petty traders, with wage laborers and urban poor increasing in number in cities and along rail lines. A large number of people migrated to Manchuria and Russia, and others to Japan for work.

Gender shaped opportunities. Women were largely excluded from formal office and higher education, but some gained access to mission schools and to new occupations such as teaching and nursing. Many worked in agriculture and in the household economy, as weavers, vendors, and domestic servants, including in Japanese households. Entertainers and gisaeng were regulated by the police under licensing rules. The expansion of girls' education and the emergence of the "new woman" belong mainly to the following decade (Chapter 57), but the institutional preconditions were laid in the 1910s.

Wage and occupational structures were discriminatory. Korean workers were typically paid less than Japanese in comparable jobs, and promotion to supervisory roles was limited. Urban employment and improved services existed but were distributed unequally, and many of the benefits of modern institutions went first to the settler community. Daily interactions, from shopping and public transport to schooling and legal proceedings, reminded Koreans of the hierarchy, and the resentments formed in this period would fuel the nationalism of the following years.

How Did Different Groups Understand the First Years of Colonial Rule?

Section 55.35

Household Registration, Family Law, Marriage, and Colonial Legal Categories

The colonial state classified people through household registration. Korean household registers, which had a long history, were reorganized under a civil registration law of 1909 and then under colonial administration, in which police conducted household investigations that supplemented the registers. Registration determined legal identity, residence, and, in the colonial system, the line between Koreans and Japanese: a person's registration in a Korean or Japanese register defined legal status. A comprehensive revision of the Korean registration system came in 1922, beyond this chapter, but the principle that the state would define people through registration was established earlier.

Family law was handled through the concept of Korean custom. The 1912 civil ordinance applied Japanese civil law in most matters but left Koreans' family relations and inheritance to be governed by custom, which the courts were to identify. The Government-General conducted surveys of customs, compiling reports on marriage, adoption, inheritance, ancestral rites, and lineage practice, and judges in the colonial courts drew on these compilations and on prior decisions. This process meant that variable local practices were interpreted and sometimes fixed in a form that courts could apply. Some historians argue that the colonial legal approach tended to freeze in law certain patriarchal and lineage-centered norms, such as the privileging of male heirs and the authority of household heads, in ways that reflected one reading of custom among many, and that this had effects on women's inheritance and status. Others emphasize that Korean practice already included such norms and that the courts also made decisions that allowed some flexibility.

Marriage regulation also changed. Rules on registration, on minimum marriage ages established under the Korean Empire in 1907 and reflected in the 1912 Chōsen Civil Ordinance, and on divorce brought state administration into an area that custom had previously governed. Child marriage and early marriage, widely practiced, drew the attention of both colonial officials and Korean reformers, who debated its social effects. The regime also took an interest in controlling prostitution and entertainment through licensing, under public-order rationales.

The result was a legal order in which the colonial state selectively preserved and reinterpreted Korean custom while reshaping social relations through registration and adjudication. Modern legal administration could thus preserve selected inequalities, notably in gender and family, while introducing procedures such as written registration and court judgment that affected how disputes were resolved. The interplay of colonial law and Korean social practice remained an important feature of the following decades.

The chronology of marriage ages illustrates why legal change should not automatically be attributed to the colonial state. In August 1907 the Korean imperial government had already set minimum ages of seventeen for males and fifteen for females, replacing the more ambitious ages announced in the 1894 Gabo reform period. The 1912 colonial civil-law framework continued an age threshold of seventeen for men and fifteen for women; later administrative rules altered the way marriages were registered and recognized. The existence of an age minimum did not establish effective enforcement in every village, and recorded marriages could differ from customary rites performed without immediate formal registration. Likewise, applying a rule identified as “Korean custom” required officials and judges to choose which accounts of local usage would become binding. Colonial courts could thereby transform flexible or disputed traditions into apparently fixed legal categories. Evaluating these developments requires separating pre-annexation precedents, colonial administrative power, and the evidence of what families actually practiced.

Section 55.36

Sinminhoe, the 105-Person Incident, and the Survival of Organized Resistance

The Sinminhoe (New People's Association) was a secret nationalist organization founded in 1907 by Korean reformers including Ahn Chang-ho, Yang Gi-tak, Yi Dong-hwi, Yi Dong-nyeong, Yi Seung-hun, and Kim Gu. It aimed to build national strength through education, industry, and organization, and to prepare for eventual independence, and its network was strongest in the northwest, in Seoul, and among Christians. After annexation, several leaders went into exile, while others remained in Korea, and the network adapted to the new conditions. The colonial authorities saw it as a principal vehicle of potential opposition.

The incident took place between 1910 and 1913. The authorities alleged that Korean nationalists, principally Christians and teachers in the northwest, had conspired to assassinate Governor-General Terauchi during a trip to the northwest in late 1910. Beginning in autumn 1911, police arrested hundreds of people, mostly in Pyongyang and the northwest provinces, and held them for long interrogation. Of those arrested, 123 were indicted, and on 28 September 1912 the Seoul District Court convicted 105 and imposed sentences of several years. The case acquired its name from this number. Defendants and missionary observers reported that confessions had been extracted by torture, and at trial many defendants retracted their statements.

On appeal in 1913, the Gyeongseong Appeals Court overturned most of the convictions. Ninety-nine defendants were acquitted, and six, among them Yun Chi-ho, Yang Gi-tak, and Yi Seung-hun, remained convicted. They were released in February 1915 through an imperial pardon; Emperor Taishō had succeeded to the throne in 1912, so the pardon should not be mistaken for an immediate consequence of accession. The outcome has been read in two ways: as evidence that the case was a fabrication that the court could not sustain, and as evidence that the legal process, flawed as it was, retained some capacity to review the prosecution's claims. The historical assessment is that the authorities used the allegation of a plot to target a network of Korean nationalists and Christians, and that the evidence of an actual assassination plot was weak and rested heavily on coerced statements, though some participants in the underlying network did have nationalist aims.

The effects were significant. The trials exposed police methods to foreign missionaries and press, damaged the reputation of the colonial courts abroad, and disrupted Christian and nationalist organization in the northwest. Many activists left for Manchuria, Siberia, China, Hawaii, and the United States, where they built institutions that became important later. Exile networks included military-training schools in Manchuria, the Young Korean Academy founded by Ahn Chang-ho in the United States in 1913, and organizations in Shanghai and the Russian Far East. Within Korea, secret societies such as the Independence Volunteer Corps (1912) and the Korea Restoration Society (1915) arose, but with limited reach. The later development of the independence movement is the subject of Chapter 59, and its 1919 culmination, Chapter 56.

Section 55.37

Measuring Colonial Development: Statistics, Evidence, and Historical Interpretation

The Government-General produced statistics on nearly everything: land area, harvests, tax revenue, railway mileage, school enrollment, trade, and population. It published them in annual reports and in statistical yearbooks and used them to demonstrate progress. Such figures are indispensable, and they show real increases in many measures during the 1910s: revenue rose, rail and road networks grew, schools multiplied, trade expanded, and land was surveyed and registered. A historian who ignored these data would misunderstand the scale of institutional change.

But statistics are produced within categories chosen by the authority that gathers them, and their meaning depends on what they count. A rise in the quantity of rice exported does not show that the producers or the population consumed more. An increase in school enrollment does not show how large the share of children in school was, or what they learned, or who was excluded. Growth in railway mileage does not say who owned or used the line. Rising tax revenue may reflect improved administration, heavier burdens, or both. Official statistics also differed in reliability: early population and production figures were estimates with large margins of error, and data for Koreans and Japanese were often collected and presented differently.

Interpretations of the first decade therefore diverge. One tradition, often associated with defenders of the colonial record, emphasizes growth in infrastructure, administration, and production as evidence of development. A second tradition, strongly represented in Korean nationalist historiography, emphasizes coercion, exploitation, and the transfer of resources and power to Japanese interests. A third, more recent body of scholarship tries to combine both by examining institutions and distribution: who gained and who lost, and how political power shaped economic outcomes. Studies of living standards, using evidence such as wage data, height, mortality, and diet, are more difficult to interpret for the 1910s because of data limitations, which is one reason why historians remain cautious about sweeping claims.

The central lesson for students is methodological. Evidence of growth is not evidence of general welfare, and evidence of coercion is not evidence that nothing changed. The responsible historian asks what is measured, by whom, for what purpose, and what is left out, and then uses multiple kinds of evidence, including Korean testimony, to construct an account that can bear scrutiny.

Section 55.38

World War I, International Change, and Colonial Korea, 1914–1918

The First World War changed Japan's position without directly involving Korea in combat. Japan joined the Allies in August 1914, seized German holdings in Shandong and the Pacific, presented its Twenty-One Demands to China in 1915, and, with Europe's industries diverted to war production, expanded its exports and industry. The resulting boom raised demand for Korean rice, beans, and raw materials, increased commercial activity in ports, and encouraged Japanese investment, while wartime inflation raised the cost of living. In 1918 Japan sent forces to Siberia as part of the Allied intervention after the Russian Revolution, which increased the strategic importance of Korea's railways and garrison, and the Japanese rice riots of that year heightened Japanese concern about food supply from the colony.

Korean political participation remained tightly restricted. There was no legal political party, no independent press, and no representative body. The change of governor-general in 1916 from Terauchi, who became prime minister, to Hasegawa Yoshimichi brought no significant change in policy, and the gendarmerie-police system continued. Koreans who sought to organize relied on religious and educational networks, including churches, Cheondogyo, Buddhist institutions, mission schools, and student associations, as well as on exile communities abroad. These networks had the means of communication, meeting places, and the habit of organization that open politics lacked.

Ideas from outside circulated despite censorship. The Russian Revolutions of 1917 introduced new models of political change, which began to attract some Korean exiles. In January 1918 the American president Woodrow Wilson set out Fourteen Points that included the principle of adjusting colonial claims with regard to the interests of populations, and in the following months discussion of national self-determination spread in international politics, though Wilson's principle was not intended to apply to Japanese colonies and the peace conference would not treat it that way. Korean activists abroad, in the United States, China, and Japan, took up the language of self-determination and began to plan appeals to the powers. Reading of these developments in Korea was limited by censorship, but news and rumors circulated through missionaries, religious networks, students returning from Japan, and the foreign press.

The war's end in November 1918 thus produced an atmosphere of expectation among Korean nationalists, who hoped that international opinion might support the end of colonial rule, even though the practical prospects were slight. What activists did with this atmosphere, including the Tokyo student declaration and the Shanghai appeals, belongs to Chapter 56. Here the point is that the war reshaped both Japan's economic and international position and the horizon of political imagination available to Koreans.

Section 55.39

The Accumulation of Grievances and the Threshold of the March First Movement

By the end of 1918, the colonial government had built a system that appeared secure. Its police were present in nearly every district, its land survey was complete, its revenue was growing, and organized open opposition had been broken. Yet the same system had generated multiple grievances among different groups. Farmers had lost access to land and common resources or faced heavier burdens of tax, rent, and compulsory labor. Landlords and notables were often favored but remained excluded from political power. Teachers and students resented limits on schooling and on the Korean language and history. Christians, Buddhists, and Cheondogyo followers faced supervision of their institutions. Korean businessmen and officials experienced discrimination in opportunity and pay. Educated Koreans saw the contrast between the promise of imperial citizenship and the reality of segregation. Beneath these particular grievances lay a general resentment of the loss of sovereignty and of the daily presence of an unaccountable authority.

These grievances had not found open political expression because the system suppressed organization, but they had accumulated and were circulating in religious networks, schools, communities, and exile groups. The system also faced a problem of legitimacy. A government that ruled by force and denied political participation could not claim the consent of the governed, and its rhetoric of benevolence and progress had little purchase on those who experienced its coercion. The official view that Koreans were passively accepting their lot reflected the success of repression more than the state of opinion.

Gojong's death on 21 January 1919 intensified these tensions. The former emperor, who had been the symbol of the old state and who lived in retirement at Deoksugung, died suddenly, and the news, announced the next day, spread quickly. Rumors that he had been poisoned circulated widely, though there was no evidence establishing it, and historians treat the cause as unproven. Preparations for a state funeral, scheduled for early March, drew large numbers of people to Seoul. Whether or not the rumors were true, the death had great symbolic significance, giving Koreans a moment of shared mourning and an opportunity for assembly, and exposing the fragility of the colonial claim to represent legitimate authority.

This chapter ends at the threshold. The regime's institutions were in place and had changed Korean society. Its limits and contradictions were becoming apparent: it could police a population but not win its allegiance; it could survey land and collect taxes but not create trust; it could expand schools and hospitals but not remove the sense of being governed as an inferior people. What happened next, the demonstrations of March 1919, the declaration of independence, the colonial government's response, and the formation of provisional governments abroad, is the subject of Chapter 56.

Cause and Effect

How Registration, Taxation, Police Authority, and Property Law Reinforced One Another

Long-term causes

Outdated and uneven Joseon land records, the unfinished Gwangmu survey, fiscal weakness of the Korean state, and Japanese expansion since 1876.

Short-term causes

The protectorate's preparatory work, the need to fund the Government-General without large subsidies, and the army's preference for tight control.

Immediate trigger

Annexation in 1910, which unified command over administration, courts, and police.

Transformation

Land was surveyed and registered, taxed in cash, protected by court-enforced title, and administered by police and township officials.

Immediate effects

Higher and more regular revenue, secure title for documented owners, weaker claims for cultivators, and a state present in village life.

Long-term consequences

A fiscal base for colonial government, stronger landlordism, agrarian inequality, and accumulated rural grievance.

Historical complexity: No single element produced the outcome. Registration without taxation would have yielded less revenue, taxation without police enforcement would have been harder to collect, and property law without courts would have been unenforceable. The mutual reinforcement is why the system proved durable, and it also explains why its effects were uneven: they depended on prior rural structures and on who could use the new rules.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan converted the annexation of Korea in August 1910 into a comprehensive colonial system of administration, surveillance, economic control, and social regulation, and how Koreans experienced and responded to that system until the eve of 1919. Its central argument is that annexation did not simply replace Korean rulers with Japanese ones. It created a new institutional structure in which executive, legislative, police, judicial-administrative, fiscal, and educational powers were gathered under a governor-general who answered to the emperor in Tokyo and not to any representative body.

The Government-General, led first by Terauchi Masatake and then by Hasegawa Yoshimichi, concentrated authority to an extent unusual within the Japanese empire. Its ordinances functioned as law, its bureaucracy reached through thirteen provinces, a reorganized and consolidated structure of counties and townships, and municipalities in the main cities, and its personnel combined Japanese officials in nearly all senior posts with Korean clerks, interpreters, township chiefs, and notables whose motives and circumstances varied widely.

The gendarmerie-police system joined military and civilian policing and made the police station the principal contact between the state and the village. Police enforced political control and also ordinary administration, from tax collection to sanitation. The courts, reorganized in 1910–1912, introduced codified law and procedures but remained part of the administration and treated Japanese and Korean subjects unequally. The Flogging Ordinance of 1912, summary adjudication, publishing controls, and restrictions on associations completed a system designed to prevent organized opposition.

The land survey of 1910–1918 was at once a measurement program, a legal transformation of property rights, and a fiscal project. It gave the state a cadastre and a regular cash tax, secured title for documented owners, and weakened customary claims and common resources. It did not confiscate most Korean land, and its effects were uneven, but it strengthened landlords, created large holdings of state land, and contributed to agrarian inequality. Historians disagree on the scale of dispossession and on how to weigh formalization against loss.

Colonial finance and enterprise linked Korea to Japan's economy. The Bank of Chōsen, the Oriental Development Company, the Company Ordinance, agricultural policy, and trade patterns shaped unequal opportunity. Korean business continued but under constraints, and Korea increasingly exported rice and raw materials to Japan while importing manufactured goods. The fiscal health of the government and the welfare of the population were related but distinct questions.

Education, religion, and cultural knowledge were arenas of contest. The 1911 Education Ordinance aimed to produce loyal subjects with practical training, yet provided schooling to only a small share of Korean children and restricted higher education. Regulations of 1911 and 1915 reduced the number of private schools and constrained missionary institutions. The Temple Ordinance and the 1915 religious regulations brought Buddhism and Christianity under supervision, while Cheondogyo and newer movements operated under surveillance. The state regulated Korean history and built its own apparatus of research, while Koreans preserved and republished their own texts.

Railways, roads, ports, urban planning, the 1915 exhibition at Gyeongbokgung, and public-health measures changed Korea's landscape and daily life. They were real and consequential, but they were designed to serve the colonial economy and government, financed in part by Korean taxes and labor, and distributed unequally. Historians debate whether such development can be separated from the coercive political order that produced it, and the most careful answers examine each institution for who controlled it, paid for it, and used it.

Everyday life under the regime was shaped by documents, inspections, and intermediaries as well as by direct force. Rural households encountered the survey, tax, police, and compulsory labor; colonial cities were divided between a better-served Japanese south and a Korean north; women and men experienced different restrictions and opportunities; and the colonial legal system classified people through household registration and the selective use of Korean custom. The Sinminhoe and the 105-Person Incident showed both the reach of the repressive system and its limits, since the case against most defendants collapsed on appeal while nationalist networks moved abroad and into religious and educational institutions.

Official statistics document real institutional growth but cannot by themselves establish improvements in living standards, and responsible interpretation requires attention to what is counted, by whom, and for what purpose. The First World War strengthened Japan's economy and international standing while spreading ideas of self-determination to which Korean activists responded, and the accumulated grievances of the decade, together with the death of Gojong on 21 January 1919, created conditions for a mass movement that the regime's institutions had not anticipated.

These developments explain why the March First Movement of 1919 became a turning point. The regime that had been built was powerful and durable, but it governed without consent, and its reach into land, law, schooling, religion, and daily life generated resentment as well as compliance. Chapter 56 turns to how Koreans, in the cities and villages and abroad, acted on that resentment.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 55 shows that colonial rule was not only an act of conquest but a project of administration. The decisive transformations of the 1910s occurred less in the dramatic moments of annexation than in surveys, registers, police stations, courts, school rules, and railway timetables. Understanding that machinery is essential for understanding why Korean society changed as it did and why Korean grievances took the forms they did.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of holding apparently opposed ideas together. Colonial institutions were real, and some of them endured and shaped later Korean life; they were also built by a government that denied Koreans political rights, and their benefits and costs were distributed unequally. Historians' disagreements about the land survey, infrastructure, and statistics are not mere quarrels over interpretation but reflect different questions: what changed, who gained and lost, and by what standard such changes should be judged.

Many themes of this chapter continue. The cities and mass culture of Chapter 57 develop on the foundations of the colonial city, the economic changes of Chapter 58 extend the agrarian and commercial structures established here, and the independence movements of Chapter 59 draw on the networks that survived repression. The regime's later shift in 1919–1920 and the intensification of assimilation in the 1930s each respond to the system built in the 1910s.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter belongs to the long sequence from the opening of 1876 through colonization to division. The institutions of this chapter would be dismantled in 1945, but legacies of land, law, state capacity, and memory carried into the two Korean states that followed, a continuity that Volume IV will examine. Students who understand how the colonial state worked are better prepared to understand both the movement of 1919 and the contested memory of colonial rule in Korea today.
