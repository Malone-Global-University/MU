The Wars for the Peninsula, 598–676

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 612, a Sui detachment that had pressed deep into Goguryeo territory struggled back toward the Liao River after a catastrophic retreat from the vicinity of Pyongyang. Traditional accounts state that of the 305,000 troops in the detached force that advanced toward the Goguryeo capital, only about 2,700 returned to Liaodong. These transmitted figures should be treated as indications of catastrophic loss rather than modern statistically exact counts, and they describe this one detached force rather than the entirety of Sui's much larger invasion army. Whatever the true numbers, the campaign's failure announced something historically significant: a newly reunified Chinese empire, commanding resources on a scale unmatched in East Asia for centuries, had been unable to conquer the northern Korean kingdom of Goguryeo.

That failure did not end the conflict. It began nearly eight decades of intermittent warfare that would ultimately transform the political map of the Korean peninsula and reshape the balance of power across Northeast Asia. This chapter follows that warfare from the Sui dynasty's first expedition against Goguryeo in 598 through the last battles of the Silla-Tang War in 676, a period bounded at one end by the reunification of China under the Sui and at the other by the consolidation of Silla's control over much of the Korean peninsula.

The temptation, when telling this story backward from its outcome, is to make it sound inevitable: a rising Silla, allied with an overwhelming Tang empire, was destined to unify Korea, while Goguryeo and Baekje were destined to fall. This chapter resists that temptation at every turn. Goguryeo survived and repelled the failed Sui campaigns of 598 and 612, fought Tang's best forces to a standstill at the fortress of Ansi in 645, and continued resisting for more than two decades afterward. Baekje's monarchy collapsed with startling speed in 660, but organized Baekje resistance continued for three more years, drawing in a naval expedition from the Japanese archipelago and very nearly reversing the conquest. Silla's own alliance with Tang, forged out of genuine strategic desperation in 648, disintegrated within a decade of the two allies' joint triumph over Baekje and Goguryeo, producing a further six years of open warfare between former partners.

Nor did the fighting produce the outcome that later national narratives sometimes suggest. Silla's victory over Tang forces in 676 secured Silla's control over much of the peninsula south of the Taedong River region, but it did not restore Goguryeo's former northern and Manchurian territories to Korean political control in any single continuous state, and it did not eliminate the communities, elites, and military traditions that had constituted Goguryeo. Within a generation, Balhae, a new state drawing together former Goguryeo and Mohe populations of Manchuria, examined in Chapter 16, would rise in the north. The wars examined in this chapter therefore produced not a unification of "Korea" in any complete or final sense, but a fundamental reordering of the peninsula's political landscape, one whose long aftermath the later chapters of Part III will trace.

Understanding why Goguryeo, which had turned back the two greatest military powers of the age, ultimately fell in 668; why Baekje's collapse in 660 did not end Baekje resistance; and why Silla's alliance with Tang converted so quickly into a war between the two former partners, requires attention to factors well beyond individual battles. Fortress geography, seasonal campaigning, supply lines stretched across hundreds of kilometers of difficult terrain, court politics and succession disputes, shifting alliances, and the ambitions of multiple governments, Sui, Tang, Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and the Wa polity of the Japanese archipelago, all shaped the outcome. This chapter treats the wars of 598 to 676 as the product of that full constellation of causes, not as the unfolding of a single people's destined triumph.

The evidence available for this period is considerable but uneven. Chinese dynastic histories, composed by the successor governments of the very empires that fought these wars, provide detailed if not disinterested accounts of Sui and Tang campaigns. Korean sources, above all the twelfth-century Samguk Sagi, were compiled many centuries after the events they describe and preserve older material of uneven reliability alongside later reinterpretation and legend. Contemporary inscriptions, including a Tang victory inscription carved onto the existing Jeongnimsa stone pagoda after the conquest of Baekje, offer rare glimpses of how participants described these events at the time, filtered inevitably through the perspective of the victors. This chapter draws on all of these kinds of evidence while remaining attentive to what each can and cannot establish.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Sui China's reunification in 589 altered the strategic environment facing Goguryeo and led to the wars of 598 and 612.
  2. Analyze the roles of logistics, geography, fortress defense, and seasonal campaigning in explaining Sui's repeated failures against Goguryeo.
  3. Distinguish the historically attested elements of the 612 campaign and the Salsu episode from later heroic elaboration surrounding Eulji Mundeok.
  4. Assess the relationship between the Goguryeo wars and the internal collapse of the Sui dynasty without treating either as the sole cause of the other.
  5. Trace the political changes within Goguryeo associated with Yeon Gaesomun's coup of 642 and evaluate competing later portrayals of his role.
  6. Explain why Tang Taizong's invasion of 645 failed at the fortress of Ansi and why the identification of its commander as "Yang Manchun" cannot be treated as securely contemporary.
  7. Describe the diplomatic and strategic circumstances that produced the Silla-Tang alliance of 648 and explain why it should not be read as evidence of a predetermined plan of national unification.
  8. Trace the conquest of Baekje in 660 and explain why the fall of the Baekje monarchy did not immediately end organized Baekje resistance.
  9. Analyze the Baekje restoration movement of 660–663 and the Battle of Baekgang, including the involvement of the Wa polity, without projecting modern national categories onto seventh-century actors.
  10. Explain the multiple causes of Goguryeo's fall in 668, including internal political conflict, cumulative wartime attrition, and coordinated Silla-Tang pressure.
  11. Evaluate why the outcomes of 668 and 676 should not be described as a complete "unification of Korea," and identify what portions of former Goguryeo territory and population remained outside Silla's control.
  12. Explain why the Silla-Tang alliance broke down after 668 and trace the course of the Silla-Tang War through its concluding engagements at Maeso and Gibeolpo.

Historical Context

By the close of the sixth century, the political world in which Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya competed, examined throughout Part II, had already produced several centuries of shifting alliances, warfare, and diplomatic maneuvering among the peninsula's kingdoms and their continental and maritime neighbors. Goguryeo had, over the fifth and sixth centuries, established itself as the dominant military power on the peninsula and in much of southern Manchuria, controlling a broad network of fortified towns along its frontiers and maintaining relations, alternately hostile and accommodating, with the fragmented Chinese states of the Northern and Southern Dynasties period. Baekje and Silla, meanwhile, had cooperated at times against Goguryeo pressure and clashed at other times over control of the Han River basin, a strategically vital region connecting the peninsula's interior to the Yellow Sea, discussed in Chapter 9 and Chapter 10.

This long-standing regional balance was transformed by a development external to the peninsula: the reunification of China under the Sui dynasty in 589, after centuries of fragmentation following the end of Han rule, interrupted by Western Jin's brief reunification in 280 before renewed division in the early fourth century. A unified Chinese empire commanding the resources of the North China Plain, the Yangzi valley, and extensive administrative and military institutions represented a categorically different kind of neighbor than the competing regional Chinese states Goguryeo had previously managed through a mixture of diplomacy, tribute, and selective confrontation. Sui's founding emperors made clear their expectation that Goguryeo would submit to a subordinate position within a Sui-centered diplomatic order, an expectation Goguryeo's rulers were not prepared to accept without resistance.

At the same time, developments within the peninsula continued on their own trajectories. Silla, having gained control of the Han River basin from Baekje in the mid-sixth century, examined in Chapter 10, found itself simultaneously exposed to Goguryeo pressure from the north and Baekje hostility from the west, a strategically precarious position that would eventually drive Silla toward seeking a continental ally. Baekje, having lost the Han River basin, nursed its own grievance against Silla and continued to cultivate close relations with polities in the Japanese archipelago, examined in Chapter 9, relations that would prove significant when Baekje's monarchy later collapsed.

This chapter begins from that convergence of continental and peninsular pressures around the year 598 and follows the resulting warfare through nearly eight decades of shifting alliances, invasions, sieges, restoration movements, and, ultimately, a war between former allies, ending in 676 with a peninsula whose political shape had been fundamentally altered but not simply unified under a single victorious state.

Section 13.1

A New East Asian Balance after 589

The Sui dynasty's reunification of China in 589, after centuries of political fragmentation following the end of Han rule, interrupted briefly by Western Jin's reunification of the empire in 280 before renewed division set in during the early fourth century, was among the most consequential political developments in East Asian history, and its consequences for the Korean peninsula were immediate and lasting. For centuries, Goguryeo had managed its relations with a succession of competing Chinese states, north and south, through a flexible mixture of tribute, diplomacy, opportunistic alliance, and occasional confrontation, never having to face a single Chinese government commanding the full military and economic resources of a reunified empire. The Sui dynasty changed this calculation entirely.

Sui's founding rulers understood their reunification of China as restoring a natural and hierarchical international order, one in which neighboring states, including Goguryeo, were expected to acknowledge Sui's superior position through tribute and, where Sui judged it necessary, direct submission to Sui strategic interests. Goguryeo's rulers, having long operated as an independent regional power controlling extensive territory in the northern peninsula and southern Manchuria, were not prepared to accept a subordinate position defined entirely on Sui's terms. This tension between Sui's assumption of hierarchical authority and Goguryeo's assertion of independent standing forms the essential background to the conflict that followed.

The immediate trigger for open warfare in 598 involved a Goguryeo military action toward the Liaoxi region, west of the Liao River, reportedly conducted with the cooperation of Malgal forces, a Tungusic-speaking people of Manchuria allied with Goguryeo at various points during this period. Sui responded to this incursion with a punitive expedition. This sequence matters for historical accuracy: the 598 war did not begin with an unprovoked Sui assault on a passive Goguryeo, but with a Goguryeo military initiative that provoked a Sui response, itself likely shaped by Goguryeo's own anxiety about growing Sui power and its desire to demonstrate continued independent military capacity before Sui could consolidate its position on the northeastern frontier.

Students should resist reading this sequence as evidence that Goguryeo bears exclusive responsibility for the resulting decades of warfare, any more than Sui's hierarchical diplomatic assumptions alone explain the conflict. Both sides approached the reunification of China as a moment requiring strategic repositioning, and both took actions, Goguryeo's frontier incursion and Sui's assertion of a tribute-based hierarchy, that made confrontation considerably more likely than continued accommodation.

Section 13.2

The War of 598

Sui's 598 expedition against Goguryeo, launched by land and sea, should be understood as the opening confrontation in a much longer conflict rather than as a smaller preview of the far better documented 612 campaign. The land force, marching through Liaoxi toward the Liao River frontier, encountered severe logistical difficulties: heavy rains disrupted supply lines, disease spread through the encamped army, and the campaigning season proved too short to achieve a decisive result before the onset of harsh conditions forced a withdrawal. The accompanying naval expedition, intended to strike along the coast, was likewise disrupted by storms and failed to achieve its objectives.

Following this failed campaign, Goguryeo's court moved toward a diplomatic accommodation with Sui, formally acknowledging a tributary relationship without conceding the substantive independence Goguryeo's rulers were determined to preserve. This outcome established a pattern that would recur throughout the Sui-Goguryeo wars: military campaigns launched with substantial resources repeatedly failed to achieve decisive victory, and periods of open warfare alternated with periods of formal, if uneasy, diplomatic accommodation.

The central historical lesson of 598 is one this chapter will return to repeatedly: campaign outcomes in this theater of warfare were shaped as much by logistics, weather, disease, and the difficulty of sustaining large armies at great distance from their home bases as by battlefield tactics or the relative skill of opposing commanders. An empire with vastly superior overall military and economic resources could nonetheless fail to translate that advantage into victory when campaigning across difficult terrain, extended supply lines, and a determined, well-fortified opponent.

Section 13.3

Fortresses, Geography, and Goguryeo Defense

Goguryeo's ability to repeatedly frustrate campaigns launched by two of the most powerful states in East Asian history rested substantially on a defensive system built around fortified strongpoints controlling key river crossings, mountain passes, and roads across the Liaodong region and the approaches to Pyongyang. Major fortresses such as Liaodong Fortress and, later in the conflict, Ansi anchored networks of smaller strongholds, watchtowers, and garrisoned positions that forced invading armies to besiege and reduce fortified points one by one rather than advancing freely toward Goguryeo's political center.

This defensive strategy interacted powerfully with the region's geography and climate. Invading armies moving from the North China Plain toward the Korean peninsula's interior had to cross the Liao River, traverse Liaodong's marshy and difficult terrain, and then continue hundreds of additional kilometers toward Pyongyang, all while maintaining supply lines vulnerable to disruption, ambush, and the seasonal cycle of rain and cold that limited effective campaigning to a relatively narrow window each year. An army delayed by fortress sieges risked being caught by autumn rains or winter cold far from resupply, a vulnerability Goguryeo's defensive planners appear to have understood and exploited.

Students should be careful, however, not to treat this relationship between geography and Goguryeo's military success as a simple case of environmental determinism, as though the landscape alone dictated the outcome of these wars. Geography created constraints and opportunities that skilled Goguryeo military and political leadership then exploited through deliberate strategic choices: the construction and maintenance of fortification networks, the stockpiling of supplies to withstand sieges, the coordination of garrison forces, and the willingness of Goguryeo commanders to withdraw the rural population and its grain stores behind fortress walls rather than allow them to fall into invading hands. Geography made effective defense possible; institutional and military planning made it actual.

Section 13.4

Sui Yangdi's Great Campaign of 612

The campaign launched in 612 by Emperor Yang of Sui, known posthumously as Sui Yangdi, represented a mobilization of resources on a scale rarely attempted in East Asian history to that point. Later Chinese dynastic histories describe an army said to number over a million combatants, supported by enormous logistical trains of laborers and supply personnel, advancing toward Goguryeo along multiple routes by both land and sea. Students should treat these totals with considerable caution. Premodern dynastic histories frequently reported army sizes in round, rhetorically impressive figures intended to convey the scale of imperial mobilization rather than to provide a modern statistically verified headcount, and the 612 figures should be read in that light: as evidence of an extraordinarily large mobilization, not as a precise modern census.

The campaign's basic outline is nonetheless clear from multiple sources. Sui forces advanced on Liaodong Fortress and other strongpoints, encountering stubborn resistance that consumed critical campaigning time. A separate naval force sailed toward the mouth of the Daedong River in an attempt to strike directly at Pyongyang, while a land detachment pushed deep into Goguryeo territory in a bid to reach the capital before the fortress sieges along the main invasion route could be completed. This detached force's advance toward Pyongyang, unsupported by adequate supply, proved to be the campaign's decisive vulnerability.

As the advancing Sui column approached the Goguryeo capital, it found itself extended far beyond secure supply lines, its soldiers reportedly carrying insufficient provisions for a prolonged engagement. Goguryeo forces, employing a strategy of calculated withdrawal that drew the invaders deeper into hostile territory while harassing their advance, allowed the Sui force to reach the vicinity of Pyongyang before beginning a coordinated counterattack as the exhausted and undersupplied invaders began their retreat. The resulting collapse of the Sui force during its withdrawal, traditionally associated with a crossing of the Salsu River, produced catastrophic losses among the retreating army.

Section 13.5

Eulji Mundeok and the Salsu Tradition

Later Korean historical tradition credits the Goguryeo commander Eulji Mundeok with masterminding the destruction of the retreating Sui force at the Salsu River, an episode that became, over succeeding centuries, one of the most celebrated events in Korean military memory. The tradition holds that Eulji Mundeok deliberately drew the Sui force into overextension through a series of feigned retreats and calculated engagements, exhausting the invaders' supplies and morale before striking as they attempted to withdraw across the river.

Historians must approach this tradition with appropriate care. The broad outline, a Sui detachment overextended toward Pyongyang and subsequently destroyed during a difficult retreat, is corroborated across multiple sources and should be treated as historically well founded. The specific tactical details attributed to Eulji Mundeok's personal generalship, including certain reported exchanges and stratagems that appear in later retellings, reflect a process of historical memory and commemoration that developed and elaborated over the centuries following the events themselves, a pattern comparable to the retrospective elaboration this textbook has already noted in connection with foundation narratives in Chapter 6.

This chapter's central methodological point bears repeating here: crediting the entire Sui failure of 612 to the individual brilliance of a single commander, however celebrated in later memory, obscures the fuller set of causes examined in this chapter. Overextended supply lines, the cumulative delay imposed by fortress sieges along the main invasion route, the physical and logistical exhaustion of an army operating far from its home bases, and the coordinated defensive strategy of the broader Goguryeo military and political leadership all contributed to the outcome. Eulji Mundeok's documented role as a Goguryeo commander during this campaign is genuine; the fully elaborated narrative of singular personal genius that later tradition constructed around him is a separate matter, valuable for understanding Korean historical memory but distinct from a complete historical explanation of Sui's defeat.

Section 13.6

613–614 and the End of the Sui Wars

Emperor Yang did not abandon his ambitions against Goguryeo after the disaster of 612. A renewed campaign was launched in 613, again committing substantial resources to the reduction of Goguryeo's frontier fortresses. This second campaign, however, was cut short not by Goguryeo's battlefield resistance alone but by a rebellion within Sui itself: the uprising of Yang Xuangan, a high-ranking official whose revolt against Sui authority forced Emperor Yang to abandon the campaign and redirect his forces toward suppressing the domestic threat.

This interruption illustrates a theme central to this chapter's treatment of causation: the outcome of the Sui-Goguryeo wars cannot be explained by military factors on the Korean peninsula alone. Sui's capacity to sustain repeated massive mobilizations against Goguryeo depended on domestic political stability and the willingness of Sui's population to bear the enormous burdens of conscription, taxation, and forced labor that these campaigns required. The Yang Xuangan rebellion signaled that these burdens were generating serious internal opposition to Sui rule, a warning sign of the broader unrest that would soon overwhelm the dynasty.

A further, smaller campaign in 614 ended with Goguryeo reaching a negotiated accommodation with Sui, again acknowledging formal tributary status without Sui achieving anything resembling the conquest or lasting submission its rulers had originally sought. This 614 settlement marked the practical end of large-scale Sui-Goguryeo warfare. Within four years, the Sui dynasty itself had collapsed amid widespread rebellion, economic exhaustion, and the accumulated costs of the Goguryeo campaigns alongside numerous other large state projects, including extensive canal construction. Students should resist the common oversimplification that Goguryeo alone destroyed the Sui dynasty. The Goguryeo wars imposed severe and well-documented costs in lives, resources, and political legitimacy, but Sui's collapse resulted from the interaction of these costs with widespread domestic rebellion, fiscal strain from multiple large state projects, and a broader crisis of political legitimacy that extended well beyond the northeastern frontier.

Section 13.7

From Sui to Tang: A Changed Imperial Strategy

The Tang dynasty, established in 618 after Sui's collapse, inherited the unresolved strategic problem of Goguryeo but did not initially pursue an identical approach. Tang's early rulers, preoccupied first with consolidating control over China itself and managing relations with steppe powers to the north and west, maintained a more cautious posture toward Goguryeo through the 620s and 630s, engaging in diplomatic exchange and periods of formally correct, if wary, relations.

This period of relative accommodation gradually gave way to renewed tension as Tang power consolidated and as developments within Goguryeo itself, examined in the following section, altered the character of Goguryeo's own governance and its posture toward its neighbors. Tang's eventual decision to renew military action against Goguryeo in the 640s reflected both a more confident and expansive Tang foreign policy under Emperor Taizong, one of Tang's most militarily active rulers, and a judgment that internal changes within Goguryeo had created new grounds, or new opportunities, for confrontation.

It is important to recognize that Tang's approach, when war did resume, was not a simple repetition of Sui strategy. Tang commanders and planners studied the failures of the Sui campaigns and adjusted their operational methods accordingly, placing greater emphasis on methodical reduction of Goguryeo's frontier fortress network, tighter coordination between land and naval forces, and more careful attention to supply arrangements. These adjustments would produce different, though still ultimately incomplete, results when full-scale war resumed in 645.

Section 13.8

Yeon Gaesomun and the Coup of 642

In 642, the Goguryeo military official Yeon Gaesomun engineered a coup that fundamentally altered the kingdom's internal politics on the eve of renewed war with Tang. According to the surviving accounts, Yeon Gaesomun orchestrated the killing of King Yeongnyu along with a substantial portion of the royal court, installed a new monarch, King Bojang, as a largely ceremonial figurehead, and concentrated effective military and political authority in his own hands, a position his family would continue to hold through his sons after his death.

The coup itself is well attested across multiple sources and should be treated as historically established, though the specific circumstances, motivations, and immediate justifications offered at the time are less securely documented than the basic fact of the seizure of power. Later interpretations of Yeon Gaesomun's role have varied dramatically depending on the political and national perspective of the interpreter. Some later Korean nationalist historiography, particularly from the early twentieth century onward, portrayed Yeon Gaesomun as a fiercely independent patriot whose uncompromising resistance to Tang encapsulated Korean national defiance against foreign domination. Chinese dynastic sources, produced by the very government Yeon Gaesomun opposed, generally portray him in a considerably more negative light, emphasizing the violence of his seizure of power and characterizing his rule as tyrannical.

This chapter treats both of these later interpretive traditions as retrospective judgments requiring careful source criticism rather than as straightforward historical fact. What can be established with reasonable confidence is that Yeon Gaesomun's coup concentrated Goguryeo's wartime decision-making in a single powerful figure at a moment when the kingdom faced renewed and eventually sustained pressure from Tang, and that this concentration of authority shaped Goguryeo's conduct of the following decades of warfare in ways that later chroniclers, writing from very different vantage points, would interpret in sharply divergent terms.

Section 13.9

Tang Taizong's Invasion of 645 and Ansi Fortress

Citing the 642 coup and Goguryeo's continued defiance of Tang's preferred diplomatic order as justification, Emperor Taizong personally led a major invasion of Goguryeo in 645, applying the revised operational approach Tang planners had developed in the years since the Sui failures. Tang forces advanced along multiple routes, methodically besieging and, in a number of cases, successfully capturing Goguryeo frontier fortresses in the Liaodong region, achieving results that in several respects exceeded what Sui had managed decades earlier.

The campaign's turning point came at the fortress of Ansi, whose garrison mounted a prolonged and highly effective defense against the besieging Tang army. Despite bringing to bear substantial siege resources, including the construction of an earthen siege mound intended to overlook and dominate the fortress walls, Tang forces were unable to force Ansi's surrender. As the campaigning season drew toward its close and the approach of winter threatened to strand the Tang army in hostile territory far from resupply, exactly the vulnerability that had doomed the Sui campaigns, Emperor Taizong made the decision to withdraw.

Students should exercise particular caution regarding the identity of Ansi's commander. Popular tradition, especially in later centuries, identifies this commander by the name Yang Manchun, celebrated for his defiant resistance and, in some tellings, for a dramatic personal exchange with Emperor Taizong himself. This name and its associated anecdotes do not appear in the earliest and most nearly contemporary sources describing the siege, which do not name the fortress's commander at all; the identification belongs to considerably later historical and literary tradition. This chapter accordingly refers to Ansi's defense as an achievement of the fortress's garrison and command without asserting the historical reliability of a commander's name that cannot be confidently traced to contemporary record. The episode nonetheless demonstrates, on solid evidentiary ground, that Tang's improved strategy still could not overcome the combination of fortress defense, logistics, and seasonal constraint that had frustrated Sui.

Section 13.10

Why Silla Sought Tang

While Goguryeo absorbed the brunt of Sui and Tang military attention through the first half of the seventh century, Silla's own strategic position had grown increasingly precarious. Baekje, still resentful of its earlier loss of the Han River basin to Silla, mounted repeated attacks against Silla's western territories during the 630s and 640s, at times threatening Silla's core territory directly. Goguryeo, meanwhile, remained a persistent threat along Silla's northern frontier, and the two western neighbors at times coordinated their pressure against Silla, leaving Silla's court acutely conscious of its exposed position, squeezed between two hostile kingdoms with no comparably powerful ally of its own on the peninsula.

Silla's response was sustained diplomatic engagement with Tang, seeking to convert Tang's ongoing hostility toward Goguryeo into a coordinated alliance that would also address Silla's conflict with Baekje. Silla emissaries, including members of the Silla royal house, traveled to the Tang court seeking military assistance and cultivating close diplomatic and, in some respects, cultural ties with Tang institutions. These efforts culminated in the formal establishment of a Silla-Tang military alliance in 648, an arrangement under which the two states committed to coordinated action against their shared adversaries.

Students should be careful not to read this alliance as evidence that Silla had already envisioned, in 648, anything resembling a project of uniting the entire Korean peninsula under Silla rule. The alliance was a strategic response to Silla's immediate and severe security concerns, an attempt to survive and reverse Baekje's pressure and to gain protection against Goguryeo, not a blueprint for the political outcome that would eventually follow nearly three decades later. The consequences of the alliance, including the destruction of two kingdoms and Silla's eventual war with its own ally, developed through a long and contingent sequence of events rather than unfolding according to a plan fixed at the moment of the alliance's formation.

Section 13.11

The Destruction of Baekje, 660

The Silla-Tang alliance produced its first decisive result in 660, when a coordinated campaign brought about the sudden collapse of Baekje's royal government. A large Tang force, transported by sea, landed near the mouth of the Geum River while a Silla army advanced from the east, converging on the Baekje capital of Sabi in a tightly coordinated pincer movement that Baekje's defenses proved unable to withstand.

The most heavily commemorated engagement of this campaign, the Battle of Hwangsanbeol, saw a Baekje force under the general Gyebaek mount a determined defense against the advancing Silla army, delaying but ultimately failing to halt Silla's approach. With Silla and Tang forces converging on the capital in rapid succession, Baekje's court found itself unable to organize effective resistance, and Sabi fell within a remarkably short span of time. King Uija and members of the royal family were captured and subsequently taken to the Tang capital, formally ending the Baekje monarchy that had governed the kingdom since its founding.

Later tradition, including elements of the Samguk Sagi, attributes Baekje's sudden collapse substantially to the personal moral failings of King Uija, portraying him as having abandoned the vigorous governance of his earlier reign in favor of dissolute indulgence in his final years. Historians should treat this moralizing explanation with considerable skepticism. Such narratives of a final ruler's personal decline are a recurring convention in East Asian dynastic historiography, often serving to explain and, in effect, justify a dynasty's fall in terms of individual virtue and vice rather than the fuller range of structural and military factors actually responsible. The far more historically persuasive explanation for Baekje's rapid collapse in 660 lies in the coordinated military strategy of the Silla-Tang alliance, the isolation of Baekje from potential external support at the critical moment, and the sheer speed and scale of the combined assault, which allowed Silla and Tang forces to reach and overwhelm the Baekje capital before an effective defense could be organized, regardless of whatever internal political conditions prevailed at Uija's court.

Section 13.12

Baekje Was Not Finished: The Restoration Movement, 660–663

The fall of Sabi and the capture of King Uija ended the Baekje royal government, but it did not end Baekje as a functioning political and military presence in the southwestern peninsula. Within a short period after the conquest, organized resistance emerged under leaders including the Buddhist monk Dochim and the Baekje royal kinsman and military leader Boksin, who worked together to establish a restored Baekje political authority from regional strongholds beyond immediate Silla-Tang control. They subsequently sought out a surviving member of the Baekje royal line, Buyeo Pung, then residing in the Japanese archipelago, to serve as a restored monarch and rallying symbol for the movement.

The restoration movement succeeded in mobilizing substantial local support and military resources, drawing on communities and garrisons that had not been directly destroyed in the initial 660 campaign and that retained loyalty to Baekje political identity even after the fall of the capital. The movement established fortified centers of resistance, most prominently a stronghold conventionally identified as Juryuseong, though the precise location of this site remains a matter of ongoing scholarly disagreement rather than settled fact. From these bases, restoration forces conducted a sustained military campaign against occupying Silla and Tang forces over roughly three years.

The restoration movement was ultimately undermined as much by internal division as by external military pressure. Sources describe an escalating sequence of factional violence within the restoration leadership: Boksin killed Dochim during a struggle for control of the movement, and Buyeo Pung later killed Boksin in turn, eliminating the movement's most experienced military leadership at a critical juncture. This internal fracture, combined with mounting Silla-Tang military pressure, set the stage for the movement's climactic defeat at the Battle of Baekgang in 663, examined in the following section. The restoration movement's three years of sustained resistance nonetheless demonstrate an essential historical point: the destruction of a kingdom's capital and the capture of its king did not automatically or immediately extinguish the kingdom's broader political community, military capacity, or the loyalty it could still command among its people.

Section 13.13

Baekgang, 663: A Regional War

The Baekje restoration movement's struggle drew in an external participant whose involvement illustrates the genuinely international character of seventh-century Northeast Asian politics: the Wa polity of the Japanese archipelago, commonly associated with the Yamato court, which maintained long-standing diplomatic and cultural ties to Baekje reaching back through the earlier centuries examined in Chapter 9. In response to appeals from the restoration movement and its own strategic interest in preventing a hostile Silla-Tang order from dominating the peninsula unopposed, the Wa court dispatched a substantial naval expedition to support the restoration effort.

This combined force of Baekje restoration troops and Wa naval forces engaged Silla and Tang forces at the Battle of Baekgang, fought at the mouth of the Geum River in 663. The available accounts, including Tang and Silla sources as well as the considerably later Japanese chronicle the Nihon Shoki, describe a decisive Silla-Tang victory, with Tang naval forces reportedly destroying a large portion of the Wa fleet in a series of engagements over several days.

Students should exercise particular care in describing this episode. The Nihon Shoki, compiled in the early eighth century, reflects the political and ideological concerns of the Yamato court at the time of its composition and should be used as evidence for the battle with the same critical caution appropriate to any retrospective source, rather than as a straightforwardly objective contemporary account. Equally important, this chapter deliberately avoids describing Baekgang using modern national categories. The polity that sent forces to Baekgang was the Wa or Yamato court, a specific seventh-century political authority in the Japanese archipelago, not a state equivalent to modern Japan, and the engagement should not be recast as an ancient prefiguration of later Korea-Japan national rivalry. Baekgang was, more precisely, a regional conflict among seventh-century political powers with overlapping interests, in which the defeat of Wa naval forces and Baekje restoration troops together shattered the main restoration coalition. Juryuseong fell soon afterward, while resistance at the stronghold of Imjonseong continued somewhat longer before the movement's final collapse later in 663.

Section 13.14

The Final Wars against Goguryeo, 661–668

Even as the Baekje restoration movement played out in the southwest, Tang renewed direct military pressure against Goguryeo, launching further campaigns in the early 660s that, while not immediately decisive, continued to strain Goguryeo's military and economic resources after decades of intermittent but recurring warfare stretching back to 598. The cumulative effect of this sustained conflict, sometimes described by historians in terms of attrition, gradually eroded Goguryeo's capacity to sustain the kind of effective, coordinated resistance that had defeated Sui and held Tang at Ansi.

Internal Goguryeo politics compounded this external pressure. Yeon Gaesomun's death in 666 removed the figure who had concentrated Goguryeo's wartime authority since 642, and the succession that followed among his sons proved deeply divisive. Disputes over leadership led to significant defections, with at least one of Yeon Gaesomun's sons and other prominent Goguryeo figures ultimately submitting to Tang or Silla authority, providing the invading coalition with valuable intelligence, guidance, and, in some cases, military support drawn from within Goguryeo's own ruling elite.

Silla, for its part, applied continuing pressure from the south, coordinating its own military operations with Tang's renewed campaigns in the north in a manner that increasingly threatened Goguryeo from multiple directions simultaneously. The explanation for Goguryeo's eventual defeat in 668, examined in the following section, must therefore account for this full convergence of causes: cumulative wartime exhaustion after decades of conflict, the loss of unified leadership following Yeon Gaesomun's death, internal succession conflict and defection, and coordinated external pressure from both Tang and Silla operating together rather than separately. No single factor among these was, by itself, sufficient to explain Goguryeo's fall; their combination proved decisive where none alone had been.

Section 13.15

The Fall of Pyongyang and the Problem of 668

In 668, combined Tang and Silla forces, benefiting from the accumulated advantages examined in the preceding section, succeeded in capturing Pyongyang, bringing an end to the Goguryeo royal government that had ruled from the city for centuries. This event marked the definitive conclusion of organized Goguryeo statehood as it had existed since the kingdom's rise to regional dominance, examined in Chapter 8, and it is properly regarded as one of the pivotal turning points in the entire history of the Korean peninsula covered by this textbook.

It is essential, however, that students resist a common and historically inaccurate shorthand: the claim that 668 represents the "unification of Korea." Several considerations make this shorthand seriously misleading. First, Tang did not simply hand over conquered Goguryeo territory to Silla; Tang established its own administrative apparatus, the Andong Protectorate, intended to govern former Goguryeo lands directly under Tang authority, an arrangement whose failure and consequences are examined in the next section. Second, Goguryeo's population, elites, and military forces did not simply disappear or transfer their loyalty to Silla; substantial numbers of former Goguryeo people remained in the north and in Manchuria, some initially under Tang administration, others maintaining independent or semi-independent existence, and many would later contribute to the formation of Balhae, examined in Chapter 16. Third, Silla's own military and administrative reach in 668 did not extend across the whole of the former Goguryeo territory, which had encompassed extensive regions of Manchuria never brought under Silla control at any point in this period.

The historically accurate description of 668, therefore, is not that Korea was unified, but that the Goguryeo royal government fell, ending one of the primary political structures of the Three Kingdoms period, while leaving the fate of most of Goguryeo's former northern and Manchurian territory, and the large populations living there, unresolved and open to a further several decades of political development that this textbook takes up beginning in Chapter 16. The peninsula's political reordering following 668 remained very much a work in progress, not a completed unification.

Section 13.16

From Allies to Enemies, 670–676

The alliance that had jointly destroyed Baekje and Goguryeo did not survive the disappearance of the shared enemies that had made it strategically necessary. Later Silla sources preserve a claim that the original 648 alliance had included a territorial understanding: that once Baekje and Goguryeo were defeated, Baekje's territory and the lands south of Pyongyang would belong to Silla. This later-recorded claim should be treated with the same source criticism appropriate to any retrospective account of a diplomatic agreement, but the chapter cannot simply assume that no prior territorial understanding existed. What is clearly documented is that Tang, following the conquests, established its own direct administrative institutions over the conquered territory: the Ungjin Commandery in the former Baekje southwest and the Andong Protectorate over former Goguryeo lands in the north. Whatever the precise terms of the 648 understanding and however each side interpreted them, these Tang institutions sharply conflicted with Silla's postwar expectations and revealed an ambition considerably broader than Silla had anticipated when it sought Tang's help.

Silla responded by supporting continuing resistance among former Baekje and Goguryeo populations against Tang administrative control, absorbing former Baekje territory into its own expanding sphere, and gradually shifting from cooperation with Tang to open confrontation. Among the clearest examples of this strategy, Silla supported the Goguryeo restoration leaders Geom Mojam and Anseung, a Goguryeo royal claimant whose precise relationship to the royal house is reported differently in the surviving sources, installing Anseung at Geumma under Silla patronage beginning around 670 and backing the resulting political community, later known as the Bodeok polity, as a check against Tang authority in the former Goguryeo south. The later incorporation and dissolution of the Bodeok polity belongs with Chapter 14's treatment of Silla's postwar political consolidation, but its founding illustrates how directly Silla mobilized former Goguryeo people and political identity in its struggle against its former ally. By 670, this escalating friction had developed into full-scale warfare between the former allies, a conflict historians generally describe as the Silla-Tang War, fought primarily across former Baekje and Goguryeo territory and lasting until 676.

The war's decisive turning points came in its final years. In 675, Silla forces achieved a major success against Tang land forces at Maeso, a victory that significantly weakened Tang's position on the peninsula and demonstrated Silla's growing capacity to confront Tang forces directly rather than merely supporting proxy resistance. The following year, in 676, a Silla naval victory at Gibeolpo further undermined Tang's ability to project and sustain military power along the peninsula's coasts. Facing these setbacks alongside pressing military concerns elsewhere along its vast frontiers, Tang chose to withdraw its main forces from the peninsula and reorient its administrative ambitions away from direct rule over Silla-controlled territory, effectively conceding Silla's consolidation of control over much of the peninsula south of the former Goguryeo heartland.

Students should avoid the simple characterization that "Tang betrayed Silla," a framing that flattens a more complicated reality, while also not erasing the documented Silla claim that Tang had disregarded an earlier territorial understanding. Silla had sought Tang's military assistance to survive Baekje's pressure and counter Goguryeo, and later Silla tradition held that Tang's postwar administrative institutions violated the terms on which that assistance had been given. Tang, for its part, had genuine strategic interests in extending its administrative reach into the newly conquered regions, interests that proved incompatible with Silla's own ambitions once the common enemies were removed. The Silla-Tang War, in this light, represents the natural consequence of a wartime alliance whose broader strategic interests were never fully aligned, despite the territorial understanding preserved in later Silla tradition, beyond the immediate goal of defeating Baekje and Goguryeo.

Historical Vocabulary
Fortress Warfare and Campaign Logistics

Definition: Fortress warfare refers to a style of military conflict organized around fortified strongpoints that an invading force must besiege and reduce individually, rather than a style of warfare decided primarily through open-field battles between mobile armies. Campaign logistics refers to the practical challenges of supplying, feeding, and maintaining a military force over the course of an extended operation, including the transport of food, equipment, and reinforcements across long distances and difficult terrain.

Historical context: Goguryeo's network of frontier fortresses across Liaodong and along the approaches to Pyongyang forced Sui and Tang invading armies to spend enormous amounts of time, manpower, and supplies reducing individual strongpoints, time that steadily narrowed the available campaigning season before the onset of autumn rains and winter cold. Sui and Tang armies operating far from their home territories depended on extended and vulnerable supply lines that Goguryeo's defensive strategy, including deliberate withdrawal of population and stores behind fortress walls, was specifically designed to strain.

Why it matters: Understanding fortress warfare and campaign logistics explains why numerically and technologically superior Sui and Tang forces repeatedly failed to conquer Goguryeo despite possessing vastly greater overall resources. Military success in this period depended less on total imperial resources than on the ability to translate those resources into effective, sustained pressure against a well-organized defensive system within a narrow operational window.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not conclude that Goguryeo's fortress strategy made it invulnerable indefinitely. The same defensive system that repeatedly frustrated Sui and Tang invasions still required enormous continuous investment to maintain, and decades of cumulative warfare, examined in Section 13.14, gradually eroded Goguryeo's capacity to sustain it.

Historical Vocabulary
Restoration Movements

Definition: A restoration movement refers to an organized effort by surviving political, military, or religious leaders to reestablish a fallen kingdom's monarchy and political authority after the conquest of its capital and the capture or death of its ruling monarch, typically conducted from regional strongholds beyond the immediate control of the conquering power.

Historical context: The Baekje restoration movement of 660–663, led initially by the monk Dochim and the royal kinsman Boksin and centered on the restored royal claimant Buyeo Pung, mobilized substantial regional support and drew in external military assistance from the Wa polity before its main coalition was shattered at Baekgang.

Why it matters: Restoration movements demonstrate that the conquest of a capital city and the capture of a reigning monarch did not automatically extinguish a kingdom's broader political community, regional military capacity, or the continued loyalty of its population. They complicate any narrative that treats a single decisive battle or the fall of a single city as the complete end of a kingdom's historical existence.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that a restoration movement's ultimate failure to succeed means it lacked genuine historical significance. The Baekje restoration movement shaped the following several years of regional warfare, drew in external participants, and represents an essential part of Baekje's, and the peninsula's, seventh-century history rather than a mere postscript to the 660 conquest.

Primary Source Window

Daedang Pyeong Baekjeguk Bimyeong, the Tang Inscription on the Pacification of Baekje, 660 CE

Composer: He Suiliang, commissioned in the context of Su Dingfang's conquest of Baekje · Calligrapher: Quan Huaisu · Date: the fifteenth day of the eighth month of 660, shortly after the fall of Sabi · Location and material context: carved into the first story of the existing five-story stone pagoda at Baekje's Jeongnimsa temple in Sabi (modern Buyeo); the same text is also preserved in a stone basin inscription · Type: Contemporary Tang victory inscription

Context: Following the swift Silla-Tang conquest of Baekje in 660, Tang authorities had this commemorative text composed and then carved directly onto an existing Baekje monument, the stone pagoda of Jeongnimsa, rather than erecting an entirely new structure. Carving the Tang victory text onto an existing Baekje religious monument can reasonably be read as a pointed political statement. As a text produced by the victorious power at the moment of apparent triumph, it offers a rare surviving example of how the conquest was described by its architects at the time, rather than through later retrospective historical writing.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the inscription's general content and tone.

The inscription praises Tang imperial authority and credits Su Dingfang and the coordinated land and naval assault with overwhelming Baekje's defenses, records the capture of King Uija and members of the Baekje elite, describes Tang's plans for reorganizing the conquered territory under its own administration, and frames the conquest as a completed triumph extending Tang order over a subdued periphery.

Analysis: The inscription is valuable to students of this chapter precisely because of what it takes for granted: it presents the 660 campaign as a complete and finished triumph, with no acknowledgment that organized Baekje resistance would continue for three more years. This gap between the inscription's triumphant tone and the subsequent history of the restoration movement, examined in Section 13.12, illustrates how a victor's inscription carved in the immediate aftermath of a dramatic success can present an incomplete or premature picture of a conflict's actual outcome.

Limitations: As a text produced by the conquering power to celebrate its own achievement, the inscription cannot be treated as a neutral or complete account of the campaign. It says nothing about Baekje's perspective, offers no acknowledgment of the human costs of the conquest for Baekje's population, and, most importantly for this chapter's purposes, could not have anticipated the restoration movement that would soon complicate its confident narrative of pacification.

Perspective: The inscription represents the perspective of the Tang military and court elite responsible for the campaign. It does not represent Baekje elite, popular, or restoration-movement perspectives, nor does it represent the Silla perspective on a campaign in which Silla forces played an essential coordinating role.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the inscription emphasize about the 660 campaign, and what does it leave out?
  2. Why might a victory inscription be carved so soon after a conquest, before its long-term outcome could be known?
  3. How does knowledge of the Baekje restoration movement change a student's reading of the inscription's confident, triumphant tone?
  4. What kinds of historical questions can a contemporary victory inscription answer, and what kinds of questions can it not answer on its own?
Historical Debate

Why Did Goguryeo Fall in 668 after Defeating Sui and Resisting Tang for Decades?

The question: Goguryeo successfully resisted the Sui campaigns of 598 and 612, forced a negotiated end to Sui hostilities in 614, and halted Tang Taizong's invasion at Ansi in 645. Given this long record of successful resistance against two of the most powerful states in East Asian history, historians must explain why the same kingdom's government ultimately fell to a renewed Tang-Silla effort in 668.

Interpretation A — Emphasis on Goguryeo's defensive strengths and their eventual erosion: This interpretation emphasizes that Goguryeo's fortress network, logistics-exploiting defensive strategy, and unified wartime leadership under Yeon Gaesomun had proven remarkably effective for decades, and that Goguryeo's fall resulted less from any inherent weakness than from the cumulative attrition of resources, manpower, and fortifications after nearly seventy years of intermittent warfare, combined with the loss of unified command following Yeon Gaesomun's death in 666.
Interpretation B — Emphasis on the changed strategic environment of the 660s: This interpretation emphasizes that the situation Goguryeo faced in the 660s differed fundamentally from earlier decades: Tang now benefited from an active Silla alliance applying simultaneous pressure from the south, Tang's own operational methods had improved considerably since the Sui era, and internal Goguryeo succession conflict after 666 produced significant defections that provided the Tang-Silla coalition with intelligence and support unavailable during the Sui wars or the 645 campaign.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: Chinese dynastic histories describing Tang and Silla military operations, the later Samguk Sagi's account of Goguryeo's internal politics and the succession conflict among Yeon Gaesomun's sons, and the broader chronological pattern of escalating pressure on Goguryeo across the 640s through 660s.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily the weighting of causes: whether internal Goguryeo political fracture after 666 was the decisive proximate cause, without which coordinated external pressure might still have failed as it had in 645, or whether the coordinated, two-front Silla-Tang strategy of the 660s represented a sufficiently changed external threat that Goguryeo's fall would likely have occurred even without the succession crisis, given the cumulative attrition of decades of warfare.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these causes shapes broader judgments about the nature of Goguryeo's fall: whether it should be understood primarily as a story of external conquest achieved through improved allied coordination, or primarily as a story of internal political collapse that a more unified Goguryeo leadership might conceivably have avoided or postponed.

Student task: Using the chronology and evidence presented in Sections 13.9 through 13.15, construct an argument for which interpretation you find more persuasive, or explain why a combination of both interpretations offers the most complete explanation. Identify what kind of additional evidence, if it existed, could help resolve the disagreement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Warfare in 612, 645, and 660–676

What Changed

  • Tang's operational approach in 645 reflected lessons drawn from Sui's failures, emphasizing more methodical fortress reduction and better-coordinated land and naval movements than the improvised elements of the 612 campaign
  • By 660, the character of the conflict had shifted from a single continental power invading the peninsula toward a genuinely coalition-based form of warfare, with Silla and Tang forces coordinating land and naval movements against Baekje in a manner distinct from earlier Sui and Tang unilateral invasions of Goguryeo
  • The introduction of the Wa naval expedition at Baekgang in 663 extended the conflict's geography to include direct military engagement between peninsular and Japanese-archipelago forces, a development without a clear precedent earlier in this chapter's timeline
  • The Silla-Tang War of 670–676 represented a further transformation: a conflict between former allies fought substantially through Silla-supported local resistance and direct battlefield confrontation rather than a straightforward invasion of one unified enemy

What Stayed the Same

  • Fortress defense and the logistical vulnerability of invading armies operating far from home remained central to the outcome of campaigns throughout the period, from the Sui failures of 598–614 through Tang's failure at Ansi in 645
  • Naval operations, whether Sui's failed coastal expedition of 598, the Tang-Silla coordination of 660, or the climactic engagements at Baekgang in 663 and Gibeolpo in 676, remained a persistent and often decisive element of the conflict throughout its nearly eight decades
  • Internal political conditions within the combatant states, including Sui's domestic unrest, Goguryeo's succession politics, and the underlying tension within the Silla-Tang alliance, repeatedly shaped military outcomes as much as battlefield tactics did

Why the pattern matters: Tracing continuity and change across these campaigns shows that the wars of 598–676 were not a single uniform conflict but a series of related yet distinct confrontations, each shaped by lessons learned, alliances formed and broken, and shifting strategic circumstances, rather than a simple repeated contest between the same two sides.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Silla-Tang Alliance Become the Silla-Tang War?

Long-term causes

Silla's exposed strategic position between hostile Baekje and Goguryeo neighbors created a deep and lasting incentive to seek a powerful external ally, while Tang's own long-standing hierarchical assumptions about its relations with neighboring states created an underlying expectation of eventual direct administrative authority over any territory it helped conquer.

Short-term causes

Later Silla sources preserve a claim that the 648 alliance included a territorial understanding promising Silla the Baekje lands and territory south of Pyongyang once the common enemies were defeated. Whatever the exact terms and how each side understood them, the rapid, jointly achieved conquests of Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668 left both allies in direct control of overlapping former enemy territory.

Immediate trigger

Tang's establishment of direct administrative institutions, the Ungjin Commandery over former Baekje territory and the Andong Protectorate over former Goguryeo lands, sharply conflicted with Silla's postwar expectations and signaled an intention to govern the conquered territories itself, prompting Silla to support local resistance against Tang authority.

Effect

Escalating friction between 668 and 670 developed into open warfare, culminating in Silla's decisive victories at Maeso in 675 and Gibeolpo in 676, after which Tang withdrew its main forces and effectively abandoned its ambition of direct rule over Silla-controlled territory.

Historical complexity: Reducing this outcome to a single cause, such as simple Tang betrayal or simple Silla ambition, overlooks the reality that both allies pursued genuine and, in crucial respects, incompatible postwar goals, goals whose precise terms are known to us chiefly through later Silla tradition and that had never required full resolution so long as a common enemy remained undefeated.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Human Cost of Nearly Eight Decades of Warfare

Behind the sieges, alliances, and named commanders discussed throughout this chapter lay the accumulated experience of ordinary people across Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, Sui, Tang, and the Wa polity, whose lives were repeatedly disrupted by the demands of sustained warfare. Conscription drew farmers and laborers away from agricultural work for months or years at a time, particularly burdensome for Sui and Tang populations required to sustain campaign after campaign across enormous distances, and for Goguryeo communities whose men were repeatedly called to garrison and defend the fortress network examined in Section 13.3.

Fortress warfare imposed its own distinctive burdens on civilian populations, who were frequently drawn into the defensive effort directly: withdrawing behind fortress walls together with their stored grain and livestock ahead of an approaching invasion, as Goguryeo's defensive strategy required, meant abandoning fields and homes to the enemy for the duration of a siege, with no guarantee that either the fortress or the surrounding farmland would survive the campaign intact. Populations in the path of the great 612 Sui campaign, the 645 Tang invasion, and the 660 conquest of Baekje experienced displacement, requisition of food and livestock, and, in the aftermath of conquest, incorporation into new political and administrative structures not of their choosing.

The conquest of Baekje in particular produced significant population displacement and captivity: members of the royal family and court were taken to the Tang capital, while common people in contested regions faced years of continued fighting during the restoration movement, further disrupting agricultural cycles and community life in the Geum River region. Families throughout the affected kingdoms, meanwhile, faced the loss of sons and husbands to a conflict that, from the perspective of any single generation living through it, showed no clear end for decades at a time, a reminder that the grand strategic narrative traced in this chapter rested upon an immense and unevenly distributed burden of human suffering and disruption across all the societies involved.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced nearly eight decades of warfare that fundamentally reordered the political landscape of the Korean peninsula and Northeast Asia, beginning with the reunification of China under the Sui dynasty in 589 and concluding with the effective end of the Silla-Tang War in 676. Throughout this period, the chapter has emphasized a central interpretive commitment: the outcomes of 668 and 676 resulted from a complex and shifting combination of imperial ambition, fortress defense, logistical constraint, internal political conflict, changing alliances, and human contingency, not from the inevitable unfolding of any single kingdom's destiny.

The chapter opened by examining how Sui's reunification of China transformed Goguryeo's strategic environment, producing the wars of 598 and, far more consequentially, 612, in which Sui's massive invasion collapsed during a catastrophic retreat traditionally associated with the Salsu River. It emphasized that Goguryeo's remarkable defensive success rested on a sophisticated fortress network exploiting the region's geography, and that later heroic narratives surrounding the commander Eulji Mundeok, while resting on a genuine historical core, should be distinguished from the fuller set of logistical and strategic causes behind Sui's defeat. It further explained that Sui's collapse in 618 resulted from the interaction of the Goguryeo wars with broader domestic rebellion and fiscal strain, not from the Goguryeo wars alone.

The chapter then followed the transfer of the unresolved Goguryeo problem to the newly founded Tang dynasty, examining how internal Goguryeo politics, transformed by Yeon Gaesomun's 642 coup, intersected with Tang's revised military strategy to produce the major but ultimately unsuccessful invasion of 645, halted at the fortress of Ansi. It cautioned against uncritical acceptance of the later tradition naming Ansi's commander as Yang Manchun, a name absent from the earliest sources.

Turning to Silla, the chapter explained the genuine strategic desperation that drove Silla's pursuit of a Tang alliance, formalized in 648, and traced the swift and devastating conquest of Baekje in 660, while insisting that Baekje's collapse be understood through military and strategic factors rather than the moralizing tradition attributing it to King Uija's personal failings. It then examined the Baekje restoration movement of 660–663, led initially by the monk Dochim and the royal kinsman Boksin before internal factional killing weakened their leadership, a sustained and nearly successful effort to reverse the conquest that drew in a Wa naval expedition and was decisively broken at Baekgang before collapsing further with the subsequent fall of Juryuseong and remaining resistance centers, an engagement this chapter has deliberately framed without anachronistic modern national categories.

The chapter's central analytical achievement concerns the fall of Goguryeo in 668. Rather than attributing this outcome to any single cause, the chapter identified a convergence of factors: decades of cumulative wartime attrition, the loss of unified leadership following Yeon Gaesomun's death in 666, internal succession conflict and defection among his sons, and coordinated Silla-Tang military pressure applied from multiple directions simultaneously. It insisted, further, that the fall of Pyongyang in 668 must not be mistaken for a complete "unification of Korea," given Tang's establishment of its own administrative institutions over the conquered territory and the survival of substantial former Goguryeo populations and territory, particularly in Manchuria, beyond Silla's control.

Finally, the chapter traced how the wartime partnership between Silla and Tang collapsed into open warfare after 668, as Tang's establishment of the Ungjin Commandery and the Andong Protectorate over former Baekje and Goguryeo territory conflicted with a territorial understanding that later Silla tradition traced to the original 648 alliance, producing the Silla-Tang War of 670–676. Along the way, Silla's support for the Goguryeo restoration leaders Geom Mojam and Anseung, and the resulting Bodeok polity, illustrated how directly Silla mobilized former Goguryeo people and political identity against its former ally. Silla's decisive victories at Maeso in 675 and Gibeolpo in 676 brought about Tang's withdrawal and effectively consolidated Silla's control over much of the peninsula, while leaving the fate of the former Goguryeo north, and the populations who had constituted that kingdom, as an open question this textbook returns to in the remainder of Part III.

Taken together, the wars of 598 to 676 destroyed the Three Kingdoms political order examined throughout Part II without replacing it with a single unified Korean state encompassing all former Goguryeo and Baekje territory. This chapter's careful attention to logistics, internal politics, alliance formation and breakdown, and the limits of Silla's eventual consolidation provides the essential foundation for understanding the genuinely divided postwar landscape, Silla in the south and the eventual rise of Balhae in the north, that the later chapters of Part III examine.

A commemorative text, such as the 660 Tang inscription on the pacification of Baekje, erected by a victorious power to celebrate a military triumph, valuable as contemporary evidence but inherently one-sided.

A historical explanation that identifies multiple interacting causes for an event rather than attributing it to a single factor, the approach this chapter applies to Goguryeo's fall in 668.

A Tungusic-speaking people of Manchuria who at various points allied with Goguryeo, including during the events preceding the 598 war.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has told the story of nearly eight decades of warfare that destroyed the Three Kingdoms political order examined throughout Part II, without replacing it with the tidy, unified outcome that popular shorthand sometimes suggests. Goguryeo's repeated successful resistance to Sui campaigns, its successful defense at Ansi against Tang, Baekje's remarkable three-year restoration movement after the fall of its capital, and the eventual war between Silla and its own ally Tang all demonstrate a central truth this textbook returns to throughout its treatment of Korean history: political outcomes emerged from contingent, multicausal processes involving logistics, internal politics, shifting alliances, and human decision-making, not from the inevitable unfolding of any single kingdom's national destiny.

The wars examined here mattered enormously for what followed. Silla's consolidation of control over much of the peninsula by 676 shaped the political, cultural, and institutional world of Unified Silla, examined in the chapters immediately following this one. Equally significant, however, is what these wars did not accomplish: the survival of former Goguryeo territory, population, and political traditions beyond Silla's reach set the stage for the rise of Balhae, examined in Chapter 16, ensuring that the peninsula's postwar political landscape remained genuinely divided between two coexisting states rather than unified under Silla alone.

Students who carry forward this chapter's central lessons, that fortress warfare and logistics shaped outcomes as much as battlefield heroics, that the fall of a capital did not mean the end of a kingdom's political existence, that alliances formed for one strategic purpose could dissolve once that purpose was achieved, and that 668 and 676 produced a reordering rather than a unification, will be well equipped to understand the divided postwar peninsula examined in Part III and the long historical consequences of these wars that echo through the volumes that follow.

The story of Korea's ancient kingdoms, begun in Part I and carried through the Three Kingdoms and Gaya in Part II, reaches in this chapter, the opening chapter of Part III, one of its most consequential turning points. Understanding that turning point accurately, with all its contingency, incompleteness, and unresolved consequences, prepares students to engage seriously with the parallel history of Silla and Balhae that the remainder of Part III now takes up.
