Admiral Yi Sun-sin and the War at Sea

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1592, while Joseon's land armies were collapsing before the Japanese advance, a naval commander at the southwestern port of Yeosu spent the weeks before the invasion inspecting fortifications, testing cannon, and keeping a diary. The fleet he commanded would not stop the invasion. It would, however, change where and how Japan could fight it.

Chapter 32 ended with a kingdom in flight: Hanyang abandoned, Pyongyang lost, King Seonjo moving northward, and much of the conventional land defense broken within weeks of the Japanese landings at Busan. This chapter turns to a different theater, the southern sea, where the war developed along very different lines. There, in the channels and island-studded waters between the Jeolla coast and the southeastern port of Busan, a Joseon naval force won a series of engagements between May and October 1592 that disrupted Japanese plans and preserved the western approaches to the peninsula.

The central figure of this story is Yi Sun-sin (1545–1598), who in 1592 held the post of Jeolla Left Naval Commander. He later became, and remains, one of the most celebrated figures in Korean memory, and he is honored in Korea far more widely than any other military commander. That later fame creates a historical problem. A narrative built around a single admiral, a single ship type, and a handful of famous victories is easy to remember, but it hides the institutions, craftsmen, rowers, gunners, informants, and fellow commanders who made those victories possible, and it invites the mistaken inference that the war was decided at sea. This chapter takes the opposite approach. It treats Yi as a gifted and unusually well-documented commander operating inside a functioning regional naval system, and it asks what naval power actually accomplished.

The chapter follows the outline's specific assignment: the panokseon and geobukseon, shipboard artillery, naval logistics, the battles of Okpo and Hansan Island, maritime strategy, and the disruption of Japanese supply networks, all without reducing the entire war to naval action alone. The land war, the righteous armies, and the arrival of Ming forces are examined in Chapters 32 and 34, and the renewed invasion of 1597, including Yi's dismissal and return, Myeongnyang, and his death, belongs to Chapter 35. Here the narrative runs from the eve of the invasion to the consolidation of a naval defensive system in 1593.

The evidence for this campaign is unusually rich but uneven. Yi's own diary, Nanjung ilgi, and his formal battle reports to the court survive, as do the royal annals, the memoir of the chief minister Yu Seong-ryong, and later biographies. Japanese evidence is thinner and mostly reflects the viewpoint of commanders and daimyo houses, though Jesuit and other contemporary accounts add outside perspectives. Each kind of source has its own purpose and bias. Reports sent to the court sought to justify action and win rewards; the diary was a private record; Japanese retrospective accounts often explained away defeat. Sorting these perspectives is part of the task of the chapter.

The chapter also introduces the technology of the Joseon navy without treating it as a collection of wonder weapons. The panokseon, a sturdy multi-deck warship, was the standard fighting vessel of the fleet; the geobukseon, or turtle ship, was a specialized covered vessel built in small numbers. Both carried cannon, and the gunnery tradition behind them was a product of fifteenth- and sixteenth-century state arsenals discussed in connection with the science and technology of earlier Joseon in Chapter 28. Understanding the war at sea requires understanding that ships, guns, and sailors were embedded in a state with a tax base, a bureaucracy, and a coastal population.

Finally, the chapter asks a question of proportion. The naval victories of 1592 were real and consequential, yet Japanese armies remained in Korea, Joseon's capital and its northern cities fell, and the war continued for years. Naval power could deny Japan the free use of the western sea; it could not by itself expel Japanese armies from the peninsula. Seeing exactly what it did and did not do is the main analytical goal.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Japanese invasion of 1592 created a naval problem for Joseon as well as a land war, and why Jeolla Province was strategically central.
  2. Describe the organization of the Joseon naval system, including regional commands, commanders, crews, and coastal communities.
  3. Distinguish the panokseon from the geobukseon and explain why the former, not the latter, was the fleet's standard warship.
  4. Analyze the role of shipboard artillery in Joseon naval tactics and compare it with Japanese naval combat methods.
  5. Trace the major naval campaigns of 1592, including Okpo, Sacheon, Dangpo, Danghangpo, Hansan Island, Angolpo, and the attack on Busan.
  6. Assess the importance of intelligence, tides, island geography, and logistics to naval operations.
  7. Evaluate what naval victory changed strategically and what it did not change in the wider war.
  8. Compare Yi Sun-sin's private diary with official battle reports as historical sources.
  9. Interpret competing historical views of the relationship between naval victory and the war's outcome.
  10. Identify the roles of commanders other than Yi, including Yi Eok-gi and Won Gyun, and explain tensions within the naval command.
  11. Connect the naval campaigns to the land war of Chapter 32 and the multinational war of Chapter 34.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Joseon's naval forces perform so differently from its land armies in the opening months of the war?
  2. To what extent were Yi Sun-sin's victories the product of individual leadership, and to what extent the product of institutions, technology, and local support?
  3. Why was the panokseon more important to the war at sea than the famous geobukseon?
  4. How did geography, tides, and knowledge of local waters shape the choice of when and where to fight?
  5. What does the Battle of Hansan Island reveal about the relationship between tactics and terrain?
  6. What was Japan's maritime plan, and how did Korean naval success alter it?
  7. What can a private diary reveal that an official battle report cannot, and vice versa?
  8. How should historians weigh naval victory against land resistance and Ming intervention in explaining the course of the war?
  9. Why has Yi Sun-sin become such a powerful figure in modern memory, and how does that memory affect historical interpretation?

Historical Context

The invasion that began in the fourth lunar month of 1592 was planned by Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who had completed the military reunification of Japan only shortly before. Its stated aim was passage through Korea toward Ming China, and Chapter 31 examined the diplomacy and political setting that preceded it. For present purposes, the important feature of the plan is that it was a joint land and sea enterprise. Large armies were carried across the Korea Strait to Busan and the nearby coast, and they depended on shipping to move troops, food, and equipment. Contemporary Japanese planning contemplated that naval transport would support an advance up the west coast toward the northwest, so that the army marching overland from Busan toward Hanyang and Pyongyang could be supplemented by coastal shipping.

On the Joseon side, the southern coast was defended by regional naval commands, a system inherited from the early dynasty and adjusted over the centuries. The coast of Gyeongsang Province was divided between Left and Right naval commands, and Jeolla Province likewise had Left and Right commands. These forces were intended chiefly to counter coastal raiding, the wokou problem discussed in earlier chapters, rather than to fight a large-scale, organized invasion by sea. Over the two centuries of relative peace since the early Joseon wars against raiders, the navy had been neglected in some places. Yet in others, especially under commanders who took the Japanese threat seriously, preparations were made in the years before the war.

Geography is the third element of context. The southern and southwestern coast of the peninsula is one of the most complex maritime environments in East Asia. It has an irregular coastline, thousands of islands, narrow channels, strong tidal flows, and sheltered bays. The sea off the southwestern corner of the peninsula is dangerous for strangers, and knowledge of its channels belonged to coastal fishing populations and local naval personnel. Chapter 2 introduced this environment in general terms; here it becomes a working variable. A fleet that could use the waters well could fight at an advantage; a fleet entering them unprepared could lose its way, run aground, or be trapped.

Finally, the political situation shaped how naval operations were understood. The court, in flight, communicated with its commanders slowly and sometimes confusedly. Provincial naval officers had to decide, sometimes without orders, whether to cross provincial boundaries to fight. This autonomy mattered. It helps explain why Joseon's naval response was not a centrally planned campaign but a series of decisions by local commanders who then reported to a court trying to catch up.

Section 33.1

A Different War on the Southern Coast

The war at sea began as a catastrophe. When the Japanese fleet reached Busan in the fourth lunar month of 1592, the Gyeongsang naval commands that were supposed to meet it did not do so effectively. Much of the Gyeongsang Left Navy dissolved, and the Gyeongsang Right Navy under Won Gyun, based on Geoje Island, lost most of its ships and equipment as commanders withdrew, scuttled vessels, or abandoned bases. The reasons were partly military and partly political: officers faced an enemy larger than any they had been prepared for, they had little reliable information, and they feared that surviving ships and stores would fall into Japanese hands. The result was that, within days, the landing zone around Busan lay open and the southeastern sea was largely unguarded.

That collapse mattered because naval power and land power in this war were connected. The Japanese armies advancing northward were large, and they depended on supplies that would eventually have to come from somewhere. Japan's plan envisaged coastal shipping carrying men and material westward along the southern coast and then north along the western sea. That was the plan's weakness: it required sea control that Japan did not yet have. The part of the Joseon navy that survived lay west of the collapsed Gyeongsang commands, in Jeolla, and it held a coastline that Japan had to pass if its fleet was to move toward the west coast.

Jeolla itself was an economic prize. Its rice plains and river valleys were among the most productive agricultural lands of the peninsula, and the province would later become the principal supplier of provisions to Joseon's armies. Defending Jeolla meant preserving the grain base of the resistance as well as its western coast. This gave Jeolla's naval commanders an interest in acting in Gyeongsang waters: to protect their own province, they might have to fight beyond its boundary.

The difference between the two theaters should not be exaggerated. The sea war was not a separate and tidy contest; it took place in the same war and was shaped by the same court, the same shortages, and the same enemy. Yet it began from a different starting point. On land, the opening defeats were so rapid that the conventional army could not reassemble. At sea, the commands in the east collapsed but those in the west remained intact and were led by officers with time to prepare.

Section 33.2

Yi Sun-sin before the First Sortie

Yi Sun-sin was not a legend in 1592. He was a provincial naval commander of middle rank, aged forty-seven, with a respectable but not dazzling career. Born in 1545 into a family of the scholar-official class that had lost political advantage, he passed the military examination relatively late, in 1576, and served in a series of frontier and provincial posts, including service against Jurchen raiders on the northern border. His career was marked by episodes of conflict with superiors and by at least one dismissal and reinstatement, which reflect both his insistence on regulations and the factional atmosphere of the court examined in Chapter 31. In early 1591 he was appointed Jeolla Left Naval Commander, stationed at Yeosu.

What distinguishes him in the period before the war is what he did with the post. His diary, Nanjung ilgi, which begins in the first lunar month of 1592, records a sustained effort of inspection and repair: he examined fortifications and stores, punished officers who neglected their duties, drilled crews, and tested cannon. Entries in the weeks immediately before the invasion mention gunnery trials on the new covered warship that would become known as the geobukseon. The impression is of a commander applying existing regulations with unusual rigor rather than inventing a new system, and the diary's value lies partly in how ordinary these activities were. Inspection tours, equipment repair, and personnel discipline were the everyday business of any naval station.

Historians therefore treat the preparations as significant for institutional rather than heroic reasons. A navy that can fight well in the first weeks of a war is one whose ships have been maintained, whose crews are assembled, whose cannon work, and whose stores exist. These conditions were not guaranteed by Joseon's regulations; they depended on whether commanders enforced them. The collapse of the Gyeongsang commands and the survival of the Jeolla Left Navy show how much that enforcement varied. Part of Yi's later success should be traced to the plain fact that his fleet was ready to sail when the war began.

It should also be noted that Yi did not sail immediately. He knew the invasion had begun within days, but he waited, in part because his command was bounded by the jurisdiction of his own province and in part because he awaited orders and better information. Won Gyun asked for help, but Yi's initial caution is documented, and he moved only after further appeals and a clearer view of the situation. The caution is itself a reminder that the first victories were not automatic and that Joseon commanders faced a real strategic decision: to leave their own coast unguarded in order to fight in someone else's waters.

Section 33.3

The Joseon Naval System: Commanders, Crews, and Coastal Communities

The fleet that fought in 1592 was not the personal instrument of any one commander. It was the product of an administrative system, the suyeong naval-station network, that assigned ships, officers, and manpower to defined coastal districts. Each of the principal provincial naval commands maintained a headquarters and a collection of subordinate stations and fortified ports. The headquarters of the Jeolla Left Navy was at Yeosu; the Jeolla Right Navy was based farther west; the Gyeongsang Right Navy was based at Geoje. Naval commanders of the rank of Yi Sun-sin were military officials of the provincial administration, answering to the court through the Ministry of War and to higher provincial officials.

A warship needed far more people than its fighting complement. Large panokseon required scores of rowers, in addition to gunners, archers, helmsmen, sailors, officers, and attendants. Many of the rowers came from the naval service population, men whose households owed labor to the state and who were drawn disproportionately from the lower orders of society, including commoners and, in some circumstances, bondservants (nobi). Historians cannot reconstruct the social composition of each crew precisely, but the sources make clear that rowers, who did hard, dangerous, and poorly recorded work, are the largest and least visible part of the fleet's human composition. Their endurance, their discipline, and their readiness to row against tides and into fire were as necessary to naval success as any commander's judgment.

Coastal communities were more deeply involved than formal organization suggests. Fishing and shipbuilding households supplied labor, materials, and knowledge. Timber for hulls came from forests managed or requisitioned by the state, and the working of that timber relied on craftsmen who knew how to build a ship of the required strength. Provisions were collected from local districts. Information on Japanese movements arrived from fishermen, refugees, local officials, and scouts. The fleet's capacity to operate was therefore bound to the coastal population's willingness and ability to cooperate under extreme stress, and the sources record both cooperation and strain.

Command relationships were awkward. Yi Sun-sin, Yi Eok-gi, and Won Gyun were roughly equal in formal rank and commanded separate regional forces. Yi Eok-gi led the Jeolla Right fleet and, once he joined the campaign, cooperated with Yi Sun-sin in ways that appear to have worked well. Won Gyun's position was more difficult. His own command had collapsed, he commanded little, and he was senior or comparable in age and record. Official reports during 1592 usually credit Yi Sun-sin as the principal operational commander, though Won Gyun appeared at several battles. Later disputes between the two, which become important in Chapter 35, had roots in these circumstances: the credit, the reports, and the question of who was leading whom. The disputes should not be read back into every incident of 1592, but they warn against a picture of a perfectly unified navy.

Section 33.4

The Panokseon: The Fleet's Principal Warship

The standard battleship of the Joseon navy in 1592 was the panokseon, a large, robustly built, flat-bottomed vessel with a multi-deck arrangement. The name is generally explained as referring to its "board-roofed" upper structure. Its basic design had developed over the previous century and a half, as the Joseon state responded to coastal raiding and sought ships able to carry heavy firepower. Official Korean historical material emphasizes the multi-deck structure and the ability to carry artillery. The ship remained in service well after the Imjin War.

Several features are central to understanding its performance. First, the upper deck formed a broad platform on which fighting personnel and guns could be placed above the rowers, who worked below or protected by the structure. This separation allowed the fighting complement to concentrate on gunnery and archery without being displaced by rowing. Second, the flat-bottomed hull suited the shallow, tidal, island-filled waters of the Korean coast, where deep-keeled ocean-going vessels could be vulnerable. A flat bottom also allowed a ship to turn within a relatively small radius, aided by its rowers, an advantage in confined channels. Third, the thick hull timbers, commonly said to be of pine, gave good resistance to the small arms and light weapons of enemy vessels, though the precise thickness and construction varied.

The panokseon's fighting identity was gunnery. A typical ship carried cannon on its sides and, according to the sources, a mixture of larger and smaller pieces, along with archers and handgunners. In action, a flotilla of such ships could present a broadside to an enemy at a distance, firing heavy shot and arrows. The sources do not give a single reliable figure for crew size, number of guns, or dimensions, and modern reconstructions vary considerably, so students should treat claims of exact specifications skeptically. The important point is that the ship was designed to be a stable, protected gun platform capable of both maneuvering and sustained fire.

The ship also had weaknesses. It was large and relatively slow to maneuver in very constricted or reef-filled waters, a point of great importance at Hansan Island. It required a large crew and substantial logistic support. And it could be vulnerable to being boarded if an enemy closed to short range, since Japanese forces were skilled in close combat. Joseon tactics were built around preventing this, and the account of the battles shows repeated efforts to fight at a distance of the commander's choosing.

Historical Vocabulary
Panokseon and Geobukseon

Definition. The panokseon was Joseon's standard multi-deck warship, a broad, flat-bottomed vessel that served as a heavily armed gun platform. The geobukseon ("turtle ship") was a specialized warship with a covered upper deck and cannon ports, built in small numbers.

Historical context. Both types were products of a state arsenal and shipbuilding system that had developed to counter coastal raiders and to defend Joseon waters. They served together in the 1592 campaigns, with the panokseon forming the main body of the fleet.

Why it matters. Understanding the difference corrects a common error in popular memory, in which the turtle ship is imagined as the main weapon of Yi Sun-sin's navy. In fact, the principal fighting force was composed of panokseon.

Potential misunderstanding. The geobukseon is sometimes described as the "world's first ironclad." That claim is a later popular one that is not supported by the contemporary evidence presented in this chapter and should be treated with great caution.

Section 33.5

Geobukseon: Innovation, Reconstruction, and Myth

The geobukseon is among the most famous ships in world naval history, and it is also one of the most surrounded by legend. The basic concept was not new to Yi Sun-sin. Joseon sources from the early fifteenth century mention a covered warship called a turtle ship, and Korean historical material describes the vessel used in 1592 as a modified development of that earlier idea rather than something created from nothing. What Yi's forces appear to have done, with their shipwrights, was refine the design and build working examples in the period before the invasion.

The descriptions that survive agree on several points. The ship's upper deck was covered by a protective roof, which sheltered the crew from arrows, small arms, and boarding. The roof is commonly described as bearing projecting spikes, intended to deter boarding. The sides had ports for cannon, and a figurehead, often described as dragon-headed, served as a gun position and, in some accounts, as a place from which smoke could be emitted. The ship was rowed, and its role was to approach enemy formations at close range, firing cannon from protected positions and disrupting lines of ships.

Reconstruction beyond these points is uncertain. The surviving written descriptions are incomplete, written for different purposes, and do not agree on details such as the exact shape of the roof, the number and placement of ports, the degree to which the roof was covered with metal, and the dimensions of the hull. Later drawings and modern models rest on interpretation and sometimes on imagination. Historians have also debated whether the dragon head and spike details were typical of every ship or of particular examples. These uncertainties are reasons for caution, not reasons to dismiss the ship, since the contemporary record leaves no doubt that such vessels existed and fought.

The ship's tactical role was specialized. Its first combat use is generally associated with the battle at Sacheon in the fifth lunar month. In the battles that followed it appears as an assault vessel, entering enemy formations and drawing fire while the larger body of panokseon concentrated their artillery. Yet the number of turtle ships in service seems to have been small, perhaps a few at any one time, and it would be a serious distortion to imagine fleets of them. The ship's historical significance lies in how it complemented the main fleet and in the Joseon naval system's capacity to experiment, not in any claim that it won the war. The legend of the turtle ship is a good example of how a single striking object can dominate memory at the expense of the less visible but more important institutions behind it.

Section 33.6

Cannon, Arquebuses, and Two Ways of Fighting at Sea

Joseon's naval advantage in 1592 is usually attributed in large part to artillery. The Korean tradition of gunpowder weapons extended back to the late Goryeo period, when the manufacture of gunpowder and cannon began to be organized, and the early Joseon state maintained arsenals that produced a range of pieces. Korean ships carried cannon known collectively as chongtong, graded by size and named with the characters of the Thousand Character Classic, such as cheonja, jija, hyeonja, and hwangja in descending order of size. Larger pieces fired heavy projectiles, including large arrow-like shot and iron or stone balls, while smaller pieces served against personnel at shorter range. Korean historical material attributes part of the navy's advantage to the substantial cannon mounted on its warships.

Artillery changed the character of naval combat. A cannon-armed ship could damage an enemy vessel from a distance, before enemy soldiers could board. The effects were not always decisive: the cannon were muzzle-loading, reloading was slow, accuracy was limited, and range, though greater than that of Japanese shipboard firearms, was not unlimited. A ship's fire was most effective when guns were concentrated, when the enemy was bunched together, and when the target ships could not easily escape. Ammunition, powder, and trained gun crews were therefore key logistical and human requirements. The state's arsenal system, which provided the guns, and the commanders' discipline, which ensured that crews were trained, were as important as the guns themselves.

Japanese naval forces fought differently. Their strength lay in the combination of arquebuses and close-quarters combat. Japanese commanders, many of whom came from daimyo domains with seafaring experience, relied on swift ships carrying large bodies of fighting men, who could close, grapple, and board. This method had worked well against less prepared opponents and was formidable when a ship could be brought alongside. The Japanese vessels of the 1592 campaign were generally lighter and, in the case of the type commonly used for transport and assault, less heavily built than the panokseon and less able to carry heavy guns. This suited them for rapid movement and landings but left them vulnerable in a fight at a distance.

The key tactical difference, then, was not that one side had a modern navy and the other a medieval one, a caricature that does not survive examination. Each side developed methods suited to its resources and its expected enemy. Joseon commanders sought to keep the fight at long range, using heavy guns to damage ships and kill crews before boarding could occur. Japanese commanders sought to close the distance and turn the engagement into a boarding action. Much of the history of the campaigns of 1592 is the history of whether Joseon commanders could impose their preferred mode of fighting, and whether Japanese commanders could avoid it. The ships were weapons platforms, not merely transports, and the contest was largely about who controlled the range and the geometry of the engagement.

Section 33.7

Okpo: The First Naval Victory

Yi Sun-sin's first sortie began on the fourth day of the fifth lunar month, according to the official Korean account, when his fleet left Yeosu. The delay since the beginning of the invasion deserves attention. Yi had received appeals from Won Gyun for help, but he needed to consider the safety of his own coast and to ascertain the situation in Gyeongsang waters. Once he decided to move, he took a fleet of panokseon, along with smaller supporting vessels, eastward, and joined with the remnants of Won Gyun's force.

The first target was a group of Japanese ships around Okpo on the northeastern coast of Geoje Island, where Japanese forces had been plundering the area. The fighting at Okpo occurred on the seventh day of the fifth lunar month, according to Korean accounts. Joseon ships attacked anchored Japanese vessels with artillery and fire arrows and destroyed a number of them; the sources give figures for ships destroyed, but these come from official reports that sought to demonstrate success, and their exactness should not be assumed. The following day the fleet continued the campaign with attacks at Happo and Jeokjinpo, where further Japanese ships were destroyed. The campaign therefore consisted of a series of actions against ships anchored near the coast, not a single set-piece fleet battle.

Okpo and the other actions of the first sortie were important out of proportion to their military scale. They occurred a few weeks after the land defeats that had led to the loss of the capital, and they showed that a Joseon force could beat Japanese forces in a fight. The news of the first successes helped sustain morale at the court and among commanders still in the field. But the victory was local. The ships destroyed were a small fraction of Japanese strength, and Japanese armies were meanwhile moving rapidly northward. The first sortie established a pattern: concentrated Joseon naval power could strike exposed enemy ships, withdraw to base, and reassemble.

For students, one source-critical point deserves emphasis. Yi's diary, which a reader might expect to describe this famous first victory in vivid detail, does not preserve an account of Okpo. The surviving manuscript's entries for the relevant period are missing, and the first naval battle that appears in the surviving diary is the one at Sacheon on the twenty-ninth day of the fifth lunar month. What is known about Okpo therefore comes principally from official reports and later sources, not from the diary. This fact is a useful reminder that even a famous firsthand source does not document every famous event.

Section 33.8

Sacheon, Dangpo, and Danghangpo: Learning How to Fight the Naval War

The second sortie began on the twenty-ninth day of the fifth lunar month, before Yi Eok-gi had arrived. Yi Sun-sin had asked the Jeolla Right commander to join him by the third day of the sixth month, but the emergency at Sacheon forced Yi to sail earlier. He therefore fought Sacheon and Dangpo together with Won Gyun. Yi Eok-gi's Jeolla Right fleet joined on the fourth day of the sixth month, expanding the combined force before Danghangpo on the fifth. The first major action was at Sacheon on the twenty-ninth day of the fifth lunar month. Japanese ships were anchored in the bay, and Joseon commanders, aware that the tide would limit the movement of their large ships in the narrow waters, used the ebb and flow to lure the Japanese out. The geobukseon is generally associated with its first wartime combat here, attacking and disrupting the Japanese formation, while panokseon fired on the ships. Yi himself was wounded by a shot in the shoulder, an incident recorded in his own diary and a reminder that commanders took the risks of battle.

The fleet then moved against other Japanese positions. At Dangpo, in the early part of the sixth lunar month, Joseon ships attacked a Japanese squadron and destroyed a large atake with a high tower that appears to have functioned as a command ship. At Danghangpo, a few days later, Yi again used reconnaissance and a luring maneuver to bring Japanese ships out of their protected anchorage into waters where the combined Joseon fleet, now including Yi Eok-gi's ships, could attack them more effectively. The fighting here and in the following days, including operations at Yulpo, completed the sortie.

These actions show a pattern more important than any single battle. First, Yi's fleet depended on reconnaissance: it located the enemy before engaging, using scouts, local informants, and the fleet's own light vessels. Second, it preferred to attack ships anchored or moored near the coast, where they could be caught while their crews were ashore or unready. Third, when the enemy was in a protected position, the commanders tried to draw ships out into more open water where large ships could maneuver. Fourth, the fleet concentrated its artillery and avoided unnecessary exposure, and when Japanese ships were burnt or driven ashore, commanders often withdrew rather than push into the unknown. The pattern reflects a combination of caution and boldness: aggressive in selecting targets, conservative in avoiding situations in which the Japanese could use their boarding strengths.

The campaign also had consequences for Japanese commanders. Losses at Okpo, Sacheon, Dangpo, and the other actions meant that Japanese naval forces could no longer assume that the Korean coast was undefended. The ships destroyed were largely transports and light warships, not the entire invading fleet, but the effect on Japanese confidence and planning was significant. By the middle of the summer the Japanese were preparing a larger naval concentration to deal with the Korean fleet, setting the stage for Hansan.

Section 33.9

Intelligence, Tides, Islands, and Naval Logistics

Naval warfare is often imagined as battle, but for commanders it was mostly preparation, information, and supply. The campaigns of 1592 illustrate this clearly. Before any battle, Yi's fleet had to learn where the enemy was, how many ships he had, what waters he occupied, and whether the conditions favored a fight. Sources of information were varied. Fishermen and coastal residents who had fled from Japanese-occupied areas reported on enemy movements; scouts in small boats observed anchorages; local officials sent messages; and the fleet itself conducted reconnaissance before committing the main force. This network was possible because the navy was embedded in a coastal society whose members knew the waters and whose loyalties, though not guaranteed, generally lay with Joseon.

Knowledge of the physical environment was equally important. The tidal ranges and currents of the southern coast, especially in narrow channels, determined when ships could pass and when they would be stranded or swept off course. Anchorages had to be chosen for shelter and for access to fresh water. Reefs and shoals could trap deep-hulled ships; islands could conceal fleets or provide observation posts. The commanders used the daily and monthly tidal rhythm in their planning, and the lunar-calendar dates given in the sources are not incidental, because lunar phase governed tides. Japanese commanders, newly arrived, lacked this local knowledge and relied on their ability to learn or to take local guides.

Logistics, in turn, determined how long and how far a fleet could operate. Rowing crews needed food and water. Gunners needed powder and shot. Hulls needed repair after battle, and damage from fire, collision, or enemy shot had to be fixed at a base before ships could sail again. Naval operations could not extend beyond the distance the fleet could support from its bases. This logistic constraint explains the pattern of sorties from Yeosu: a campaign of days or weeks, followed by return to base to rest, repair, and resupply. It also explains why holding a forward base, such as Hansan Island in 1593, mattered. The capacity to remain at sea was a strategic resource as much as a tactical one.

For Japan, the logistic problem was reversed. A fleet carrying armies across the Korea Strait needed ports, secure anchorages, and protected routes. When Joseon forces attacked ships anchored along the coast, or threatened sea lanes, Japan faced a choice between committing more naval strength to the protection of its supply lines and relying more heavily on overland routes from Busan. The ability to threaten the sea route did not require annihilating the Japanese fleet; it required making movement dangerous and costly.

Section 33.10

Hansan Island and the Battle of Open Water

By the seventh lunar month, Japanese commanders had resolved to confront the Joseon fleet directly. Japanese naval units, including a squadron under Wakizaka Yasuharu, were assembled in the Geoje area, and other Japanese naval forces were gathering. Wakizaka completed his preparations first and sailed before Kuki Yoshitaka and Kato Yoshiaki were ready. Some later accounts attribute his decision partly to competition for military distinction, but the secure point is that he engaged before the Japanese forces had concentrated. Meanwhile, Yi Sun-sin concentrated his own fleet. The ships of the Jeolla Left, those of Yi Eok-gi's Jeolla Right, and those of Won Gyun's Gyeongsang Right assembled in the waters off the southern coast, and the combined fleet moved to engage.

Joseon scouts located the Japanese fleet in the area of Gyeonnaeryang, the narrow strait between Geoje Island and the mainland. Yi's judgment, described in the official Korean sources, was that this channel was unfavorable. It was narrow, studded with reefs, and unsuitable for the maneuvers of his large panokseon. A fleet attacking in such waters risked collision, grounding, and being drawn into the close combat that favored the enemy. Instead of attacking, Yi decided to lure the Japanese into more open water off Hansan Island, where his ships would have room to maneuver.

The battle took place on the eighth day of the seventh lunar month, in mid-August 1592. A detachment of Joseon ships feigned retreat, and the Japanese, pursuing what looked like a weaker or withdrawing force, followed out of the strait and into the open sea. When the Japanese ships were in the open water, the Joseon fleet turned and deployed in a formation traditionally called hakikjin, the "crane wing" formation, in which the ships formed a broad curved line that could envelop the pursuing enemy. The Japanese fleet found itself under concentrated fire from artillery on several ships, with limited opportunity to close and board. Japanese ships were destroyed or burnt in large numbers, and survivors fled. Wakizaka himself escaped but suffered heavy losses.

Students should be careful with the hakikjin. Later retellings sometimes treat it as a mysterious or magical formation that automatically produced victory. The evidence supports a more modest and more interesting reading. The formation was a way of organizing a fleet in open water so that its ships could bring artillery to bear on an enemy that had advanced in a column or loose pursuit. What mattered was the combination of choosing the field, drawing the enemy out of confined waters, and using concentrated fire at a distance. The formation's name has become famous, but the underlying principle of controlling the geometry of the engagement was the real tactical achievement. The accounts of the battle also vary in detail, and numbers of ships and casualties reported in sources should be treated cautiously.

The strategic effect was large. Japanese records and Korean official histories agree that the defeat damaged Japanese naval power and altered their plans. Korean historical material notes that Japan had originally intended to advance by land and sea, and that Japanese naval forces were prevented from moving freely into the western sea. After Hansan, Hideyoshi is generally reported to have ordered his commanders to avoid further engagements with the Korean fleet, and Japanese forces turned toward defensive coastal positions. The battle did not end the war, but it significantly narrowed Japan's options at sea.

Section 33.11

Angolpo and the Limits of Tactical Victory

Hansan did not conclude the campaign. Two days later, on the tenth day of the seventh lunar month, Yi's fleet found another Japanese force at Angolpo, a bay on the Gyeongsang coast to the east. Here the Japanese ships, including those under Kuki Yoshitaka and Kato Yoshiaki, had not repeated Wakizaka's mistake. They stayed in a sheltered anchorage and did not attempt a pursuit. The circumstances differed from Hansan: the enemy was in protected, shallower waters, and the tide and the channel limited the Joseon ships' room for maneuver.

Yi adapted. Instead of pressing straight into the bay, he rotated attacking groups of ships to fire upon the Japanese in turn, tried again to draw them out, and in the end destroyed a number of Japanese vessels, though many Japanese ships and men escaped, abandoning their ships and fleeing ashore. The result was a victory but not an annihilation. It is worth noting because it demonstrates that Japanese commanders learned from the earlier defeat. It also shows that geography, which had helped Yi at Hansan, could limit him when the enemy held a protected position.

The lesson for students is that naval victory was not a procession of effortless triumphs. Each battle was shaped by circumstances: the location of the enemy, the state of the tide, the nature of the waters, and the commanders' willingness to take or avoid risk. Yi's success was partly a record of choosing when not to fight, when to withdraw, and when to break off a pursuit. The Angolpo action also anticipates the Japanese response: seeing that open engagement against the Korean fleet was costly, they would soon turn to defending positions rather than seeking battle.

Section 33.12

The Attack on Busan: Striking the Japanese Maritime Base

The largest operation of the campaign came at the beginning of the ninth lunar month, around the first week of October 1592. By then the Joseon fleet, now combining ships from the Jeolla and Gyeongsang commands, moved toward Busan, the principal Japanese port of entry and shipping base. The target was different from earlier ones. Instead of isolated squadrons, Busan held a large concentration of Japanese ships, many of them transports, together with shore positions and defenses. Attacking it meant moving against the heart of the Japanese maritime system.

The operation was bold. The Joseon fleet reached the waters off Busan and attacked Japanese ships in the harbor, destroying a substantial number. But the Japanese did not come out to fight a conventional open-water battle. They fought from protected positions and from the shore, using land defenses and firearms to counter the attackers. The sources report that Joseon forces suffered losses, including the death of the commander Jeong Un, a significant officer who fell in the fighting. After causing damage, the fleet withdrew. The operation was a significant strike against Japan's logistical base, though not the capture of Busan.

This needs emphasis because later memory sometimes misstates it. Yi did not "liberate Busan" in 1592; Busan remained in Japanese hands, and Japanese forces would retain positions in the southeast for years. What the attack accomplished was to demonstrate that even Japan's main base could be attacked from the sea, to destroy ships and stores, and to reinforce the message that Japanese maritime movement was dangerous. It was a raid on a base, not an amphibious reconquest, because the Joseon fleet had no army to land and hold the ground. Without land forces to follow up, naval power could damage but not retake the port.

Section 33.13

What Naval Victory Actually Changed

It is now possible to assess what the naval campaigns accomplished. Their effects were substantial and can be stated in specific terms. First, they blocked or severely constrained Japanese westward naval movement. A Japanese fleet that could not safely pass Jeolla's coast could not carry troops and supplies along the western sea. Second, they protected the Jeolla coast and with it access to one of the peninsula's most important agricultural regions, whose grain would later be critical to Joseon's armies. Third, they made the envisioned combined land and sea advance toward the west and north far more difficult. Fourth, they threatened Japanese coastal transport and communications, forcing the Japanese to commit more resources to defending shipping. Fifth, they pushed Japanese forces to rely more heavily on protected bases, coastal fortifications, and land communications. Sixth, they bought time for Joseon resistance to reorganize.

That list is significant, but it is also bounded. Naval victory did not sever every Japanese supply connection to Busan; Japanese ships continued to cross the Korea Strait, and the southeastern coast remained a Japanese zone of operations. It did not eject Japanese armies from Korea. It did not recapture Hanyang or Pyongyang, which remained in Japanese hands for the time being, as the following chapter shows. And it did not replace the contributions of the local righteous armies, the guerrilla resistance of ordinary Koreans, or the intervention of Ming China. The war was too large and too varied for a single theater to decide it.

Historians therefore describe the naval effect as one of denial rather than conquest. Joseon's fleet could deny Japan the free use of a sea route; it could not by itself command the entire sea or compel a Japanese withdrawal. This difference between sea denial and total command of the sea is one of the most useful concepts for understanding the war. Japan's strategic problem was not that its armies lacked strength but that the supplementary naval part of its plan failed, leaving the armies more dependent on long overland lines through hostile territory. That dependence became a crucial weakness over the following year, but it operated alongside other pressures.

Section 33.14

From Mobile Campaigns to a Maritime Defensive System, 1593

After the autumn of 1592, the character of the naval war changed. Japanese commanders, having suffered repeated defeats at sea, generally avoided fleet engagements and strengthened their positions on the southeastern coast, building fortified bases of the kind Japanese sources call wajo. The Joseon fleet, for its part, continued to apply pressure around Busan and the nearby coast. Korean historical material notes renewed operations around Busan and Ungcheon in 1593, where the navy again attacked, though Japanese forces held protected positions and the results were limited.

The more important development was institutional. In the seventh lunar month of 1593, Yi moved his headquarters from Yeosu to Hansan Island, a position much closer to the Japanese in the southeast, from which his fleet could watch the strait, guard the approaches to the west, and respond quickly to movements. Soon afterward, the court created a unified naval command, and Yi was appointed Three Provinces Naval Commander, with authority over the naval forces of Jeolla, Gyeongsang, and Chungcheong. The appointment ended the awkward division of command among regional commanders and recognized in formal terms the coordinating role Yi had already played.

The move to Hansan marks a transition from mobile raids to a sustained maritime defensive system. The fleet no longer simply sortied and returned; it maintained a forward base, organized logistics and manpower for a long period, and built supporting facilities. This was not a spectacular change, and it produced no famous battle, but it stabilized the situation in the south. This chapter stops here. The dismissal of Yi Sun-sin, the disaster at Chilcheollyang, his return, the victory at Myeongnyang, the death of Hideyoshi, and the final naval campaign belong to Chapter 35, which examines the renewed invasion of 1597. Between this chapter and that one, Chapter 34 turns to the righteous armies, the Buddhist fighters, and Ming intervention that transformed the war on land.

Historical Vocabulary
Sugun, Suyeong, and Chongtong

Definition. Sugun means "water army," the Joseon term for naval forces. A suyeong was a naval station or command headquarters. Chongtong was the general term for cannon and gunpowder tubes, with named grades such as cheonja and jija.

Historical context. These terms describe the organizational and technological basis of the navy: regional commands with defined districts, fortified harbors, and a state arsenal that produced the guns carried on ships.

Why it matters. The terms remind students that the 1592 victories drew on state institutions and manufacturing, not on a single commander's improvisation.

Primary Source Window

Yi Sun-sin's Nanjung ilgi (War Diary), 1592

Creator: Yi Sun-sin · Date: kept from the first lunar month of 1592 onward during the war · Type: Private daily diary by a military commander

Context: The diary was kept by Yi as a personal record during his service. It is made up of short daily entries noting weather, inspections, visitors, military business, health, and personal feelings, and it continued through later years of the war. It survives in manuscript and was not written as an official report.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of the diary's character, not a quotation.

Entries from the weeks before the invasion record inspections of fortifications and equipment, the testing of cannon, and the state of the weather. Entries after the fighting begins note the movement of ships, the course of engagements, and, for the action at Sacheon, that Yi was struck by a shot in the shoulder.

Analysis: The diary shows a commander engaged in the detail of preparation and administration. It reveals the routine character of the work behind the famous victories and lets historians see the commander's private concerns, which are absent from official reports.

Limitations: The surviving diary does not preserve an account of the battle at Okpo; the first naval battle appearing in the surviving text is Sacheon. It also tells us only what Yi chose to write, and it says little about the experiences of rowers, gunners, and coastal communities.

Perspective: The source represents a senior officer's private viewpoint. Perspectives not represented include those of ordinary crews, Japanese commanders, and the court. A useful comparison is with the formal battle reports (janggye) Yi sent to the court, which were written for an audience that evaluated and rewarded his conduct.

  1. What does the diary record, and what kinds of activities does it emphasize?
  2. Who created it, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped a private diary, as opposed to a report to the court?
  4. What can historians learn about preparation and command from it?
  5. What cannot be established from the diary alone, including the matter of Okpo?
Historical Debate

Did Naval Victory Change the Outcome of the Imjin War?

The question: How much did Joseon's naval victories determine the course and outcome of the war?

Interpretation A — The hero-centered view: Older and popular accounts, especially influential in modern Korean memory, present Yi Sun-sin's victories as the decisive factor that saved the kingdom by cutting Japanese supply lines and ending any hope of conquest.
Interpretation B — The maritime-logistics view: Some historians stress that Japan's plan depended on sea transport, so denial of the western sea strained the Japanese army and shaped the strategic situation, even though it did not sever every supply route.
Interpretation C — The integrated view: Many scholars emphasize that naval power worked together with the survival of the Joseon state, the righteous armies and local resistance, and Ming intervention, so that no single factor can be credited with the outcome.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on Korean official reports and histories, Yi's diary, Japanese commanders' records and orders, Ming and Joseon diplomatic and military records, and modern studies of logistics.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale and causation, namely how severely Japanese logistics were affected, and interpretation, namely how to weigh naval against land factors.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether the war is understood as a naval story, a land story, or a multinational one, and how Yi's place in memory is related to the historical record.

Student task: Identify evidence that would help test each interpretation, such as records of Japanese supply shortages, and explain which interpretation the evidence in this chapter best supports.

Multiple Perspectives

Hansan Island Seen from Different Decks

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from any named person.

The Joseon fleet commander: Hansan was a calculated decision about terrain and timing: an unfavorable strait avoided, an enemy lured into open water, and a formation used to concentrate artillery. For the commander, victory depended on judgment and preparation.
A Joseon rower or gunner: The battle meant physical exertion, danger under fire, and obedience to orders in a large, coordinated maneuver. Such participants left almost no record of their own, and their experience is reconstructed from indirect evidence.
A Japanese commander: The advance into the open sea was an attempt to destroy the Korean fleet and win credit among rival commanders. Defeat taught that Korean heavy guns and open-water maneuver could overcome the Japanese advantage in boarding.
A coastal villager: The battle meant both danger and relief. Coastal residents who had suffered plunder were affected by the outcome, and some contributed information and labor to the fleet.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Joseon Navy from Peacetime to War

What Changed

  • The navy's mission shifted from countering coastal raiding to fighting an organized invasion fleet
  • Provincial commands moved from separate operation toward coordinated action and, in 1593, unified command
  • Operations moved from base-bound defense to mobile campaigns and then to a forward base at Hansan

What Stayed the Same

  • The state arsenal, shipbuilding system, and naval-station network remained the institutional base
  • The panokseon remained the standard warship
  • Rowing labor and coastal communities continued to supply the manpower and local knowledge

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven and came through adaptation within existing institutions. The shock of invasion exposed how differently commands had been maintained and made improvisation, coordination, and logistics matters of urgency.

Cause and Effect

Why the Japanese Fleet Could Not Move Freely into the Western Sea

Long-term causes

A state arsenal and shipbuilding tradition producing heavily armed ships, a naval-station network, and coastal knowledge built up over generations of defense against raiders.

Short-term causes

Prewar preparation in the Jeolla Left Navy, intact Jeolla fleets, local intelligence, and commanders willing to cross provincial boundaries.

Immediate trigger

The invasion and the collapse of Gyeongsang naval defenses, which left Jeolla's navy as the main obstacle to a Japanese move westward.

Effects

Japanese ships were destroyed at Okpo, Sacheon, Hansan, and elsewhere; Japanese commanders avoided open engagement; the westward naval plan was checked and the army relied more on land lines.

Historical complexity: Naval denial was one factor among several. It did not end Japanese supply by sea in the southeast or remove Japanese armies, and its effects interacted with local resistance, the Joseon court's survival, and Ming involvement.

Everyday Life and Social History

The People Who Made a Fleet

A fleet needed carpenters, caulkers, smiths, gunpowder makers, cooks, cargo handlers, messengers, and, above all, rowers. Many came from households that owed labor service to the state. Rowers pulled heavy oars for long hours, often in tidal currents, and were exposed to enemy fire, disease, hunger, and exhaustion. The sources, written by officers and officials, say little about their individual experiences, but occasional references to desertion, sickness, and discipline suggest the pressures they faced.

Coastal households endured the war directly. Villages near the Japanese-held areas suffered plunder and flight, and refugees crowded onto islands. At the same time, fishermen and coastal people provided information that guided the fleet. For the households that supplied provisions and labor, the navy was both a protector and an additional burden, since grain, timber, and manpower were demanded from communities already strained by war. Because most evidence comes from elite writers, historians must read official documents and diaries for indirect traces of these ordinary experiences, supplemented by archaeology of ships and, for later periods, local traditions.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the war at sea in the first phase of the Imjin War, from the eve of the invasion in 1592 to the consolidation of a naval defensive system in 1593. Its central argument is that Joseon's naval success was real and strategically important but cannot be reduced to one commander, one ship, or a chain of dramatic battles, and that it did not by itself decide the war.

The war began with the collapse of the Gyeongsang naval commands, which left the Jeolla fleets as the main surviving naval force. That asymmetry between a broken eastern command and an intact western one was the starting condition for the entire campaign. It also gave the defense of Jeolla special strategic weight because of the province's agricultural importance and its position along the western sea route.

Yi Sun-sin, appointed Jeolla Left Naval Commander in 1591, appears in the sources as a rigorous administrator who inspected, repaired, trained, and tested the instruments of his command before the war. His preparations were significant not as heroics but as institutional maintenance that other commands lacked. His diary, the Nanjung ilgi, documents this routine side of command.

The fleet was an institution as well as a commander's tool. It rested on regional commands, naval stations, state arsenals, shipbuilders, rowers, gunners, fishermen, and local officials. Yi Eok-gi's fleet and Won Gyun's remnants joined Yi's forces, though relations among commanders were not free of friction.

The panokseon was the standard warship, a multi-deck, flat-bottomed gun platform suited to Korean waters, and the geobukseon was a specialized covered assault ship built in small numbers. Cannon gave Joseon ships the ability to fight at a distance, while Japanese forces were strong in arquebus fire and boarding. Much of the naval contest was a struggle over range and geometry.

The campaigns of 1592 followed a pattern. At Okpo, Sacheon, Dangpo, and Danghangpo, Joseon forces located the enemy, attacked anchored or coastal ships, drew them into favorable waters when possible, concentrated artillery, and withdrew rather than pursue recklessly. Intelligence from coastal communities, knowledge of tides and channels, and logistics from bases at Yeosu underlay these operations.

Hansan Island was the principal fleet battle. Yi judged the narrow Gyeonnaeryang channel unfavorable, lured the Japanese into open water, and used a broad formation to envelop them. The result checked Japanese naval ambitions and led Japanese commanders to avoid fleet engagements. Angolpo, however, showed that protected waters could limit a victory, and the attack on Busan showed that a fleet could strike a base but could not retake it without land forces.

The strategic consequences were considerable: constrained Japanese westward movement, protection of Jeolla, a more difficult combined advance, and time for Joseon to reorganize. Yet naval victory did not sever every supply line, expel Japanese armies, retake the capital, or substitute for local resistance and Ming intervention. The best description is sea denial, not total command of the sea.

In 1593 the fleet moved its headquarters to Hansan Island and a unified command was established, marking a shift from mobile campaigns to a sustained maritime defensive system. The evidence base, including the diary, official reports, and Japanese sources, has to be read critically, and later memory of the war has sometimes inflated particular elements.

The chapter thus supports an integrated reading: the navy mattered because it worked within a surviving state and alongside other forms of resistance. The next chapter turns to the righteous armies, Buddhist fighters, and Ming intervention that made the war a multinational conflict.

Building a Source Dossier on the Battle of Hansan Island

This project asks you to compare how different kinds of sources describe a single naval engagement and to decide what can be established.

  1. Collect sources. Using library resources, locate translated or summarized versions of at least three kinds of evidence about Hansan: an official Korean report, a private account such as Yi's diary or a contemporary memoir, and a Japanese or other outside account.

Chapter Reflection

The war at sea matters because it shows how a state in the middle of a military catastrophe could still retain the capacity to resist. Joseon's land defenses collapsed in weeks, yet its southwestern navy, built on prewar preparation, state manufacture, local knowledge, and disciplined command, remained effective. The contrast illustrates that military outcomes depend on institutions and maintenance as much as on numbers or reputations.

The chapter also reveals how a particular geography shapes warfare. The southern coast, with its islands, tides, and channels, gave advantages to those who understood it. Yi's most significant decisions, such as refusing to fight in Gyeonnaeryang and drawing the enemy to Hansan, were decisions about terrain. The campaign shows that environment is not a backdrop but an active variable in strategy.

At the same time, the chapter has insisted on proportion. Naval victory denied Japan a sea route and altered Japanese planning, but it left Japanese armies in the peninsula, the capital in enemy hands, and the war unfinished. The resolution of that situation required local resistance, Ming intervention, and negotiation, which the next chapters examine. To tell the war as only a naval story would be to repeat a simplification that modern memory has encouraged.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger interest in how history is remembered. Yi Sun-sin has become a symbol in modern Korea, and the turtle ship an emblem of national ingenuity. Historians can respect that significance while distinguishing memory from evidence. Doing so makes Yi's achievement more intelligible, because it places him within the ships, crews, institutions, and communities that made it possible. Chapter 34 turns to the land war and its multinational dimension, and Chapter 35 returns to the sea in 1597 to follow the story of Yi's fall, his return, and the final campaigns.
