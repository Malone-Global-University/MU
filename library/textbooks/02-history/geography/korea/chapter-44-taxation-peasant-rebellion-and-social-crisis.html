Taxation, Peasant Rebellion, and Social Crisis

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the early spring of 1862, a crowd of villagers in the southern city of Jinju, many of them wearing white cloths bound around their heads, marched on the local administrative center. Their grievance was not an abstract doctrine. It was the arithmetic of obligations: grain they had never borrowed but were told to repay, cloth levies assigned to men long dead, and land taxes collected at rates no statute had authorized. To understand why people who had paid taxes for generations turned to collective violence, one must first understand what the tax system had become.

Chapter 43 examined the nineteenth-century Joseon state from above: a weak monarchy, powerful in-law families, a fragile fiscal structure, and a provincial administration whose offices were increasingly bought, sold, and exploited. This chapter turns the same landscape upside down and examines it from the village. It asks how the abstract machinery of land tax, military-service payment, and grain lending was experienced by farming households; how those households and the communities around them responded; and why, in 1811–1812 and again in 1862, response escalated from petition to armed uprising.

Two great waves of resistance anchor the chapter. The first was the rebellion led by Hong Gyeong-nae in the northwestern province of Pyeongan, which began in the twelfth lunar month of 1811 and ended with the fall of Jeongju fortress in the fourth lunar month of 1812. It was a regionally rooted movement that drew together discontented local elites, wealthy merchants, mine laborers, and poor villagers, and it was explicitly political in its denunciation of the court and of the treatment of the northwest. The second wave, the Imsul uprisings of 1862, began in Danseong, produced the dramatic Jinju uprising, and spread across dozens of counties in the southern and central provinces. These were not directed from one headquarters; they were linked local movements centered on the fiscal abuses of particular officials and clerks.

The central argument of this chapter is that nineteenth-century peasant resistance grew from the interaction of three things: economic hardship, unequal social and regional power, and the failure of established institutions to resolve grievances. Poverty alone does not explain a rebellion; many poor communities endured hardship without rising. What distinguished the communities that rebelled was that they had exhausted, or concluded they had exhausted, the lawful channels of redress, and that they could draw on organizers, shared grievances, and local networks capable of coordinating collective action. The rebellions were organized political acts with identifiable causes, participants, and demands, not merely spontaneous eruptions of hunger.

The chapter relies on evidence that is rich but heavily filtered. Court annals, memoranda to the throne, interrogation records, reports from provincial governors and special inspectors, and local gazetteers provide detailed accounts of both waves of rebellion. Nearly all of them were produced by officials, many in the course of investigating or prosecuting rebels. A small number of documents, most famously Hong Gyeong-nae’s proclamation, preserve something closer to rebel voices, though even these reach us through official hands. Historians therefore read these sources against the grain, asking what the authors wished to prove and whose experience is missing.

The chapter also respects its boundaries within the larger textbook. Chapter 43 treated the structure of royal weakness and in-law rule; this chapter refers to it only to explain village conditions. Chapters 45 through 47 turn to Catholic persecution, foreign intervention, Donghak, and the reforms of the Daewongun, and appear here only as context. The much larger Donghak Peasant Revolution of 1894 belongs to Chapter 51. What this chapter provides is the foundation for those later developments: an account of how and why the rural communities of Joseon came to doubt that the existing order could protect them.

One note on dates. Korean sources record events by the lunar calendar. Unless otherwise stated, month and day references in this chapter are lunar-calendar dates as given in Korean sources, and conversions to the Gregorian calendar in English-language works are sometimes inconsistent. Where a Gregorian year is given, it may differ by a few weeks from the lunar date, since a lunar twelfth month usually falls at the end of one Gregorian year and the beginning of the next.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how landownership, tenancy, debt, and dependence structured inequality in nineteenth-century Korean villages.
  2. Describe the three interlocking fiscal systems known as the samjeong, and distinguish lawful obligations from the abuses that grew up around them.
  3. Trace how the grain-loan system (hwan’gok) changed from a relief institution into a mechanism of fiscal extraction.
  4. Analyze why petitions and lawful appeals so often failed to remedy grievances, and why some communities turned to rebellion.
  5. Reconstruct the origins, coalition, course, and defeat of Hong Gyeong-nae’s rebellion of 1811–1812, distinguishing early rebel successes from the later siege.
  6. Evaluate the regional, political, and social character of Hong’s rebellion, and distinguish rebel goals from later historians’ interpretations.
  7. Compare the rebellion of 1811–1812 with the Imsul uprisings of 1862 in causes, leadership, participants, and demands.
  8. Reconstruct the Danseong and Jinju uprisings and explain how protest spread across Joseon without a single central command.
  9. Assess the government’s investigation of the 1862 uprisings and the Samjeong Reform Office, and explain why its reforms had limited lasting effect.
  10. Interpret a rebel proclamation as historical evidence, identifying its audience, purposes, and limitations.
  11. Evaluate competing scholarly interpretations of whether the rebellions challenged abusive officials or the broader social order.
  12. Connect rural resistance to the crisis of political legitimacy that shaped the era examined in Chapters 45 through 51.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why do rebellions occur in some distressed communities and not in others? What besides hardship was required?
  2. How could three fiscal systems that were designed as lawful state institutions become instruments of abuse without ever being formally abolished?
  3. What does it reveal about a political order when its relief institutions function as revenue-collecting ones?
  4. Why did lawful appeals fail so often, and what did communities conclude from those failures?
  5. In what ways was Hong Gyeong-nae’s rebellion a regional political movement rather than simply a peasant revolt?
  6. How do historians reconstruct the aims of rebels when most surviving evidence was produced by officials investigating them?
  7. Were the 1862 uprisings a connected movement, or a series of similar but separate local protests?
  8. Why did the government’s reform effort of 1862 produce so little durable change?
  9. How should historians weigh economic hardship, political exclusion, and institutional failure when explaining collective resistance?

Historical Context

By 1800, Joseon had been governed by the same dynasty for more than four centuries. As Chapters 37 through 42 explained, the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries brought recovery from the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions, agricultural intensification, commercial expansion, a more mobile population, and intellectual ferment. The fiscal order that emerged from those centuries reflected the commercializing economy: the Daedong reform converted much of the tribute-in-kind burden into rice, cloth, or coin payments levied on land, and the Gyunyeok reform of 1750 reduced the military cloth levy to one bolt per male subject in an effort to equalize a burden that had fallen unevenly on commoners.

These reforms were not failures. They represented genuine attempts to rationalize taxation. But they left the state dependent on a complex system of local collection in which county magistrates, provincial offices, and above all hereditary local clerks (hyangni and related runners and functionaries) mediated between the state’s demands and the villagers’ capacity to pay. The magistrate was an outsider, usually serving a short term and often having paid or lobbied for his post. The clerks were local, permanent, and knowledgeable. The villagers depended on both, and neither was reliably accountable to them.

Nineteenth-century political conditions made this arrangement more dangerous. After the death of King Jeongjo in 1800, the throne passed to child or weak monarchs, and power concentrated in the hands of in-law families, a pattern examined in Chapter 43. Offices became more expensive to obtain, and officeholders had stronger incentives to recoup their outlays through the revenue system. At the same time, central supervision weakened, the registers that were supposed to record land, households, and grain became stale or manipulated, and the state’s capacity to detect abuses declined.

Rural society was also changing. Commercial agriculture, tenancy, wage labor, mining, and long-distance trade created new forms of wealth and dependence. Status lines were becoming blurred in some places as wealthy commoners purchased or claimed higher status, and as the yangban elite grew in number and diversified in wealth, as discussed in Chapters 29 and 42. Religious and intellectual movements outside the official Neo-Confucian orthodoxy, including Catholic communities and popular prophetic literature, offered alternative imaginations of social order, though their direct role in the rebellions of this chapter was limited and varied.

Finally, the regions of Joseon differed. The southern provinces of Gyeongsang, Jeolla, and Chungcheong were the agricultural heartland of the kingdom and the source of the bulk of its land revenue. The northwestern provinces, especially Pyeongan, were connected to Qing China through overland trade and had commercial wealth, mining, and a long-standing grievance about political exclusion. Treating “the peasantry” as a single bloc would obscure these differences. This chapter therefore keeps regional variation in view.

Section 44.1

The Rural Crisis of Nineteenth-Century Joseon

The phrase “rural crisis” can mislead if it implies uniform collapse. Joseon’s countryside in the early and mid-nineteenth century was not a single poverty-stricken landscape. Some villages prospered through commercial crops or proximity to markets; some households accumulated land; some local families rose in wealth if not always in formal status. Yet the countryside also contained large numbers of households operating at the margin of subsistence, dependent on tenancy, seasonal wage work, or the whims of landlords and clerks, and vulnerable to harvest failure and arbitrary assessment. The crisis was not that every household suffered equally but that the institutions meant to stabilize rural life increasingly added to its instability.

The crisis had a chronological shape. It was visible in the great northwestern rebellion of 1811–1812, which showed that regional and political grievance could combine with economic tension to produce armed insurrection. It continued in smaller disturbances across the following decades, and it crested in 1862, when a series of local uprisings revealed a similar pattern of fiscal abuse across much of the southern kingdom. Between these peaks, historians find no continuous revolutionary organization. What they find instead is persistent grievance, repeated local protest, and the slow accumulation of fiscal distortion.

The evidence for this crisis comes primarily from the state. The Veritable Records and Daily Records of the Royal Secretariat preserve memorials, edicts, and discussions of unrest. Interrogation records (chuan) preserve the testimony of arrested rebels, though testimony taken under duress by officials seeking convictions must be read cautiously. Local gazetteers, private diaries of scholars and local gentry, literary collections, and a small number of petitions and placards add other voices. What is largely absent is the written testimony of ordinary tenant farmers, laborers, and village women, whose experience must often be inferred from what officials chose to record about their complaints.

This chapter therefore proceeds in four movements. It first establishes the social and fiscal foundations of the rural crisis (Sections 44.2 through 44.8). It then examines the northwestern rebellion of Hong Gyeong-nae (Sections 44.9 through 44.13). It follows the decades between the great rebellions and the build-up to 1862 (Sections 44.14 and 44.15). Finally it reconstructs the Imsul uprisings and the government response (Sections 44.16 through 44.20), before closing with an integrated review.

Section 44.2

Landownership, Tenancy, and Rural Inequality

Land was the foundation of both wealth and taxation in Joseon. Rural households stood in very different relations to it. At one end were landowning families, including yangban lineages and wealthy commoners, who held substantial estates and rented plots to others. At the other end were landless or nearly landless households, who survived through tenancy, farm labor, handicraft, fishing, gathering, or migration. Between them lay a broad middle of smallholders cultivating their own plots, often supplemented by renting additional land, and often very vulnerable to a single bad year.

Tenancy was widespread by the nineteenth century, though its precise extent varied by region and cannot be reduced to a single national figure. Tenants typically paid rent in kind, sometimes as a share of the harvest and sometimes as a fixed amount, and rents could consume a large fraction of the crop. Tenancy was not necessarily permanent or uniformly oppressive; some tenants held long-standing customary rights to cultivate particular plots, and some enterprising tenants prospered. But tenancy left households exposed to rent increases, eviction, and landlord favoritism, and it left them with little surplus to cushion fiscal shocks.

The relationship between landownership and taxation was crucial. In principle, land tax was levied on the cultivated land recorded in registers, so that owners carried the burden. In practice, burdens could be shifted. Landlords sometimes passed tax obligations to tenants through rent arrangements, and powerful landowners were sometimes able to conceal land from the registers, leaving the tax burden to fall more heavily on those whose holdings were fully recorded. Poorer households thus often paid more relative to their means than wealthier ones, a pattern that fed a sense of injustice well before any rebellion.

Debt was an additional dependence. Households borrowed grain, coin, or goods from landlords, merchants, moneylenders, and official granaries, often at high effective interest. A bad harvest could convert a temporary shortfall into long-term indebtedness and sometimes the loss of land. Because official grain loans (examined in Section 44.7) were another form of lending, the line between private and public debt blurred in village life. Social hierarchy compounded these economic dependencies: yangban families commanded deference, access to officials, and legal advantages that commoners and unfree people (nobi) did not. These relations were not unchanging, as Chapter 42 explained, but they provided the context in which fiscal demands were experienced.

Section 44.3

Population Pressures, Harvest Failures, and Rural Hardship

Historians face real difficulty in measuring nineteenth-century Korean demography. Household registers and population counts compiled by the state were incomplete, because households under-reported members to reduce obligations and because officials had incentives to manipulate figures. The broad picture, however, is one of a population that had recovered from the seventeenth-century catastrophes and, in the following centuries, grew unevenly and put increasing pressure on cultivable land in some regions. The pressure was not uniform. Some districts had room for expansion; others, especially densely settled southern lowlands, had little.

Environmental shocks interacted with this pressure. Drought, flood, and cold years could devastate harvests in particular provinces. Epidemic disease also left traces in the record; cholera, for example, is generally dated to Korea’s first major outbreak in the early 1820s. But environmental events must be treated as variables whose effects depended on social conditions: a drought was far more dangerous to a tenant household owing rent and grain-loan repayment than to a landlord with stored reserves, and the effectiveness of official relief depended on the condition of the granaries and the honesty of the officials who administered them. Harvest failure did not automatically produce rebellion; it intensified grievances that arose elsewhere.

The vulnerability of households varied by composition and circumstance. Households with many working adults could diversify income; households led by widows, elderly persons, or the sick were more exposed. Women carried heavy burdens in agricultural and textile production, though their labor is poorly documented. Children were affected by food insecurity, and some families sent members away to seek wage work, join mining labor, or migrate to cities or frontier zones. These patterns of mobility are important: many of the people who joined rebel movements in Pyeongan, as Section 44.10 shows, were not settled farmers but mine workers and itinerant laborers.

Regional variation should remain central. The northwest’s commercial economy and mineral resources differed from the rice-growing south. Gyeongsang and Jeolla produced much of the kingdom’s grain and land revenue, and thus bore the weight of fiscal demand. Mountainous areas supported different economies, with upland slash-and-burn farming, forestry, and gathering. Jeju Island had its own tax and tribute problems tied to its remote location and distinct economy. The rebellion evidence reflects these differences, and later sections will not treat them as interchangeable.

Section 44.4

The Three Administrations: Samjeong

Contemporaries and later historians describe the core of Joseon’s fiscal order as the samjeong, the “three administrations” or “three governances.” They were the land-tax system (jeonjeong), the military-service system (gunjeong), and the grain-loan system (hwan’gok, sometimes called hwanjeong). The grouping is partly a convenience of later analysis and partly a reflection of contemporary language; officials and reformers of the 1860s used the term explicitly when they diagnosed the crisis. Together the three administrations absorbed a large share of the economic surplus of farming households and were the chief points at which the state and the village met.

What made the system fragile was its dependence on local discretion. The state set principles, quotas, and registers, but local officials determined assessments, collected goods, stored and lent grain, and reported results. Each administration generated opportunities for manipulation, and the three interacted: a shortfall in one could be covered by additional demands in another; a clerk managing the grain granary might also assist in the cloth assessment; a magistrate under pressure to deliver revenue might transfer burdens across categories. By the nineteenth century this interaction had made the whole system opaque to villagers and difficult even for central officials to supervise.

It is important not to treat the entire system as inherently abusive. Many of its provisions reflected sound if limited purposes. A fixed land tax stabilized obligations; military cloth payments funded defense; grain lending provided seed and food in the lean season and stabilized prices. Reformers in the eighteenth century regarded these as accomplishments. The nineteenth-century problem lay less in the design than in how its operation drifted: written rates lost meaning, registers decayed, additional levies were piled on top, and the distinction between lawful obligation and illegal exaction blurred.

Historical Vocabulary
Samjeong (三政)

Definition: The “three administrations” of late Joseon taxation: the land tax (jeonjeong), the military-service levy (gunjeong), and the grain-loan system (hwan’gok).

Historical context: The term gained particular currency in the 1860s, when inspectors and reformers used it to describe the interlocking sources of rural grievance, and the government’s reform office of 1862 bore the name Samjeong Ijeongcheong, “Office for the Rectification of the Three Administrations.”

Why it matters: The concept lets students see the fiscal order as a system in which burdens could shift across categories, which explains why local abuses were so hard to correct one at a time.

Potential misunderstanding: “Samjeong” is not an additional tax; it is a way of grouping three separate institutions. It also does not mean that all three were corrupt from the beginning.

Section 44.5

Land Taxation and Abusive Assessment

The land tax was calculated on units of assessed productive capacity called gyeol, which depended on both the area and fertility of land rather than area alone. After the seventeenth-century reforms, the basic rate per gyeol was relatively low and fixed, which in principle protected cultivators from arbitrary increases. But the legal rate was only part of the total burden. Additional levies for transport, storage, wastage, and various other purposes accumulated on top of it, and by the nineteenth century the sum of these charges often substantially exceeded the formal base.

Problems arose at several points. The land registers on which assessments depended had not been comprehensively resurveyed for long periods, so that they diverged from actual cultivation. Land that had been abandoned remained on the books and continued to generate tax obligations; land that had been newly reclaimed, or deliberately concealed through bribery, might not be recorded at all (so-called eun-gyeol, “hidden land”). When the state demanded a fixed total from a county, the shortfall created by abandoned or concealed land did not vanish; it was redistributed to taxpayers whose land was visible. In effect, the diligent and powerless were taxed for the absent and the connected.

Collection practices added further distortion. In many places, in-kind payment was replaced by payment in cash at conversion rates set by local officials, a practice often called dogyeol in the sources of this period. When officials set the conversion rate above the market value of the goods owed, the difference became a surplus that could be diverted. Clerks might also embezzle tax receipts and then demand repayment again from the villagers, or divert goods stored for the state and later make up the deficit through emergency levies. Because villagers rarely saw the registers or the official accounts, they had little ability to verify what they owed.

Magistrates and clerks did not operate in a vacuum. The costs of obtaining and keeping office, expected gifts to superiors, and the pressure to deliver quotas to the province all shaped their behavior. Historians generally distinguish systemic pressure from individual dishonesty, but they also note that not all officials behaved alike: some magistrates were remembered for restraint and some for rapacity. Villagers’ responses to abuse could therefore be targeted at specific officials rather than at the state as a whole, a pattern visible in the 1862 uprisings.

Section 44.6

Military Cloth Levies and Unequal Service Burdens

Joseon’s military system had long since shifted from direct conscription to a payment system. Instead of serving, most eligible male subjects paid cloth (gunpo), which the state used to support standing forces and other military expenses. The eighteenth-century Gyunyeok reform reduced the standard payment to one bolt per man and sought to replace some lost revenue through other taxes, aiming to ease and equalize the burden. Yangban status offered de facto exemption, so the levy fell on commoners.

The weakness of the system was its dependence on registers of eligible men. Because the state’s need for revenue persisted regardless of population change, quotas for each county remained roughly fixed even as individuals died, migrated, or aged out of eligibility. Officials responded by assigning obligations to others. The sources describe several abuses that became notorious: levying on men already dead, sometimes called the “white bones” levy; registering infants and young boys as eligible, the “yellow mouths” levy; and charging neighbors or relatives to cover the cloth owed by absent or deceased individuals, so-called neighbor and kin levies. Villages also pooled obligations through collective cloth levies (dongpo), which spread burdens within the community but also created conflicts over fairness.

These abuses were not simply rhetorical complaints. Officials of the time recognized and condemned them in memorials, and reformers of the 1860s treated them as central problems. They point to a basic injustice: the burden of a defense system no longer based on service fell on those least able to evade it. A farming family could find itself responsible not only for its own members but for the dead and the vanished. The levy also intersected with status: families seeking to escape the burden sought yangban status, genuine or forged, which in turn shifted the burden to those who could not.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village Household’s Fiscal Year: An Illustrative Reconstruction

Note: the following is an illustrative composite, not the record of any particular family. It is built from patterns documented in official memorials and reform proposals and should not be read as a specific historical case.

Consider a tenant household in a southern county, cultivating rented rice paddy and a small vegetable plot. In spring, with stored grain running low, the household might be told by the local clerk to take a loan from the county granary, whether or not it needed one, in a quantity set by the village’s assigned quota. Through the summer, the family’s work was divided: men on the paddies, women on weeding, textile work, and food preparation, children on herding and gathering. After the autumn harvest, the household owed rent to the landlord, repayment with interest on the grain loan, and, depending on the village’s arrangements, a share of the land tax passed down through the rent or through village assessments.

Late in the year came the cloth levy. If an elderly father had died but remained on the register, or a son had left to find work in a mine or port, the household or its neighbors might be told to pay for them. In a bad year, the sum of these obligations could exceed what the harvest left behind, forcing the household to borrow again from private lenders, to sell livestock or tools, or to leave. Such pressures help explain why communities sometimes responded collectively: the burdens were shared, assessed through village-level bodies, and recognizable as common injustices.

Because most documentation was created by officials and by the educated elite, this reconstruction necessarily emphasizes what the state asked of households rather than what households said. Women, hired laborers, and unfree people appear mostly indirectly. Historians reconstruct their experience by combining fiscal records, reform memorials, and sparse local sources, and they must remain cautious about filling gaps with imagination.

Section 44.7

Hwan’gok: From Grain Relief to Fiscal Extraction

The third administration, hwan’gok, was in origin a humane institution. State, county, and village granaries stored grain and lent it to farming households in the spring lean season, to be repaid after the autumn harvest. Repayment included a modest additional amount, conventionally described as around one-tenth, to cover spoilage and losses. The system was intended to help households survive until harvest, to provide seed, and to stabilize grain prices, and in earlier centuries it did serve those functions in many places.

Over time, however, the granaries became important sources of revenue for local governments, military units, and other offices. The interest on loans funded operating expenses, and officials were tempted to treat the granary as a fiscal resource rather than a relief store. As formal state funding for local offices proved insufficient, the interest and the stored grain itself were drawn upon to pay for administration. The result was a structural transformation: relief became revenue.

The abuses that followed are among the most frequently documented in the sources. Officials forced loans on households that did not need or want them, because the interest was the point. In some places loans were recorded though no grain was actually distributed, or grain was distributed in poor quality or mixed with chaff, while full repayment was still demanded. Granaries were emptied through embezzlement or diversion, leaving fictitious entries on the books, and when auditors or superiors required the deficit to be covered, the shortfall was sometimes imposed on villages as collective repayment obligations. A household might thus owe grain it never received, to a granary that no longer contained any.

The transformation of hwan’gok mattered more than its individual abuses because it undermined the moral logic of the system. A relief institution that demands repayment for nonexistent grain violates the reciprocal expectation, widely held in agrarian societies, that rulers will protect subsistence. Historians sometimes use the phrase “moral economy” to describe such expectations, though they debate how closely it fits Korean evidence. In 1862 reformers themselves would identify hwan’gok as the worst of the three administrations.

Historical Vocabulary
Hwan’gok (還穀)

Definition: The state grain-loan system under which granaries lent grain in spring and collected repayment, with a small additional amount, after the autumn harvest.

Historical context: Originally a relief and price-stabilization system, it became in the nineteenth century a major source of revenue for local and military offices and a major site of abuse.

Why it matters: It illustrates how a welfare function can be converted into extraction without any formal change in the law, and why reformers of 1862 targeted it above the other administrations.

Potential misunderstanding: The word is sometimes translated simply as “grain tax,” but it was not a tax in principle. It was a loan, and the abuse lay in turning it into a compulsory obligation.

Section 44.8

Petitions, Local Protest, and the Limits of Redress

Joseon had established channels through which subjects could seek redress. Individuals and groups could submit petitions (sojang) to the magistrate, the provincial governor, or the central government, and the throne itself received appeals. A long-standing practice allowed subjects to strike a drum or gong or to appeal to the king during royal processions. Village assemblies and kin groups could present collective complaints, and local scholars sometimes drafted petitions on behalf of less literate neighbors. These channels were not empty gestures; in earlier periods petitions had produced results, including corrections of particular abuses.

In the nineteenth century, however, the system of redress faced structural obstacles. Petitions to the magistrate had to be received by the clerks and runners whose conduct was often the subject of complaint, and a petitioner might face retaliation. Appeals to higher officials were routinely returned to the county for investigation, placing the accused in charge of his own case. Distance, cost, and literacy limited access to the capital. Officials who had purchased or lobbied for their posts, or who depended on patrons, had little incentive to expose misconduct in their own jurisdictions. Petitioners who persisted risked arrest as troublemakers.

Communities therefore developed forms of collective protest that escalated in steps. These included petitions signed in circular form so that no single author could be singled out, public gatherings at the county office, anonymous placards (bangseo) posted in public places, and written circulars (tongmun) calling villagers to assemble. Many such actions ended in negotiation or concession. Where officials refused or retaliated, protests could grow into direct action against clerks’ homes and offices, which were targets of choice because they were closely identified with abuse. Only a minority of disputes reached the level of armed uprising, and understanding why requires attention to leadership, local networks, and the perception that nothing else would work.

Historians therefore treat the rebellions as the end of a chain rather than an isolated outburst. A community that had petitioned unsuccessfully, watched an official escape accountability, and seen neighbors punished for complaining had reason to believe that lawful redress was closed. The shift from petition to uprising was a political judgment about the state of the available institutions, not simply a loss of patience.

Section 44.9

Pyeongan Province and the Roots of Regional Discontent

Pyeongan Province lay in the northwest, bordering the Amnok (Yalu) River and connected by overland trade routes to Qing China. Its largest city, Pyeongyang, and the border city of Uiju were centers of commerce, and the province contained mines, including gold and other mineral workings, that attracted labor and capital. Merchants of the northwest were involved in cross-border trade, and the province’s wealth was in some respects greater than the political standing of its elite.

That gap was central to regional grievance. Since the founding of Joseon, the northwestern provinces had been treated with suspicion by the court, partly because of their frontier location and partly because of their distance from the capital’s political networks. Educated men of the northwest could and did study and sit the civil service examinations, and a minority passed them, but in practice they found it very difficult to obtain high office or to rise within the capital’s patronage networks. Many in the region regarded this as long-standing discrimination. The perception, whatever its exact statistical basis, was widely shared and politically potent.

Fiscal burdens contributed. The northwest bore military and diplomatic obligations tied to the border and the Qing relationship, and it was subject to the same abuses in land, cloth, and grain administration as elsewhere. In the early nineteenth century, the province also experienced hardship and disorder that officials recorded, though historians stress that conditions varied locally. The combination of commercial wealth, political exclusion, and fiscal pressure created a distinctive environment in which educated local men, wealthy merchants, and poor laborers all had reasons for resentment, though not identical ones.

Section 44.10

Hong Gyeong-nae and the Formation of a Rebel Coalition

Hong Gyeong-nae was born in Pyeongan Province in the late eighteenth century, apparently into a family of local standing without significant influence at court; reference works disagree on whether he was born in 1771 or 1780. He was educated and attempted the civil service examinations, and the traditional account holds that his failure and his sense of exclusion shaped his outlook. Some later sources, including records produced by his opponents, attribute to him knowledge of geomancy and prophetic literature, which would have been useful for building an organization and for appealing to people already inclined to believe the dynasty’s time was passing. How far these details reflect actual practice rather than later embellishment is uncertain.

According to accounts preserved largely in official and interrogation records, Hong spent years traveling in the northwest and building relationships with like-minded men. The movement did not rest on Hong alone. Among his chief collaborators was U Gun-chik, a geomancer and strategist who helped organize the enterprise. Merchant supporters, including Yi Hui-jeo, provided funds and supplies; other figures, including men with scholarly backgrounds, took roles as lieutenants and commanders. The sources name several of these individuals, though their identities and status are sometimes reported inconsistently, and historians treat the details cautiously.

The coalition was socially diverse. It included educated local men who felt excluded from office, merchants and men of means who financed and supplied the movement, and large numbers of poorer people recruited from villages and from the labor force of the mines, whose workers were mobile, accustomed to hard labor, and available for armed service. This diversity is crucial to interpreting the rebellion. It was not a simple uprising of the poor against the rich, since wealthy backers were central; nor was it merely a revolt of elites, since many followers were laborers and villagers with their own grievances. Recruitment appears to have relied on personal networks, promises, and the shared sense of regional injury.

Hong and his followers prepared for a considerable period and issued a proclamation setting out their grievances and justifying their action. Historians regard the proclamation as both propaganda and evidence: propaganda because its purpose was to recruit and legitimize, and evidence because it reveals the arguments rebels thought would persuade their audience.

Primary Source Window

Hong Gyeong-nae’s Proclamation to the People of the Northwest, 1811

Context: The proclamation was composed to explain and justify an armed rising to a regional audience. It survives in versions transmitted through the state’s own records, which means it was preserved because officials considered it important evidence of rebel intent. Students should treat the surviving text as filtered through that process and consult scholarly editions for exact wording.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of its general themes, not a quotation.

The document argues that the northwestern region has been treated with contempt by the court for centuries despite producing capable men; condemns the dominance of powerful in-law families and the weakness of a young king; describes the sufferings of the people under misrule and disaster; and presents the rising as a righteous act to remove corrupt influence and relieve the people.

Analysis: The proclamation shows that the rebels framed their grievance in explicitly political and regional terms. It targets court politics and regional discrimination more than particular tax abuses, and it appeals to people’s sense of injury rather than to a program of social transformation. It also shows that rebels expected the language of legitimate protest against corrupt rule, not the rejection of monarchy as such, to attract support.

Limitations: The text cannot tell us what ordinary followers, especially poor villagers and mine laborers, thought, or whether they shared the leaders’ priorities. It was written to persuade, so it may overstate unity and righteousness. The version that survives may reflect editing, and some details reported about the rebels appear only in hostile sources.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the rebel leadership, probably educated men writing for a broad regional audience. Missing are the voices of tenants, laborers, women, and loyalist neighbors, as well as the perspective of officials who suffered in the rising.

Section 44.11

The Rebellion of December 1811

The rebellion broke out on the eighteenth day of the twelfth lunar month of 1811, a date often rendered in English as December 1811. Rebel forces seized control of Gasan, a county in northern Pyeongan, and moved quickly against neighboring towns. Accounts describe an organized campaign in which rebel groups, rather than a single column, moved against several locations. Commonly, historians describe the movement as divided into a northern and a southern force operating in parallel, with Hong Gyeong-nae taking a leading command role in the southern force.

The initial successes were striking. Several county seats in the region fell with little resistance. In some cases rebel groups took towns by surprise, in others local officials were killed or fled, and in others local residents or officials cooperated or acquiesced. Reports record that magistrates and military officers were captured or killed, and that rebels distributed provisions and weapons drawn from official stores. The speed of these early gains suggests both careful preparation and the weakness of local military defenses, which were often poorly maintained and led by officials unprepared for armed rebellion.

Local participation varied. Some residents joined the rebels enthusiastically, some complied under pressure, and some, particularly those linked to officialdom or threatened by the rebels, resisted or fled. Historians caution that official accounts may exaggerate coercion, since officials had every reason to portray the population as victims, and rebel-friendly accounts may exaggerate enthusiasm. The reality was probably mixed and varied by locality. The rebel organization tried to administer captured places by appointing officials and issuing orders, which suggests aspirations to govern as well as to fight.

The court responded by naming commanders and dispatching troops from the capital and from other provinces, while local forces and loyal officials and notables organized defense. The government’s early response was slow and its forces were initially not decisive, but over the following weeks the balance shifted. Rebel columns encountered government forces in the field, and a major engagement went against the southern force, driving rebel groups to consolidate in a defensible position. That position was the walled county town of Jeongju.

Section 44.12

The Siege of Jeongju and the Rebellion’s Defeat, 1812

Rebel forces gathered at Jeongju and held it for roughly four months against government troops. The walled town gave defenders a strong position, and the rebels fortified it, collected supplies, and attempted to maintain order and morale. Government forces, assembled under senior commanders, surrounded the town and settled into a siege that tested both sides. The rebels sallied out to attack besiegers and attempted to gain relief or further recruits, while the government attempted to cut off supplies and intercept messengers.

The siege was costly. Soldiers on both sides suffered from exposure, disease, and the difficulties of supply in a winter and spring campaign. Civilians in and around the town faced hardship, requisition, and violence. The rebels’ capacity to sustain the defense depended on the stores within the town and on whatever help they could obtain from outside, which proved limited as government forces tightened control of the surrounding region and as the initial wave of local support receded.

The siege ended in the fourth lunar month of 1812. According to the commonly accepted account, government forces tunneled beneath a section of the wall and detonated an explosive charge, creating a breach through which troops entered the town. Fighting inside the town was fierce, and Hong Gyeong-nae was killed. Historians generally accept this sequence while noting that details vary among sources.

The aftermath was severe. Captured rebels and townspeople were interrogated, and large numbers were executed; the sources record that captured men above a certain age were put to death while some others, including women and children, were enslaved or otherwise punished. Casualty and execution figures in the sources differ and are difficult to verify, and this chapter does not rely on a single number, but the scale of reprisal is not in doubt and was extreme even by the standards of the age. The treatment of survivors left a lasting mark on the region and on memory of the rebellion, and it reinforced the northwest’s sense of being treated as a suspect territory.

Section 44.13

The Meaning and Legacy of Hong Gyeong-nae’s Rebellion

The rebellion’s character resists simple classification. It was regional, because its leadership, recruitment, and grievances were rooted in the northwest. It was political, because it targeted the court’s conduct and the treatment of the region, not merely local officials. It was social, because it mobilized laborers and villagers whose own grievances included economic hardship. And it was economic, because its backers included merchants and men of means whose interests in trade and mining shaped both their resources and their resentments. Any account that reduces it to a single dimension misses part of the picture.

The rebels’ own goals are harder to reconstruct than the movement’s course. The proclamation suggests an aim of removing corrupt influence and redressing regional injustice, but it does not clearly set out a program for a new political order. Some later historians, especially in the twentieth century, have read the rebellion as an early expression of social opposition to the old regime, or as part of a longer sequence of resistance anticipating the Donghak movement. Others emphasize its regional and elite-led character and caution against reading later categories into the movement. These are interpretations, not facts established by the sources, and the Historical Debate below considers them.

The rebellion’s immediate effects were mixed. It was crushed, and the court’s response included harsh reprisals and a degree of suspicion toward the northwest. The government made limited efforts to address local grievances, but the structural problems of fiscal administration and political exclusion remained largely untouched. The rebellion also alarmed the elite and demonstrated the vulnerability of local defenses. For the people of the northwest, its memory remained complex, shaped by loss and by lingering resentment.

Historical Debate

Were the Rebellions Protests Against Abusive Officials, or Challenges to the Social Order?

The question: When villagers and regional elites rose in 1811–1812 and 1862, were they asking the existing order to function properly, or were they challenging its legitimacy and structure? The question affects how historians connect these rebellions to the later reform era, the Donghak movement, and modernization.

Interpretation A — protest within the system: Many scholars emphasize that the 1862 uprisings in particular targeted identifiable officials and clerks, demanded relief from specific levies, and did not call for abolition of the monarchy or the status system. On this view, rebels sought restoration of what they regarded as lawful and just administration, and often expected the king to correct abuses once informed.
Interpretation B — challenge to the broader order: Others stress that the rebels’ actions, including attacks on yangban households, seizure of official property, disregard for status hierarchy in collective action, and in Pyeongan the explicit denunciation of the court, represented a growing popular willingness to contest authority. On this view, the rebellions contributed to the erosion of the old order’s legitimacy even where rebels did not articulate a new one.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same sources: proclamations, petitions, interrogation records, and official investigations. The sources show that rebels often framed demands in terms of just administration, but also that collective action disrupted social hierarchy in practice.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology more than basic facts. It turns on how much weight to give rebels’ stated aims versus the effects of their actions, and on whether challenging officials is the same as challenging the order.

Why it matters: If the rebellions were primarily protests within the system, the later reforms might be seen as a missed opportunity for repair. If they were challenges to the order, they appear as symptoms of a deeper legitimacy crisis. The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine them.

Student task: Select one rebellion from this chapter and identify evidence supporting each interpretation. Then explain whether the two interpretations can be reconciled and what additional evidence would help.

Section 44.14

Resistance Between 1812 and 1862

The fifty years between the two great rebellions did not see a continuous revolutionary movement. They saw instead a pattern of local disturbances, conspiracies, and protests, many of them small and short-lived, scattered across the kingdom. Officials recorded placard incidents, in which anonymous messages attacking the government or its officials were posted in public places, plots and alleged plots against the throne, disputes over tax collection, and urban disturbances. Among incidents noted in the literature are a conspiracy centered on Jeju in the early 1810s and an urban rice disturbance in the capital in the early 1830s, though students should treat such items as examples rather than a complete inventory.

These events varied widely in character. Some were elite conspiracies, some were popular protests over food or fiscal burdens, and some were prosecutions of alleged disloyalty whose actual extent is unclear. Historians must be careful not to assemble them into a story of continuous national rebellion; their locations, leaders, and aims differed, and the government sometimes exaggerated threats. What the incidents show is the persistence of grievance, the availability of protest repertoires, and the state’s limited ability to resolve underlying problems.

Within villages, the era saw ongoing struggles over fiscal fairness. Village assemblies negotiated how to divide collective cloth payments and grain repayment obligations, and disputes over those divisions sometimes pitted poorer households against wealthier ones or villagers against clerks. These everyday conflicts helped build the organizational experience on which larger actions in 1862 would draw: practice in drafting collective petitions, selecting representatives, and acting together.

The government’s responses during these decades were generally reactive. It dispatched inspectors, punished particular officials, and issued edicts against abuses, but it did not undertake comprehensive fiscal reform. This pattern helped create the conditions for 1862: grievances accumulated, redress remained inconsistent, and the system’s underlying incentives were unchanged.

Section 44.15

The Deepening Fiscal Crisis of the 1850s and Early 1860s

By the 1850s, the fiscal structures described earlier had been under strain for decades. The state’s need for revenue persisted, but the registers on which collection depended had decayed further, granaries had been depleted by years of diversion and unpaid loans, and local offices relied heavily on irregular levies to finance their operations. The result was an increase in the frequency and weight of emergency demands imposed on villages, particularly in the grain-producing south.

Two features of this period stand out. First, grievances became more concentrated in particular counties and offices. Where a magistrate, a provincial army commander, or a clerical clique was especially rapacious, the combined weight of land, cloth, and grain demands could become intolerable for the communities under them. Second, the accumulated deficits in granaries and treasuries created large obligations that had to be covered somehow. Officials responded by imposing collective levies on villages to make up the shortfalls, which turned past misconduct into present burdens on innocent households.

The political context mattered as well. King Cheoljong, who had come to the throne in 1849 as a ruler from a remote background, was dependent on the in-law families who dominated the court. Central supervision of provincial administration remained weak, and the sale and exchange of offices continued. Officials seeking to recoup the cost of their posts had strong incentives to exploit the revenue system, and the chances of being held accountable were low.

Cause and Effect

How Fiscal Transformation and Failed Redress Contributed to Collective Resistance

Long-term causes

Decay of land and household registers; conversion of relief and military institutions into revenue sources; tenancy and rural inequality; weak central supervision; the cost and trade of office under in-law rule.

Short-term causes

Accumulated granary deficits; emergency collective levies imposed on villages; harvest and weather shocks in particular districts; especially rapacious local officials.

Immediate trigger

A specific new or intensified levy, such as a collective repayment demand, following failed petitions, in a community with organizers and a tradition of collective action.

Event or transformation

Escalation from petition and assembly to attacks on clerks’ homes, county offices, and in some places local elites, and in the largest cases to the occupation of administrative centers.

Immediate effects

Official investigations, punishment of some officials, execution of leaders, temporary concessions, and a proliferation of similar protests in nearby districts.

Long-term consequences

Erosion of confidence in local and central institutions; pressure for fiscal reform; a legacy of collective action that fed later movements.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains why one community rebelled while another did not. Hardship was widespread; rebellion required the added ingredients of organization, leadership, a focal grievance, and a judgment that lawful redress had failed. The causes of 1811–1812 and 1862 overlapped without being identical.

Section 44.16

Danseong: The Opening of the 1862 Uprisings

The year 1862 is known in Korean historiography as Imsul, after its place in the sexagenary cycle, and the uprisings of that year are called the Imsul uprisings (Imsul minran). The first major outbreak occurred in early 1862 in Danseong, a county in Gyeongsang Province in the south. Its causes were local and fiscal: villagers and local figures protested abuses in the administration of grain and land taxes by the county’s officials and clerks.

The Danseong outbreak drew on a pattern that would recur. Villagers and local educated men organized, gathered, and presented complaints; when these were not satisfactorily answered, crowds confronted local officials and attacked the properties of clerks associated with the abuses. The participants were not exclusively poor; in Danseong and elsewhere, the sources suggest that locally educated men, some of whom had grievances of their own, played a role in organizing and articulating protest. The extent to which the Danseong organizers communicated with those who later rose at Jinju is not clearly established, though the events occurred within a short time and in the same province, and news of Danseong plausibly encouraged others.

Danseong matters for the history of the Imsul uprisings because it showed that collective protest could succeed, at least temporarily, in pressuring officials and in drawing the attention of the provincial and central governments. Its relationship to the larger Jinju uprising is best described as one of proximity, example, and shared conditions rather than of command.

Section 44.17

The Jinju Peasant Uprising

Jinju, a major administrative and military center in southern Gyeongsang, was the site of the best-known uprising of 1862. The immediate grievance concerned fiscal measures imposed by local officials. The sources associate the outbreak with the Gyeongsang army commander, Baek Nak-sin, and the magistrate of Jinju, Hong Byeong-won, and with efforts to cover large deficits in military and grain accounts by imposing collective levies on villages. Accounts report that these measures fell on households that had no connection to the original shortfalls, and that appeals to officials had produced no remedy.

The uprising was organized by local leaders, including Yu Gye-chun, who appears in the sources as a rural figure of modest standing, and was joined by large numbers of villagers, among them woodcutters and others from the countryside around the town. Participants are reported to have worn white headbands and to have gathered in large crowds, circulating circulars that summoned villagers to assemble. The crowd confronted officials, forced concessions regarding the levies, and attacked and burned the homes of clerks and of some wealthy local figures associated with the abuses. Officials were humiliated or compelled to flee, and for a period the authorities lost control of the situation.

The targets of the violence are historically significant. Crowds directed their anger primarily at clerks and at those they associated with fiscal exploitation, not at the monarchy or the dynasty. At the same time, the open defiance of the army commander and the magistrate, the destruction of property, and the temporary paralysis of local government represented a serious breach of order. Historians point out that the rebels’ actions combined traditional elements, such as collective petitioning and appeals to just administration, with a boldness that went beyond conventional protest.

The government responded by sending an inspector and by prosecuting those it identified as leaders. Several leaders were executed or punished, while some officials found responsible for abuses were also disciplined or removed. The balance of punishment, in which commoner leaders faced severe penalties and officials often milder ones, is itself part of the historical record and a point students may debate in light of the evidence. Jinju gave the movement its symbolic center and drew the central government’s attention, which in turn shaped the reform response.

Section 44.18

The Spread of the Imsul Uprisings across Joseon

After Danseong and Jinju, protests of a similar kind appeared in many other counties over the following months. They were concentrated in the southern provinces of Gyeongsang and Jeolla and also occurred in Chungcheong, with scattered cases reported elsewhere, including on Jeju. Scholarly estimates of the total number of affected counties differ, and the figure commonly cited is several dozen, with estimates reaching around seventy locations depending on how incidents are counted. Students should treat the exact number as uncertain.

The process of diffusion was not centrally directed. There was no single headquarters, no unified command, and no common program. Rather, news of Jinju and other outbreaks spread through markets, travelers, official reports, and informal networks, and communities facing similar grievances adopted similar methods. The reports describe repeated patterns: circulars calling villagers to assemble, collective petitions, confrontations at county offices, and attacks on clerks’ homes.

Demands and composition varied by locality. In many places, protests focused on the land tax and on collective levies to cover deficits in the grain system. In others, the grain loan was the dominant issue, or the cloth levy, or the conduct of a particular clerk. Participants included smallholders, tenants, laborers, and in some places locally prominent men who took leadership roles. In some cases, protest leaders came from the lower ranks of local society; in others, they were educated men with their own reasons for dissatisfaction. Jeju’s uprising reflected the island’s particular circumstances and its own local grievances, and should not be treated as a mere extension of the mainland pattern.

Official reaction varied too. Some officials negotiated or made concessions, some fled, and some used force. As reports reached the capital, the court was compelled to treat the protests as a general problem rather than a series of isolated incidents, and the combined pressure of events across several provinces was a major reason the government decided to act.

Section 44.19

Government Investigations and the Samjeong Reform Office

The government sent special inspectors (anhaek-sa) to the affected areas to investigate and restore order. One of them was Park Gyu-su, a scholar-official of distinguished family and intellectual background, who was dispatched to Jinju. His report and memorials are among the most important sources for the uprising, and they are notable because they traced the unrest not merely to the faults of individual officials but to systemic problems in the fiscal administration, especially the grain-loan and related levies. His analysis helped shape the court’s understanding of the crisis.

In response to the unrest, King Cheoljong invited advice on reform, and the court established the Samjeong Reform Office (Samjeong Ijeongcheong) in the fifth lunar month of 1862. The office was charged with reviewing the three administrations and proposing remedies, and it issued its reform regulations in the leap eighth lunar month before being dissolved. Officials and scholars submitted ideas, and the office drafted regulations. Among the proposals associated with this effort was a plan to transform the grain-loan system by ending its function as a compulsory lending system and attaching its revenue needs to the land tax, a proposal that would have shifted the burden toward landholders.

The reform effort had very limited lasting effect. The office was short-lived, and within months the court retreated from its main proposals and largely restored existing arrangements. Historians point to several reasons. Proposals that raised burdens on landholders encountered resistance from the powerful, many of whom were within or near the political elite. The government lacked a reliable administrative apparatus to implement fundamental change, and the fiscal needs that had driven abuses remained. The immediate threat appeared to ease as the uprisings were suppressed, reducing pressure. And the political structure of in-law rule offered few incentives to confront entrenched interests.

The episode nonetheless mattered. It showed that the court recognized the seriousness of the problem, it left a body of analysis and proposals that later reformers would draw upon, and the failure of the reform sharpened the sense that the existing institutions could not reform themselves. Later fiscal measures, discussed in Chapter 47, built on this experience.

Multiple Perspectives

One Fiscal Crisis, Many Experiences

These groups did not hold equal power. Officials and elites could shape the records and the outcomes; tenants and laborers bore the greatest risk and left the fewest documents.

Section 44.20

Peasant Resistance and the Crisis of the Old Order

Taken together, the rebellions of 1811–1812 and 1862 reveal a political order under strain. They show that the fiscal system had drifted from its design, that the institutions of redress had lost credibility for many communities, and that ordinary people were capable of organizing and sustaining collective action. They also show that the Joseon state, despite weakness, retained the capacity to suppress open rebellion, and that it often did so with severity.

The rebellions did not, in themselves, bring down the dynasty, nor did most participants seek that outcome. Their significance lies in what they revealed and what they set in motion. They exposed the limits of the in-law-dominated court’s ability to govern; they demonstrated that grievance could be translated into organized action; and they accustomed rural communities to the idea of collective political action. They also contributed to a climate of insecurity and reform discussion among officials and intellectuals. The approaching era, which Chapters 45 through 48 examine, brought additional pressures: Western ships and missionaries, the spread of Donghak, the Daewongun’s reforms, and the forced opening of 1876. Rural unrest formed the domestic backdrop to all of these developments.

The relationship to later events requires caution. The Donghak Peasant Revolution of 1894, treated in Chapter 51, drew on grievances that overlapped with those of 1862, including fiscal abuse, and on a tradition of collective action, but it also had distinct religious, organizational, and international dimensions. Historians avoid describing it as the inevitable culmination of earlier rebellions. The chapter’s argument is more modest: the crises of 1811 and 1862 helped create the conditions in which later movements could arise, without determining their form or outcome.

  • The underlying fiscal structure of land, cloth, and grain obligations and its dependence on local discretion
  • The role of local clerks and officials as the most visible targets of anger
  • The failure of lawful redress as a precondition for escalation
  • The absence of a program to abolish monarchy or the status order

Why the pattern matters: Comparing the waves prevents two errors: treating them as identical expressions of one peasant movement, or ignoring the shared structural conditions that linked them. Change was uneven and gradual, and those closest to fiscal administration experienced the continuities most directly.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined nineteenth-century Joseon from the perspective of the rural communities that bore the burden of its fiscal system and that, in 1811–1812 and again in 1862, rose in organized rebellion. Its central argument is that resistance grew from the interaction of economic hardship, unequal social and regional power, and the failure of established institutions to resolve grievances. The rebellions were organized political actions with identifiable causes, participants, and demands, not merely spontaneous responses to hunger.

The social foundation was a rural society marked by inequality in landholding, widespread tenancy, and dependence on landlords, moneylenders, and officials. Demographic and environmental pressures varied by region and did not affect all households equally, but they heightened vulnerability, especially for tenants and poorer households. Evidence for these conditions comes largely from official sources, which must be read critically.

The fiscal order centered on the three administrations: the land tax, the military cloth levy, and the grain-loan system. Each was designed for a legitimate purpose, but each came to depend on local discretion, decaying registers, and the incentives of officials who had paid for their posts. Land assessments were distorted by hidden and abandoned land and by cash conversions; the military levy fell on commoners and was extended to the dead, the very young, and the neighbors of the absent; and the grain-loan system was transformed from relief into revenue, producing obligations for grain never received.

Lawful channels of redress, including petitions and appeals, were often blocked by the very officials complained against. Communities developed escalating forms of collective protest, and only where these failed, and where leadership and networks existed, did protest become rebellion. This pattern explains why hardship was widespread but rebellion selective.

Hong Gyeong-nae’s rebellion in Pyeongan Province grew from a distinctive regional setting: a commercial and mining economy, a political elite excluded in practice from high office, and fiscal pressures shared with other regions. Its coalition combined educated local men, wealthy backers, and poorer followers, including mine laborers. Its proclamation emphasized regional discrimination and misrule at court. The rebels won early victories, were checked in the field, and were besieged at Jeongju, which fell after a siege of about four months, followed by severe reprisals.

Interpretations of the rebellion differ. It was regional, political, social, and economic at once, and the sources do not support a single clear program. The Historical Debate showed that scholars differ about whether such rebellions challenged only abusive officials or the broader social order, and that the two readings can be partly reconciled.

The decades after 1812 saw scattered conspiracies and disputes, but no continuous national rebellion. Meanwhile, the fiscal crisis deepened, as granary deficits and emergency levies concentrated grievance in particular counties. In 1862, uprisings beginning at Danseong and Jinju spread across the southern and central provinces, including Jeju. They were linked by shared conditions and methods, not by a central command, and their targets were mainly clerks and fiscal abuses.

The government responded by sending investigators, among them Park Gyu-su, whose analysis located the problem in the fiscal system itself, and by establishing the Samjeong Reform Office. Its proposals, including restructuring the grain-loan system, were largely abandoned within months because of elite resistance, administrative weakness, and the easing of immediate pressure.

The rebellions did not overthrow the dynasty, but they revealed the weakness of its political legitimacy and fiscal capacity and gave rural communities experience in collective action. They formed the domestic backdrop to the pressures examined in the following chapters, though historians avoid treating later movements as their inevitable outcome.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it brings the nineteenth-century crisis down from the court to the village. Understanding the fall of an old order requires attention not only to kings and ministers but to the daily workings of institutions through which ordinary people met the state. The history of taxation, grain lending, and cloth levies is not a technical footnote; it is where political legitimacy was either maintained or lost.

The chapter also reveals how institutions can fail without being abolished. Joseon’s fiscal systems were not overthrown; they drifted, through incentives, decay, and discretion, from their purposes. The rebellions were responses to that drift, and the failure of reform in 1862 showed how difficult it was to correct it from within a political order whose power structure benefited from the status quo.

The chapter shaped what followed. Peasant resistance, regional grievance, and fiscal crisis formed the domestic setting for the pressures of Western encounter, the rise of Donghak, the Daewongun’s reforms, and the forced opening of 1876. It would be a mistake to treat these as the inevitable results of rural unrest, but it would equally be a mistake to understand them without it.

Finally, the chapter offers a lesson in historical method. The rebels’ voices reach us through the records of their opponents, and historians must read carefully to recover aims, experiences, and diversity. That challenge, and the commitment to place communities rather than only rulers at the center of the story, is one the rest of this textbook continues to take seriously.
