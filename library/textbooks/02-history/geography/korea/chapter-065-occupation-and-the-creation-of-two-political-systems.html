Occupation and the Creation of Two Political Systems

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

A line drawn in August 1945 to decide where two armies would accept a Japanese surrender was never meant to divide a country. Within two years, on either side of it, different institutions were collecting taxes, redistributing or defending property, policing the streets, licensing parties, and deciding who could speak for Korea. How did a temporary military boundary become the seam between two systems of political authority before either Korean state formally existed?

Imagine two farming households in the winter of 1946–1947, one a few days' walk north of the 38th parallel and one a few days' walk south of it. Both had lived under the same colonial government until the previous August; both had heard the news of liberation; both had expected that independence would mean changes in how land, grain, and authority were organized. Historians cannot reconstruct any particular household's thoughts, and this chapter will not pretend to. But the institutional record shows that by that winter the two families lived under different governing arrangements. In the north, a locally administered land reform had transferred tenanted land to cultivators without compensation to owners, and a committee system answerable to a Soviet-supervised Korean leadership was extending its reach into villages. In the south, tenancy largely persisted under rent limits that were unevenly enforced, grain was subject to unpopular collection, and an American military government was gradually sharing administrative responsibility with Korean officials while policing political dissent. The two households no longer shared a political world.

This chapter explains how that happened. Its scope runs from the arrival of the occupation armies in September 1945 to the United Nations General Assembly resolution of November 14, 1947, which marked the failure of the original plan for a jointly administered transition to a unified Korean government. Chapter 64 traced the sudden end of Japanese rule, Korean celebrations, the people's committees that formed across the peninsula, and the arrival of foreign troops. Chapter 66 will take up the violence of 1948, the separate elections, and the founding of the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. Chapter 65 occupies the interval in between, the period when neither Korean state existed but the institutions that would become the foundations of both were being built.

The chapter's central argument is that Korea's political division was produced by interaction among several forces rather than by any one of them. Soviet and American occupation policies mattered decisively, because the two commands held the ultimate military authority and the power to recognize or exclude Korean organizations. But Korean political competition also mattered: leaders and parties disagreed profoundly about land, collaboration, trusteeship, anti-communism, and the meaning of democracy, and they chose strategies that sometimes widened and sometimes narrowed the room for compromise. Social conflict mattered, because tenancy, food scarcity, unemployment, and resentment of colonial-era officials generated pressures that no administration could ignore. And the emerging global rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union mattered, because it progressively changed what each occupation power was willing to tolerate in the other's zone and among Korean partners.

The chapter is deliberately comparative. It does not narrate the north as a Soviet story and the south as an American story. Soviet commanders and American generals could and did make decisions that Koreans could not overrule, but in both zones Korean committees, parties, workers, farmers, landowners, police officers, students, and religious communities shaped what policy meant in practice. The comparison also requires care about terminology. Between 1945 and 1947 there were occupation administrations, provisional committees, interim assemblies, and competing claimants to national leadership, but no sovereign Korean states. The North Korean Provisional People's Committee of 1946, the North Korean People's Committee of 1947, and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea of 1948 were different institutions; so were the U.S. Army Military Government in Korea, the South Korean Interim Government of 1947, and the Republic of Korea. Keeping these distinctions visible is part of the chapter's method.

The evidence is uneven in revealing ways. For the American zone, the historian has the records of the military government and of the XXIV Corps, American diplomatic correspondence, press coverage, and a large body of Korean memoirs, newspapers, and later testimony. For the Soviet zone, the record was long dominated by documents captured by American forces during the Korean War and by North Korean official histories shaped by later political needs; since the end of the Soviet Union, Russian archival material has allowed scholars to test many older assumptions, though access remains partial. In both cases, historians must ask who created a record and why, and whose experience it leaves out. Political interpretations of this period also remain entangled with the founding narratives of both modern Korean states, so the chapter distinguishes documented measures from claims made about them afterward.

Finally, the chapter treats division as a process with alternatives rather than as an outcome fixed in 1945. The Moscow agreement of December 1945, the U.S.–Soviet Joint Commission, the left–right coalition movement, and proposals for an all-Korean provisional government were real attempts to create unified institutions. They failed for reasons that can be identified and weighed, and that failure was not predetermined. Students should finish the chapter able to explain both why those attempts collapsed and why, by the end of 1947, the peninsula contained two increasingly incompatible systems of authority. The question to carry through every section is the one that organizes the chapter: how did an initially temporary military division develop into opposing systems of political authority before either Korean state was formally founded?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why occupation constitutes a form of governing authority and distinguish the legal and practical powers of the Soviet command and of the U.S. Army Military Government in Korea.
  2. Compare how Soviet and American authorities worked with, constrained, or excluded Korean political organizations in their respective zones.
  3. Trace the institutional sequence in northern Korea from local people's committees to the Provisional People's Committee of 1946 and the People's Committee of 1947.
  4. Analyze the northern land reform of March 1946, its mechanics, its social effects, and its political consequences, distinguishing it from later southern land reform.
  5. Assess the labor, gender-equality, and nationalization measures of 1946 as both social reforms and instruments of political consolidation.
  6. Describe the economic consequences of dividing an integrated colonial economy, including food, inflation, vested Japanese property, and interregional trade.
  7. Evaluate competing interpretations of the September–October 1946 strike and uprising in the south, using different kinds of evidence.
  8. Distinguish the actual terms of the December 1945 Moscow decision from contemporary press reports and from later political memory.
  9. Explain why the Joint Commissions and the left–right coalition movement failed, identifying structural, institutional, and contingent causes.
  10. Differentiate proposals, enacted measures, operating institutions, and outcomes, and apply that distinction to the chronology of 1945–1947.
  11. Interpret primary sources, including a proclamation, a decree, a diplomatic decision, and a United Nations resolution, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limits.
  12. Contextualize the creation of two political systems within Korean social conflict and the emerging Cold War, avoiding teleological accounts of inevitable division.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did Korean organizations that formed in August 1945 respond to the arrival of two occupation commands, and which of them gained or lost position as a result?
  3. Why did the northern and southern occupation zones adopt such different approaches to land, industry, policing, and political parties?
  4. In what respects were northern reforms driven by Soviet policy, and in what respects by Korean actors, local committees, and social demand?

  9. What evidence allows historians to distinguish democratic claims from democratic practice in each zone?

Historical Context

Students need five pieces of background, most of them developed in earlier chapters. First, the colonial economy and state that Japan left behind had been built as an integrated whole, but with a regional division of labor. Heavy industry, mining, chemicals, and hydroelectric power were concentrated disproportionately in the north, partly because of wartime industrial expansion and the strategic position of Manchuria (Chapter 61). Rice cultivation, light industry, and the densest tenant-farming populations were concentrated disproportionately in the south, where roughly two-thirds of the peninsula's people lived. Rail lines, power grids, and commercial networks ran north to south across the 38th parallel, which had no previous administrative significance.

Second, the colonial state had been a military-police regime with a large bureaucracy staffed at its upper levels by Japanese and at its lower levels by many Koreans (Chapters 55 and 60). When it collapsed in August 1945, Koreans faced two urgent questions: who would run the administrative machinery, and what would be done about those who had served it. Third, as Chapter 64 explained, Koreans responded to Japan's surrender with a wave of self-organization. Local committees formed to maintain order, distribute food, and take over offices and factories, and on August 15 the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence under Yeo Un-hyeong began to link them. In early September a national assembly proclaimed the Korean People's Republic. Korea did not enter the occupation as a political vacuum.

Fourth, the Allies had made only general commitments about Korea's future. The Cairo Declaration of 1943 had promised that Korea would become free and independent "in due course," a phrase Koreans read with suspicion, and wartime American planning had floated the idea of a multi-power trusteeship that might last decades. The line at the 38th parallel was a late improvisation. In the closing days of the war, American military planners proposed it as the boundary for receiving Japanese surrenders, and the Soviet command did not object. It was a military convenience, but it assigned the Soviet Army the north and American forces the south, and it placed the old capital, Seoul, in the American zone.

Fifth, the international setting was changing. The wartime alliance between the United States and the Soviet Union was fraying over Eastern Europe, Iran, atomic weapons, and the occupation of Germany and Japan. Korea was not initially a central issue for either power, and neither had a detailed plan for it. That ambiguity left decisions to local commanders, visiting officials, and Korean intermediaries to a degree that later Cold War framing tends to obscure. This chapter treats the intensifying rivalry as a growing pressure on Korean affairs between 1945 and 1947, not as a cause already fully formed in September 1945.

Division I — Two Occupation Zones and the Struggle for Political Authority

Sections 65.1–65.4 · September–December 1945. Two foreign commands held ultimate military authority, but Korean organizations actively contested how political power should be exercised.

Section 65.1

From Liberation to Military Occupation

The surrender arrangements that governed Korea were made by militaries for military purposes. In mid-August 1945, as the Soviet Union entered the war against Japan and Japanese resistance collapsed, American planners in Washington needed a boundary that would specify where Soviet forces and American forces would accept the surrender of Japanese troops on the peninsula. Staff officers proposed the 38th parallel, a line that placed Seoul to the south while leaving a substantial portion of the peninsula to the Soviet side. Soviet forces, already advancing into the north, accepted. General Order No. 1, issued under Allied authority, formalized the division of surrender responsibilities. The documentary record does not show a decision to partition Korea politically. It shows a decision about who would receive a surrender, taken quickly, with little consultation of Koreans and with limited attention to the economic and administrative consequences.

Yet the effects of that decision were never merely military. Soviet troops reached Pyongyang in late August; American troops of the XXIV Corps under Lieutenant General John R. Hodge landed at Incheon on September 8, and a surrender ceremony in Seoul followed on September 9. Each army arrived to a country in which Koreans had already begun organizing, and each army arrived with incomplete knowledge of Korean politics. Few American officers spoke Korean; few Soviet officers had detailed knowledge of local Korean society, although the Soviet side could draw on Korean-speaking personnel, including ethnic Koreans from the Soviet Union. Both commands brought instructions to keep order, disarm and repatriate Japanese forces, and prevent chaos. Neither arrived with a worked-out program for building a Korean government.

It is important to understand occupation as a form of governing authority rather than simply the presence of soldiers. An occupation command asserts the right to make binding rules, to decide which existing institutions may continue to function, to control the movement of people and goods across the zone's boundaries, to detain and try persons who violate its orders, and to recognize or withhold recognition from local authorities. In both zones, the occupation command in practice exercised each of these powers. That authority was real even though it was legally limited and politically provisional. Neither command claimed sovereignty over Korea, and both stated that Korea would eventually be independent. But the interval between the departure of Japanese power and the creation of a Korean government lasted three years, and decisions made by occupation authorities during it, about property, policing, parties, and publications, had durable consequences.

Koreans in both zones expected independence, and they expected it soon. The record of newspapers, petitions, and committee declarations from the autumn of 1945 shows a broad assumption that liberation meant a Korean government, and strong disagreement about what kind. This expectation was a political force in its own right. It made any occupation policy that appeared to delay or condition independence a source of immediate controversy, and it gave Korean organizations reason to compete for recognition from the occupying armies. The absence of any internationally recognized Korean government, the Provisional Government in exile having been recognized by no major power, meant that foreign commands held the power to confer legitimacy, and Korean groups understood this.

Chapter 64 treated the arrival of the occupation armies as the end of the liberation narrative. This chapter begins where that account stops, with the question of what the armies did once they were established. The answer differed between zones from the start, not because policy had already diverged but because the two commands inherited different circumstances. In the north the Soviet Army entered a region with strong local committees, a concentrated industrial base, and a predominantly rural society with a substantial tenant population. In the south the American command entered a zone with a larger and more politically diverse population, the colonial capital, and the greatest concentration of landlords and of the colonial bureaucracy's Korean personnel. These starting conditions did not determine what followed, but they shaped the choices available to each command and to Korean actors within each zone.

A border that separated military commands also separated jurisdictions. A traveler carrying rice, newspaper copies, or a party message across the line now encountered decisions by two sets of authorities. Transport permits and military checkpoints did not immediately create an international frontier; nevertheless, they generated practical obstacles to an integrated political life. This is why the term temporary must describe the original purpose of the arrangement, not its social effects. An improvised boundary could become consequential before anyone declared that it would be permanent.

Section 65.2

Soviet Administration in Northern Korea, 1945

Soviet forces in northern Korea were organized under the Soviet 25th Army, commanded by Colonel General Ivan Chistyakov, part of the larger Soviet force that had fought Japan in Manchuria. The army established a headquarters in Pyongyang and set up a civil administration to deal with day-to-day governance. In the first weeks, Soviet commanders issued public statements telling Koreans that the army had come to defeat Japan and that Koreans were free to reestablish political and economic life. These statements were sincere in the sense that they described actual early practice: the Soviet command did not immediately dissolve the local committees that Koreans had formed, and for some weeks it used them as administrative partners.

Those committees had varied origins and varied politics. In many places they were local coalitions of nationalists, Christians, teachers, former officials, landowners, and communists who had come together to maintain order after Japanese authority collapsed. In Pyongyang and South Pyongan Province, the committee was chaired by Cho Man-sik, a Christian nationalist with a long record of nonviolent activism and wide local respect. In other regions, communists and leftists were stronger. The Soviet command worked with this mixed field. It recognized committees, issued orders through them, and also began identifying Korean figures with whom it could build a more reliable political structure. Historians of the period, drawing on both Soviet and Korean sources, emphasize that the initial northern political scene was more plural than later official narratives admit, and that the shift toward centralization was gradual.

The Soviet command's priorities were security, supply, and the establishment of a friendly government on its border. Soviet authorities seized or controlled Japanese-owned industrial assets, stopped the departure of some resources, arranged the repatriation of Japanese civilians and soldiers, and, in several documented cases, removed equipment and materials to the Soviet Union as war compensation. They also supervised food collection and distribution, an area in which the troops themselves were a burden on local supplies. Soldiers' conduct toward civilians became a source of resentment; the record includes reports of theft, violence, and sexual assault by Soviet troops in the first months, and Korean memoirs from the north commonly recall them. Soviet authorities attempted to discipline some of this behavior, with uneven success.

Political direction came increasingly from Soviet officers charged with Korean affairs, among them Terenty Shtykov, who became the dominant Soviet voice in northern politics. These officers began promoting a Korean leadership that would cooperate with Soviet aims. Kim Il Sung, who returned to Korea in September 1945 after years in the Soviet Far East and an earlier career as a guerrilla commander in Manchuria, was introduced to the Pyongyang public in October. Historians continue to debate how thoroughly Soviet authorities had planned to elevate him from the outset and how much his position resulted from contingencies of the autumn, but there is little doubt that Soviet sponsorship was decisive. Other northern figures also had backing: Korean communists who had remained in the peninsula, communists who had returned from the Soviet Union, and the Yan'an group that returned from China in December.

A distinction between initial diversity and subsequent centralization is essential. In late 1945, Cho Man-sik's group, Christian networks, local nationalists, and communist organizations competed and sometimes cooperated. Over the following months, however, the Soviet command and its Korean allies built central administrative departments, a communist-led organizational apparatus, and mechanisms to limit the autonomy of rival organizations. Disputes over trusteeship in early 1946, discussed in Section 65.13, accelerated the process. Protest in Sinuiju by students in November 1945, which Soviet and Korean security forces suppressed with casualties, showed that opposition existed and that the new authorities would respond forcefully.

The early northern administration was simultaneously military and local. A provincial committee might continue to organize food supplies and schools while Soviet commanders determined which police officers could serve, which factories could resume operations, and how Japanese property would be treated. Korean-speaking intermediaries were politically influential because they translated instructions, interpreted local disputes, and recommended personnel. These mundane decisions mattered as much as speeches by prominent leaders: a committee able to distribute grain, make arrests, or reopen a mill acquired authority over residents' daily lives. Korean initiative therefore cannot be inferred simply from the Korean name on an office door; historians must ask who could overrule its decisions, and with what instruments.

Cho Man-sik's role illustrates another feature of the transition. He could command broad respect as a Christian nationalist without controlling the military resources on which the administration ultimately depended. His relationship with the Soviet command deteriorated during the trusteeship controversy, but the political field before that rupture contained genuine uncertainty about who might lead an eventual northern government. Later official histories, which portray the rise of one leader as an uninterrupted popular mandate, obscure those alternatives. Conversely, accounts that treat every northern reform as mechanically dictated from Moscow underestimate the local constituencies that supported changes in landholding and economic power.

Section 65.3

The United States Army Military Government in Korea

American authority in the south was formalized before American troops landed. On September 7, 1945, General Douglas MacArthur, as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, issued Proclamation No. 1 to the people of Korea. The proclamation announced that American forces had occupied the territory south of the 38th parallel, that all powers of government over that territory and its people would be exercised under MacArthur's authority, and that the population should obey. It also stated that the English text would prevail in cases of divergence from Korean translations. The proclamation was a military instrument of occupation, not a charter for a Korean government.

When Hodge's XXIV Corps arrived, it created the U.S. Army Military Government in Korea, usually abbreviated USAMGIK. The structure placed a military governor over departments headed by American officers, with Hodge as commander holding ultimate authority. Initial policy was shaped by an early blunder that has become well known: in the first days, American commanders indicated that Japanese officials, including the governor-general and many Japanese administrators, would continue to carry out functions during the transition. Korean outrage was immediate and intense. Within days the Americans reversed course, removing the Japanese governor-general and ordering Japanese personnel out of office, but the episode damaged American credibility and suggested to many Koreans that the occupiers valued administrative continuity over Korean aspirations.

USAMGIK faced difficulties of capacity. The occupation force was not trained for civil administration in a country whose language, history, and politics were unfamiliar to most of its officers. Interpreters became crucial intermediaries, and the Korean elites who spoke English, often Christians educated in American missions, professionals, and landowners, gained disproportionate access to American decision-makers. American officers consulted a Korean advisory body in autumn 1945, drawn largely from conservative and propertied circles, and in February 1946 helped form the Representative Democratic Council, chaired by Syngman Rhee. These choices shaped southern politics by favoring groups that were anti-communist, had resources, and could communicate with the military government.

USAMGIK also retained a significant part of the colonial administrative apparatus, especially at lower levels. This was partly a matter of necessity: the colonial government had employed tens of thousands of Koreans as clerks, technicians, teachers, and police, and dismissing them all would have halted services. Retention was also a matter of policy and ideology, since American officers valued experience and order and distrusted the committees that had formed after liberation, many of which were led by leftists. The consequences, explored in Section 65.11, were far-reaching, because the continuation of officials who had served Japan clashed with popular expectations of a clean break.

The relationship to the Korean People's Republic is a key moment. The KPR, proclaimed on September 6 by an assembly in Seoul, claimed to be the national government of Korea and had a network of local people's committees. Hodge's command rejected it. In December 1945, USAMGIK issued a statement declaring that the military government was the only government in southern Korea. Americans understood the KPR as dominated by communists and as a potential rival to occupation authority; many Koreans, including non-communist participants in the KPR, saw it as a legitimate expression of liberation. In the ensuing months, military government increasingly treated local committees as illegitimate, dissolving them or replacing them with appointed officials, which in many places provoked conflict.

Historical Vocabulary
Military Government

Definition: A form of administration in which an occupying army exercises governmental powers over a territory, usually under the authority of its commander, until civilian rule is established or transferred.

Historical context: USAMGIK governed southern Korea from September 1945 to August 1948. Its legal basis was MacArthur's Proclamation No. 1 and its own ordinances, issued by the military governor. The Soviet command in the north exercised comparable authority through a different institutional form, working through Korean committees while retaining ultimate military control.

Why it matters: Students should recognize that military government is not the same as a colonial administration or a sovereign state. It was legally temporary, subject to Allied policy, and accountable in theory to a later transfer of power to Koreans.

Potential misunderstanding: "Military government" does not mean every official was a soldier. In practice, American officers supervised a growing number of Korean civil servants, and Korean departments gradually gained responsibility, as Section 65.16 shows.

The difference between legality and perceived legitimacy became visible almost immediately. Officials failed when they assumed one problem could be solved without addressing the other.

Section 65.4

Korean Political Parties and Competing Claims to Authority

While occupation armies held military power, Korean politics in the autumn of 1945 was exuberant, crowded, and divided. Dozens of parties and organizations formed within weeks, many of them small or short-lived. Understanding the major clusters is essential, because the competition among them shaped how each occupation command chose partners and how Korean politics evolved.

The Korean People's Republic and the people's committees formed the broadest left-of-center and popular network. The KPR drew on the Preparatory Committee under Yeo Un-hyeong, on activists released from colonial prisons, on leftist and nationalist participants, and on local committees. Its proclamation named exiled and absent leaders, including Syngman Rhee and Kim Ku, to leadership posts, which showed an aim of national inclusiveness but also created disputes when those figures refused to accept the offices. Its program included land reform, punishment of collaborators, and workers' rights. The KPR was neither a communist creation nor a uniform movement, though communists were prominent within it, and historians still debate how much of its local network operated as a national government in practice.

The Korean Communist Party, reorganized in Seoul in September 1945 under Pak Hon-yong, sought to lead the left. It operated alongside, and sometimes in competition with, other leftist organizations and with the Pyongyang communist leadership, which was becoming a distinct center. Yeo Un-hyeong's own Korean People's Party sought a moderate-left position, supporting social reform but opposing the dominance of any single party. Yeo became the most visible advocate of compromise among Korean factions, a position that would define his later role.

On the right and center-right, the Korean Democratic Party, founded in mid-September 1945, gathered landowners, businessmen, professionals, and former officials, among them Kim Seong-su and Song Jin-woo. It supported the Provisional Government in principle, resisted the KPR, opposed rapid land redistribution, and cultivated ties with American officers. Opponents attacked the KDP as a refuge for people who had cooperated with Japanese rule; supporters answered that many of its members had contributed to nationalist causes and that the KDP defended property and order against a radical left. Both claims had some evidential support, and both were used politically.

Returning leaders added further poles. Syngman Rhee, who returned in October 1945 after decades abroad, organized the Independence Promotion Central Council and rapidly became the best-known right-wing nationalist, combining firm anti-communism with demands for immediate independence. Kim Ku and other Provisional Government members returned in November and December, and Kim Ku's Korean Independence Party advocated a Provisional Government–centered path and, in time, opposed trusteeship fiercely. Kim Kyu-sik, a veteran nationalist, took a more centrist position. Ahn Jae-hong, a nationalist who held advisory posts, mediated between right-wing groups and American authorities.

These organizations differed not only in who they represented but in what they meant by democracy and independence. Leftists emphasized economic democracy, land redistribution, and the removal of collaborators. Conservatives emphasized national sovereignty, order, property rights, and anti-communism, and many regarded immediate popular mobilization as dangerous. Moderates emphasized compromise and broad participation. All invoked democracy; their definitions conflicted. The competition took place not in a neutral arena but under occupation commands that favored some partners. The American command preferred groups it considered anti-communist and cooperative, which tilted the southern field toward the right. In the north, as Section 65.2 showed, Soviet support began to tilt the field toward the left, in particular toward communists aligned with Soviet policy.

It is therefore wrong to describe the autumn of 1945 as a clash between a united Korean people and two foreign armies, and equally wrong to describe it as a mere proxy contest. Korean groups had their own histories, from the colonial-era nationalist and socialist movements examined in Chapters 59 and 63, and their own goals. The occupation commands altered the opportunities and risks they faced, however, and the interplay between Korean choices and occupation preferences would produce, by 1947, increasingly separate political environments.

The parties' social bases also changed as the occupation proceeded. The organization capable of opening offices, financing newspapers, and placing advisers near the military government acquired opportunities unavailable to groups whose members were workers or political detainees. Yet membership labels are imperfect guides to political belief. A local committee could include religious nationalists, socialists, and independent townspeople; a conservative group could include people whose chief priority was a rapid end to foreign rule. The cleavages were increasingly sharpened by occupation decisions about recognition, press access, and policing. This helps explain why the same politician could support compromise at one moment and withdraw after a particular arrest, appointment, or diplomatic announcement.

Division II — Political Consolidation and Social Transformation in Northern Korea

Sections 65.5–65.8 · Northern Korea, 1945–1947. Soviet-backed institutions and Korean political actors established the foundations of a distinctive northern governing system before the DPRK existed.

Section 65.5

The North Korean Provisional People's Committee and Kim Il Sung's Rise

By late 1945 the northern provinces were governed through a patchwork: local and provincial people's committees, Soviet military and civil officers, and a growing set of regional administrative bureaus. Coordination was difficult. Each province had its own committee with its own politics, and economic management, taxation, and security required common rules. Between the autumn and the winter, Korean and Soviet officials created central administrative departments to coordinate finance, industry, transport, agriculture, and public order. These departments were the institutional ancestors of a northern government.

The formal step came in February 1946. On February 8, a conference of representatives of northern political parties, social organizations, and administrative bodies established the North Korean Provisional People's Committee, with Kim Il Sung as chairman. The committee was designated provisional: it claimed no authority over the whole of Korea and was understood by its founders as a transitional body pending the creation of a national government. It combined executive, administrative, and, at first, quasi-legislative functions. It issued decrees, supervised departments, and coordinated the lower-level people's committees. Its establishment signaled that northern authorities were no longer content with provincial arrangements and that Soviet policy was now committed to the construction of an effective regional administration.

Kim Il Sung's leadership of the committee reflected several converging facts. He had a record as a guerrilla leader against Japan in Manchuria that, whatever its exact scale (a subject examined in Chapter 63 and later in Chapter 77), gave him nationalist credentials; he had spent years in the Soviet Far East and enjoyed Soviet trust; he headed the northern organization of the Communist Party from December 1945; and he was younger than rivals such as Cho Man-sik and the Yan'an returnees and therefore perhaps less threatening as a nominee. Soviet support was decisive, but historians stress that Korean acceptance also mattered. Kim's early public speeches emphasized national unity, anti-Japanese struggle, and reform, rather than socialism as such, and the broad coalition around the committee included non-communists, at least at first.

The displacement of Cho Man-sik illustrates how that coalition narrowed. Cho opposed the trusteeship provision of the Moscow decision, and in early January 1946, when Soviet authorities and the northern communists pressed the northern committees to support the decision, he refused. He was placed under house arrest and removed from political life, and his fate thereafter was obscure; historians generally conclude he died in custody, probably during the Korean War, though the record is incomplete. His removal sent a message to other non-communist leaders. Those who remained in the Democratic Party under new leadership, notably Choe Yong-geon, accepted a subordinate role within a managed coalition.

The committee's responsibilities grew quickly. Within weeks of its creation it issued the land-reform decree discussed in Section 65.7, followed by laws on labor, gender equality, and the nationalization of industry. It administered newly assumed property, coordinated state purchasing of grain, and organized a network of departments and local offices. It also began to build security forces: a police system, and, in time, militarized units that would be precursors of an army. In all of this, Korean officials did the day-to-day work, but Soviet advisers supervised, reviewed, and sometimes initiated decisions. The balance between Soviet direction and Korean initiative varied by issue. The land-reform decree, for example, rested on long-standing Korean communist and nationalist demands as well as on Soviet advice, whereas some security and economic matters were more tightly controlled by the Soviet command.

It is important not to read later history back into this stage. The Provisional People's Committee was not the government of the DPRK, and its chairman was not yet the sole leader of the north. Competitors existed within the northern leadership, including domestic communists, Soviet Koreans, and Yan'an veterans, with different backgrounds and loyalties. The process by which Kim Il Sung consolidated personal dominance over these factions took years and is treated in Chapter 77. What the Provisional Committee established in 1946 was a structure of effective regional government that Koreans staffed and that Soviet authorities supervised.

Administrative centralization required more than selecting a chairman. Departments had to prepare budgets, coordinate provincial committees, collect information, issue regulations, and supervise the security institutions that enforced decisions. Personnel selection was especially consequential. Koreans who had acquired administrative experience under Japanese rule worked alongside returnees from the Soviet Union and China, each group bringing different networks and political reputations. The new executive increasingly served as a link between provincial bodies and the Soviet military command. Its rise did not eliminate the influence of individual provincial leaders at once, but it established procedures through which local disagreements could be settled by officials above them.

Section 65.6

Parties, People's Committees, Police, and Political Control in the North

The political structure of northern Korea combined formal pluralism with growing control. The Communist Party's North Korean branch, formed in October 1945 and led by Kim Il Sung from December, built a nucleus of organized cadres. In 1946 it merged with the New People's Party, led by Kim Tu-bong and composed largely of Yan'an returnees, to form the Workers' Party of North Korea at a congress in late August. Kim Tu-bong became chairman and Kim Il Sung vice-chairman, an arrangement that reflected the party's effort to unite different communist streams. The merger broadened the party into a mass organization; by the end of the year its membership numbered in the hundreds of thousands, with large numbers of peasants and workers joining.

Other parties continued to exist. The Democratic Party, founded in late 1945 by Cho Man-sik with support from Christians, merchants, and small property-holders, remained legal under new leadership, and the Chondoist Chongu Party was organized in early 1946 to draw on the Cheondogyo religious community, a movement with deep rural roots (Chapter 46). In July 1946, northern authorities formed the Democratic National United Front, an umbrella that bound the parties and mass organizations into a common framework led by the communists. Parties other than the Workers' Party kept names, offices, and some members, but operated within limits set by the front and were prevented from forming genuine opposition. This arrangement is sometimes called a controlled multiparty system; its character was that of a managed coalition.

Mass organizations extended party influence. Unions, peasant associations, youth leagues, and women's leagues organized much of the population and mobilized it for reforms, meetings, and elections. They also served as channels for surveillance and for the diffusion of party messages. For many ordinary participants, joining brought tangible benefits: access to land, jobs, education, and status. It also brought obligations to attend meetings and to avoid expressing opposition. The voluntary and coercive dimensions of participation were intertwined, and the evidence, including later defector testimony and captured records, shows both genuine enthusiasm among beneficiaries of reform and fear among those who were excluded.

People's committees at the local level were reorganized through elections. In November 1946, northern authorities held elections for provincial, city, and county people's committees. Voters were presented with a single list of candidates approved by the Democratic National United Front, and official reports recorded overwhelming approval. The format permitted voters to express assent or dissent, in the sense that they could vote for or against the list, but did not offer competing candidates. The elections therefore conferred legitimacy and built administrative structures, but they cannot be described as competitive. The distinction between democratic claims and democratic practice is central here: authorities described the committees as expressions of popular sovereignty, a claim grounded partly in genuine popular participation in land reform and local administration, while restricting the organized opposition that competitive democracy requires.

Historical Vocabulary
People's Democracy

Definition: A term used by Soviet-aligned governments in the 1940s to describe a transitional political order led by a coalition of parties under communist guidance, in which "anti-feudal" and "anti-imperialist" reforms were to precede socialism.

Historical context: Northern Korean leaders used the language of democracy and people's committees to describe their system, stressing land reform, workers' rights, and national independence. Opponents, in the south and abroad, described the same system as a communist dictatorship.

Why it matters: Understanding how participants used the term allows students to evaluate claims and counterclaims about democracy without simply accepting or dismissing either.

Potential misunderstanding: "People's democracy" did not mean competitive multiparty elections of the liberal-democratic kind, nor was it simply a label for the later DPRK system, which developed distinctive features such as Juche and hereditary leadership (Chapter 77).

Security institutions developed in parallel. In late 1945 and 1946, northern authorities built police and security organizations, staffed initially by a mixture of former anti-Japanese activists, local men, and some former colonial police, though the latter were screened and many dismissed. Soviet advisers trained and supervised them. The security services monitored political opponents, landlords, collaborators, Christians perceived as hostile, and members of rival parties. Detentions, trials, and expulsions occurred, and many persons identified as hostile fled to the south. The scale of that outflow, in the hundreds of thousands by 1947 according to varying estimates, shaped both zones. In the north it removed critics and property-holders; in the south it added refugees with strongly anti-communist views.

Soviet involvement in the construction of this ruling coalition is well documented but must be described precisely. Soviet officers did not draft every decree or manage every committee. They did set limits, approve key personnel, supply material support, and intervene when Korean decisions conflicted with Soviet priorities. Korean leaders, in turn, used Soviet backing to defeat rivals and to implement programs they favored. The relationship was asymmetric but not one-directional, and it changed over time as Korean institutions gained experience and confidence. The result by the end of 1946 was a centralized ruling coalition with real social roots among beneficiaries of reform and with substantial coercive capacity.

The November 1946 local committee elections are a useful test of the difference between constitutional language and political choice. Election rules formally referred to broad suffrage and a secret ballot, yet only organizations within the approved united front could nominate candidates. Contemporary arrangements that placed affirmative and negative votes in visibly different ballot boxes weakened secrecy and made opposition conspicuous. A strong official turnout or approval figure therefore cannot by itself establish unrestricted electoral competition. The elections nevertheless served a practical organizational function: they linked local committees, mass organizations, party officials, and the central administration into a structured hierarchy, and the elected bodies supplied delegates to the assembly that met in February 1947.

Section 65.7

Land Reform in Northern Korea, March 1946

The land reform of March 1946 was the most socially consequential measure taken in northern Korea in these years. Agrarian conditions explain why. Under Japanese rule, a substantial share of cultivators in Korea were tenants or part-tenants, paying rents that often consumed a large portion of the harvest. Rural poverty and the demand for land redistribution had been central themes of the colonial-era peasant and socialist movements (Chapter 58), and the demand was taken up by committees across the peninsula in autumn 1945. In the north, authorities initially limited rents, reducing tenants' payments, before moving to full redistribution.

The decree of March 5, 1946, issued by the Provisional People's Committee, confiscated land belonging to the Japanese state and individuals, to Koreans classified as collaborators, to the landlords who held more than a stated maximum of land (five jeongbo, roughly five hectares), and to all landowners who rented out their land rather than farming it themselves. The confiscated land was distributed free to landless peasants, tenant farmers, and farm laborers, who received it as private property but could not sell, mortgage, or lease it. No compensation was paid to the former owners. Livestock, farm implements, and certain buildings of confiscated landlords were also redistributed. A subsequent agricultural tax in kind, set at a quarter of the harvest, replaced rents.

Implementation was rapid. Rural committees composed of hired laborers, poor peasants, and tenants identified land, classified owners, and allocated plots, in many places within a matter of weeks. Estimates of the amount of land transferred are on the order of one million hectares, distributed to several hundred thousand households; figures vary by source, and students should treat precise totals cautiously. For many poor families the reform meant for the first time owning the land they worked, and the record of oral testimony and contemporary northern accounts indicates strong support among beneficiaries. The process also drew poor villagers into committees and mass organizations, building a social base for the new authorities.

The reform's costs fell on landlords and their families. Former owners lost their holdings and, in many cases, their standing in the village. Some were allowed to receive land in other districts if they would farm it themselves, and some did. Others were subjected to harassment, denunciation, and in some cases violence, and many moved south. Christian landowners and clergy, who often held land and distrusted communist rule, were disproportionately among those who left. The reform therefore produced both a redistribution of land and a redistribution of population, adding to the movement of refugees across the parallel.

The reform had limits. Because distribution followed local conditions and classification judgments, some families received little and disputes over allocations were common. The prohibition on sale and mortgage meant the new owners' rights were constrained, and the tax in kind imposed a substantial burden. Later in the decade and in the 1950s, northern authorities moved toward collectivization, ending the individual smallholding that the 1946 reform had created (Chapter 77). The reform's political effect was nevertheless strong. It destroyed the landlord class as an economic and political force, bound many peasants to the new order, and gave northern leaders a concrete achievement to contrast with the unresolved land question in the south. It must not be confused with later land reform in the Republic of Korea, which differed in timing, administration, compensation, and political consequences and belongs to a later chronology.

Students should separate the reform's legal categories from its numerical outcomes. The surviving statistical series commonly reproduced in northern yearbooks listed approximately 1,000,325 chŏngbo of confiscated land, but the amount actually assigned to farming households was lower, approximately 981,390 in that series, with about 724,522 recipient households. A chŏngbo was approximately one hectare, so the units should not be casually changed into acres or interpreted as an exact count of productive fields. The statistics originated in a government that regarded agrarian reform as a central political achievement; historians should therefore verify both arithmetic and categories rather than treating the totals as an independent census. The distinction between confiscation, distribution, and land reserved or otherwise disposed of is indispensable to any serious comparison with later southern reform.

Distribution involved political decisions at village level. Committees had to identify landlords, distinguish rented land from owner-operated plots, decide whether a household was entitled to receive land, and resolve disputes over boundaries and soil quality. A claim of ownership did not itself supply tools, seed, irrigation, or protection against a bad harvest. Officials therefore linked reform to tax collection, the reorganization of village leadership, and efforts to restore production. In areas where cultivators had long worked for a landlord, the psychological change could be substantial: rents were no longer paid to a private proprietor, but obligations to the new administration remained. That distinction helps prevent students from equating redistribution with complete economic independence.

Competing descriptions of the process should be read against their political contexts. Northern official histories emphasized grateful recipients and peaceful implementation; refugee recollections stressed coercion, denunciations, and the loss of family property. Both kinds of evidence illuminate different positions in the rural hierarchy. Scholars using archival material can compare decrees, local reports, statistical tables, memoirs, and subsequent migration patterns. The reform profoundly weakened the landed elite and strengthened the emerging administration, while creating genuine beneficiaries whose support cannot be explained solely by coercion. Its achievement and its political function were two aspects of the same historical process.

Section 65.8

Industry, Labor, Gender, Education, and the Northern Social Revolution

Land reform was followed by a series of measures that extended the transformation to industry, labor, family law, and education. In the summer of 1946 the Provisional People's Committee issued a labor law, usually dated to June 24, that established an eight-hour working day, paid leave, social insurance provisions, and protections for young workers and women. On July 30, it issued a law on the equality of the sexes. And on August 10, it issued a decree nationalizing major industries, mines, railways, transport and communications, and banks, particularly those formerly owned by the Japanese or by Koreans classified as collaborators.

The nationalization decree had large practical effects. Much of the north's heavy industry had been Japanese-owned, and following liberation it had been taken over by Soviet authorities, workers' committees, or the people's committees. The decree put these assets under the state and incorporated them into planning. The north's industrial base, though damaged by the war's end and by the removal of some equipment to the Soviet Union, became the core of a state-owned economy. Korean technicians and managers, many of whom had worked under Japanese direction, were retained or promoted, and the shortage of trained personnel after the departure of Japanese specialists was a persistent constraint.

The gender-equality law is one of the most significant social measures of the period. It declared women equal to men in political, economic, cultural, and family life, granted women the right to vote and be elected, established equal pay for equal work, and prohibited practices such as concubinage and the buying and selling of women, while granting women the right to initiate divorce and to inherit property. The law responded to demands articulated by Korean women's organizations since the colonial period (Chapter 57). Implementation was uneven. Women entered schools, factories, and local committees in considerable numbers, and the Democratic Women's League mobilized many of them. But patriarchal norms persisted in households and villages, and women's participation was channeled through party-directed organizations rather than autonomous groups.

Education and literacy campaigns were a further pillar. Authorities opened schools, expanded access to primary education, organized adult literacy classes, and began building institutions of higher education, including a university in Pyongyang founded in 1946. Literacy drives reached many adults who had never attended school, among them women and farmers, and the curriculum emphasized anti-colonial history, national culture, and the new order. The campaigns offered real opportunities for people previously excluded from schooling and also served as instruments of political socialization.

The northern social revolution should be assessed in both of its aspects. For many peasants, workers, and women, the reforms brought tangible gains: land, shorter hours, legal equality, and schooling. These gains explain the genuine support the authorities received in some quarters. At the same time, the reforms were carried out by a leadership that restricted opposition, expelled or detained critics, and monitored the population. People classified as landlords, collaborators, or hostile elements lost property and status and had few legal recourses. Reform and coercion were not separate phases but simultaneous features of the same process. Historians who emphasize the reforms' popular dimension and those who emphasize their coercive dimension both draw on real evidence, and a full account requires both.

By the end of 1946 the north had put in place institutional foundations that were distinctive: a central committee system, a communist-led coalition, a nationalized industrial sector, a redistributed countryside, and mass organizations. These were not yet a state: Soviet military authority remained, the committees claimed only provisional status, and the national question remained open. But they gave the north a head start in institution-building over the south, and they created conditions that would make any future unification on terms acceptable to southern conservatives and northern leaders extremely difficult.

The legal reforms did not reach society with equal speed. The labor law of June 24, 1946 provided for an eight-hour workday, protections for mothers and children, and improved conditions for wage earners; the women's equality law of July 30 promised rights in employment, inheritance, political participation, marriage, and divorce. Those provisions were significant departures from many inherited practices, but legal rights depended on functioning courts, employers' compliance, literacy, and the ability of women to participate in organizations without coercion or stigma. Women mobilized through new associations also encountered a leadership system that remained heavily male. A textbook should therefore distinguish laws on paper from the uneven social negotiations through which their promises acquired practical meaning.

Division III — The American Occupation and the Social Crisis in Southern Korea

Sections 65.9–65.12 · Southern Korea, 1945–1947. The military government struggled to establish legitimacy, and political conflict intensified around policing, land, food, labor, and anti-communism.

Section 65.9

Economic Dislocation, Japanese Property, Inflation, and Food Shortages

The southern economy in 1945–1947 faced a combination of shocks that would have strained any administration. The first was the severing of the colonial economy's regional connections. Industrial inputs, coal, chemical fertilizer, and much of the electric power that southern factories and cities relied on came from the north. The south in turn had supplied rice and light manufactures. Once the parallel became a zone boundary controlled by two commands with no agreed arrangements for exchange, these flows were disrupted. Rail traffic across the line was restricted, and negotiations to arrange commerce, called for in the Moscow decision, did not produce durable arrangements.

The second shock was demographic. More than a million Koreans returned from Japan, Manchuria, and other parts of the former empire in 1945–1946 in what was among the largest population movements of the period, and a large share settled in the south. Hundreds of thousands more crossed from the north, driven by land reform, political pressure, and religious or class background. At the same time, Japanese residents departed. The south's population thus rose sharply while jobs, housing, and food supplies were scarce, and returnees from abroad frequently arrived with few possessions. Urban unemployment and crowded slums contributed to political volatility.

Third, ownership of the economy was in question. Under Japanese rule, a very large share of industrial, commercial, and agricultural property had belonged to Japanese individuals and companies. On December 6, 1945, USAMGIK issued Ordinance No. 33, which vested in the military government all property formerly owned by the Japanese government and by Japanese individuals and companies, effective retroactively to August 1945. This measure prevented unauthorized sale and the seizure of assets by committees or individuals, and it gave the military government a vast portfolio of enterprises, farmland, and buildings. It also put American officers, and in time Korean administrators, in charge of managing and eventually disposing of the property.

The consequences were contentious. Workers and factory committees, which had taken over many enterprises after liberation, resented the transfer of control to appointed managers, many of them former Japanese-era employees or businessmen with connections to the military government. Management by inexperienced officers produced inefficiencies, and there were charges of favoritism and corruption in appointments and sales. The question of who would eventually own vested property, whether the state, workers, or private buyers, became a central issue. The disposition of the property and of the business class that acquired it later shaped the character of South Korean capitalism (Chapters 75 and 76), but in 1945–1947 its immediate effect was to link the legitimacy of the military government to arguments about fairness.

Food was the most immediate crisis. The colonial government had operated a system of rice collection and rationing to supply the military and cities. In October 1945, USAMGIK, influenced by free-market views among its advisers, abolished the controls and declared a free market in rice. Hoarding and speculation followed, and prices soared. In January 1946, the military government reversed course, reintroducing compulsory collection and rationing, but the system proved hard to administer. Collection quotas were set at levels that many farmers resisted, officials charged with collecting rice were often police and former colonial personnel, and urban consumers faced shortages and rising prices. The U.S. Army's own occupation histories record the failure of procurement and the political damage it caused.

Inflation compounded the problem. The money supply expanded as the military government and the Japanese colonial authorities in their last weeks had issued currency, and as the military government financed its expenditures, while the supply of goods shrank. Prices rose sharply through 1946 and 1947, eroding wages. For urban workers, civil servants on fixed salaries, and the unemployed, rising prices meant real hardship, and for farmers who could sell on the black market, opportunity. The result was an economy in which many people judged the military government by its failure to deliver rice and stable prices, a perception that fed the unrest examined in Section 65.12.

The zone boundary severed trade, power, and transport; Japanese personnel left; returnees and refugees increased demand; occupation policy shifted between free market and controls.

Immediate trigger

Surrender arrangements divided authority along the 38th parallel without agreed rules for the exchange of goods, while Ordinance No. 33 shifted control of former Japanese property to the military government.

Immediate effects

Shortages of power, fertilizer, and food in the south; inflation and unemployment; disputes over management and ownership of vested enterprises.

Long-term consequences

Separate economic trajectories in the north and south; political legitimacy of the southern administration tied to food and prices; lasting disputes over property.

Historical complexity

Economic dislocation did not by itself cause political division, but it intensified grievances and limited the capacity of each administration to build legitimacy.

Student task: Explain why attributing the southern economic crisis solely to American mismanagement, or solely to the division itself, would be incomplete.

Division did not create two self-sufficient economies. Northern hydroelectric generation and heavy industrial inputs had been tied to southern manufacturing, transportation, and consumption; southern agriculture and commercial centers had supplied food and services needed by northern industrial populations. A train delayed at the parallel could interrupt not only the movement of passengers but the delivery of coal, machine parts, grain, and household goods. When political relations worsened, each administration increasingly treated cross-border exchange as a security issue. The immediate economic crisis was therefore not simply a shortage of currency or food. It was a failure of coordination across an inherited production system whose institutions had been designed to operate together.

Former Japanese property posed a special dilemma. Factories, banks, warehouses, houses, and farms could not remain ownerless in practice: someone had to decide who might work on them, repair them, collect rent, or sell the output. Ordinance No. 33 vested Japanese assets in the military government, creating a legal framework for management but not eliminating disputes about who deserved control. Korean workers who had restarted an enterprise after liberation might argue that their labor entitled them to participate in management. A returning tenant might question why a corporation rather than the cultivator controlled former Japanese farmland. Conservative business figures might favor a rapid transfer into private ownership. The stakes of seemingly technical property policy were thus intensely political.

Section 65.10

Farmers, Tenants, Labor Organizations, and the Land Question

In the south, the countryside remained dominated by the landlord–tenant relationship inherited from the colonial period. A majority of farm households in the south were tenants or part-tenants, and customary rents commonly took a large fraction of the crop, often half or more. The expectation of land reform, nourished by the committees of autumn 1945 and by the promises of the KPR, ran into the fact that southern landlords were influential in the conservative parties that the military government treated as partners, and that American officers distrusted redistribution as a radical measure.

The military government's response was moderate. In October 1945, Ordinance No. 9 limited rents to no more than one-third of the crop, a significant formal reduction that benefited tenants where it was enforced. Enforcement, however, was uneven. Landlords found ways to evade the rule, tenants who pressed claims risked eviction, and local officials were often tied to landowners. The military government created the New Korea Company by Ordinance No. 52 issued on February 21, 1946, effective March 3. This corporation administered former Japanese-owned farmland, a substantial share of cultivated land; tenants on those lands continued to pay rent pending subsequent disposal and sale policies. Debates over broader redistribution continued in the political arena, and the proposals discussed by the left–right coalition and the Interim Legislative Assembly (Sections 65.15 and 65.16) did not result in an enacted comprehensive reform during the period. Large-scale South Korean land redistribution belongs to a later chronology.

The contrast with the north is instructive but should be drawn carefully. In the north, redistribution was decreed by a provisional authority that had assumed power over a landlord class that had comparatively little political leverage with the Soviet command, and implemented by committees of the rural poor. In the south, landlords had organized representation, and the American command was inclined to postpone structural reform until a Korean government existed, arguing that a military government should not make permanent changes in property relations. Critics, then and later, argued that postponement protected the landlords' position and left rural grievances unaddressed. Defenders argued that rent controls provided relief and that hasty expropriation would have disrupted production. Both arguments rested on real considerations, and neither was neutral.

Labor organization was a second axis of conflict. After liberation, workers formed factory committees and unions. In November 1945 the left-led National Council of Korean Trade Unions, known by its Korean abbreviation Jeonpyeong, was founded and quickly claimed a large membership. In response, conservative nationalists backed a rival federation formed in 1946, supported by Rhee and Kim Ku circles and tolerated or encouraged by American officials. The competition between the two federations was bitter, and it overlapped with disputes over management of vested enterprises, wages, and rice rations. Workers demanded wage increases tied to prices, the right to organize, and participation in management; employers and military government officials often treated these demands as political challenges.

Everyday Life and Social History

Two Farm Families, Two Zones

The following comparison is a composite built from patterns documented in institutional records and later testimony. It does not describe any individual family. A formerly tenant household in the north in 1946 might have received a plot by decision of the village committee, with ownership but not the right to sell. It would owe a grain tax of roughly a quarter of its harvest, be asked to join the peasant association, and perhaps send children to a newly opened school. Its experience depended on its classification: neighbors designated as landlords might have lost land, left the village, or faced hostility.

A tenant household in the south in the same year would most likely still rent land, paying a share fixed by custom, nominally capped at a third where the ordinance was enforced. It would face procurement of grain, often conducted by police, and shortages of cloth, fertilizer, and cash as prices rose. Family members might have gone to the city or to a returnee's job, only to find unemployment. Its political experience depended on locality: where people's committees had been strong in autumn 1945 and were later dissolved, memories of liberation and of the committees' removal were often bitter.

Historians reconstruct such experiences from oral histories, memoirs, local gazetteers, administrative reports, and sometimes court and police records, each shaped by its creator's purposes. Records created by authorities describe households as categories; the voices of women and of the poorest families are the hardest to recover.

Southern agrarian politics depended on more than the nominal rent percentage. The rent ceiling established in October 1945 could be undermined by informal payments, disputes over crop measurement, and dependence on landlords for seed, animals, and credit. Local officials sometimes lacked the independence to enforce a tenant's complaint against an influential landowner. Tenants also faced a conflict between urgent household needs and political organizing: missing time at planting or harvest to attend a demonstration carried real costs. This context explains why the demand for free distribution of land resonated broadly but also why rural people did not respond uniformly to party appeals.

Former Japanese-owned farmland raised a related but different question. Because the military government held legal title to vested Japanese property, it could choose how to manage and eventually dispose of land without immediately confronting every Korean landlord. Yet the New Korea Company, established under Ordinance No. 52 of February 21, 1946 and effective March 3, became responsible for a large agricultural portfolio and therefore for relationships with many tenant cultivators. A change in corporate management was not the same as transfer of ownership to those cultivators. The gap between administrative control and genuine agrarian reform helped sustain criticism that the benefits of liberation had not reached the countryside.

Industrial labor disputes developed along similarly unequal lines. Workers in transportation and utilities could disrupt essential services, giving their demands visibility and bargaining power, but also making their unions targets for security measures. Employees of small workshops had fewer means of collective leverage. Wages had to be evaluated against inflation and rationing: an announced pay increase might leave a household worse off if rice prices rose more rapidly. In the political language of the period, the same strike could be described as a campaign for subsistence, an effort to democratize a workplace, or a communist challenge to military authority. The historian must examine the demands and the participants before deciding which interpretation is most persuasive.

Section 65.11

Police, Courts, Colonial Continuity, and Political Repression

No institution generated more southern resentment than the police. At liberation, the colonial police had been the most hated instrument of Japanese rule, and in many places Korean police officers were attacked or fled. The American command, concerned about order and distrustful of the people's committees, rebuilt the police quickly. It reestablished a national police under a Department of Public Safety, appointed senior Korean officials who had contacts with the military government, and recruited heavily from among men with prior police experience, who were the only ones with training. Among the senior and mid-level ranks, a large proportion had served under the Japanese, and while exact figures vary among sources, the pattern of substantial colonial continuity is well established.

The reasons for continuity were mixed. American officers wanted trained personnel and feared disorder; many conservative Koreans in the Korean Democratic Party and allied circles supported retaining experienced officials against what they saw as a leftist threat; and police officers themselves, fearful of retaliation, had strong incentives to align with the new authority and to prove their usefulness by suppressing the left. Popular opposition was strong, however, and the sight of familiar officers back in uniform became a symbol, for many Koreans, of a liberation that had not delivered a break with the past.

Legal and judicial institutions followed a similar pattern of reconstruction with continuity. The military government issued ordinances, enforced through military and Korean courts, that regulated political parties, assembly, and the press and that defined offenses against occupation forces. In early 1946, an ordinance required political parties to register, and authorities closed or restricted newspapers and organizations considered hostile. Courts staffed in part by judges and prosecutors with colonial-era careers administered the new rules. Anti-communist policing intensified as American officers grew convinced that the left was directed from Pyongyang and Moscow. Left-wing leaders were arrested, offices raided, and publications suspended, especially from mid-1946.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Policing and the Colonial Administrative Legacy, 1945–1947

What Changed

  • Japanese officers and officials were removed; Koreans filled senior posts
  • The formal authority above the police shifted from the Government-General to the military government and then to a partly Korean administration
  • The declared purpose changed from imperial control to democratic order, with new ordinances guaranteeing certain rights

What Stayed the Same

  • Many officers, methods, and organizational structures carried over from the colonial police
  • Surveillance and suppression of the left continued under a new anti-communist rationale
  • Police remained responsible for rice collection and public order, bringing them into conflict with farmers and workers

Why the pattern matters: Change was abrupt at the top and gradual below. Those who experienced it most directly, such as former political prisoners and returned activists, saw continuity; those who valued order, particularly propertied groups, saw a necessary stabilization. The pattern was uneven, contested, and consequential for the later South Korean state.

Distinguishing official intentions from popular experience is essential here. American administrators generally described police rebuilding as the restoration of order under law, and some officials tried to restrain abuses. Korean newspapers, petitions, and later testimony document torture in custody, arbitrary arrests, and violence by police and by right-wing youth groups allied with them. The extent of abuse varied by place and period and is difficult to measure, but its documented existence helped turn the police into a focus of the unrest in the autumn of 1946, and it is central to the evidence presented in the next section.

The continuity question had a personal dimension. A Korean who had worked as a colonial policeman might possess expertise in criminal investigation and local administration, yet be remembered by residents for enforcing political surveillance or wartime mobilization. The American command's preference for trained personnel made institutional reconstruction easier in the short term, but it often placed people with damaged local reputations in positions of authority. Police power also changed its targets: colonial restrictions against Korean nationalism gave way to surveillance of suspected communists and opponents of the occupation. The legal vocabulary changed while some methods of detention, interrogation, and reliance on informants survived. Such continuity should be demonstrated through specific practices rather than assumed from the mere presence of former personnel.

Courts faced problems beyond the wording of statutes. Judges had to determine which colonial-era laws still applied, how new military ordinances affected them, which language governed proceedings, and whether defendants could secure representation. A military government could create an administrative rule more quickly than a credible system for reviewing abuses. Protest leaders who regarded the police as politically biased likewise doubted whether courts would treat them impartially. Some judicial officers sought continuity because it promised predictable procedure; critics saw the same continuity as an obstacle to reckoning with colonial collaboration. These conflicting arguments were not abstract: they influenced whether people reported a crime, accepted an arrest, or trusted the verdict of a local tribunal.

The expanding security system also reinforced party polarization. A raid on a union office could disrupt a strike, provide evidence for a prosecution, or persuade previously undecided workers that legal organizing was impossible. Conversely, bombings, assaults, and intimidation by rival political militants strengthened officials' insistence that order required aggressive intervention. The relationship between repression and resistance became reciprocal. That does not make the parties equally powerful: the police and military authorities possessed capacities that private organizations did not. It does mean historians should explain how violence, fear, and institutional choices altered the field in which political parties competed.

Section 65.12

The September General Strike and the October 1946 Uprising

The unrest that began in the autumn of 1946 grew from the grievances described above, and from a sharpening political conflict. In May, American authorities had uncovered a counterfeiting affair involving a printing house linked to the Communist Party, and in the summer they moved against party leaders and press. In September, warrants were issued for several leftist leaders, including Pak Hon-yong, who left Seoul. At the same time, rice shortages and price rises had made the winter look threatening to urban workers.

The strike began among railway workers in Busan in late September, with demands that included increased rice rations, wage increases, and the release of arrested union leaders. It spread rapidly to rail workers elsewhere, to postal and other transport workers, and to factories, becoming a general strike with participation by tens of thousands. The left-led federation backed it, and the Communist Party and its allies sought to give it political direction. The military government responded by arresting strike leaders, using police and rightist youth groups to break the strike, and in some cases restoring services with strikebreakers.

Violence broke out in Daegu on October 1. Crowds of workers, students, and residents demonstrated over food and in protest against arrests; police fired on demonstrators, killing at least one; and in the following days crowds attacked police stations and officials. Martial law was declared in Daegu, and the unrest spread across the southeastern provinces and then to other regions, including Jeolla, Gyeonggi, Chungcheong, and Gangwon, continuing in places into December. Rural participation was extensive: farmers, many of them aggrieved by rice collection and by landlords, joined crowds, attacked police officers and local officials, and in many places targeted officials who had served under Japan. American and Korean forces, police, and rightist groups suppressed the uprising, with significant casualties on both sides. Estimates of deaths vary widely and should be treated cautiously; both police officers and civilians were killed, and thousands were arrested.

Interpretation of the uprising remains disputed, partly because the political stakes were high. American officials, and later many conservative Korean historians, emphasized the role of the Communist Party's leadership, which had instructed local branches to support the strike. Leftist accounts, including later North Korean ones, portrayed a popular insurrection against American occupation and colonial remnants. Many Korean historians of the later twentieth century, drawing on local studies, have stressed the spontaneous and varied character of rural participation, rooted in rice collection, police abuse, and the memory of dissolved committees. The Korean terms in use, among them incident, resistance, and uprising, themselves carry interpretive weight.

Evidence Comparison

The weight of the evidence supports a multicausal explanation. Organized left-wing leadership was real and sought to direct the strike. But food shortages, the unpopular rice collection, police violence, the persistence of colonial-era officials, unemployment, and the dissolution of the committees gave the unrest a mass base that organization alone could not have produced. Conversely, the suppression of the uprising deepened polarization, strengthened the police and the right-wing youth groups, drove many leftists underground or north, and convinced many Americans that radical opposition had to be contained. The October 1946 events belong in this chapter; the Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon events of 1948 belong in Chapter 66.

Chronology is essential to understanding the 1946 crisis. Railway workers in Busan stopped work on September 23, and the strike spread to Seoul and other workplaces. Organizers demanded improved rice rations, wage adjustments, and protections for unions and employees; these were material demands as well as political claims. The September 24 expansion into a general strike disrupted transportation and helped spread news of the confrontation. In Daegu, food protests by women and other residents intersected with the strike, student activism, and anger at police conduct. Contemporary newspaper reporting on the events, despite censorship and partisan language, identifies these overlapping constituencies rather than a single uniform crowd.

The October violence developed through local chains of escalation. A confrontation at a police station or a fatal shooting could draw into the streets students, market traders, workers, and farmers who had not been directed by a central party committee. In some districts demonstrators occupied offices, opened detention facilities, or attacked people identified with the old police system; elsewhere the movement took the form of marches and attempts to enforce economic demands. Government troops and police used force, declared restrictions, and arrested participants. Accounts of casualties vary because records were compiled by institutions with sharply different interests, and because the category of 'victim' could include protesters, police, bystanders, and those killed after initial clashes. Unattributed totals should not be presented as a settled statistical series.

American military histories provide valuable information about troop movements and official perceptions, but their descriptions of protesters as agitators can conceal the grievances that made unrest possible. Korean newspapers and later testimony can restore the voices of workers and residents, although partisan newspapers also selected facts to support their arguments. The chapter's evidence exercise should lead students to distinguish a demonstrated organizational role for the Communist Party from the stronger, less securely established claim that every protest was planned or controlled from a single center. The outcome accelerated surveillance and anti-communist organizing, weakened moderates, and made the politics of compromise more difficult precisely when the left–right coalition was attempting to establish common principles.

Section 65.13

The Moscow Conference and the Trusteeship Controversy

In mid-December 1945 the foreign ministers of the United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union met in Moscow to settle a range of postwar questions. Korea was one item on a crowded agenda. The ministers' agreement, announced on December 27, addressed Korea in four points. It called for the reestablishment of Korea as an independent state and the creation of a provisional Korean democratic government to organize the country's administrative, economic, and cultural development. It established a Joint Commission, composed of representatives of the American and Soviet commands in Korea, to consult Korean democratic parties and social organizations in forming that government. It provided that the Joint Commission, together with the provisional government, would work out measures for a trusteeship of Korea by the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and China, for a period of up to five years. And it called for a conference of the two commands within two weeks to address urgent economic and administrative questions.

Public reaction was shaped by how the decision was reported. Korean newspapers on December 27 and 28 published accounts, initially based on fragmentary and partly inaccurate information, that emphasized trusteeship and in some cases claimed the Soviet Union had demanded it while the United States had urged immediate independence. This was not an accurate summary of the record: trusteeship as a concept had been promoted in American wartime planning, and the final text was a joint compromise. But the reports landed in a country that had just been liberated and that read "trusteeship" as a continuation of foreign tutelage, recalling the treatment of Korea as unready for self-rule by colonial ideologues.

The response was swift and intense. Kim Ku and the Provisional Government circle organized a committee to oppose trusteeship, and on December 31 they announced an attempt to take over governing functions, which Hodge rejected and which collapsed within days. Syngman Rhee's organization and the Korean Democratic Party joined the opposition. Demonstrations and strikes by Korean employees of the military government occurred. On December 30, Song Jin-woo of the Korean Democratic Party, who had been willing to consider the Moscow decision, was assassinated; the party's leadership then moved firmly into the anti-trusteeship camp. The movement fused anti-trusteeship nationalism with anti-communism, since the left was seen as supporting the decision on Soviet instructions.

The left's position shifted. In late December, leftist organizations also opposed trusteeship, but in early January 1946 the Communist Party and allied groups announced support for the Moscow decision, interpreting it as the framework for a provisional government and arguing that trusteeship would be limited and consultative. Critics charged them with following Soviet orders; defenders replied that they had read the text rather than the headlines. Both explanations have evidential support: Soviet pressure on northern and southern communists to support the decision is documented, and the text's provisions do differ from the press accounts. The effect was to align trusteeship with the left and anti-trusteeship with the right, a division that made agreement on a provisional government harder.

The controversy's political consequences were large. It gave the right a nationalist cause that could be used to claim that it alone defended independence, and it allowed critics to portray the left as willing to subordinate Korean sovereignty. It also made the consultation clause, which the Joint Commission would have to implement, intensely contested, because the Soviet side would insist on consulting only groups that supported the decision, while many of the most prominent southern groups opposed it. In the north, the controversy was used to remove Cho Man-sik, as Section 65.5 described. The Moscow decision thus did not simply fail; its reception reorganized Korean politics in a way that made its own implementation more difficult.

The negotiation record makes the trusteeship issue more complicated than its immediate Korean reception suggested. At the Moscow foreign ministers' meeting, James F. Byrnes presented an American proposal on December 17, 1945 that envisaged a unified administration and a four-power trusteeship. The Soviet delegation submitted a counterproposal on December 20 placing the formation of a Korean provisional democratic government at the forefront of the agreement and providing for consultation with Korean parties through a Joint Commission. American delegates proposed further wording on December 21. The final announcement was a negotiated result based substantially on the Soviet counterdraft, not proof that only the Soviet Union wanted trusteeship. Indeed, an American draft permitted an initial five-year term and a possible additional term, whereas the negotiated provision limited the proposed trusteeship to a period of up to five years.

That diplomatic sequence matters because the Korean public did not receive the entire negotiating history when the news broke. People who had just experienced liberation often heard the politically explosive term trusteeship before they encountered the proposal for a Korean provisional government. Reporting that portrayed immediate independence as the American position and tutelage as the Soviet position offered ready-made language for partisan mobilization. The alliance of newspaper interpretation and street politics transformed the issue from a dispute over the design of transitional administration into a contest over which Korean faction was genuinely patriotic. For historians the original drafts are therefore as important as the final communiqué: only a comparison of all three can identify what each government actually proposed.

Section 65.14

The First U.S.–Soviet Joint Commission, 1946

The Joint Commission opened in Seoul on March 20, 1946, after preliminary talks between the two commands on urgent economic questions that produced limited results. Its American delegation was headed by Major General Archibald Arnold, and its Soviet delegation by Colonel General Terenty Shtykov. The commission's mandate, taken from the Moscow decision, was to consult Korean democratic parties and social organizations and to make recommendations toward a provisional government. Expectations were high among many Koreans and low among some of the principals.

The dispute that stalled it concerned the criterion for consultation. The Soviet delegation argued that only organizations that supported the Moscow decision, including trusteeship, should be consulted, since opponents of the decision opposed the very work the commission was meant to do. The American delegation argued that freedom of speech must be respected and that groups that had criticized trusteeship should not be excluded for having done so. A compromise formula, issued in April, allowed organizations to be consulted if they signed a declaration of support for the aims of the Moscow decision. Many right-wing groups initially refused; others signed while stating reservations; and the delegations then disagreed about whether groups whose leaders continued to oppose trusteeship publicly could properly be treated as signatories.

A related disagreement concerned which organizations qualified as "democratic" and how many representatives each side's favored groups should have. The Soviet delegation sought a larger role for the mass organizations of the left, such as the unions and peasant associations, while the American delegation wanted to ensure the inclusion of the right-wing groups it viewed as representing the south's anti-communist majority. Each side doubted the other's good faith, suspecting that the other aimed to create a provisional government under its own client. By early May the talks had reached an impasse, and the commission was suspended. Neither side declared the process dead, but for a year it remained suspended.

The consequences were significant. The suspension confirmed to many Korean leaders that the framework would not produce an early government and encouraged alternatives. In early June, Syngman Rhee, in a speech in Jeongeup, argued that if a unified government could not be formed, a government should be established in the south, a statement widely taken as a call for a separate southern government. It provoked criticism from moderates and from Kim Ku's circle, who feared it would entrench division, and drew concern from some American officials, though it also found a favorable audience. At the same time, the American command, frustrated with the Joint Commission, began exploring support for a centrist coalition as an alternative, as Section 65.15 shows.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Joint Commission Fail?

The question: The commission was supposed to produce a unified provisional government. Why did it deadlock in 1946 and again in 1947? Historians differ in how they weigh the explanations.

Interpretation A — Diplomatic and procedural failure: The impasse over consultation criteria reflected differing interpretations of democratic procedure and a reasonable disagreement about free speech and consultation. On this view, better drafting or more flexibility might have allowed progress, and the breakdown was not predetermined.
Interpretation B — Incompatible political expectations: Each occupation power expected a government friendly to its interests. The Soviet side wanted a government led by the left and willing to align with Moscow; the American side wanted a government that would exclude communist dominance. Consultation procedures became the arena in which these incompatible aims were fought.
Interpretation C — Korean divisions and Cold War escalation: Deep Korean disagreements about land, collaboration, and trusteeship, together with the broader deterioration of U.S.–Soviet relations, left little room for compromise. In this view, the commission could not succeed because the Korean political field was already polarized and because decisions elsewhere, in Washington and Moscow, increasingly treated Korea as a Cold War arena.

Evidence: The principal evidence includes the commission's own communiqués and minutes, diplomatic correspondence in the published American record, Soviet documents opened since the 1990s, and Korean press and memoir sources. Each has limits: published diplomatic records are selective, Soviet documents remain partially accessible, and Korean sources reflect partisan perspectives.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation: how far procedural issues were pretexts for deeper aims, how much room existed for compromise, and what weight to give Korean agency relative to great-power policy. They also differ over the intentions of each side, a matter where the evidence is incomplete.

Why it matters: If failure was primarily procedural, the later division seems more contingent; if structural, it seems more likely. This chapter treats these interpretations as complementary rather than exclusive, while rejecting the claim that failure was inevitable in March 1946.

Student task: Identify a piece of evidence that would distinguish Interpretation A from Interpretation B, and explain what it would need to show.

Consultation was not a technical formality. The commission was supposed to help create a Korean provisional government, which meant it needed some definition of whose opinions represented Korean political society. The Soviets feared that including organizations committed to overthrowing the Moscow arrangement would make a government based on that arrangement impossible. Americans argued that barring the large anti-trusteeship movement would empty the proposed consultation of legitimacy and reward groups favored by the Soviet occupation. Each argument had an internal logic, but each also strengthened allies in its own zone. The practical question was whether a party could object to trusteeship while agreeing to participate in other parts of the Moscow decision, and who would judge that sincerity.

Documents relating to the meetings should be read at several levels. A procedural memorandum could record agreement on wording without indicating whether the two military commands would accept the same Korean delegates. An occupation officer might describe a proposed compromise as reasonable while Korean activists experienced the procedure as exclusion. Neither side could safely concede control over the consultation list, since the list would help determine the personnel and character of the eventual government. The commission thus exposed a contradiction between the promise of Korean self-government and the foreign armies' continuing authority to decide who was eligible to speak.

Failure to reach agreement had immediate Korean consequences. Parties preparing to participate invested time in membership rolls, petitions, public statements, and attempts to show geographical reach. Suspensions and renewed talks made organization around the commission a recurring political campaign rather than a single diplomatic event. At the same time, decisions about land and police could not be postponed until Moscow and Washington agreed. Northern and southern authorities therefore continued constructing their own institutions, making later negotiations harder: every month created additional laws, offices, and constituencies that any unified administration would have to reconcile.

Section 65.15

Yeo Un-hyeong, Kim Kyu-sik, and the Left–Right Coalition Movement

With the Joint Commission suspended and polarization growing, moderates on both sides of the Korean political spectrum sought a middle course. The effort was led by Yeo Un-hyeong, who had been prominent on the left since liberation, and Kim Kyu-sik, a respected centrist nationalist. They proposed a coalition of left and right to create a basis for a provisional government that would satisfy the Joint Commission's requirements and provide Koreans with a common program. American officials, including Hodge's political advisers, saw the effort as a way to build a moderate center between the extremes and gave it support, an involvement that critics later cited as evidence of American manipulation and supporters as evidence of American commitment to a unified government.

Negotiations began in the summer of 1946. A Left–Right Coalition Committee was formed in July with representatives of moderate-right and moderate-left groups. The Communist Party and its allies initially participated but insisted on conditions, announcing a set of Five Principles on October 4 that included support for the Moscow decision, rapid land reform with confiscation and free distribution, and the release of arrested leftists. The right, led by the Korean Democratic Party and by Rhee and Kim Ku, rejected the coalition's premises, opposing any concession on trusteeship and demanding compensation in any land reform. The committee's moderate members sought a compromise.

On October 7, the coalition committee announced its Seven Principles. The seven points endorsed a north–south, left–right provisional democratic government under the Moscow decision; called for resumption of the Joint Commission; and specified confiscation, conditional confiscation, and graduated compensated purchase of land, followed by its free distribution to cultivators. They addressed urban sites and major buildings, nationalization of important industries, social and labor legislation, local self-government, monetary and livelihood problems, and political freedoms. They further proposed legislation for dealing with pro-Japanese collaborators and national traitors; release of political detainees across both zones and an end to terror by both extremes; a negotiated design for an interim legislature; and guarantees of speech, assembly, association, publication, movement, and voting. This is an editorial paraphrase of the October 7 original rather than a direct translation of its seven numbered clauses. The program combined redistributive aims with a graduated compensation mechanism and protections against political coercion.

Reception was largely negative at the extremes. The Korean Democratic Party and Rhee's followers attacked the principles as too sympathetic to communism; the Communist Party leadership, which had already moved toward confrontation after the autumn events, regarded the coalition as a tool of American policy and, in November, merged leftist groups into the South Korean Workers' Party, while Yeo moved toward a separate moderate party. The strikes and the October uprising occurred as the committee deliberated, and the resulting polarization narrowed the coalition's room. Right-wing violence against moderates also rose. Hodge's command continued to encourage the coalition but also supported the conservative-dominated legislature, sending mixed signals about American preferences.

Multiple Perspectives

The Coalition Movement as Seen by Different Groups

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from any single named actor, and the groups did not have equal power.

Moderate coalition advocates: The coalition offered the only route to a government acceptable to the Joint Commission and to a majority of Koreans, and avoiding division required concessions from both sides. They had moral authority and some American support but few organized followers and little coercive power.
Right-wing anti-trusteeship groups: Supporting the Moscow decision meant accepting foreign tutelage and opening the door to Soviet-backed communism. Compromise on land and trusteeship threatened both property and national independence. They commanded organized networks, money, and, in the south, the sympathy of much of the police and of many American officers.
Korean communists: The coalition could be useful if it advanced the Moscow decision and reform, but it risked diluting a program they believed popular support justified. After the autumn arrests and the suppression of the uprising, many saw the coalition as a cover for repression.
Occupation authorities: American officials hoped a centrist coalition would marginalize extremes and win Soviet acceptance, while also protecting the position of anti-communist partners. Soviet officials viewed coalition efforts in the south as attempts to construct an acceptable alternative to the left and judged them by their effect on the left's position.

Historical complexity: The perspectives differed in power and in room for maneuver. The moderates had the least institutional leverage, which helps explain why their program, though widely respected, could not be enforced.

Yeo Un-hyeong's assassination on July 19, 1947, in Seoul, was a decisive blow. He was shot by a young man connected to right-wing circles, and although the exact networks behind the assassination have been debated, the killing removed the movement's most effective organizer. Kim Kyu-sik continued to work for coalition, and the committee limped on, but by late 1947 it had dissolved. The coalition movement did not fail because its program was incoherent; it failed because the political field had polarized faster than moderates could build support, because key institutions, including the police and the occupation commands, were not committed to it, and because violence eliminated its leaders.

The Seven Principles were more detailed than a general appeal for moderation. Their third principle combined confiscation, conditional confiscation, and graduated compensated purchase of land with free distribution to cultivators; it also called for the handling of urban land and major buildings, nationalization of important industries, labor legislation, local self-government, and measures addressing currency and livelihoods. A separate principle proposed legislation to deal with collaborators and national traitors. Others called for the release of arrested political activists in both zones, the cessation of political terror, a plan for the interim legislature, and protection of speech, publication, association, movement, and voting. This was a program of national political reconstruction, not simply a compromise over one diplomatic formula.

Those provisions illuminate why the coalition lacked a secure constituency despite its seriousness. Landlords might accept reform in principle while rejecting confiscation; radical labor activists might favor redistribution but distrust conditional compensation; anti-trusteeship nationalists could agree on civil liberties while refusing the Moscow framework. The violence of autumn 1946 made joint commitments to end terror especially difficult, since the parties disagreed over who was responsible for violence and whether police repression counted as political terror. The program's strengths as a bridge across ideological lines were also its vulnerabilities: every compromise provision could be denounced as insufficient by one of the extremes.

Yeo Un-hyeong and Kim Kyu-sik should also be understood as political actors with different social bases, not interchangeable symbols of centrism. Yeo's influence drew heavily on popular organizing, his role during liberation, and his contacts among moderate leftists. Kim Kyu-sik's authority derived from diplomatic service, nationalist standing, and access to intellectual and conservative circles. Their partnership was a deliberate attempt to bring groups into the same constitutional process that otherwise might not meet. It failed not simply because two men lacked skill, but because their program had to operate amid armed occupation, unequal institutional sponsorship, and attacks on the possibility of independent political mediation.

Section 65.16

The South Korean Interim Legislative Assembly and Limited Self-Government

As the Joint Commission stalled, the American command moved to expand Korean participation in southern government. On August 24, 1946, USAMGIK issued Ordinance No. 118, which established a South Korean Interim Legislative Assembly. The assembly was to have ninety members, half elected indirectly through local procedures held in the autumn and half appointed by the military governor. The design reflected American goals: to give Korean representatives a role, to provide a platform for moderate leaders, and to build institutions that could eventually support a Korean government, while keeping ultimate authority with the military governor.

The elections, held in October amid the strikes and uprising, were indirect and shaped by local notables and by the influence of the police and right-wing organizations; they produced a strong showing for the right-wing parties and the groups around Rhee and the Korean Democratic Party. The left and many moderates boycotted or were excluded. Alarmed at the lopsided results, Hodge used the appointment powers to add moderates and some leftist-leaning members, an action that angered the right. The assembly opened in December 1946, with Kim Kyu-sik as chairman. Its sessions were lively and often contentious.

Its powers were limited. The military governor could veto legislation, and the assembly's laws had force only if approved. It debated and passed measures on matters including suffrage and the treatment of collaborators, and it discussed land reform. Several of its more consequential measures, including a bill concerning punishment of collaborators, were delayed or did not take effect under the occupation. Even so, the assembly gave Korean politicians experience in legislative procedure, a forum for public debate, and a claim to a role in national politics, and it made the south's political institutions more Korean in personnel and more conservative in orientation.

Executive responsibilities also shifted. Korean officials were appointed to head more departments under American supervision, and in 1947 the administration was described as the South Korean Interim Government. Under Appointment No. 116, dated February 4, Ahn Jae-hong was named Korean Civil Administrator effective February 5, 1947, subject to confirmation by the Interim Legislative Assembly. Korean judges, police officials, and administrators gained authority, though American officers retained oversight and final decision-making power. These changes made the military government more Korean in appearance and more dependent on Korean elites, but did not alter its legal status: it remained an occupation administration, and the supremacy of the military governor continued.

The institution-building had a double meaning. For American officials, it was a step toward the eventual transfer of power to a Korean government, whether national or southern. For the left and many moderates, it was an instrument for entrenching conservative control under American protection. For Korean conservatives, it was a means to build the cadres and legitimacy for a future government. All of these interpretations fit parts of the evidence. What is clear is that, by the end of 1946, the south had a legislative forum and a growing Korean administration; the north had its own, different, institutions; and the plan for a single provisional government had stalled.

Timing reveals the distinction between institutional form and authority. Ordinance No. 118 of August 24, 1946 provided a framework for the assembly before elections were conducted or the body convened in December. Appointment powers and the military governor's veto limited the representatives' independence. This did not render every debate meaningless: Korean legislators could formulate proposals, question administrative decisions, and make public claims about property and collaborators. But the distinction between proposing a statute, securing a vote in the assembly, and obtaining promulgation under military government remained decisive. Students should follow a contested bill through these stages rather than assume that parliamentary approval itself changed the law.

Ahn Jae-hong's appointment provides a precise example of gradual transfer without sovereignty. Appointment No. 116 was issued on February 4, 1947; it named him Korean Civil Administrator effective February 5, subject to confirmation by the Interim Legislative Assembly. The title did not make him an independent prime minister, and American personnel continued to exercise superior powers. Nevertheless, such appointments changed who appeared before the public as the administrator of Korean affairs and gave Korean officials opportunities to manage departments and argue over policy. The same ambiguity encouraged both the claim that the south was developing responsible self-government and the criticism that the occupation was selecting political intermediaries rather than yielding authority.

Section 65.17

Northern Korea in 1947: A Consolidating Political System

In February 1947, northern authorities took a further institutional step. A congress of representatives of provincial, city, and county people's committees, chosen in the elections of November 1946, met in Pyongyang and elected a North Korean People's Assembly. The assembly in turn established the North Korean People's Committee, with Kim Il Sung as chairman. The new committee replaced the Provisional People's Committee of 1946. The change of name and structure marked a shift from a provisional body based on a conference of parties and organizations to an executive answerable, formally, to an assembly elected through the committee system.

The new structure centralized administration. The People's Committee supervised departments for industry, agriculture, finance, education, transport, and security, and its decisions reached down through provincial and local committees. The Workers' Party, by now the dominant organization, provided leadership personnel and political direction, and the principle of democratic centralism, in which lower bodies are subordinate to higher ones, governed both party and state. Other parties continued to exist inside the united front, but their independent role diminished further.

Economic planning advanced. Northern authorities drew up a plan for 1947 covering industrial production, agriculture, trade, and education, the first formal annual plan, and they directed the nationalized enterprises toward reconstruction and expansion. Production recovered from the low point of 1945–1946 in several sectors, supported by Soviet trade and technical assistance, and by the labor mobilization that reform and party organization made possible. Shortages and disruptions persisted, and the economy remained dependent on the Soviet Union as a trading partner and source of equipment, but the north's leaders had a framework for state direction that the south lacked.

The security apparatus also expanded. Police and internal security organs strengthened their reach, building networks of informants and monitoring of party members, officials, and the general population. Militarized units, trained with Soviet help, grew in size and organization; the formal creation of a national army belongs to 1948, but the foundations were laid in 1946–1947. These organizations served to defend the new order against internal and external opponents, and they also provided a channel of advancement for loyal cadres, including veterans of the anti-Japanese resistance.

The relationship between Soviet supervision and Korean authority evolved. Soviet advisers remained in the north in large numbers, reviewing decisions, supplying expertise, and connecting the north to Moscow's policies. The Soviet command's representative in the Joint Commission negotiations, Shtykov, was also the most important Soviet figure in northern politics. At the same time, Korean institutions were gaining administrative competence, and Korean leaders, Kim Il Sung foremost, increasingly acted as principals rather than agents. The relationship was one of asymmetry and interdependence, and historians who emphasize Soviet control and those who emphasize Korean initiative both point to genuine evidence.

Students should be careful about the description of the north in 1947. It had state-like institutions: a legislature, an executive, planning, a security apparatus, and a ruling party. But it was not yet the DPRK. Its committees claimed provisional status, its leaders spoke of the national task of unification, and Soviet military authority remained. The northern system's distinctive features, including the later cult of personality, Juche, and hereditary succession, were not yet present, and the factional struggles within the leadership were unresolved. The north in 1947 was a consolidating system, not a finished one.

The 1947 institutional consolidation depended on the preceding year's local elections. Those elections selected committee members from candidates filtered through the united-front structure, and the resulting delegates helped constitute the February 1947 People's Assembly. The new committee's authority rested on a chain of formally Korean institutions, but its personnel and acceptable political positions were shaped by the Workers' Party and Soviet advisers. The system could therefore exhibit regular sessions, budgets, and policy implementation while restricting the kinds of opposition capable of reaching office. Its institutional sophistication and its limited competition must be analyzed together.

Section 65.18

Southern Korea in 1947: Party Competition, Anti-Communism, and Military Government

The southern political scene in 1947 was more open than the northern in some respects and more violent in others. Multiple parties competed, newspapers of varied orientations appeared, and the Interim Legislative Assembly provided a forum for debate. At the same time, the left was under sustained pressure: leaders had been arrested, offices closed, and many activists forced underground or north, and right-wing youth organizations operated with a degree of impunity that frightened moderates. Political violence, including assassinations and clashes, was frequent. Freedom of expression existed in practice for some groups and not for others.

Conservative and nationalist networks became dominant. The Korean Democratic Party, Rhee's organizations, and allied groups controlled much of the legislature, had strong representation in the police and administration, and enjoyed the confidence of important American officers. Kim Ku's circle, though a rival to Rhee on some questions, shared with him opposition to the left and to trusteeship. The coalition of conservatives was not unified: Rhee, Kim Ku, and the Democratic Party competed for leadership, and their different attitudes toward a separate southern government created tensions. But in the competition with the left and moderates, they had the advantage of occupation support and organizational resources.

The question of a separate government divided opinion. Rhee had advocated a southern government since mid-1946; many in the Democratic Party came to share the view as the Joint Commission failed; Kim Ku and Kim Kyu-sik opposed it, fearing it would make division permanent. Some American officials, including Hodge, were uneasy about proceeding with a separate government, while others in Washington increasingly saw it as a possible outcome. The left rejected a southern government as a creation of the occupation, while some leftists in the south looked to the north for leadership. These disagreements show that, in 1947, separate statehood was a contested possibility rather than a settled course.

Economic insecurity persisted. Rice shortages recurred, inflation continued, and unemployment remained high, while American aid, mainly emergency supplies of food, fertilizer, and other goods, partially eased the situation. The military government's disposition of vested property proceeded, and a business class connected to the administration took shape. Public demonstrations and strikes continued, and the police and rightist groups frequently suppressed them. The assassination of Yeo Un-hyeong in July deepened the sense that moderate politics was perilous.

Comparison with the north requires care. The southern system was less centralized and less coherent than the northern: it combined American military authority, a growing Korean administration, a legislative forum, competing parties, and a professional police, without a single directing party. It was also less transformative in social terms, since it left the property structure largely intact and focused on order and gradual institution-building. The northern system, by contrast, had carried out a social revolution through centralized, party-directed means. Neither should be described as already possessing the constitutional order of its later state. The south in 1947 did not have the Republic of Korea's constitution, president, or National Assembly; it had a military government in transition.

What the two zones shared was a growing sense among Koreans that the future of the country would be decided within them. Political leaders in each zone invoked the whole of Korea, but their organizations, resources, and constituencies were increasingly local. People moved, in small numbers, between zones, bringing information and rumor, and the experiences of refugees colored attitudes in the receiving zone. By late 1947, a southern conservative and a northern communist could each describe the other zone as occupied and illegitimate, and each could point to institutions in their own zone as the nucleus of a future Korean government.

Southern politics in 1947 also required attention to the unequal distribution of protection. Conservative organizers increasingly operated near the institutions that controlled licensing, security, and administration, while police pressure constrained left-wing unions and parties. Nonetheless, the political order was not a perfectly coordinated conservative bloc: American commanders, Korean Democrats, Rhee's followers, moderates, and former provisional-government leaders often disagreed about pace, diplomatic strategy, and appointments. A newspaper surviving in this environment may reveal vigorous disagreement among accepted actors while providing little evidence that excluded organizations could operate on equal terms. The concept of pluralism is therefore insufficient without examining access to institutions and the practical cost of dissent.

Section 65.19

The Second Joint Commission, the United Nations, and the Failure of a Unified Settlement

In the spring of 1947, the United States and the Soviet Union exchanged letters at the level of foreign ministers, and agreed to resume the Joint Commission. The commission reopened in Seoul on May 21, 1947. By then, American policy toward the Soviet Union had hardened, the broader Cold War was developing in Europe and the Mediterranean, and the Truman administration was considering a more active program of support for anti-communist governments. Soviet policy likewise had hardened, and Soviet officials were concerned that a unified Korea would be dominated by anti-communist forces backed by the United States.

The commission quickly returned to the same questions of consultation. The Soviet delegation again asserted that groups opposed to the Moscow decision should be excluded, while the American delegation insisted that exclusion of large nationalist organizations would be undemocratic. The commission circulated lists of organizations and received thousands of registrations, largely, in the case of the right, from organizations created to be counted, which the Soviet side considered an effort to swamp the process. Despite sustained meetings through the summer, no compromise emerged. Korean groups mobilized for and against the commission, and the assassination of Yeo Un-hyeong in July occurred in the midst of its work. By late summer the commission had effectively ceased functioning.

American officials then made a decisive shift. In September 1947, Secretary of State George Marshall announced that the United States would refer the Korean question to the General Assembly of the United Nations. The Soviet delegation proposed instead that both armies withdraw from Korea at the start of 1948 and leave Koreans to form a government, a proposal the American side regarded as a means to leave the field to the better-organized north and rejected. The referral reflected the American assessment that the bilateral framework had failed and that the General Assembly, where the United States could count on majority support, was a more favorable forum. The Soviet Union objected that the matter belonged to the Moscow framework, not to the United Nations.

Section 65.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how an initially temporary military division of Korea developed into two opposing systems of political authority between September 1945 and November 1947, before either Korean state was formally founded. Its central argument is that division resulted from the interaction of Soviet and American occupation policies, Korean political competition, competing programs of social and economic reform, institutional choices, and the intensifying Cold War, and was not an inevitable consequence of liberation.

The occupation armies arrived in September 1945 to receive a Japanese surrender, not to build governments, and they inherited a country in which Koreans had already organized. Occupation was nonetheless a form of governing authority: each command could make binding rules, recognize or exclude Korean bodies, and control movement and property. In the north the Soviet command initially worked with a plural field of people's committees and then built, with Korean allies, a centralized system. In the south the U.S. Army Military Government rejected the Korean People's Republic, retained much of the colonial administrative apparatus, and relied on conservative, English-speaking, and propertied Koreans as partners.

In the north, the Provisional People's Committee of February 1946, chaired by Kim Il Sung, carried out land reform in March, labor and gender-equality legislation, and nationalization in the summer. A communist-led united front, a Workers' Party formed through merger, mass organizations, and security institutions provided political control. The reforms brought real gains to many peasants, workers, and women, and were accompanied by the exclusion of opponents, the removal of Cho Man-sik, and the flight of many property-holders and Christians to the south. The People's Committee of February 1947 extended centralization.

In the south, the military government faced dislocation from the severing of the colonial economy, the arrival of returnees and refugees, inflation, and food shortages, with its rice policy shifting from a free market to compulsory collection. Ordinance No. 33 placed Japanese property under its control and created ownership disputes. Tenancy persisted under rent limits that were unevenly enforced, comprehensive land redistribution was not enacted in this period, and the police, rebuilt with substantial colonial-era personnel, became a focus of resentment. The strike and the Daegu-centered uprising of autumn 1946 grew from organized left-wing leadership and from broad social grievances, and their suppression deepened polarization.

Efforts at unification were real. The Moscow decision provided for a provisional democratic Korean government, a Joint Commission, and a trusteeship of up to five years, but its reception as a trusteeship plan reorganized Korean politics, aligning anti-trusteeship nationalism with the right and acceptance with the left. The first Joint Commission stalled over consultation criteria, reflecting incompatible political expectations as well as procedural disagreement. The left–right coalition movement produced the Seven Principles in October 1946, but the extremes rejected it and Yeo Un-hyeong's assassination in July 1947 removed its leading organizer.

Meanwhile, the south acquired a partly elected Interim Legislative Assembly and a growing Korean administration, still under the supremacy of the military governor. By 1947 the north's institutions were more centralized and socially transformative, and the south's more pluralistic, conservative, and violent at the margins. The second Joint Commission failed in 1947, the United States referred the question to the United Nations, and Resolution 112 (II) of November 14 created the Temporary Commission on Korea, anticipating national elections that the northern authorities and the Soviet Union rejected.

The chapter's evidence is varied and politically charged. Occupation records, decrees, communiqués, newspapers, memoirs, oral history, and captured and newly opened Soviet documents each illuminate part of the story, and each reflects its creator's perspective. Historians continue to debate Soviet control versus Korean initiative in the north, the causes of the uprising, and the reasons for the Joint Commission's failure. This chapter has treated these as questions requiring evidence rather than as matters of allegiance to either modern state's narrative.

By the end of 1947 Korea had not formally become two countries, but it had developed two increasingly incompatible political systems. Those systems were not merely imposed from abroad; they emerged through the interaction of occupation policy, Korean political struggles, economic reform, social conflict, and the failure of international cooperation. That conclusion connects directly to Chapter 66, which examines the violence, separate elections, and state foundings of 1948.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter occupies a narrow span of time, barely two years, but it explains much of what follows. The institutions built in this interval, northern committees and a ruling party, a southern military government and a conservative-led legislature, a rebuilt police, a redistributed or unredistributed countryside, became the working materials from which two states would be assembled in 1948. Understanding their construction explains why the states looked as they did and why unification became so hard to negotiate.

The chapter also shows how history can be written without teleology. The Moscow decision, the Joint Commissions, the coalition movement, and the UN resolution were real attempts to create a single government. Their failures had identifiable causes, among them mutual suspicion between the occupying powers, Korean polarization over land, collaboration, and trusteeship, violence against moderates, and the broader Cold War. Recognizing these causes does not make the outcome inevitable; it makes it explicable, which is a different and more useful thing.

Finally, the chapter shows why careful comparison matters. A reader who sees only Soviet orders in the north or only American policy in the south misses the farmers who took land, the workers who struck, the police officers who stayed in uniform, the politicians who chose between compromise and confrontation. Their choices, made within severe constraints, were part of how division happened. Chapter 66 turns to the violence, elections, and state foundings of 1948, where the systems constructed here would be formalized, and where the cost of failing to find a common government would become tragically visible.
