Christianity, Missions, the Inquisition, and Colonial Culture

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Three Colonial Moments

In 1536 Indigenous noblemen studied Latin grammar at a Franciscan college in Tlatelolco; in 1562 Franciscan investigators tortured Maya villagers at Maní and burned their sacred books; in 1691 a nun in Mexico City wrote a defense of women's right to study. All three episodes belong to the same history of Christianity in colonial Latin America, and no single one of them is that history.

The Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco, founded in 1536, trained the sons of Nahua nobility in Latin, rhetoric, and Christian doctrine. Its graduates later served as teachers, translators, and collaborators in some of the most important records of Indigenous life ever produced, including the work associated with the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún. A generation later, in the Yucatán town of Maní, the Franciscan provincial Diego de Landa conducted an investigation of Maya "idolatry" in 1562 that relied on torture, caused, by the count of an official inquiry in 1565, roughly 157 deaths under torture and some thirty suicides, documented or presumed, and destroyed numerous books and images. In 1691 Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, a Hieronymite nun and the most accomplished writer of her colonial world, responded to a bishop's rebuke with a sustained argument that learning was compatible with, and even required by, her religious vocation. The same church that educated Nahua scholars also punished Maya believers, and the same institutional world that confined Sor Juana also gave her a library, a patron, and an audience.

This chapter argues that Christianity became one of the defining institutions of colonial Latin America, but that the religious cultures that emerged were neither uniform nor simply imposed from Europe. They developed through conquest, evangelization, coercion, Indigenous and African participation, negotiation, conflict, and cultural creation. Catholicism supplied a justification for empire and became embedded in colonial government through royal patronage. It also became a language in which Indigenous communities, Africans and their descendants, women, and ordinary parishioners made claims, built institutions, produced art and scholarship, and organized their lives. Religion was an instrument of colonial power and a sphere in which colonial peoples created distinctive intellectual, social, artistic, and spiritual worlds.

The chapter follows five lines of inquiry across roughly 1500 to 1800. The first concerns how evangelization began and who carried it out, including the religious orders, the interpreters and teachers on whom they depended, and the Indigenous converts who accepted, reshaped, or resisted what they were taught. The second concerns the institutions through which Christianity was rooted in place: dioceses, parishes, and missions. The third concerns the policing of belief, including the Inquisition, whose authority differed sharply between Spanish America and Portuguese Brazil. The fourth concerns the religious lives of Indigenous and African-descended people, including saints, shrines, confraternities, healing, and festivals. The fifth concerns education, women's religious communities, Sor Juana, and the colonial Baroque.

Regional variety is essential. Evangelization in the densely settled Nahua towns of central Mexico differed from the Andean highlands, where sacred landscapes and ancestor veneration shaped religious life, and from the Guaraní lowlands of the Río de la Plata region, where Jesuits organized large mission towns. Portuguese Brazil developed its church through different arrangements, with fewer bishops, no sustained, authorized printing until 1808, and lay brotherhoods carrying much of local religious life. The Caribbean, where Indigenous populations collapsed early, and African-descended majorities came to dominate many societies, posed other problems again. A history of colonial religion built only on Mexico City or Lima would misrepresent most of the region.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually problematic. Missionary chronicles, catechisms, sermons, inquisitorial trial records, visitation reports, parish registers, and ecclesiastical court files survive in large numbers, but nearly all were produced by clergy or church officials with institutional purposes. Indigenous-authored sources, including Nahuatl annals, the Quechua Huarochirí Manuscript, Felipe Guaman Poma's illustrated letter to the king, and Maya books of Chilam Balam, offer other vantage points, though they too were written under colonial conditions. Material evidence, from church architecture and altarpieces to mission ruins and ritual objects, adds further testimony. Historians therefore read these sources against one another and ask what each was designed to accomplish.

The chapter sits within Part X, which examines colonial society from below as well as above. Chapter 44 described African slavery and the making of Afro-Latin America, and Chapter 45 examined race, casta, family, and gender. This chapter takes up the religious and cultural life that ran through both, and it leaves large Indigenous uprisings, maroon societies, and the politics of resistance to Chapter 47. The expulsion of the Jesuits in 1759 and 1767 belongs to Chapter 48, and this chapter refers to it only where needed to understand the later history of missions.

Learning Objectives

  2. Distinguish conquest, baptism, instruction, and sustained religious change, and explain why they were not the same process.
  3. Compare the goals, methods, and institutional positions of Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians, and Jesuits.
  4. Analyze the role of language, translation, catechisms, and Indigenous teachers, scribes, and interpreters in religious instruction.
  5. Assess missions as communities that combined protection, education, labor, discipline, and coercion, using the Guaraní reductions and Brazilian aldeamentos as cases.
  6. Distinguish inquisitorial, episcopal, and missionary authority, and explain why Indigenous people were largely outside Holy Office jurisdiction in Spanish America.
  7. Contrast the Spanish-American tribunals with Portuguese inquisitorial administration in Brazil.
  8. Interpret the evidence for Indigenous Christianity, African religious practice, and Afro-Latin Christianities while avoiding anachronism.
  9. Evaluate historians' competing interpretations of conversion, syncretism, and religious negotiation.
  10. Analyze Sor Juana's Respuesta as a source for gender, learning, and religious authority.
  11. Explain the colonial Baroque as a regionally varied art shaped by Indigenous and African-descended artisans as well as European patrons.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part X's argument that colonial society was built from below as well as imposed from above.

Historical Context

Chapters 25 through 29 explained the Iberian background and the Atlantic encounter. Castile and Portugal emerged from centuries of warfare against Muslim-ruled territories with strong ideas linking monarchy, military expansion, and the defense and spread of Christianity. In 1492 the Castilian crown completed the conquest of Granada and ordered the expulsion of Jews who would not convert, and in 1497 Portugal forced Jews to accept baptism. The resulting New Christian populations, converts and their descendants whose sincerity was often suspected, became principal targets of the Iberian Inquisitions and will matter in Division III.

Chapters 30 through 39 narrated conquest. By the mid-sixteenth century, Spaniards had overthrown the Mexica and Inca states, Portuguese settlers and their Indigenous allies and enemies had established coastal Brazil, and Indigenous populations across the Americas had suffered catastrophic losses from epidemic disease and violence (Chapter 29). Chapters 40 through 43 described the governments and economies that followed: viceroyalties, audiencias, and cabildos; the Portuguese captaincies and governors-general; silver mining; plantations; and cities. Religion ran through each of these institutions. Bishops sat in colonial councils, parish priests recorded baptisms and marriages, and tithes helped finance church building.

Chapter 44 and Chapter 45 supply the social setting. Enslaved Africans and their descendants brought languages, ritual knowledge, and healing practices from West and Central Africa, and colonial classifications of race and status shaped who could enter schools, convents, and clerical office. Many of the most important religious institutions of the period, from Indigenous town churches to Black confraternities, were organized along lines of ancestry and legal standing.

Several interpretive cautions govern this chapter. Colonial writers described Indigenous religion in categories drawn from Christian experience, speaking of "idols," "temples," "priests," and "devils," and these words carry assumptions that historians must set aside. The modern term "Catholic Church" can conceal how diverse the institution was, since bishops, parish priests, mendicant friars, Jesuits, inquisitors, and lay confraternities frequently disagreed with one another. Finally, modern religious traditions such as Candomblé and Santería took shape over long periods, and their nineteenth- and twentieth-century forms should not be read back into the colonial record without evidence.

Conquest, Christianity, and the Beginnings of Evangelization
Section 46.1

Catholic Christianity, Iberian Expansion, and Imperial Authority

Spanish and Portuguese overseas expansion was commercial, military, and dynastic, but it was also religious. Both monarchies claimed a special relationship to the Catholic faith, shaped by the long Iberian wars against Muslim-ruled territories, and both justified their American claims partly by an obligation to bring Christianity to peoples who had not heard it. In 1493 Pope Alexander VI issued bulls, notably Inter caetera, that supported Castile's claims in the lands Columbus had reached, and later settlements between the two Iberian crowns, discussed in Chapter 28, divided their spheres. In return for the pope's grants, the crowns assumed responsibility for evangelizing the peoples they governed.

The crucial institution was royal patronage. Over several decades popes gave the Spanish crown, and finally in 1508 through the bull Universalis ecclesiae, authority over the organization of the church in the Americas, including the right to propose bishops, approve the creation of dioceses, collect and allocate tithes, and regulate the passage of clergy. The Portuguese crown exercised similar authority through the padroado, which derived from earlier privileges linked to the Order of Christ. These arrangements made the church an arm of colonial government in a literal sense. A bishop was a royal appointee as well as a spiritual official, and the crown often preferred to rule its overseas church directly rather than through Rome.

Historical Vocabulary
Patronato real / Padroado

The patronato real (Spanish) and padroado (Portuguese) were the rights and duties granted by the papacy to the Iberian crowns to organize and finance the church in overseas territories. The crown nominated bishops, controlled the flow of clergy, and collected tithes, and in return was responsible for supporting missionary activity.

The arrangement mattered because it fused religious and political authority. It did not make the crown the head of the church in doctrine, and tensions with Rome and with local bishops recurred. Modern ideas of separation between church and state do not apply, and students should avoid reading the colonial church as a purely spiritual institution.

The link between religion and sovereignty also generated moral debate from the beginning. The Dominican Antonio de Montesinos preached at Santo Domingo in 1511 against the treatment of Indigenous people on Hispaniola, and in 1513 the Spanish crown adopted the Requerimiento, a text read aloud to Indigenous communities demanding that they acknowledge Christian doctrine and royal authority or face war, a procedure that was widely ridiculed even by contemporaries. Theologians such as Francisco de Vitoria questioned whether differences in belief justified conquest. These debates, discussed again in Section 46.3, did not stop violence, but they shaped the language of colonial law. Throughout the chapter, students should distinguish between religious ideals stated in royal instructions and papal bulls and the practices that unfolded in distant, poorly supervised settlements.

Brazil's arrangement differed in ways that mattered. The Portuguese crown placed evangelization in the hands of the Society of Jesus and other orders but organized fewer dioceses and appointed fewer clergy, and Brazil received its first bishop in 1551 and its first archbishop only in 1676. Spanish America developed a more elaborate ecclesiastical hierarchy earlier, in part because of the density of Indigenous populations in Mesoamerica and the Andes. This difference would shape the role of lay brotherhoods, discussed in Section 46.18.

Section 46.2

Conquest and the First Campaigns of Evangelization

In the years after the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 (Chapter 32), Spanish forces and their Indigenous allies pulled down or repurposed temples, and churches rose on or near many Indigenous sacred sites. Cortés and the first Franciscans, who arrived in 1524 in a group remembered as "the Twelve," carried out campaigns of baptism, and Franciscan reports claimed enormous numbers of baptisms in the first decade, in the hundreds of thousands or more. These figures cannot be verified and are likely inflated, but they show the missionaries' conviction that rapid conversion was both possible and urgent. Historians have long used the phrase "spiritual conquest," made famous by Robert Ricard in 1933, to describe this process, though many later scholars argue that the phrase exaggerates the speed and completeness of religious change.

Pressures on Indigenous communities were severe. Epidemics (Chapter 29) had killed large numbers, undermining the authority of priests and rulers, and Spanish demands for tribute and labor reshaped everyday life. The destruction of images and temples carried political as well as religious meaning, because shrines were bound to the legitimacy of local rulers and to the identity of each altepetl. Sustained religious change, however, required more than demolition. It required instruction, community cooperation, and the replacement of rituals that structured the agricultural year, marriage, and burial. Many Indigenous people accepted baptism quickly, in some cases for reasons of protection, status, or curiosity, while understanding Christian teachings through categories of their own.

Early evangelization also provoked sharp debate among Spaniards. Missionaries disagreed over whether Indigenous people could be admitted to the Eucharist and to the priesthood, how forceful conversion could be, and how to treat Indigenous elites. The Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas condemned violent conquest, and the New Laws of 1542 sought to limit the encomienda (Chapter 33). In Yucatán in 1562, the Franciscan Diego de Landa responded to the discovery of Maya ritual activity by conducting investigations at Maní, using interrogation under torture. The 1565 inquiry into Landa's conduct recorded roughly 157 deaths under torture and some thirty suicides, documented or presumed, figures that should be read as approximate, and Landa's men destroyed numerous images and burned Maya books. Landa was recalled to Spain to answer for his conduct, was cleared, and returned as bishop of Yucatán. His later written account of Maya life, composed partly from information provided by Maya informants, remains one of the most important and most troubling sources for Maya history.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Conquest and the Transformation of Indigenous Religious Institutions

Long-term causes

Iberian Christian expansion and royal patronage; papal grants tied to evangelization; earlier Caribbean experience with forced labor and conversion; the political role of sacred institutions in Indigenous states.

Short-term causes

The military defeat of ruling elites; epidemic mortality; the arrival of mendicant friars with royal support; demands for tribute and labor that disrupted ritual calendars.

Event or transformation

Temples were demolished or converted, baptism became the entry point into colonial society, and religious authority moved toward churches, friars, and Christian officials.

Immediate effects

Public state ritual was suppressed; some communities embraced baptism; others hid images or continued private ceremonies; conflicts erupted over the treatment of Indigenous religious leaders.

Long-term consequences

Christianity was embedded in local government, but Indigenous communities reshaped Christian practice around their own saints, calendars, and sacred places.

Historical complexity

The transformation combined coercion and choice, destruction and creative adaptation. It cannot be summarized as either imposed uniformity or voluntary blending.
Section 46.3

Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians, and Jesuits

The religious orders were the main agents of early evangelization. The mendicant orders, so called because their members had originally lived by begging, arrived first: Franciscans in Mexico in 1524, Dominicans in 1526, and Augustinians in 1533, with other groups following in the Andes and Caribbean. They were regular clergy, meaning they lived under a rule within their own organizations, rather than secular clergy who served in parishes under bishops. Because the crown relied on the friars to reach populations that bishops and parish priests could not, the orders held powers that would later cause conflict with bishops, and they often built fortress-like monasteries that served as churches, schools, and administrative centers.

The orders differed in tradition and temperament, although generalizations hide individual variation. Franciscans emphasized poverty, preaching, and the creation of Indigenous schools, and many shared an expectation that the conversion of the Americas would hasten the end of the world, a strand of thought historians call millenarian. Dominicans were associated with theological training and, in the work of Las Casas and others, with sharp criticism of conquest. Augustinians built impressive monasteries in Mexico and worked in the Andes, where they became linked to the shrine of Copacabana. The Society of Jesus, founded in 1540, arrived in Brazil in 1549 with Manuel da Nóbrega, in Peru in 1568, and in New Spain in 1572, and distinguished itself by a systematic educational program and a flexible approach to local conditions.

Some friars became exceptional students of Indigenous societies. Bernardino de Sahagún arrived in Mexico in 1529 and spent decades directing Nahua scholars in collecting accounts of Nahua religion, society, and the conquest, a project preserved in the Florentine Codex, discussed in Section 46.21. Sahagún's motives were religious, since he sought to help missionaries detect surviving "idolatry," but the work preserved knowledge on a scale that no other colonial source matches. Bartolomé de las Casas, who arrived in the Caribbean in 1502 and became a Dominican in 1522, argued before the crown and in the Valladolid debates of 1550–1551 that Indigenous people were rational beings with legitimate political institutions. The orders' relations with the crown were also uneasy. They resisted episcopal supervision of their parish work, and Bourbon reformers in the eighteenth century would transfer many mendicant parishes to secular clergy.

These differences affected Indigenous communities directly. A town served by a Franciscan monastery might have a school, a choir, a hospital, and a church built by community labor. A Jesuit college in a colonial city might train the sons of colonial elites in rhetoric and philosophy. In frontier zones, the presence of a single missionary could shape relations with colonial officials and neighboring peoples. Indigenous people learned to distinguish among the orders, to play friars against one another, and to appeal to bishops or royal officials when it served them.

Section 46.4

Language, Translation, Catechisms, and Religious Instruction

Christian teaching depends on words, and translating it into Nahuatl, Quechua, Aymara, Maya languages, Tupi, and Guaraní was one of the most difficult intellectual tasks of the colonial period. Missionaries produced grammars, dictionaries, sermons, and catechisms on a large scale. Alonso de Molina published a Nahuatl-Spanish dictionary in 1555, expanded in 1571. José de Anchieta, a Jesuit in Brazil, wrote a grammar of Tupi, published in 1595. In Peru the Third Council of Lima (1582–1583) mandated a standard catechism, which was printed in Lima in 1584 in Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara, one of the first books printed in South America. Printing came to Mexico City in 1539. In Brazil, a printing workshop set up in Rio de Janeiro by Antônio Isidoro da Fonseca in 1747 was suppressed by the crown, and sustained, officially authorized printing began only in 1808, a difference that shaped its colonial religious culture.

Translation raised hard problems. Missionaries faced the question of whether to translate terms such as God, soul, sin, and heaven into Indigenous equivalents, which risked carrying pre-Christian meanings, or to use Spanish or Latin words, which risked leaving the ideas unintelligible. Many chose to preserve key Spanish terms, such as Dios in Nahuatl texts, and to translate others, producing a Christian vocabulary that was neither fully European nor fully Indigenous. Scholars such as Louise Burkhart have shown that Nahuas read these texts through their own concepts of balance, moral fault, and ritual obligation, so that the same words could mean different things to different audiences.

This intellectual labor was never European alone. The Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco trained Nahua nobles who could write Latin, Spanish, and Nahuatl, and graduates such as Antonio Valeriano worked as teachers, translators, and collaborators with Sahagún. Indigenous interpreters, scribes, and catechists in towns carried out much of the day-to-day instruction, since friars were few. In the Andes, Indigenous assistants called fiscales in some regions helped missionaries manage parishes, and in many Mesoamerican towns, Indigenous choirmasters and sacristans became central to local religious life. The institutional records often credit friars, but the work frequently rested on Indigenous people whose names are poorly preserved.

Instruction was uneven in practice. Catechisms were taught by repetition, and a baptized adult might memorize prayers without grasping their theological content. Church authorities themselves recognized this, and councils repeatedly complained of poorly instructed Indigenous parishioners and of priests who did not know local languages. By the late sixteenth century, the crown required parish clergy in Indigenous areas to know the local language, a rule honored irregularly. Language therefore became a field of both Christianization and Indigenous cultural survival, since the continued use of Indigenous languages in sermons, songs, and records helped preserve Indigenous communities' identities.

Section 46.5

Indigenous Converts, Community Leaders, and the Transformation of Religious Life

Indigenous people responded to Christianity in many ways, and the evidence permits few simple conclusions. Some leaders accepted baptism quickly and sponsored churches, hoping to protect their communities and maintain status in the new order. In Mexico, many Nahua elites became patrons of local churches and sponsors of saints' festivals, and in the Andes, kurakas (Indigenous lords) used Christian sponsorship to confirm their standing. Other people converted from conviction, finding in Christian teaching explanations of suffering and death that matched their experience of catastrophe. Still others adopted selected rituals while continuing older ones, and some resisted in ways that could provoke violent response.

One notable case shows the dangers. Don Carlos Ometochtzin of Texcoco, a nobleman descended from the city's ruling house, was tried in 1539 by Bishop Juan de Zumárraga, acting as apostolic inquisitor, on charges that he had spoken against Christianity and encouraged traditional practices. He was convicted and burned. The crown reprimanded Zumárraga, and in the following decades Indigenous people were removed from the jurisdiction of the Holy Office (Section 46.11), a recognition that people recently baptized and poorly instructed should not be treated as heretics.

Formal baptism and sustained religious transformation must be distinguished. Baptism could occur within days, while changes in understanding, ritual practice, and community life took generations and were never complete or uniform. Historians infer the extent of change from several kinds of evidence: wills in which Nahua testators arranged Christian burials and masses, records of confraternities, Indigenous-language devotional texts, and legal cases in which communities appealed to their patron saints. Such records show Indigenous people living inside Christian institutions, but also show how they integrated them into communal obligations such as feasting, mutual aid, and tribute.

Indigenous religious specialists faced particular pressure. Priests of the old order were targeted for suppression, and some were executed or driven into hiding, while diviners, healers, and calendar specialists adapted by moving from public to private roles. Women played significant parts as healers, midwives, and keepers of household devotion, roles that sometimes attracted suspicion but also allowed religious knowledge to survive in domestic settings. The next division turns from the beginnings of evangelization to the institutions that sought to root Christianity in place.

Section 46.6

The Colonial Church: Bishops, Parishes, Clergy, and Religious Institutions

Behind the missionary campaigns stood a permanent church organization. Dioceses were created early in the Caribbean, with Santo Domingo established in 1511, and spread across the mainland: Mexico received its first bishop, Zumárraga, in the 1520s–1530s and became an archdiocese in 1546, and Lima became an archbishopric in 1546. In Brazil, the first diocese was established at Bahia in 1551, and Bahia and Rio de Janeiro became ecclesiastical centers later in the seventeenth century. Bishops presided over cathedral chapters, supervised parishes, and held church courts. Provincial and diocesan councils, such as the Mexican council of 1585 and the Lima councils, set rules for clergy and instruction.

The relationship between regular and secular clergy shaped colonial religious life. Friars administered doctrinas, parishes in Indigenous communities, in the sixteenth century and well beyond, but bishops pressed to place these parishes under secular priests who would answer to episcopal authority. The conflict ran through the seventeenth century and culminated in the Bourbon-era reforms taken up in Chapter 48, including the royal order of 1749 to transfer many doctrinas to the secular clergy. Parish priests were paid through a combination of Indigenous community contributions, fees for sacraments, and royal subsidies, and their economic relationships with parishioners, sometimes exploitative, were a source of frequent complaint.

The church's economic and social presence was vast. It collected the tithe, owned urban and rural property through monasteries, convents, and charitable foundations, and made loans, acting as an important colonial lender. It ran hospitals, orphanages, and schools, and the lay Misericórdia brotherhoods in Brazil supported hospitals and charity. Church bells marked the day, and baptisms, marriages, and burials recorded in parish registers, which survive in great numbers, became the foundation of colonial family and demographic history. Everyday contact with the church was thus frequent even where doctrinal knowledge was thin.

Brazil's church differed in structure. With fewer bishops and parish clergy, much religious life in rural areas and mining regions depended on lay brotherhoods, private chapels attached to plantations and ranches, and itinerant priests. In Minas Gerais after the gold rush of the 1690s, the crown restricted the entry of religious orders in an effort to control the mining economy, and lay brotherhoods built many of the region's finest churches. Spanish America's thicker clerical hierarchy and Brazil's lay-centered pattern did not reflect different levels of belief so much as different institutional arrangements.

Section 46.7

Missions as Communities: Conversion, Labor, Protection, and Control

Missions were settlements organized to convert and govern Indigenous peoples who lived beyond established colonial towns or who were gathered from scattered settlements. Their form varied, but common features included a church at the center of a planned town, a resident missionary or small team, instruction in doctrine, common agricultural land, workshops, and an Indigenous government that was usually subordinate to the missionary but sometimes held real authority. Spanish officials called such settlements reducciones (reductions), meaning that dispersed people were "brought back" into planned communities, a term whose coercive implications were evident to Indigenous residents.

Historical Vocabulary
Reducción / Aldeamento

A reducción (Spanish) or aldeamento (Portuguese, also aldeia) was a planned settlement of Indigenous people under missionary or colonial supervision. Gathering dispersed people made them easier to instruct, tax, and govern, and it often reorganized older kinship and territorial ties.

The term has two meanings that students should keep apart. In Peru in the 1570s, viceroy Francisco de Toledo's resettlements, also called reducciones, were secular programs to concentrate Indigenous populations. In frontier mission zones, the term referred to missionary-run towns. Neither should be understood as voluntary communities, though they were not all identical in practice.

Mission life was organized around routines. Residents attended daily prayers and Mass, children learned doctrine and music, men and women worked fields and workshops, and community leaders enforced discipline, which could include flogging. The protection missions offered was real and conditional. Missionaries could shield residents from enslavement by settlers, defend land claims, and provide food during famine, but they also confined people, restricted movement, punished unsanctioned religious activity, and enforced labor. Epidemics were a constant danger because gathering people into dense settlements increased disease transmission, and many missions suffered repeated population losses.

Indigenous residents were not passive. Leaders negotiated with missionaries, and some became powerful local officials. People fled, returned, married outside the mission, and used mission institutions to pursue their own goals. Historians therefore describe missions as sites of negotiation, in which missionaries held considerable power but needed Indigenous cooperation to function. The next sections show how this worked in particular regions.

The Guaraní reductions show how mission government worked in practice. Each town was normally served by two Jesuits, a priest and an assistant, who were few against populations of several thousand, so daily administration rested on an Indigenous cabildo with an alcalde and councilors, supplemented by caciques who retained rank and led militia contingents. Land was divided between family plots, called abambaé, and communal fields, called tupambaé, whose produce supported widows, the sick, church expenses, and tribute and trade obligations. Residents worked on a set schedule, children learned prayers and music, and punishments for absence, theft, or sexual offenses were administered by Indigenous officers under missionary oversight. Villagers could leave for hunting or gathering expeditions, and flight, though costly, occurred.

Family life was shaped by mission rules, and rules differed by region. In the California missions, unmarried girls and women were typically housed at night in a separate dormitory under supervision, a practice that missionaries defended as protection and Indigenous families experienced as confinement. Parish and mission registers of baptisms, marriages, and burials, which survive in large numbers, let historians reconstruct the demography of these communities, including the high infant mortality and the fall in births that followed epidemics. Such records come from the missionaries, and they say little about how residents themselves described their lives, a gap that Indigenous testimony and archaeological work partly address.

Section 46.8

Christianity in New Spain and the Northern Frontiers

In central Mexico, Christianity was rooted through monasteries, parish churches, and Indigenous town governments. Churches rose in the center of each Nahua, Mixtec, Zapotec, or Otomí town, often near the former temple precinct, and community labor built them. In many towns Indigenous leaders controlled the church's finances, organized saints' festivals, and appointed church officers. The Indigenous language of instruction, Nahuatl in central Mexico, became a vehicle for Christian writing, and Nahua authors produced sermons, plays, and devotional texts. In Mixtec and Zapotec regions, Dominican missionaries studied local languages, and in Yucatán, Franciscans created a Maya-language catechism and parish structure.

Religious sites were reshaped rather than simply erased. At Cholula, the Spanish built a church on top of the great pyramid, and in Mexico City the cathedral rose near the former precinct of Tenochtitlan's sacred center. Such placement expressed Christian triumph but also reflected the continued sacredness of the places in Indigenous understanding. Local saints often became associated with older patrons, and communities sometimes treated hills, springs, and caves as sites of Christian devotion. Historians debate how far these reflect continuity of older beliefs or new Christian meaning attached to familiar places.

On the northern frontier, missions followed a different pattern. Franciscans, Jesuits, and others established mission settlements among seminomadic and agricultural peoples of what are now northern Mexico and the present-day southwestern United States. Jesuits in the Pimería Alta, a region including parts of Sonora and Arizona, organized missions through the work of figures such as Eusebio Kino, and in New Mexico Franciscans established missions in Pueblo communities that would face the major uprising of 1680, discussed in Chapter 47. Frontier missions were often small, poorly supplied, and dependent on military escorts, and mortality from disease was high.

The California missions of the late colonial period offer a final example. Beginning with San Diego in 1769, Franciscans under Junípero Serra and his successors founded a chain of twenty-one missions by 1823. Indigenous people of many language groups were gathered into the missions as neophytes, instructed in Christianity, and set to agricultural and craft labor. Mission records show high mortality, particularly among children, and episodes of flight and resistance. Historians continue to debate how to weigh the missionaries' intentions against the coercion and mortality of mission life, and Indigenous communities and descendants have contributed their own interpretations of that history, including debates over the commemoration of Serra himself.

Section 46.9

The Andes: Parish Christianity and the Extirpation of Idolatry

In the Andes, evangelization began with the first Spanish presence in the 1530s but took lasting institutional form in the 1570s and 1580s, as the viceroy Francisco de Toledo resettled Indigenous populations into planned towns (the reducciones noted above) and the Third Council of Lima standardized parish instruction. Andean communities had a religious world organized around ancestors, mountains, springs, and shrines known as huacas, and around the ayllu, the kin-based community. Christianity arrived into a landscape in which sacred power was embedded in specific places and in the bodies of ancestors, whose mummies were venerated.

Andean responses ranged from rapid adoption of Christian forms to careful preservation of older practices. The Taki Onqoy movement of the 1560s, in which preachers called on Andeans to abandon Christianity and return to the huacas, was discovered and suppressed, and is treated in Chapter 47 as part of the history of resistance. More commonly, Andean communities built churches, joined confraternities, and honored saints while continuing rituals for ancestors and mountains. Parish priests, who were often poorly paid and sometimes exploited their parishioners, varied widely in their knowledge and attitude.

Church authorities launched organized campaigns against practices judged idolatrous, called the extirpación de idolatrías, beginning with visitations in the 1570s and intensifying after 1609, when Archbishop Bartolomé Lobo Guerrero authorized visitors to investigate Indigenous communities in the archdiocese of Lima. Visitors such as Francisco de Ávila, who led campaigns in the highlands of Huarochirí, collected testimony, seized and destroyed objects, and punished practitioners with flogging, shearing of hair, and confinement. Pablo José de Arriaga's 1621 manual on the extirpation of idolatry in Peru instructed visitors on how to identify and suppress practices.

These campaigns produced evidence of extraordinary value and significant bias. The Huarochirí Manuscript, written in Quechua around 1608 in connection with Ávila's campaign, records stories of local deities and rituals in Indigenous voices, though it was compiled for a priest with the aim of aiding his work. Visitation records preserve testimony taken under pressure, filtered through translators and legal conventions. Historians such as Kenneth Mills and Sabine MacCormack have shown how such sources can reveal Andean religion and the ways Andeans negotiated with clergy, while insisting that they must be read as products of an inquisitorial setting. Unlike central Mexico, Andean idolatry cases were handled through episcopal visitors and church courts and not by the Holy Office.

Section 46.10

The Jesuit-Guaraní Missions, Portuguese Brazil, and the Caribbean

The most famous mission system was that of the Jesuits and Guaraní, in the borderlands of present-day Paraguay, Argentina, Brazil, and Uruguay. Beginning in 1609–1610 with San Ignacio Guazú and other settlements, Jesuits gathered Guaraní-speaking communities into reductions. The Guaraní had a tradition of kin-based villages with leaders called caciques, and many accepted the missions as a defense against both Spanish settlers seeking labor under the encomienda and Portuguese slave raiders from São Paulo, known as bandeirantes. After devastating raids in the 1620s and 1630s destroyed the northern settlements of Guairá, Jesuits and Guaraní migrated southward, and in 1641 Guaraní forces equipped with firearms by royal permission defeated a bandeirante force at Mbororé.

By the early eighteenth century about thirty reductions existed, with a population that by some counts reached roughly 140,000 in the 1730s, though figures varied with epidemics. Each town had a plaza, church, school, workshops, and communal and family agricultural plots. Guaraní cabildos governed daily affairs under the missionaries' supervision, caciques retained prestige, and community music became renowned, supported by instrument workshops and Indigenous musicians. The reductions produced yerba mate, cotton, and cattle for sale, and they supplied militia to the Spanish crown. The system also imposed communal labor, Christian discipline, and limits on movement, and missionaries controlled the surplus, a source of settler criticism and later political conflict. The wars that followed the Treaty of Madrid in 1750 and the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 belong to Chapter 48.

In Portuguese Brazil, Jesuits arrived with the first governor-general in 1549 and established aldeamentos along the coast and later in Amazonia. Manuel da Nóbrega and José de Anchieta wrote of the need to settle Tupi-speaking communities in villages under Jesuit guidance, and São Paulo grew out of a Jesuit college founded in 1554. Brazilian missions were closely entangled with the settler economy, since settlers demanded Indigenous workers and the crown regulated when Indigenous people could be taken into service. The Jesuit Antônio Vieira, active in Maranhão and Pará in the 1650s, denounced Indigenous enslavement and was expelled by colonists in 1661. Brazilian missions faced persistent epidemic mortality and competition with slaving expeditions, and in Amazonia the Carmelites, Franciscans, and Mercedarians also held missions.

The Caribbean presented an earlier and darker case. Indigenous populations of the Greater Antilles declined drastically within a few decades of 1492 (Chapter 29), and evangelization there became bound up with African slavery. In the Spanish islands, parish churches and monasteries served mixed populations, and in the eastern Caribbean French and later British and Dutch arrangements involved Protestant and Catholic missionaries with distinct policies toward enslaved people. Catholic instruction of enslaved Africans was nominally required under Spanish law, but was applied unevenly, and enslaved Africans in Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico developed their own religious communities through confraternities, discussed in Section 46.18.

Religious Authority, the Inquisition, and the Policing of Belief
Section 46.11

The Spanish Inquisition in Colonial America

The Spanish Inquisition, established in Castile in 1478 under royal authority, investigated heresy among baptized Christians, with a focus in its early decades on converts from Judaism and Islam. In the Americas, the first inquisitorial actions were carried out by bishops and by friars holding delegated powers, as in the trial of Don Carlos of Texcoco in 1539. In 1569 Philip II established permanent tribunals, which began work in Lima in 1570 and in Mexico City in 1571, and a third tribunal opened at Cartagena de Indias in 1610. Each tribunal had inquisitors, secretaries, prosecutors, and a network of local commissioners and lay familiars, and answered to the Suprema, the Inquisition's governing council in Spain.

The central jurisdictional point is that Indigenous people were excluded. After the Zumárraga case, royal policy and later decrees exempted Indigenous people from the Holy Office, treating them as new and poorly instructed Christians who fell under episcopal jurisdiction. Cases of "idolatry" among Indigenous people were handled by bishops and their courts, including the Provisorato de Indios in Mexico City, and in the Andes by visitors. The Holy Office instead prosecuted Spaniards, creoles, people of African descent, and other non-Indigenous subjects, including mestizos and mulatos, though practice and categories varied. Students should therefore resist the common assumption that the Inquisition was the main persecutor of Indigenous religion in Spanish America.

The tribunals' territories were vast and their resources thin. The Lima tribunal nominally served the viceroyalty of Peru, including Chile and the Río de la Plata region, the Mexico City tribunal served New Spain, Central America, and the Philippines, and Cartagena served New Granada and the Caribbean coast. Because inquisitors could not be everywhere, they relied on commissioners among the clergy and lay familiars in towns and ports, and on the cooperation of bishops, customs officials, and informers. Distance meant slow communication with the Suprema in Spain, and local inquisitors acted with considerable independence, sometimes in conflict with viceroys and bishops over precedence and jurisdiction.

The Holy Office also policed books and ideas. Inspectors boarded ships and examined cargoes for prohibited works, tribunals issued edicts and maintained lists based on the Index, and cases of reading forbidden books or holding propositions judged dangerous reached the courts. The large number of denunciations, many of them minor, did not translate into a large number of executions, and many cases ended in warnings, fines, or penance. The Inquisition's chief effect was often less the punishment of a few than the climate of surveillance it created among colonial Spaniards, creoles, and people of African descent, and its confiscations gave it an economic interest in certain accusations.

Historical Vocabulary
Holy Office, Episcopal Jurisdiction, Auto de fe

The Holy Office was the formal name of the Inquisition, an ecclesiastical court under royal supervision that investigated heresy and related offenses among baptized Christians. Episcopal jurisdiction was the authority of a bishop and his courts over clergy and laypeople in a diocese, including cases of religious offense in Indigenous communities. An auto de fe ("act of faith") was a public ceremony at which sentences were announced and reconciled penitents received penalties.

The distinction matters because colonial repression was not the work of a single institution. Bishops, inquisitors, missionaries, and civil courts each had their own powers and priorities. Modern use of "the Inquisition" to cover all religious repression obscures these differences.

Section 46.12

Inquisitorial Procedure, Accusation, Punishment, and Historical Evidence

Inquisitorial cases began with denunciation. Colonial subjects were obliged to report heresy, and the tribunals proclaimed edicts of faith requiring people to come forward. Commissioners took testimony, and inquisitors reviewed it with theological advisers. Accused persons were often arrested and held in secret prisons, their property was sequestered, and the evidence against them was not fully revealed. Trials followed a written procedure with advocates assigned to defendants, and torture could be applied in cases where the court judged that proof was incomplete. Sentences ranged from abjuration and penance to confiscation of property, imprisonment, galley service, and, for those who refused to recant or who relapsed, relaxation to the secular authorities for execution.

The public auto de fe was a central ritual. Convicted people appeared in penitential garments, sentences were read before an audience of officials and townspeople, and the ceremony combined religion, law, and spectacle. Autos were held infrequently, and many cases ended without them. The offenses prosecuted included suspected Judaizing, meaning secret adherence to Jewish practices by New Christians, Protestant belief, bigamy, blasphemy, sorcery and superstition, false claims to holiness, and solicitation in the confessional. Spanish America's tribunals handled a smaller volume of executions than those of the Iberian Peninsula, and the majority of cases involved moral and ritual offenses and not formal heresy.

Episodes of intense persecution did occur. In Mexico, Luis de Carvajal the Younger, a converso of Portuguese descent, was executed in 1596 for Judaizing. In Lima, the tribunal prosecuted a wave of Portuguese New Christian merchants in the 1630s, culminating in a large auto de fe in 1639. Such cases reflected both religious suspicion and economic rivalry, since converso merchants had come to dominate some trades, and confiscated property funded the tribunals. Historians continue to debate how far genuine crypto-Judaism existed among the accused and how far denunciations reflected local conflicts.

Inquisitorial records are among the richest sources for colonial social history and among the most distorted. They preserve voices of ordinary people, including women, enslaved people, and mestizo and mulato defendants, and describe daily life in vivid detail. But testimony was taken under compulsion, filtered through notaries who recorded in formulaic language, and shaped by the questions the court asked. Fear, denunciation, and the possibility of torture influenced what people said. Historians read these sources for what they reveal about beliefs and communities while recognizing that the court's categories organized the story, and that those who were never accused, or whose cases were destroyed, are largely invisible.

Section 46.13

The Portuguese Inquisition and Religious Surveillance in Brazil

The Portuguese Inquisition was established in 1536, largely in response to the presence of New Christians, many of whom had been forced to convert in 1497. Its tribunals sat in Lisbon, Coimbra, and Évora, and unlike Spain, Portugal never established a permanent tribunal in Brazil. Instead, the Lisbon tribunal exercised authority over the colony through a combination of visitations, local commissioners, lay familiars, and the cooperation of bishops and clergy. Suspects were arrested in Brazil and sent to Lisbon for trial, a journey that could take months and was itself punishment.

The principal visitations occurred in Bahia in 1591–1593 under Heitor Furtado de Mendonça, in Pernambuco in the following years, in Bahia again in 1618, and in Grão-Pará in the 1760s. Visitors published edicts inviting confessions and denunciations, and their records preserve thousands of declarations. Cases involved New Christians accused of Judaizing, sexual offenses such as sodomy and bigamy, blasphemy, and magical and heterodox practices. The first Bahian visitation also recorded the Indigenous-influenced movement known as the Santidade of Jaguaripe, in which participants combined Christian and Tupi elements, though Indigenous people as such were not subject to Holy Office prosecution in the same sense.

The institutional difference with Spanish America should not be exaggerated into a contrast between "strong" and "weak" repression. Brazil's inquisitorial surveillance depended on local networks that brought many cases into the system, and New Christians in the sugar economy of Bahia and Pernambuco were vulnerable. But with no resident tribunal, most religious life in Brazil unfolded without inquisitorial interference, and ecclesiastical and secular courts handled many offenses locally. The large number of familiars and commissioners also reflected the prestige and protection that Inquisition affiliation conferred on colonial elites.

The case of Rosa Maria Egipcíaca illustrates how the institution handled religious and racial difference. Born in West Africa, enslaved in Brazil, and forced into prostitution in Minas Gerais before converting, she became a visionary, was supported by sympathetic clergy, and helped establish a house of religious women in Rio de Janeiro. Church authorities arrested her in 1762, she appeared before the Lisbon tribunal in August 1763, and she died on 12 October 1771 while working in the kitchen of the Inquisition's household; the surviving record does not establish that she was imprisoned continuously in the intervening years. The case shows an African-born woman claiming religious authority, drawing patrons among the clergy, and then falling afoul of an institution that distrusted unregulated female sanctity.

Section 46.14

Religious Orthodoxy, Popular Belief, Healing, and Gender

Much religious regulation concerned everyday practice. Church authorities worried about healers who combined prayers, herbs, and amulets, about love magic, divination, and the use of ritual objects, and about popular devotions that escaped clerical supervision. Such practices were common across colonial society and were pursued by Spaniards, creoles, Indigenous people, and people of African descent. The category of "superstition" was fluid, and what one priest tolerated another condemned. Authorities generally treated these practices as moral offenses requiring correction and not as formal heresy, and most cases ended in warnings or modest penalties.

Women figured prominently in these records, as healers, midwives, and practitioners of love magic, and as accusers and witnesses. Historians have emphasized that women's knowledge of healing and reproduction gave them influence within households and communities, even as church and medical authorities distrusted it. Inquisition cases in Mexico, Cartagena, and Lima include African-descended women accused of using ritual practices to heal or influence others, and the testimony shows both the persistence of African knowledge and the way colonial categories of race, gender, and sorcery shaped accusation. Enslaved and free Black practitioners were often vulnerable because they stood outside the protection of powerful patrons.

Women's religious authority could also be claimed through sanctity. Visionaries, mystics, and beatas, lay women who adopted religious lives, attracted followings and sometimes attracted suspicion. Confessors encouraged some, while inquisitors investigated others for false holiness. The line between sanctity and heresy depended on the support of male clerical patrons and on whether visionary claims remained within institutional limits. Rosa Egipcíaca, discussed in the previous section, is one example, and Sor Juana's controversy, examined in Section 46.23, is another.

Students should keep the institutions distinct. A parish priest correcting a parishioner, a bishop's court trying an Indigenous practitioner, a visitor in the Andes destroying huacas, and an inquisitorial tribunal prosecuting a converso merchant operated under different authorities with different procedures. All were part of an effort to regulate belief, and all coexisted with a society in which many people held religious ideas that did not fit official categories. The following division turns from the institutions of regulation to the ways Indigenous and African-descended people shaped religious life.

Division IV · Sections 46.15–46.19
Indigenous Christianity, African Traditions, and Religious Blending
Section 46.15

Indigenous Christianity and the Persistence of Sacred Traditions

By the seventeenth century, most Indigenous communities in the core regions of Spanish America were Christian in the sense that their members were baptized, married in church, attended festivals, and buried in churchyards. What this meant in daily life varied. Historians who study Nahuatl-language sources, such as James Lockhart and Louise Burkhart, describe communities that took Christian saints, churches, and festivals into their own organization of town life, treating the saint as the patron of the community in a way that recalled the patron deity of the altepetl. In Yucatán, Nancy Farriss and others showed that Maya communities preserved elements of their ritual life, including the rain ceremonies and the authority of local ritual specialists, within a Christian framework.

Older sacred traditions persisted in several forms. Communities continued to honor ancestors, to make offerings at springs, hills, and fields, and to consult healers and diviners. Calendars governing agricultural work, ritual, and marriage continued alongside the Christian calendar, and in some regions the two were aligned, as when festivals for saints coincided with planting or harvest. In the Andes, mountain spirits and ancestors remained objects of ritual, and households maintained ceremonies for the fertility of fields and herds. Church authorities noticed such practices, sometimes tolerated them, and sometimes responded with campaigns of suppression.

Historians caution against two opposite errors. One reads all Indigenous Christianity as a mask for hidden resistance, assuming that sincere belief was impossible. The other assumes that baptism meant full adoption of European Christianity. The evidence points to a range of commitments, including people for whom Christianity was a source of real faith and comfort, people who adapted it to local needs, and people who observed it as a social obligation. Indigenous Christians also criticized the clergy, appealed to church authority against abusive priests, and used church institutions to defend land and community rights.

Indigenous religious leadership continued in new forms. Lay officials, including cantors, sacristans, and catechists, held positions of respect and influence, and in many communities, the offices of the confraternities and festival sponsors replaced older religious roles with Christian ones. Some Indigenous communities maintained that they were Christians of long standing, and in later centuries drew on this identity in disputes with the state. Indigenous Christianity was not a residue of the past but a creation of the colonial period that has shaped Indigenous life ever since.

Section 46.16

Saints, Marian Devotion, Pilgrimage, and Sacred Geography

Devotion to saints and especially to the Virgin Mary became a central form of colonial religion. Every town had a patron saint, whose festival organized the year, and churches and chapels housed images that people believed could intercede. Images were often associated with miracle stories and with specific places, creating a sacred geography of shrines. Pilgrimages drew people from surrounding regions, and shrines became centers of trade, social exchange, and local identity.

Our Lady of Guadalupe at Tepeyac, near Mexico City, became the best known of these devotions. A shrine to the Virgin existed at Tepeyac by the mid-sixteenth century, and Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar promoted it in the 1550s, while the Franciscan Sahagún expressed concern that devotion at a site once associated with an Aztec goddess might perpetuate pre-Christian worship. The account of the Virgin's appearance to the Indigenous man Juan Diego in 1531 is first documented in detail in publications of the 1640s, notably a Spanish text by Miguel Sánchez in 1648 and a Nahuatl account published in 1649 associated with Luis Laso de la Vega. Historians debate when and how the narrative developed, and the question is separate from the devotion's importance, which grew steadily among creoles in the seventeenth century and among Indigenous and mestizo communities. Its later role as a symbol of Mexican nationhood, taken up in the independence era (Chapter 52), is a further development.

In the Andes, Our Lady of Copacabana on Lake Titicaca offers another case. An image of the Virgin was carved in the 1580s by Francisco Tito Yupanqui, an Indigenous sculptor of Inca descent, and became the focus of a major pilgrimage center in the region. The shrine's location on a lake and peninsula associated with Inca sacred geography, and its administration by the Augustinians, illustrate how Christian devotion could be placed in landscapes already charged with meaning. Copacabana devotion also spread to other regions, and Bolivian Copacabana is named for it.

These devotions show how sacred geography was built through accumulation. Pilgrimage paths followed older routes, shrines clustered at places associated with miracles, and saints' images circulated through processions. Devotional accounts, which narrate miraculous origins, must be distinguished from the documented history of the shrines, and later nationalist reinterpretations must be distinguished from the beliefs of the colonial worshippers. For students, the key point is that devotions grew from the interaction of Spanish clergy, creole elites, and Indigenous and African-descended worshippers, each of whom contributed.

Section 46.17

African Religious Traditions and Afro-Latin Christianities

Enslaved Africans arrived in the Americas from a variety of regions, including West Central Africa (Kongo and Angola), the Bight of Benin, the Gold Coast, and Upper Guinea, and brought languages, ritual specialists' knowledge, healing traditions, and conceptions of the spiritual world (Chapter 44). Many had already encountered Christianity before arrival, particularly those from the Kingdom of Kongo, where Catholicism had been adopted by the ruling elite in the late fifteenth century, or through Portuguese contact in coastal Africa. Enslavers were required to ensure that enslaved people were baptized, and most were baptized soon after arrival, often without instruction.

African religious practice survived in forms shaped by the conditions of slavery. Healing, divination, and rituals for the dead continued, in the form of practices that colonial documents describe with terms such as calundu in Brazil, where seventeenth- and eighteenth-century sources describe gatherings involving drumming, spirit possession, and healing. In Spanish America and the Caribbean, Inquisition and church records describe African-descended healers and ritual specialists. The sources, produced by accusers, do not reveal how participants understood their own practices, and the terms used by authorities do not map neatly onto particular African traditions.

Africans and their descendants also developed Afro-Latin Christianities. They entered Catholic institutions, joined confraternities, venerated saints such as Our Lady of the Rosary and Saint Benedict, and organized festivals. In Brazil, Black Catholic brotherhoods held coronation ceremonies of "kings" and "queens" of the Kongo that were rooted in central African political culture, a tradition that continued in later congada festivals. Religious identity could be a means of claiming dignity and community in a society that denied it.

Care is needed in treating later traditions. Candomblé in Brazil, Santería in Cuba, Vodou in Haiti, and other religions took organized form largely in the nineteenth century and later, drawing on centuries of accumulated practice. Historians identify colonial antecedents, such as the calundus, the Cuban cabildos de nación (associations of Africans by ethnic origin), and Haitian ritual gatherings, but they do not assume that colonial practice was identical to later organized religion. The Haitian case, in which religion and revolution intersected, is addressed in Chapter 50. This section's evidence supports a picture of varied, adapting traditions and not a single "African religion" preserved intact.

Section 46.18

Confraternities, Festivals, and Everyday Religious Communities

Lay religious associations, known as cofradías in Spanish America and irmandades or confrarias in Brazil, were among the most important institutions of colonial religious life. A confraternity brought together members devoted to a saint or to the Rosary, the Blessed Sacrament, or the Souls in Purgatory. Members paid dues, attended processions and Masses, provided burial and funeral rites for deceased members, and in some cases offered mutual aid in illness and poverty. Officers were elected, and the associations owned property, funded festivals, and sometimes built chapels.

Confraternities served social groups defined by ancestry, occupation, or origin. Spaniards and creoles formed elite brotherhoods, artisans' guilds had patron saints, and Indigenous towns organized brotherhoods that managed festival and burial resources. People of African descent, enslaved and free, formed their own associations, often devoted to Our Lady of the Rosary or Saint Benedict, and these provided funerary rites, mutual aid, and in some cases funds for purchasing freedom. In Brazil, associations such as the irmandades of the Rosary of the Blacks were central to Black social life, and in Minas Gerais they built notable churches. Authorities sometimes viewed Black confraternities with suspicion, and in Mexico City they were restricted after rumors of a conspiracy in 1612.

Documented examples show how these associations worked. In Salvador and in mining towns such as Ouro Preto, Black brotherhoods of Our Lady of the Rosary built their own churches over decades, funded by dues, bequests, and festival collections, and elected annual officers, including a "king" and "queen" who presided at festivals. In Lima, Black confraternities were commonly organized by African ethnic designations used in the colony, such as Congo and Mina, which reflected both the origins recorded by slave traders and affiliations that enslaved people embraced and reshaped. In central Mexico, Nahua cofradías were often endowed with community land or livestock, and their officers kept accounts, hosted the saint's feast, and paid for funeral Masses, so that a confraternity functioned as a communal treasury as well as a devotional group. Andean brotherhoods in Cusco and elsewhere linked Indigenous nobles and commoners in sponsorship of Corpus Christi and other feasts. Disputes over money, office, and precedence were frequent, and they appear in the records as lawsuits before church and royal courts.

Everyday Life and Social History

Festivals, Funerals, and Community Membership

A saint's festival organized work, money, and sociability. In a typical Indigenous town, a sponsor (mayordomo) paid for candles, food, music, and fireworks, and families contributed labor and goods. Festivals offered communal meals, dance, and drink, and reinforced obligations among kin and neighbors. Sponsorship could be expensive, and church authorities complained of festival drinking and cost, while communities valued the status and cohesion it produced. Processions carried images through the streets, and carrying the image was both honor and labor.

Funerals mattered equally. Members expected their brotherhood to attend the burial, pay for Masses, and bury them in consecrated ground, and an enslaved person's brotherhood might pay for a dignified funeral that the owner would not. Women participated as members, officers in some confraternities, cooks, and keepers of household altars, and widows and unmarried women could find in religious associations a social network. Records of confraternities, wills, and parish registers preserve these routines unevenly, and they say less about poorer people, who left fewer written traces.

Confraternities could reproduce hierarchy while creating spaces for social organization and cultural expression. They sorted people by status and race, limited membership, and enforced Christian conformity. They also gave Indigenous and African-descended people institutions they governed, resources they controlled, and public roles in processions and festivals. Historians therefore treat them as sites where colonial order and colonial communities took shape together.

Section 46.19

Religious Blending, Negotiation, and Cultural Transformation

How should historians describe the interaction among Catholic, Indigenous, and African religious traditions? The older word was syncretism, the blending of elements from different religions. The term captured something real, since colonial religious practice combined elements from several traditions, but it also suggested a mixture of fixed components and sometimes implied that the result was impure or inauthentic. Scholars now often prefer terms such as adaptation, negotiation, appropriation, or cultural translation, emphasizing that people actively reinterpreted what they encountered, and that outcomes varied with place and power.

Regional evidence shows the variety. In central Mexico, Nahua communities treated Christian saints as patrons in a way that paralleled older community deities, and translated Christian concepts through Nahuatl categories of obligation and balance. In the Andes, ancestors and mountains retained ritual importance within a Christian frame, and Christian images were placed in landscapes charged with sacred meaning. In Brazil, enslaved Africans and Afro-Brazilians organized brotherhoods and gatherings in which central African elements and Catholic devotions coexisted. In the Guaraní missions, Christian ritual and Indigenous music and leadership merged in a distinct mission culture. No single model of blending fits all these cases.

The terminology itself has a history. The term syncretism was used by earlier scholars, and in the twentieth century it was applied to African-American religions by anthropologists such as Melville Herskovits, who traced African survivals in the Americas. Later scholars argued that it implied pure traditions that were then mixed, and offered alternatives. James Lockhart described how Nahuas and Spaniards each assumed they understood the other's institutions, a pattern he called a kind of productive misunderstanding. Louise Burkhart examined how Nahua concepts of moral balance shaped the reception of Christian ideas of sin. Serge Gruzinski emphasized the circulation of images and the creation of hybrid forms. These approaches are not interchangeable, and each fits some evidence better than others.

A concrete case shows the problem. Sahagún worried that the Virgin of Guadalupe at Tepeyac might be worshipped as a continuation of an older goddess whom the Nahuas called by a title meaning "our mother," and he said so in a text written decades later. Whether sixteenth-century Nahua pilgrims saw Mary, the earlier goddess, or both is not directly documented, and no source lets historians reconstruct every worshipper's private belief; the surviving sources are written by Spaniards and by Nahua authors working within Christian frameworks. Historians therefore infer the meaning of devotion from patterns of practice, wills, and later texts, and treat any single label as a hypothesis.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Christianization Suppress Indigenous and African Religious Cultures, or Create New Ones?

The question: How should historians understand the religious change of the colonial period, as the destruction of older religions, as the adoption of Christianity, or as the creation of new religious cultures?

Interpretation A — Suppression and coercion: Scholars in this tradition, shaped by studies of the conquest and by the work of Robert Ricard on the "spiritual conquest," emphasize the destruction of temples and books, the persecution of religious specialists, the coercive context of baptism, and the loss of institutions that sustained earlier religions. On this view, colonial Christianity was imposed on populations with little choice, and surviving older practices were fragments preserved against pressure.
Interpretation B — Adaptation and creation: Others, working from Indigenous-language sources and local records, emphasize that Indigenous and African-descended people actively shaped Christianity, taking up institutions such as confraternities and saints' cults, translating concepts, and producing new art, music, and ritual. On this view, colonial religion was a creative field in which communities made their own meanings, and the result cannot be reduced to loss.

Evidence: Interpretation A relies on missionary reports, trial records, and accounts of destruction such as those at Maní. Interpretation B draws on Nahuatl wills, confraternity records, Indigenous-authored texts, and material evidence such as church art. Each also depends on the sources' perspective, since missionary sources emphasize both Christian success and idolatry, and Indigenous sources were composed under colonial constraints.

Why it matters: The two readings produce different histories, one of cultural destruction and the other of cultural creativity. Most historians now combine them, recognizing that coercion, persecution, genuine belief, selective adaptation, and persistence occurred within the same societies and sometimes the same individuals. The debate also affects how modern Indigenous and Afro-Latin American religious identities are understood.

The strongest recent work treats religious change as a negotiation under unequal power. Missionaries and priests held authority and could punish, but they depended on Indigenous and African-descended collaborators and could not control interpretation. Believers adapted what they received to their circumstances, sometimes in ways clergy tolerated and sometimes in ways they condemned. The result was a variety of colonial Christianities, each marked by its region's history, and the following division turns to the cultural and intellectual expressions of this religious world.

Section 46.20

Schools, Colleges, Universities, and the Colonial World of Learning

Education was largely a church enterprise. Monasteries taught reading and doctrine, parish schools instructed children, and Jesuit colleges, which spread across Spanish and Portuguese America after 1549, offered Latin, rhetoric, philosophy, and theology to sons of colonial elites. The Jesuits' college system was among the most important educational networks of the colonial world, and Jesuit schools in cities such as Lima, Mexico City, Córdoba, and Bahia trained future clergy, officials, and professionals. Universities followed: Santo Domingo received a papal charter in 1538, and the royal and pontifical universities of Mexico City and Lima (San Marcos) were founded in 1551, with teaching beginning in the early 1550s.

Access was unequal. Women were generally excluded from universities and from formal secondary education, though convents and private tutors educated some. Indigenous people were educated in missions and a few colleges, such as Tlatelolco, but formal advanced education for Indigenous students declined after the sixteenth century, and the priesthood was effectively closed to most Indigenous and African-descended candidates by restrictions on ancestry. Enslaved people received religious instruction at best. Literacy rates were low, concentrated among urban elites, clergy, and officials, and many people encountered texts through oral reading and sermons.

Printing and libraries shaped learned communities. Mexico City's press began in 1539 and Lima's in the 1580s, and they produced catechisms, grammars, legal and devotional texts, and occasional poetry. Private and monastic libraries held works of theology, law, classical literature, and science, and viceregal courts supported poets and scholars. Portuguese America diverged sharply: the crown suppressed the short-lived Rio de Janeiro press of Antônio Isidoro da Fonseca (1747), sustained authorized printing began only in 1808, and the crown maintained no university in the colony, so Brazilian students seeking higher education went to Coimbra. This difference contributed to Brazil's distinct intellectual and political development, which later chapters take up.

Specific institutions show the pattern. The Jesuit college in Bahia, begun in 1550 after the order's arrival in Brazil, taught reading, writing, and Latin to settlers' sons and to some Indigenous and mixed-ancestry boys, and later trained men for the priesthood. In Lima and Cusco, Jesuits and others opened colleges for the sons of Indigenous lords, such as the Colegio del Príncipe in Lima, founded around 1619 (sources place its establishment between about 1618 and 1620), to prepare them as intermediaries between their communities and colonial authority. Curricula emphasized scholastic philosophy and theology based on Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas, with some attention to mathematics and astronomy; learned figures such as the Mexican scholar Carlos de Sigüenza y Góngora combined clerical training with work in science and history.

Girls' education was largely informal. Small neighborhood schools, called amigas in Spanish America, taught reading, prayers, and sewing to girls of modest means, and elite girls learned at home or in convents. Because many women could read but not write, literacy statistics based on signatures understate reading ability. Access to learning therefore depended on a combination of sex, ancestry, wealth, and place, and the colonial world of learning was both a means of advancement for a few and a barrier for most.

Section 46.21

Indigenous Literacy, Scholarship, and the Written Record

Indigenous people produced much of the surviving record of colonial religious and historical life. The Florentine Codex, compiled in the 1550s–1570s under Bernardino de Sahagún, is a twelve-book encyclopedia of Nahua life in Nahuatl and Spanish with hundreds of illustrations. Sahagún directed the project, but Nahua scholars, including Antonio Valeriano and others trained at Tlatelolco, conducted interviews with elders, wrote the Nahuatl text, and supervised painters. The work is therefore a collaboration, shaped by Sahagún's religious aims and Nahua scholars' choices of what to record, and it remains indispensable for understanding the Nahua world.

Other Indigenous authors wrote their own histories. Domingo Chimalpahin of Chalco, born in 1579, wrote annals in Nahuatl that combined local tradition with colonial events. In the Andes, Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, an Indigenous noble, wrote and illustrated around 1615 a very large letter to Philip III, the Nueva corónica y buen gobierno, which criticized colonial abuses, recounted Andean history, and proposed reform. Garcilaso Inca de la Vega, a mestizo born in Cusco and living in Spain, published his Comentarios reales in 1609, offering an Inca-centered history. Maya books of Chilam Balam and the K'iche' Popol Vuh, preserved in later copies, carried older traditions into alphabetic writing.

These texts need careful reading. Authors wrote in colonial conditions, often with ecclesiastical oversight and for specific audiences, and adopted Christian categories. Many carried messages to Spanish officials, such as Guaman Poma's argument that Andean elites could govern better than abusive colonial officials. They preserve Indigenous perspectives and also show the constraints on expression. Historians use them to recover Indigenous histories, community institutions, and ideas about the colonial order, while remembering that texts that were suppressed or never written are missing.

The making of the Florentine Codex shows how this scholarship worked. Sahagún began gathering information in the late 1550s in towns near Mexico City, interviewing elders and having Nahua scholars write down what they said, and he revised the material repeatedly with Nahua assistants over several decades. The final manuscript, with Nahuatl and Spanish columns and many illustrations, was sent to Spain and is now held in Florence, which gives the work its name. The bilingual manuscript was completed by 1579 and sent to Spain. Its twelfth book narrates the conquest from a Nahua point of view, and in 1585 Sahagún produced a separate revised account of the conquest, a distinct text and not a revision of the surviving manuscript in place. The collaborators' choices shaped what survived, and their own accounts of how they worked are scarce.

Guaman Poma's manuscript, of roughly 1,200 pages with some 400 drawings, was addressed to the king but was probably never read by him, and it lay unnoticed in the Royal Library in Copenhagen until its rediscovery in 1908. Titu Cusi Yupanqui, ruler of the Neo-Inca state of Vilcabamba, dictated an account of the conquest in 1570 to a Spanish friar and a secretary, a reminder that Indigenous authorship often passed through intermediaries. Together these works show Indigenous writers using Christian and legal vocabulary to argue about history, legitimacy, and good government.

Section 46.22

Convents, Religious Women, and Intellectual Life

Convents were prominent institutions in colonial cities. Mexico City, Lima, Puebla, Cusco, Quito, and Salvador held multiple convents, some with hundreds of residents including nuns, servants, enslaved women, and children. Women entered for religious reasons, for family strategy, or because they lacked acceptable marriage options. Entry typically required a dowry and proof of respectable birth, so wealthy and well-connected families sent daughters to the most prestigious houses. Convents served as places of education, spiritual leadership, and cultural production, with music, drama, and manuscript writing among their activities.

Convents also reproduced social hierarchy. Wealthy "black veil" nuns could hold offices and own cells and servants, while "white veil" nuns served the community, and enslaved women performed domestic labor. Indigenous women's convents existed, such as Corpus Christi in Mexico City, founded in 1724 for Indigenous noblewomen, though they were exceptional. Many women who lacked dowries entered recogimientos or lay religious houses, or remained as beatas. Convents were also financial institutions that held property and loaned money.

Women's religious writing flourished within these walls. Nuns wrote spiritual autobiographies at the request of confessors, including the Colombian Madre Francisca Josefa del Castillo, and visions and devotional works circulated in manuscript. Convents offered intellectual opportunities denied elsewhere, along with surveillance by confessors and bishops who could censor and discipline. Sor Juana is the most famous of these women, though her circumstances should not be taken as typical of nuns or of colonial women.

Convent economy and daily life illuminate these hierarchies. Entering a prestigious convent in New Spain or Peru typically required a dowry that could amount to thousands of pesos, and convents invested these funds in loans and urban property. Large houses, such as Santa Catalina in Arequipa, founded in 1579, grew into walled districts with streets, cells, kitchens, and gardens, where nuns lived with servants and enslaved women. The day was organized around the Divine Office, communal prayer, meals, and work, and wealthier nuns could cultivate music, writing, and sweet-making that became a cultural tradition. Convents also served as refuges for widows and divorcées, and as schools for girls boarded there.

Surveillance was constant. Confessors directed nuns' spiritual lives and could demand written accounts of their inner experience, which is how many spiritual autobiographies came to exist. Madre Francisca Josefa del Castillo, who lived in Tunja in New Granada from 1671 to 1742, wrote a spiritual autobiography and devotional meditations at the request of confessors, and her work shows both an intense inner life and the pressure of clerical oversight. Bishops visited convents to enforce enclosure and reform, and conflicts between nuns and clerical authorities over elections, property, and discipline appear in many records.

Section 46.23

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz: Knowledge, Gender, and Religious Authority

Juana Ramírez de Asbaje was born around 1648–1651 in San Miguel Nepantla, near Mexico City, the illegitimate daughter of a Spanish father and a creole mother. By her own later account, she learned to read as a young child and taught herself from her grandfather's library. As a teenager she lived at the viceregal court as a lady-in-waiting to the vicereine, and in 1669 she entered the convent of San Jerónimo, choosing religious life partly to secure the time and security to study. Over the next two decades she accumulated a large library and wrote poetry, plays, and theological and philosophical texts, and the viceregal court, especially the vicereine María Luisa Manrique de Lara, Countess of Paredes, became her patron.

Her works include love poems and philosophical verse, among them a long poem, Primero sueño, on the soul's attempt to grasp the universe; the sacramental play El divino Narciso; religious songs called villancicos, some of which included Black and Nahuatl voices; and satirical verse on gender, such as the poem beginning "Hombres necios." The controversy that defined her late career began in 1690 when she wrote, at a man's request, a critique of a famous sermon on Christ's greatest favor to humanity, a sermon preached in Lisbon in 1650 by the Jesuit Antônio Vieira, whose career is part of Brazil's story. The Bishop of Puebla, Manuel Fernández de Santa Cruz, published her critique as the Carta atenagórica, and then wrote to her under the pen name Sor Filotea de la Cruz, praising her intelligence while urging her to devote herself to sacred rather than secular letters.

The Respuesta is as much an argument as an autobiography. Sor Juana begins by apologizing for her silence and for her presumption, then narrates how she learned to read as a child, begged to dress as a boy in order to attend university, and entered the convent as the most suitable life available to someone who wished to study. She argues that no branch of knowledge is useless for understanding scripture, since the interpreter of sacred texts needs grammar, rhetoric, history, natural philosophy, and more. She lists learned and holy women from scripture and antiquity, and addresses the Pauline instruction that women keep silent in church, arguing that it concerns public preaching and not private study or the teaching of other women by learned women. She says that she wrote on her own initiative only one work, the poem Primero sueño, and that most of her writing was done at others' request, a statement that serves her defense of the limited and obedient character of her activity.

The Carta atenagórica had made a theological argument that would have been bold even for a male theologian, since it disagreed with a celebrated Jesuit preacher about the nature of Christ's greatest favor to humankind. The bishop's response, which praised her talents while telling her to turn from worldly letters, accepted the intellectual standing of the work while rejecting its author's direction. Sor Juana's reply neither renounces learning nor claims equality of status. It claims a place for women within the Catholic order of knowledge, and it does so with irony, learning, and the careful self-presentation that her position required.

Section 46.24

The Colonial Baroque: Architecture, Painting, Sculpture, and Sacred Images

The Baroque, an ornate and dramatic style that emerged in seventeenth-century Europe, flourished in colonial Latin America as a vehicle for religious devotion and political display. Churches featured elaborate carved altarpieces, painted and gilded images, and facades with dense sculpture. Church building was funded by the crown, bishops, religious orders, confraternities, and individual patrons, and construction employed large workforces. Notable examples include the Church of La Compañía in Cusco, built in the seventeenth century, the Rosary Chapel in Puebla, and in the eighteenth century the church of Santa Prisca in Taxco, as well as the churches of Salvador and Minas Gerais in Brazil.

Indigenous and African-descended artisans were central to this art. Indigenous craftsmen carved, painted, and gilded, and continued techniques such as feather mosaic in Mexico and the use of lightweight maize-stalk paste for Christ figures among the Purépecha. In the Andes, Cusco-school painters, among them Indigenous artists such as Diego Quispe Tito, produced devotional paintings with local landscapes and motifs, including the well-known Last Supper in Cusco's cathedral, painted around 1753 by Marcos Zapata, which includes a roasted guinea pig. The Quito school included the Indigenous sculptor Manuel Chili, known as Caspicara. In Brazil, Antônio Francisco Lisboa, called Aleijadinho (c. 1738–1814), the son of a Portuguese father and an enslaved African mother, produced the Passion sculptures at the sanctuary of Bom Jesus de Matosinhos in Congonhas in the late 1790s and the twelve prophets there between 1800 and 1805, a sequence that shows Baroque production in Minas Gerais continuing across the turn of the century, and he designed churches in Minas Gerais.

Regional variation was marked. New Spain's churches combined wealth from silver mining with a dense decorative style, in the "Churrigueresque" style of the eighteenth century. Andean Baroque, shaped by mestizo and Indigenous workshops, developed distinctive carved facades. Brazil's Baroque ranged from the gilded interiors of Salvador to the sculptural austerity of Minas churches built by lay brotherhoods. Talavera tile, carved stone, local woods, and pigments reflected local materials. Treating the Baroque as an import misses the way local artisans, patrons, and audiences made it their own, and the names of most of these artists are not recorded.

Section 46.25

Music, Theater, Festivals, and the Making of Colonial Religious Culture

Music and performance carried religion into public life. Cathedral choirs and instrumentalists performed polyphonic works, and composers such as Juan de Araujo, active in Lima and La Plata (Sucre), and Manuel de Sumaya in Mexico City produced sacred music. In 1701 the Spaniard Tomás de Torrejón y Velasco composed in Lima the opera La púrpura de la rosa, the first known opera in the Americas. Villancicos, sung at major feasts, were performed in Spanish with passages in African and Indigenous languages or imitations of them, mixing Black and Indigenous characters into the sacred festival, in ways that were sometimes affectionate and sometimes caricaturing. The Jesuit missions of Chiquitos and the Guaraní reductions maintained Indigenous choirs and instrumental workshops, and their music survives in archives.

Religious drama and festivals were equally important. Autos sacramentales, including Sor Juana's El divino Narciso, were performed at Corpus Christi. Corpus Christi processions in Cusco displayed Indigenous nobles in elaborate costume, and Cusco's Corpus Christi paintings preserve a record of this public ritual. Carnival, saints' days, and civic celebrations combined religious and secular elements, and festivals offered Indigenous and African-descended people occasions to perform identity in public space. Authorities regulated festivals and complained of disorder, but they also relied on them to display order and unity.

By the late eighteenth century, colonial religious culture was a patchwork of regional Christianities. Parish churches and mission towns, confraternities and convents, Indigenous festivals and Black brotherhoods, inquisitorial courts and church schools, Baroque altarpieces and sung villancicos together made a religious world that was distinctly Latin American without being uniform. Bourbon and Pombaline reforms, taken up in Chapter 48, would challenge several of its institutions, particularly the Jesuit missions and the privileges of religious orders, but the religious cultures created in the previous centuries would survive and shape later developments.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Religious Life from the Sixteenth to the Eighteenth Century

What Changed

  • Evangelization moved from mass baptism by itinerant friars to parish structures, permanent dioceses, and confraternities
  • Institutions of regulation developed, including tribunals and visitation systems
  • Indigenous and African-descended communities developed distinctive Christian practices, saints' cults, and festival systems
  • Colonial art, music, and literature matured into regionally distinct forms

What Stayed the Same

  • The crown's authority over the church through patronage
  • Unequal access to clerical office, education, and convents by ancestry, gender, and wealth
  • Indigenous and African-descended reliance on community institutions to organize religious life
  • The gap between official doctrine and local practice

Who experienced change most directly: Indigenous communities in the sixteenth century faced the sharpest disruption, and Africans and their descendants confronted the changes of enslavement and forced baptism. Greater continuity: Elite clergy and officials continued to hold authority over religious institutions, and everyday agricultural and household rituals persisted in many communities. Why the pattern matters: The colonial church shaped Latin American societies deeply while being reshaped by the people it sought to govern.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Christianity became a defining institution of colonial Latin America and that the religious cultures it produced were neither uniform nor simply imposed from Europe. They emerged from conquest, evangelization, coercion, Indigenous and African participation, negotiation, conflict, and cultural creation. Religion was both an instrument of colonial power and a field in which colonial peoples built distinctive intellectual, social, artistic, and spiritual worlds.

Division I showed how Christianity became tied to empire. Through royal patronage, the Spanish and Portuguese crowns controlled the organization of the American church and assumed responsibility for evangelization, fusing religious and political authority. After conquest, friars launched campaigns of baptism and destroyed temples, and episodes such as Maní show the violence of early efforts. The religious orders differed in tradition and approach, and the work of translation, catechism, and instruction depended on Indigenous scribes, teachers, and interpreters. Indigenous converts responded in varied ways, and baptism was not the same as sustained religious transformation.

Division II examined the institutions that rooted Christianity in place. Dioceses, parishes, and religious orders formed the colonial church, with tension between regular and secular clergy. Missions were planned communities that offered protection, instruction, and economic organization while imposing labor, discipline, and confinement. Regional cases varied. Central Mexico worked through Indigenous town governments and monasteries, the northern frontier through small and vulnerable missions, the Andes through parishes and campaigns against idolatry, the Guaraní region through large Jesuit reductions, and Brazil through aldeamentos and lay brotherhoods.

Division III distinguished the institutions that policed belief. The Spanish Inquisition's permanent tribunals at Lima, Mexico City, and Cartagena prosecuted non-Indigenous subjects, while Indigenous religious offenses fell under episcopal authority. Procedure was based on denunciation, secret imprisonment, and written trials, and records preserve voices of the accused under compulsion. Portuguese Brazil had no resident tribunal and was reached through visitations and local agents. Regulation of popular belief and healing involved multiple authorities, and women and African-descended practitioners were prominent in the records.

Division IV turned to the religious lives of Indigenous and African-descended people. Indigenous Christianity combined real commitment, local reinterpretation, and persistent older practices. Marian devotions and saints' cults created sacred geographies, with Guadalupe and Copacabana as examples that require distinguishing documented history from devotional narrative. African religious knowledge survived in transformed forms, and Afro-Latin Christianities developed through confraternities and festivals. Confraternities provided mutual aid, funerary rites, and community identity while reproducing social hierarchy. Historians' debates over syncretism and adaptation show that the picture is neither simple destruction nor simple blending.

Division V examined education, women's religious communities, and the Baroque. Church-supported schools, Jesuit colleges, and universities educated colonial elites, while access was limited by gender, ancestry, and wealth, and Brazil lacked a press or university. Indigenous authors such as Chimalpahin and Guaman Poma, and the Nahua scholars of the Florentine Codex, created enduring records. Convents offered women religious and intellectual opportunity within hierarchy. Sor Juana's career and her Respuesta show both the achievements and the constraints of women's learning. The colonial Baroque, shaped by Indigenous and African-descended artisans, took distinct regional forms, and music and festivals carried religious culture into public life.

Regional comparison runs through the chapter. New Spain and the Andes shared thick ecclesiastical hierarchies but differed in the character of Indigenous religious life and in the instruments of suppression. The Río de la Plata produced a mission system of unusual scale, Brazil relied on lay brotherhoods and distant inquisitorial supervision, and the Caribbean's religious life was shaped early by Indigenous collapse and African slavery. The Spanish and Portuguese empires used similar religious justifications with different institutions, and these differences affected education, printing, and clerical presence.

Evidence shapes the history throughout. Missionary chronicles, inquisitorial trials, visitation reports, and parish records were produced by institutions with purposes of their own, and Indigenous-authored texts, wills, confraternity records, art, and architecture offer other perspectives. Historians read these sources against one another, asking who produced them and whose voices are missing. The chapter has shown that many of the best-known sources about Indigenous and African religion were produced by those seeking to suppress or correct it.

The chapter leaves several themes for later chapters. Large Indigenous uprisings, maroon communities, and other forms of resistance belong to Chapter 47. The Bourbon and Pombaline reforms, including the expulsion of the Jesuits, belong to Chapter 48 and began the dismantling of the mission system. The religious cultures described here also shaped independence-era politics and nineteenth-century debates over church property, education, and citizenship. Students should carry forward the understanding that colonial Christianity was a contested and creative field, built by many hands.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Christianity was woven into nearly every institution of colonial Latin America, from royal government and local parishes to schools, convents, festivals, and art. It was a vehicle of conquest and a resource for the conquered, a means of surveillance and a language of community. Understanding it requires attention to institutions and to the people who lived within and around them.

The chapter also shows how much religious change depended on Indigenous and African-descended participation. Nahua scribes, Andean artists, Guaraní musicians, Black confraternity officers, and women in convents and households shaped what Christianity meant in practice. The surviving evidence, produced largely by clergy and officials, must be read with attention to its purposes and its silences.

These religious cultures shaped later history. Saints' cults and confraternities provided models of community organization, Indigenous and Afro-Latin traditions continued to develop, and the colonial church's property and privileges became central issues in nineteenth-century politics. The Baroque, the literary achievements of figures such as Sor Juana, and the written records of Indigenous authors remain central to how the colonial past is understood.

Chapter 47 turns from religious institutions and cultural creation to resistance and survival: Indigenous revolts, maroon communities, legal challenges, and everyday negotiation with colonial authority. Students should carry forward the view that religion, culture, and resistance were closely connected, and that colonial society was shaped by people at every level.
