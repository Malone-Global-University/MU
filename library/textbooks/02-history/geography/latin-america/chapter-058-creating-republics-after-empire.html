Creating Republics after Empire

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Constitution Is Not a State

Independence answered the question of who would no longer rule. It did not answer who would rule, by what right, over whom, and with what revenue.

In the decades after 1810, Latin Americans wrote dozens of constitutions, convened assemblies, held elections, and argued bitterly about federalism, citizenship, the Church, and the army. Mexico adopted a federal constitution in 1824, the same year that Brazil’s emperor issued a charter with a Moderating Power and the Central American provinces formed a federation. Bolívar drafted a constitution for Bolivia in 1826 with a president for life, and Chile adopted a strong-executive constitution in 1833 that lasted for decades. This chapter asks how former imperial territories attempted to become functioning, legitimate, and durable states, and why that effort proved so difficult.

Its central argument is that independence did not automatically produce an effective nation-state. New governments had to decide who held sovereignty, who counted as a citizen, how power would be divided, who would collect revenue, how armies would be controlled, and whether the populations they claimed to govern accepted the new order. These were separate problems, solved at different speeds and in different ways. A government could win recognition abroad without controlling its provinces, write a liberal constitution without being able to collect taxes, or hold elections that conferred legitimacy on some groups while excluding others.

The chapter therefore treats the early republics as more than a record of unstable governments and military strongmen. The period saw genuine constitutional experimentation: assemblies debated sovereignty and rights, legislatures bargained over budgets, courts applied inherited law to new disputes, elections mobilized voters, newspapers created a public sphere, and codification movements tried to replace colonial law. It also saw recurring pronunciamientos, civil wars, defaults, and the use of emergency powers. Historians disagree about how to weigh these developments, and the chapter presents the disagreement in a Historical Debate.

The chapter looks at the whole region, not only at Spanish America. Brazil’s constitutional monarchy, Haiti’s postrevolutionary constitutions, and the continued colonial status of Cuba and Puerto Rico are part of the comparison, and Indigenous communities, enslaved and free people of African descent, women, soldiers, and clergy appear as participants in these politics, not merely as subjects of it. The United States and Britain enter where recognition, loans, and trade shaped policy, but the central actors are Latin American.

The evidence comes from constitutions, assembly debates, electoral records, newspapers, petitions, court and notarial records, fiscal accounts, and military correspondence. Each kind of source reflects its makers’ purposes. Constitutions record aspirations as much as practice, newspapers speak for urban and literate readers, and the records of ordinary people survive mainly when they entered litigation or petition. Six Primary Source Windows compare constitutional texts from Gran Colombia, Mexico, Brazil, Bolivia, Haiti, and Chile, and the chapter treats each as evidence to be analyzed, not as a simple description of how governments worked.

The thirty-two sections are organized in seven divisions: the transition from empire to republic; the design of institutions; citizenship, race, slavery, Indigenous status, and gender; finance, economy, territory, and administration; ideas, religion, and public culture; armies, caudillos, and civil conflict; and comparative paths to nationhood. Examples from different countries are used to illustrate general problems, and the detailed national histories are reserved for the chapters that follow.

This chapter belongs to Part XIII of Volume III. Chapter 57 completed the account of independence with Central America and the Spanish Caribbean, and this chapter establishes the conceptual and institutional framework for Chapters 59 to 62, which follow Mexico, Gran Colombia and the Andes, the Brazilian Empire, and the Southern Cone and Central America through the middle of the nineteenth century.

Historical Context

Colonial government in Spanish America rested on a hierarchy of viceroys, captains-general, audiencias, intendants, and town councils, together with a Church that held the royal patronage and kept the parish registers. Law consisted of Castilian codes, the legislation for the Indies, royal decrees, and custom, applied through courts that included separate jurisdictions for the clergy and the army. Indigenous communities held a distinct status as the “republic of Indians,” paid tribute, and had protected rights to communal land, while a system of legal and social categories classified the rest of the population. Chapters 40 through 47 describe these structures in detail.

Portuguese America had its own institutions, which changed after 1808, when Napoleon’s invasion of Portugal drove the royal court to Rio de Janeiro. Brazil became the seat of the monarchy, was raised to a kingdom within a United Kingdom in 1815, and declared independence in 1822 under a prince of the ruling house. The continuity of the dynasty distinguished its path from that of Spanish America, as Chapters 56 and 61 explain.

The crisis that began in 1808 with the French occupation of the Iberian peninsula removed the authority that had held the empire together. In Spanish America, juntas formed in the name of the captive king, and the Cádiz Cortes gave Americans experience of representation and constitutional government, and when Ferdinand VII returned in 1814 he suppressed both. The wars of independence that followed, treated in Chapters 52 through 57, were multiple conflicts shaped by regional politics, social tensions, and military campaigns, and they ended unevenly between 1821 and 1826, with Spanish forces holding out in a few places longer.

The wars left damaged economies, indebted treasuries, large armies, and strong regional leaders. Haiti, independent since 1804, offered both a model of emancipation and a warning to slaveholding elites, as Chapter 50 describes. Britain and the United States sought trade and recognition, and Spain continued to refuse it. Into this setting, new governments introduced written constitutions and assemblies that had to operate among the continuing structures of colonial law, property, and social rank. The next thirty-two sections explain how they did so.

Section 58.1

58.1 Independence Won, Government Unsettled

The wars of independence ended in stages, and so did the colonial order they overthrew. Royalist forces in Peru were defeated at Ayacucho in December 1824, but the fortress of Callao held out until January 1826, the island of Chiloé fell the same month, and the Spanish garrison at San Juan de Ulúa, off Veracruz, surrendered only in November 1825. Spain did not recognize Mexico until 1836 and the South American republics until the middle decades of the century, so the new governments governed for years under the threat of reconquest. In this setting a victorious army was not the same as a legitimate government: generals could win battles, but the authority to tax, to appoint officials, and to command obedience had to be re-established on a new foundation after the Crown that had supplied it was gone.

That authority did not have to be built from nothing, because the machinery of colonial government survived the transfer of sovereignty. Viceroys and captains-general vanished, yet the audiencias became supreme courts, intendants were replaced by prefects or governors with similar duties, town councils continued to meet, and the parish clergy continued to keep the registers of baptism, marriage, and death that served as the only reliable population records. Tax collectors, notaries, and clerks often kept their posts under the new flag. This continuity gave the republics a working administration on the first day, but it also meant that regimes depended on officials whose training and loyalties had been formed under the monarchy, and whose willingness to serve could not be assumed to rest on belief in the new order.

Peru between 1821 and 1826 shows how provisional authority operated in practice. San Martín took the title of Protector after entering Lima in 1821 and governed by decree until he left in September 1822; a congress then met but was soon overshadowed by military events, and in February 1824 it granted Bolívar dictatorial powers to finish the war. Within five years Peru was ruled successively by a protector, a congress, a junta, a dictator, and a council of ministers, each justified by the emergency. Such arrangements show that the early republics often governed through exceptional powers granted in the name of the war before ordinary constitutional procedures could function, and that the shift back to constitutional government was itself a political contest.

Many groups had stakes in how the transition was handled. Patriot officers and soldiers expected pay, promotion, pensions, and land, while the treasury was empty. Former royalists wanted protection of their persons and property, and the sequestration of royalist estates (secuestro) created both aggrieved owners and eager claimants among the victors. Creole merchants and landowners wanted order and open trade, Indigenous and Afro-descended communities wanted recognition of promises made during the war about tribute, land, and freedom, and provincial towns wanted to keep the autonomy they had exercised while the central authority was weak. The recurring political conflicts of the following decades arose from these competing claims, not from a failure to understand republican forms.

The significance of this transition for the rest of the chapter is that legitimacy had to be earned continuously, through the capacity to defend territory, collect revenue, and deliver justice. Governments that could do these things gained recognition at home and abroad, and those that could not found themselves facing pronunciamientos, provincial secession, or foreign pressure. The sections that follow examine the intellectual sources of republican government, the institutions designed to give it form, and the social, fiscal, and military conditions that tested them.

Section 58.2

58.2 Cádiz, Atlantic Constitutionalism, and the Origins of Republican Thought

The constitutional experience that most directly shaped Spanish America’s first republics was Spanish. The Cortes that met at Cádiz in 1810–1812, while most of the peninsula was under French occupation, included deputies from the American territories, and its Constitution of 1812 declared sovereignty to reside in the nation, created a single national legislature, limited the monarch’s powers, and established a three-stage system of indirect elections from parishes through districts to provinces. It also created constitutional town councils (ayuntamientos) in communities of a thousand inhabitants or more, and elected provincial deputations. Its definition of the Spanish nation included Indigenous people of the Americas as citizens, and the Cortes abolished Indigenous tribute in 1811 and the mita and other forced personal services in 1812.

The constitution was also restrictive. Its citizenship provisions excluded people of African descent, who could obtain citizenship only through an individual grant of the Cortes for merit, and it kept the Catholic faith as the sole religion of the monarchy. Its effects were nonetheless wide. Elections held under its rules in 1812–1814 and again after its restoration in 1820 drew thousands of voters into formal politics in Mexico, Peru, Quito, and elsewhere, and in some Andean towns Indigenous electors and officials took part. When Ferdinand VII annulled the constitution in 1814 and restored tribute and other exactions, many Americans had already experienced elected local government and the principle that taxation required consent.

Insurgent governments in the Americas wrote their own charters in the same years. Venezuela’s constitution of 1811 created a federal republic of provinces, the Mexican insurgents’ Decree of Apatzingán of 1814 asserted popular sovereignty and a collective executive, and the Río de la Plata’s Assembly of 1813 passed measures including a free-womb law. These texts borrowed from the United States, the French Revolution, and British political writers, but they were also arguments over local problems such as provincial rights, Indigenous tribute, and the status of free people of color. Bolívar’s Jamaica Letter of 1815 and his address to the Congress of Angostura in 1819 show a leader adapting liberal ideas to what he took to be Spanish America’s social conditions, arguing for a strong executive and a hereditary senate as a counterweight to instability.

A further source was the older Hispanic political tradition. Neo-scholastic theorists, including the sixteenth-century Jesuit Francisco Suárez, taught that political authority passes from God to the community, which transfers it to a ruler; when the king was captured by Napoleon in 1808, American juntas argued that sovereignty had reverted to the people or to the kingdoms that composed the monarchy. This argument could be turned to different ends, justifying either loyalty to a captive king or independence from a usurping regime, and it gave provincial councils a ready-made claim to authority that would reappear in debates over federalism.

The result was not an imported blueprint but a repertoire. Drafters chose provisions, such as indirect election, municipal councils, bicameral legislatures, and declarations of rights, from several traditions and combined them with local demands. Historians therefore read these early constitutions as arguments within particular political situations, and the differences among them as evidence of the different problems their authors faced.

Section 58.3

58.3 Constituent Assemblies and the Problem of Popular Sovereignty

A constituent assembly, a body assembled to write a constitution, claimed to speak for a sovereign people that did not yet have an agreed definition. Who convened such a body, how its members were chosen, and who accepted its decisions determined whether the resulting text carried authority. Drafters used the vocabulary of popular sovereignty, but in practice electors were a small minority, voting was usually indirect, and wartime conditions meant that some regions were absent or under royalist control. The Congress of Tucumán in 1816, which declared the independence of the United Provinces of South America, spoke only for the provinces that sent delegates, and the Congress of Cúcuta in 1821 united Venezuela and New Granada while Quito was still contested.

Delegates were overwhelmingly lawyers, clergy, military officers, landowners, and merchants, many of them with experience in town councils or in the Cádiz elections. They debated rights, the structure of government, and the status of the Church, but they also had to decide the practical question of who qualified as a voter and who would be represented. Apportionment by population or by province favored different regions, and these decisions shaped the balance of power for the new state. Assemblies were also sites of conflict between delegates and the military leaders on whom they depended, since an assembly’s decisions could be enforced only if the army accepted them.

The authority of assemblies was often disputed by those who had created them. Mexico’s first constituent congress was dissolved by Agustín de Iturbide in October 1822, and Pedro I of Brazil closed the elected constituent assembly in November 1823 and then issued his own constitution in March 1824 (Chapter 59 and Chapter 61 treat these events). In each case the executive claimed to represent the nation more truly than its delegates, and the new constitution rested on the ruler’s initiative rather than on a representative process. These episodes show the limits of popular sovereignty as an operating principle, since the same doctrine that justified assemblies could be used to override them.

Provincial claims complicated the picture further. Where central authority had collapsed, provincial councils argued that sovereignty had returned to them, and that a national constitution required their consent. In the Río de la Plata this reasoning led to the refusal of several provinces to accept the unitary constitution drafted in 1819, and the central government dissolved in 1820. Acceptance of a constitution thus depended on whether regional elites, local militias, and town councils chose to recognize it, which is why many early constitutions were revised or discarded within a few years. The legitimacy of a constitution rested on consent that could be given or withdrawn.

Historical Vocabulary
Popular sovereignty

Popular sovereignty is the principle that political authority derives from the people or the nation and not from a monarch or God. In the Hispanic world it had roots in neo-scholastic theory, which held that authority passed from God to the community, and in Enlightenment ideas. The Cádiz Constitution of 1812 declared that sovereignty resides in the nation, and independence leaders adopted similar language to justify new governments.

In practice, the concept left open who “the people” were. Spanish uses distinguished los pueblos, the towns and provinces, from el pueblo or the nation, which allowed provinces to claim sovereignty for themselves and to resist national assemblies. Constituent assemblies spoke for the people while representing a narrow group chosen through limited, indirect elections. The doctrine also justified overthrows: rebels could claim that a government had lost the people’s consent.

It should not be equated with modern democracy or interpreted as a universal rejection of religious sources of political authority. In this period popular sovereignty coexisted with property qualifications, the exclusion of women, slavery, and limited participation, and its main historical function was to provide a source of legitimacy once the monarchy had disappeared.

Section 58.4

58.4 Republic, Monarchy, Federation, or Centralized State?

Independence did not determine what kind of state would replace Spanish or Portuguese rule, and the choice among monarchy, republic, federation, and centralized state was open. Several leaders considered monarchy seriously. In 1816 Manuel Belgrano proposed to the Congress of Tucumán a constitutional monarchy under a descendant of the Incas, an idea that appealed to some delegates as a way to win Indigenous support and European recognition; San Martín explored a constitutional monarchy for Peru in 1820–1821; and the Plan of Iguala of 1821 in Mexico offered the throne to Ferdinand VII or another European prince. These proposals reflected a belief that kings gave stability and standing in a world of monarchies, not nostalgia for Spain.

Brazil took the monarchical path, and dynastic continuity helped its leaders preserve territorial unity amid substantial provincial resistance. The royal court had moved to Rio de Janeiro in 1808, Prince Pedro declared independence in 1822, and he became emperor of a state that kept the Braganza dynasty, the institution of slavery, and the continental extent of Portuguese America. Slaveholding elites, who had watched the Haitian Revolution destroy the planter class of Saint-Domingue, valued a crown that could guarantee order, and the monarchy’s presence in the country helped contain some provincial separatist pressures, though significant revolts challenged imperial unity. Chapter 61 follows the Brazilian Empire in detail.

Haiti followed a distinct sequence. Jean-Jacques Dessalines declared himself emperor in 1804, was killed in 1806, and the country divided into a northern state that became a kingdom under Henry Christophe in 1811 and a southern republic under Alexandre Pétion. Cuba and Puerto Rico remained under Spain, with their planters unwilling to risk the upheaval of a war of independence. Within the republics themselves, the major constitutional question was whether authority should be concentrated in a national government or divided with provinces. Federalism drew on the autonomy that provinces and towns had exercised during the war and on colonial traditions of local self-government, while centralists argued that a divided state could not defend its territory, repay its debts, or restrain regional rivalries.

The options were bound up with social fears as well as political theory. Elites who favored strong executives or monarchy often did so to prevent revolt by enslaved people, Indigenous communities, or armed commoners, while liberal federalists feared that a powerful ruler would revive royal absolutism. These arguments shaped the constitutions examined in Division II. The outcome of 1810–1850 was not a single republican model but several, and the persistence of monarchy in Brazil and colonial rule in the Spanish Caribbean shows how diverse the region’s political settlements were.

Section 58.5

58.5 Constitutions as Frameworks for Political Order

A constitution in this period served three purposes at once: it set out the structure of government, it declared the rights of citizens, and it announced to the population and to foreign governments that a new political community existed. Most Spanish American charters began with the nation or state, defined its territory by reference to colonial jurisdictions, named the religion of the state, and then divided power among executive, legislative, and judicial authorities. The differences among them lay in how power was distributed. Mexico’s federal constitution of 1824 gave states a formal role in national politics, Gran Colombia’s charter of 1821 vested most authority in the national government, Brazil’s of 1824 gave the emperor a separate Moderating Power, and Bolivia’s of 1826 added an electoral power as a fourth branch alongside a lifetime executive.

Declarations of rights followed the Atlantic revolutionary pattern, promising liberty, equality before the law, security, and property, and in several texts freedom of the press. They were limited in ways that reflected the society that wrote them. Catholicism was the sole or official religion in nearly every state, so religious freedom was either excluded or, in Brazil, confined to private worship by other faiths. Slavery continued to be lawful in many states, and the privileges of the clergy and the army, the fueros, remained, which meant that legal equality was a goal in the text more than a condition in the courtroom. Rights also attached to citizens, and the qualifications that defined citizenship, examined in Division III, narrowed their reach.

Amendment rules reveal what drafters most wanted to protect and what they thought could change. Mexico’s Article 171 placed the independence of the nation, its religion, its form of government, the liberty of the press, and the division of powers beyond reform. Gran Colombia’s constitution of 1821 provided for a general review after ten years, a provision that Bolívar’s opponents and supporters later used to argue about when and by whom the text could be revised. Brazil’s Article 178 distinguished provisions that concerned the limits and powers of the constitutional authorities and political rights, which required a special procedure to change, from the rest of the charter, which ordinary legislation could alter, and the Additional Act of 1834 used this flexibility to reform the system without a new constitution.

The distance between text and practice is the central interpretive problem. Most states lacked the courts, police, and tax collectors needed to enforce constitutional rights in rural areas, and constitutions were often replaced or suspended within a decade. Yet historians no longer treat these documents as empty “paper constitutions.” Litigants, journalists, assemblies, and rebels all invoked them to justify actions, and a pronunciamiento usually claimed to defend the constitution. The texts supplied a shared vocabulary of legitimacy, and the arguments over their interpretation drove the political conflicts examined in later sections.

Section 58.6

58.6 The Executive: Presidents, Authority, and Emergency Powers

The executive was the most contested institution because the wars had produced both a fear of kings and a dependence on commanders. Republican theory held that power should be limited and divided, but the first decade of independence taught many leaders that armies, provinces, and creditors required a government able to act quickly. Drafters therefore faced a practical problem: how to create an executive strong enough to keep order and weak enough that it could not become a monarch. They answered with different formulas on the length of the term, the possibility of re-election, the choice between a single president and a collegial executive, and the existence of a vice president or council.

Constitutions gave executives powers of appointment, command of the armed forces, the right to propose or veto legislation, and the direction of ministers, and most also provided for extraordinary powers in emergencies. Gran Colombia’s Article 128 allowed the president to assume extraordinary faculties in areas threatened by invasion or rebellion, and the Chilean constitution of 1833 let the executive declare a state of siege in threatened provinces with the approval of the Council of State and allowed Congress to grant further powers by law. Mexico’s 1824 text had states elect the president and vice president, with the runner-up becoming vice president, a rule that built rivalry into the executive itself. Emergency provisions were justified as protection for the republic, yet they gave presidents a legal path to suspend rights for the duration of a crisis.

The most debated model was Bolívar’s constitution for Bolivia in 1826. It created a president for life, who was not accountable under ordinary procedures and who nominated a vice president, subject to legislative approval, to succeed him. Bolívar defended the design in an address to the Bolivian congress as a means of combining the stability of a hereditary monarchy with the elective principle, and he proposed it as a model for other states. Peru adopted it through its electoral colleges in December 1826, but the Bolivarian regime that sponsored it collapsed amid opposition to Bolívar’s influence and to the Colombian troops stationed in Lima, and the charter was repudiated on 27 January 1827 and formally annulled in June. Critics, including supporters of Santander in Gran Colombia, saw in it a monarchy by another name.

Chile’s solution in the constitution of 1833 was less dramatic and more durable. It gave the president a five-year term with the possibility of one immediate re-election, the power to appoint ministers, provincial intendants, and governors, and a leading role in selecting judges from lists prepared by the Council of State. Conservatives around Diego Portales, who held office as a minister rather than as president, argued that such concentrated authority was needed to end the conflicts of the 1820s. The system held for decades, and the contrast with Bolivia shows that executive strength depended on the support of landowners, the army, and the Church, and not simply on constitutional text. Executives became the main point of stability in the republics and also the main source of abuse, and the argument over how to constrain them runs through the rest of Part XIII.

Section 58.7

58.7 Legislatures, Representation, and Lawmaking

Legislatures were the institutions through which new states claimed to represent the people, and they carried more political weight than their small electorates suggest. Constitutions gave them the authority to write laws, approve taxes and budgets, confirm appointments, declare war, and in some systems elect or remove executives. Most adopted two chambers, partly on the Anglo-American model and partly because drafters wanted a senate of men of property to temper an elected lower house. Representation was normally based on population or on provinces, and elections were indirect, so deputies often reflected networks of local notables rather than organized parties.

The designs differed in ways that mattered for politics. Mexico’s Congress of 1824 combined a Chamber of Deputies apportioned by population with a Senate of two members per state chosen by state legislatures, which gave the states a direct role in national lawmaking. Brazil’s General Assembly of 1824 consisted of an elected Chamber of Deputies with fixed terms and a Senate whose members served for life and were appointed by the emperor from lists of candidates produced in provincial elections, an arrangement intended to give the monarchy a continuing influence in the legislature. Gran Colombia’s bicameral congress, elected through indirect voting, gave senators longer terms than deputies. These variations shaped how far legislatures could resist the executive and what kind of constituencies they represented.

Legislative power operated chiefly through control of money and appointments. Because governments depended on customs revenue and loans, congresses could delay budgets, refuse taxes, or investigate ministers, and presidents could respond by governing through decree, proroguing sessions, or appealing to provinces and officers. Disputes over the size of the army, the sale of public lands, the terms of foreign loans, and the apportionment of seats appeared in every republic, and they tested whether constitutional rules could contain political conflict.

The most important documented confrontation was the Convention of Ocaña in 1828. Although the Cúcuta constitution envisaged a general review after ten years, political conflict brought an attempted revision forward: a convention convened at Ocaña in April 1828. Its delegates divided between supporters of Bolívar, who wanted a stronger executive and a more centralized state, and followers of Francisco de Paula Santander, who defended limits on executive power and greater provincial autonomy. In June the Bolivarian delegates withdrew, leaving the assembly without a quorum, and it dissolved without a new text. In August Bolívar assumed dictatorial powers, a step that deepened the regional opposition that contributed to the breakup of the union in 1830 (Chapter 60).

Ocaña illustrates why legislatures mattered as institutions of bargaining and legitimacy. They offered a forum where factions and regions could negotiate, and their failure left force as the main arbiter. Where legislatures survived, as in Chile and Brazil, they gradually acquired influence over budgets and ministries; where they were shut or ignored, politics tended to shift to barracks and provinces. Their significance lies less in the laws they passed than in the legitimacy they conferred or withheld.

Section 58.8

58.8 Federalism, Centralism, and Provincial Autonomy

Federalism entered Latin American politics from several directions. Colonial administration had left a tradition of local self-government in town councils and provincial audiencias, and the crisis of 1808 had given provincial juntas real power. The U.S. example offered a model of a union of states, but Latin American federalists rarely copied it directly. Most defended federation as a means of protecting provincial control over revenue, militias, and local appointments, and many saw it as a guarantee against a revival of centralized, monarchical rule. Centralists answered that a country exhausted by war could not afford divided authority, a duplicated administration, and rival tax systems, and that federalism would invite foreign powers to exploit the weakness of the states.

The Mexican federation of 1824 shows how the system worked in practice. The constitution set out the powers of the national government and left the rest to the states, which wrote their own constitutions, elected their own governors and legislatures, and maintained militias. The central government’s revenue came from customs duties and from contributions (contingentes) assigned to the states, which were frequently late or unpaid, leaving the national treasury short while state governments spent more freely. The Central American Federation, formed in 1824 from five provinces with its capital at Guatemala City, had a similar structure and similar fiscal weakness. In the Río de la Plata, the central government dissolved in 1820, and the Federal Pact of 1831 among Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, Entre Ríos, and Corrientes created a loose confederation without a national constitution.

Material interests shaped these choices. Provinces with ports and customs houses, such as Buenos Aires, wanted to keep their revenue and resisted sharing it, while interior provinces sought protection from competing imports and a share of the port’s income. Landowners and provincial elites often preferred federation because it left control of labor, land, and local office in their hands, and municipal councils defended their own rights against both provincial and national governments. Questions of sovereignty were therefore bound up with questions of money and social control.

Federalism cannot be equated with liberalism or centralism with conservatism. Liberals could be centralists who sought to override local privileges, and conservatives sometimes defended provincial autonomy against a distant capital. By the 1830s several federal systems had given way to more centralized arrangements, and the reasons, taken up in Chapters 59 and 62, reflected experiences of fiscal strain and civil war. The debate nevertheless left a lasting legacy in regional identities and in the argument over how sovereignty should be divided among a nation, its provinces, and its towns.

Historical Vocabulary
Federalism

Federalism is a system in which sovereignty is shared between a national government and provincial or state governments, each with its own powers. In Latin America it drew on colonial traditions of municipal and provincial autonomy and on the example of the United States, but it was adapted to local conditions. Mexico’s 1824 constitution and the Central American constitution of the same year are the best-known examples.

Federalists often described the union as a compact among provinces that retained their sovereignty, an interpretation that appears in the Río de la Plata’s Federal Pact of 1831. In practice, federal states depended on contributions from their members, such as the Mexican contingentes, and on provincial militias, which meant that provinces could withhold support from the center. Provincial elites often favored federalism as a way to protect their control of revenue, land, and labor.

The term should be distinguished from confederation, a looser association of independent states, and it is not synonymous with liberalism or democracy. Some federalists were conservative and some centralists were liberal, and the meaning of the word changed according to who used it and against whom.

Section 58.9

58.9 Courts, Legal Continuity, and the Administration of Justice

Colonial justice had been exercised through a layered system of audiencias, local magistrates, town councils, ecclesiastical courts, military courts, and merchant tribunals, applying a body of law that included the medieval Siete Partidas, the Recopilación de las Leyes de los Reinos de las Indias of 1680, royal decrees, and local custom. Independence changed the source of authority but not this legal inheritance. Most Spanish American states declared the old law valid until replaced, the audiencias became supreme or superior courts, and Brazil kept the Portuguese Ordenações Filipinas for civil matters, which remained in force until the Civil Code of 1916. The practical effect was that lawyers, judges, and notaries trained under the monarchy continued to administer the law of the republic.

New courts were created gradually. Mexico’s 1824 constitution established a Supreme Court of eleven ministers and an attorney general, which began work in 1825; Brazil replaced its high court with a Supreme Tribunal of Justice in 1828; and Chile’s constitution of 1833 provided for a supreme court and courts of appeal. Judges were appointed by the executive, sometimes from lists prepared by councils or legislatures, and constitutions usually guaranteed tenure during good conduct, though governments could and did remove judges in political crises. Special jurisdictions for the clergy and the military survived, and in Mexico the ecclesiastical and military fueros remained a source of conflict until the reforms of the 1850s (Chapter 63).

Codification was the main intellectual project of republican law. Reformers wanted clear national codes to replace the layers of colonial legislation and to apply uniform rules to all citizens. Oaxaca produced a civil code between 1827 and 1829, the first in Latin America; Andrés de Santa Cruz promulgated a Bolivian civil code in 1830 modeled on the French Napoleonic code; and Brazil adopted a criminal code in 1830 and a code of criminal procedure in 1832. The Brazilian procedure code expanded the powers of elected justices of the peace and introduced trial by jury, giving local communities a voice in justice while also placing it in the hands of local notables. Chile’s civil code, drafted by Andrés Bello, came later, in 1855.

Most people met the law through local officials rather than through national codes. Disputes over land, inheritance, debts, and labor went to local judges, town councils, and priests, and in many rural areas landowners and customary authorities decided matters informally. The extension of national law across territories with different languages, customs, and legal traditions was slow, and Indigenous communities continued to use colonial titles and procedures in litigation, an area examined in §58.14. Courts nonetheless became places where property was defined and where citizens, including some of limited means, tested the meaning of republican equality.

The records they produced are among the best surviving evidence of everyday social life. Notarial registers, wills, land suits, and criminal cases show how households managed property, how communities defended boundaries, and how enslaved and free people of color used the courts to claim rights. Historians read them with caution, since litigants and lawyers presented their cases strategically, but they show that the legal system, however weakly enforced, shaped how ordinary people framed their claims against each other and against the state.

Section 58.10

58.10 Elections, Municipal Government, and Political Participation

Elections in the early republics followed rules inherited from Cádiz: voters assembled in parishes or districts, chose electors, and the electors chose deputies, senators, or other officials. The stages meant that popular participation was filtered through local notables before reaching the legislature. Election boards, usually composed of local officials, determined who was eligible, supervised the voting, and counted the results, so that control of those boards gave a faction great influence over the outcome. Ballots were frequently cast in public or by voice, which left voters open to pressure from employers, landlords, priests, and military commanders. Complaints of fraud, intimidation, and armed intervention at polling places recur in the sources, and in Brazil the violence of some contests earned them the label eleições do cacete, elections by club.

Brazil’s rules show how qualifications shaped the electorate. Under the 1824 charter, parish assemblies admitted adult men with an annual income of at least one hundred thousand réis, excluding most servants, members of religious communities, and men under twenty-five, with some exceptions. Freed persons could vote in the parish assemblies but could not serve as electors, who needed twice that income. The electors then chose deputies and senatorial candidates. Because income, not literacy or property alone, defined the vote, artisans, small traders, and some free people of color took part in local elections that gave the empire a broader base of participation than a simple reading of the text suggests.

Municipal government gave elections their most immediate meaning. Town councils, the ayuntamientos and câmaras municipais, controlled local taxes, markets, public works, the militia, and the administration of local justice, and they were the institution through which most people dealt with the state. Their position varied. Brazil’s Municipal Law of 1828 subordinated the councils to provincial authorities and reduced their independence, while in Mexico and Central America the states regulated them according to their own constitutions. Indigenous communities often kept their own councils under Spanish-derived names, which allowed them to elect authorities, collect local dues, and defend communal lands.

Elections mattered even where participation was limited. They legitimized governments by giving them a claim to represent the nation, and losers who denounced the results often did so in the language of the constitution, which shows that the procedure was valued. Campaigns generated newspapers, clubs, patronage networks, and rival slates, which created political habits that survived the regimes that first used them. The effect on marginal groups was uneven: men of limited means might gain influence as clients of a candidate or through militia service, while women, most servants, and the enslaved had no formal role. How far elections reflected popular preferences remains a matter of study, but they were a principal arena in which republican authority was made and unmade.

Citizenship, Social Hierarchy, and the Limits of Equality

Division III asks who belonged to the new political communities and on what terms. Republican constitutions announced legal equality, but they also set qualifications, preserved slavery in many places, and left inherited hierarchies of race, status, and gender largely intact. Membership was negotiated through law, local practice, military service, and petition.

Section 58.11

58.11 Defining the Citizen: Rights, Duties, and Qualifications

Three concepts need to be distinguished to understand republican membership. Nationality referred to belonging to the state; citizenship conferred civil and political standing; and suffrage, the right to vote, was a further privilege attached to some citizens. The Cádiz Constitution treated all free people born in Spanish dominions as Spanish nationals, reserved citizenship for those with ancestry from both hemispheres, which excluded people of African descent, and then restricted voting through further conditions. The republics that followed used similar layers, so that a person could be a national without being a citizen and a citizen without being a voter.

The qualifications for voting turned on age, marital status, property, income, occupation, and, in some constitutions, literacy. Gran Colombia’s 1821 constitution allowed adult men to vote in parish assemblies if they owned real property of a specified value or practiced an independent trade or profession, and it provided that voters must be able to read and write after 1840. Chile’s 1833 constitution set age and property or capital requirements and likewise deferred its literacy test until after 1840. Mexico’s 1824 constitution left the matter to the states, which produced varied rules, many of which required residence and an honest means of living and excluded domestic servants. Because the qualifications were set in money or in social standing, local officials had broad discretion over who appeared on the electoral rolls.

Citizenship also carried obligations. Citizens owed taxes, service in the militia or army, participation in juries and local offices, and obedience to the law. For ordinary men these duties were often more tangible than the vote. Forced recruitment, known as leva, fell most heavily on the poor, on Indigenous men, and on free men of color, and avoidance of the draft became a common form of resistance. Some constitutions rewarded military service with civic rights, which tied citizenship to the wars that had created the states and gave veterans a claim to inclusion. Others used taxation or property ownership as proof of a stake in the community, which linked political standing to economic position.

These rules produced electorates that were small but not uniform. In places with many small proprietors or with artisans who met income tests, a substantial share of adult men could vote; in plantation or Indigenous regions the share was far lower. Voter registers, militia rolls, and tax lists are the main sources for measuring this, and historians use them to show that the line between citizen and non-citizen was drawn differently from place to place. The central point is that republican citizenship was conditional and graduated, and that many people held legal standing that fell between full membership and exclusion.

Section 58.12

58.12 Race, Colonial Classification, and Republican Inequality

Colonial Spanish America had classified its population through a system of legal and social categories, and independence leaders faced the question of what to do with it. Many declared the labels abolished. The Plan of Iguala of 1821 proclaimed that all inhabitants of New Spain, whether European, African, or Indian, were citizens, and the use of caste designations in official records ended in Mexico in the following years. Other states reduced or removed such labels from legal documents or ended the separate legal personality of Indigenous people. These measures reflected the revolutionary principle of equality before the law and the practical need of the armies for the support of people of every background.

The abolition of labels did not end discrimination, because status in these societies had depended on ancestry, occupation, reputation, wealth, and honor, and those factors persisted. Elites used terms such as education, civilization, and merit to justify unequal access to office, officer commissions, schools, and property. In some places the colonial requirement of proof of purity of blood for certain offices and universities was dropped, but informal barriers remained. People of African, Indigenous, and mixed descent used the language of equality to claim places in the army, the militia, and local government, and some succeeded, though rarely in the highest offices.

The fear of racial conflict, often expressed through the memory of Haiti, shaped how elites responded. Bolívar feared what elites called pardocracia, the dominance of free people of color, and two prominent officers of mixed ancestry were executed on his authority. Manuel Piar, a general born in Curaçao, was arrested in September 1817, convicted by a court martial of treason, conspiracy, and desertion, and shot at Angostura on 16 October after Bolívar confirmed the sentence; historians debate how far fear of a race war lay behind the charges. José Prudencio Padilla, a naval commander from the Caribbean coast, often called an admiral, who had served as a senator and had a political following in Cartagena, was arrested there in April 1828 after political clashes and taken to Bogotá. He was tried for conspiracy and treason in connection with the plot of September 1828, though sources report that he refused to join those who tried to free him, and was shot on 2 October. The two cases differ in their circumstances, but both show how the prominence of men of color in the patriot cause could become politically dangerous in the eyes of elites.

Haiti followed a different route. Its constitution of 1805 under Dessalines declared all Haitians black regardless of skin color, a political category that included the Polish and German soldiers who had joined the revolution, and barred whites from owning property. The revised constitution of 1816 retained restrictions on white property ownership, with narrowly defined exceptions for white residents already recognized as Haitians; Article 44 recognized arriving Africans, Indigenous people, and their descendants as Haitian on settlement but deferred exercise of citizenship rights for one year. The provisions were responses to the threat of French reconquest as much as statements of racial theory.

Brazil’s 1824 charter, like those of Spanish America, did not write racial categories into law, yet color terms such as pardo and preto continued in everyday records, and slavery and its legacies shaped social rank. Historians interpret the period as one in which formal legal equality coexisted with a flexible but powerful hierarchy of color and status. Later claims that Latin American societies were free of racial discrimination ignore the persistence of these inequalities, which are examined in the chapters on abolition and the export economies.

Section 58.13

58.13 Slavery, Emancipation, and Uneven Republican Freedom

Slavery was woven into the economies the new states inherited: it sustained sugar and coffee plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean, cacao and sugar estates in Venezuela, mining and agriculture in the Cauca valley of New Granada, the coastal estates of Peru, and a large domestic and artisan workforce in cities. Patriot leaders had also relied on the support of enslaved men and women, and the example of Haiti, where the revolution had ended slavery in 1793–1794 and destroyed the planter class, offered both inspiration and warning. Bolívar received aid from Haiti’s President Pétion in 1816 in return for a promise to emancipate enslaved people, and he issued decrees freeing those who joined the patriot armies, but a general abolition did not follow. In the republics’ constituent debates, the question was less whether slavery should end than how, how fast, and at whose expense.

The commonest compromise was gradual emancipation through free-womb laws, which declared children born to enslaved mothers free after a date but did not free the mothers. Gran Colombia’s law, approved by the Congress of Cúcuta on 19 July 1821 and published two days later, banned the importation of enslaved people, created local manumission juntas to free a limited number of the “most honest and industrious” enslaved people from a fund drawn from inheritance taxes, and declared children of enslaved women born after the law free but obliged to serve their mothers’ owners until the age of eighteen. The juntas were chronically short of money and freed few people. Historians such as Yesenia Barragán have argued that the law, though presented as a step toward abolition, prolonged forms of bondage by keeping children in the owners’ households under conditions that were free in name only.

Other states chose faster abolition where the economic cost was lower. Chile abolished slavery in 1823 and the Central American Federation did so in 1824, both with relatively small enslaved populations. Mexico’s decree of September 1829, issued under President Vicente Guerrero, declared slavery abolished, but enforcement was contested, and Texas, where Anglo-American settlers held enslaved workers, received an exemption that December. Bolivia’s 1826 constitution, Article 11(5), formally declared existing enslaved people free but required them to remain with their former masters until a special law regulated departure, exposing the limits of constitutional emancipation. In societies where plantations or estates depended on enslaved labor, abolition came decades later: in Colombia through a law of 1851 that took effect in 1852, in Argentina through the constitution of 1853, and in Venezuela and Peru in 1854. Brazil and Cuba expanded slavery and are treated in Chapters 66 and 67.

Enslaved people shaped the outcome through their own actions. Many enlisted in patriot or royalist armies in return for promised freedom, sued for freedom or for better treatment in the courts, bought their own freedom, fled to maroon communities, or petitioned authorities, and juntas and courts recorded thousands of such cases. Freed people, known as libertos, faced legal limits on their rights in many constitutions and economic pressure to remain on estates. Slaveholders argued for compensation and delay, and governments dependent on landowners usually obliged. The resulting patchwork meant that freedom, like citizenship, was graduated and often incomplete, and that emancipation was a long process shaped by law, labor demand, and the persistent initiative of the enslaved themselves.

Section 58.14

58.14 Indigenous Communities, Land Rights, and Republican Sovereignty

Under Spanish rule, Indigenous communities held a distinct legal status as the “republic of Indians,” with their own officials, protected rights to communal land, a royal defender, and obligations to pay tribute and, in some regions, to provide labor. Republican equality ended these categories in principle. San Martín’s government in Peru abolished tribute and forced labor in 1821, and Bolívar repeated the abolition in 1824 and 1825 and decreed the distribution of communal lands among Indigenous families with the sale of any surplus. The reforms were intended to make Indigenous people citizens and property owners on liberal terms, and to end exactions that were widely seen as colonial.

Fiscal needs soon undermined the reforms. Tribute had been a dependable source of revenue, unlike mining or trade taxes, which fluctuated, and the new states had few alternatives. Sucre’s government in Bolivia and Peru’s Bolivarian regime restored the tax under new names, the contribución indigenal in Bolivia and a contribution of Indigenous people in Peru in 1826, and Santa Cruz formalized the Bolivian system with a census regulation in 1829. One study of Bolivian finances found that the tax amounted to about 30 percent of national revenue in 1830, and other estimates for the nineteenth century run higher. Peru’s tax remained a major source of income until it was abolished in 1854.

Land was the central issue. Liberal reformers saw communal ownership as an obstacle to progress and wanted individual title, while communities relied on collective control of fields, pastures, and water for subsistence and for the payment of tax. Many communities defended their holdings by presenting colonial titles in court, using town councils to administer land, and continuing customary systems of authority. The anthropologist Tristan Platt interpreted the Bolivian arrangement as a tributary pact in which payment of tribute secured recognition of communal land rights, a reading that explains why some communities defended the tax even as they resented it. Where individualization of title did proceed, it often favored neighboring landowners, who could purchase land from impoverished families.

Indigenous people were not passive in this process. They voted and held office in local councils, served in militias and armies, sued in courts, petitioned governments, and sometimes rebelled or allied with factions that promised to protect them. In many regions, such as the southern Chilean frontier, Amazonia, the pampas and Patagonia, and the northern Mexican borderlands, Indigenous societies stayed outside effective state control, so that constitutional claims to sovereignty coexisted with real autonomy. The difference between legal and effective authority, taken up in §58.19 and in Chapter 62, is essential for understanding the Indigenous experience of the early republics.

Section 58.15

58.15 Women, Family Law, and Political Participation Beyond Voting

Women’s legal position was determined by the Iberian law that survived independence. Under Castilian and Portuguese law, daughters inherited alongside sons, women kept their dowries as their own property, and marriage created a community of acquired goods administered by the husband. Married women were nevertheless under their husbands’ authority and generally needed his permission to make contracts or go to court, while widows and unmarried adult women had greater legal capacity. The Church had jurisdiction over marriage, and separation or annulment was possible only through ecclesiastical courts. None of this changed with the new constitutions, which said little about women and left family matters to inherited law until later civil codes began to address them.

Women’s work was central to household and commercial economies. They sold goods in markets, ran small shops and workshops, managed estates as widows, worked as seamstresses, laundresses, and cooks, and in rural areas shared agricultural labor. Enslaved and domestic women formed a large part of urban workforces, and free-womb laws placed their reproduction at the center of emancipation policy, since their children’s legal status was the main object of the laws. In cities a significant proportion of households were headed by women, whose economic activity sustained families during years of war and fiscal crisis.

The wars gave some women public roles. Policarpa Salavarrieta, who carried information for patriots in Bogotá, was executed in November 1817. Leona Vicario of Mexico City supported the insurgents financially and was imprisoned before joining them, and later wrote for the press. Juana Azurduy, with her husband Manuel Ascencio Padilla, led guerrilla forces in Upper Peru and received an honorary military rank, but spent her last years in poverty. Manuela Sáenz of Quito supported the patriot cause in Lima, was honored by San Martín’s government, became Bolívar’s companion, and helped him escape an assassination attempt in Bogotá in September 1828; she later died in exile in Paita. Such women were celebrated in later national memory, though they received little reward in their own lifetimes.

After independence women remained excluded from voting and office, and political life was organized around male citizens and soldiers. They nevertheless took part in public life through petitions for pensions and compensation, participation in civic festivals and processions, hosting political gatherings, and writing for or reading newspapers. Governments and educators began to expand schooling for girls in the 1820s and 1830s, often justified by the idea that educated mothers would raise republican citizens, a conception historians have described as republican motherhood. These roles gave women influence without formal rights and set the terms for the later struggles over suffrage and civil equality.

Division IV — §§58.16–58.20

Building the State: Finance, Economy, and Territory

Division IV turns from constitutions to the material problems that decided whether a state could function. Governments needed revenue, credit, secure borders, and officials, and they had to obtain them after years of war, while competing with provinces, merchants, and foreign powers.

Section 58.16

58.16 The Fiscal Crisis of Independence

The colonial fiscal system had combined many sources of revenue: tribute from Indigenous communities, sales taxes (alcabalas), royal monopolies on tobacco, gunpowder, and spirits, tithes, taxes on mining and silver, customs duties, and transfers (situados) from richer treasuries to poorer or militarized ones. The Bourbon reforms of the eighteenth century had increased the efficiency of collection and the burden on colonial taxpayers, and large amounts of silver were remitted to Spain. Independence ended those remittances, which relieved the colonies of one demand, but the wars that achieved it destroyed much of the productive base that supported the treasuries.

Mines had been flooded or abandoned, mercury supplies from Spain were interrupted, estates were raided or neglected, livestock was requisitioned, and trade was disrupted. Silver production fell sharply in Mexico and Upper Peru during the wars and recovered only partially in the following decades. The new governments abolished some unpopular levies, including the mita, tribute in many places, and certain monopolies, but they could not function without revenue and soon restored taxes in modified form. Customs duties on imports and exports became the largest single source of income in most states, which tied public finances to foreign trade and to the ports that collected the duties.

Expenditure grew faster than revenue. Armies and navies inherited from the wars had to be paid, officials and pensioners expected salaries, and the governments needed to build administrations that the colonial state had provided through the crown. In Mexico the army was the largest single item of spending, and states often failed to pay the contributions assigned to them under the federal system. In Peru, restored tribute was one of the main sources of revenue until the guano trade began to supply income in the 1840s. Governments met shortfalls by forced loans on merchants, requisitions, delayed salaries, paper issues, and borrowing, the last of which is examined in the next section.

The consequences were political as much as financial. Soldiers and officials who went unpaid were open to appeals from rival leaders, which made pronunciamientos more likely. Private lenders, such as the agiotistas of Mexico, gained influence by advancing money at high interest in exchange for future revenue, and merchants who paid forced loans pressed for protection of their interests. Provinces that controlled ports or mines resisted sending revenue to the capital. Contraband flourished where duties were high or enforcement weak. Fiscal weakness thus limited what governments could do and weakened their claim to authority, linking money to legitimacy in a way that the constitutions themselves did not capture.

Section 58.17

58.17 Public Debt, Foreign Loans, and International Recognition

Between 1822 and 1825 the new governments, seeking funds for armies, ships, and administration that customs duties could not supply, borrowed on the London bond market. According to Carlos Marichal’s compilation, the loans issued in these years included £1 million for Chile in 1822, £2 million for Colombia in 1822 and £4.75 million in 1824, £450,000 and £616,000 for Peru, £1 million for Buenos Aires through the house of Baring, £1.2 million for Brazil, £163,000 for Central America, and two loans totaling £6.4 million for Mexico, for a total of about £20 million. Bonds were sold at discounts, so the governments received less than the nominal sum, and commissions and the repayment of earlier advances reduced the proceeds further. Interest was set at 5 or 6 percent, with the customs revenue commonly pledged as security.

The market collapsed in the London financial crisis that began in December 1825. Peru and Gran Colombia suspended payments in 1826, Mexico in 1827, and most other borrowers followed, so that the loans of the early 1820s became the first Latin American debt crisis. Brazil was an exception because its borrowing had a specific purpose and it continued payments. The defaults left creditors, bondholders, and merchant bankers with claims that lasted for decades. When Gran Colombia dissolved, a convention signed in December 1834 divided its debt among its successors, with New Granada assuming half, Venezuela 28.5 percent, and Ecuador 21.5 percent; ratification and implementation by the three states followed in the years afterward. Argentine borrowing was not settled until the 1850s, and Mexico’s debt was repeatedly renegotiated.

Recognition was linked to the same financial and commercial interests. The United States recognized Gran Colombia and Mexico in 1822 and Brazil in 1824, partly out of commercial interest and partly to assert its principle against European intervention. Britain announced its recognition of Buenos Aires, Colombia, and Mexico in December 1824, and negotiated commercial treaties in the following years, motivated by trade and by its wish to keep other powers from regaining influence. Brazil’s independence was recognized by Portugal in a treaty signed on 29 August 1825 and ratified in November, in which Brazil agreed to pay two million pounds; under secret codicils, £1.4 million of that sum covered Portuguese debts to Britain, and the payment was financed in part by borrowing in London. Spain refused recognition for years, and its claims remained a diplomatic issue for decades.

Haiti’s experience shows how recognition could be turned into a financial burden. In April 1825 Charles X of France issued an ordinance granting recognition of Haitian independence in return for an indemnity of 150 million francs to compensate former colonists, payable over five years and accompanied by reduced duties for French ships. President Boyer accepted in July 1825 under the threat of a French naval squadron, and Haiti borrowed from French banks to make the first payment. A treaty in 1838 reduced the indemnity to 90 million francs, but the payments and the debts used to meet them absorbed a large share of the state’s revenue for generations. In each case, the combination of debt and recognition gave foreign creditors, merchants, and governments leverage over tariffs, trade treaties, and political decisions, and limited the independence that the new states had fought to win.

Section 58.18

58.18 Property, Commerce, and the Economic Interests of the New States

The economies of the new states were shaped by the opening to international trade and by the survival of colonial structures of property. Spain’s commercial monopoly had ended, ports were open to foreign ships, and British merchants and manufacturers sold cotton goods, hardware, and other products at prices that local producers could not match. Port cities such as Buenos Aires, Valparaíso, Lima-Callao, and Rio de Janeiro grew, and consumers benefited from cheaper imports. Brazil’s trade treaty with Britain of 1810, which limited duties on British goods to 15 percent and was renewed in 1827, constrained the empire’s tariff policy until it expired in 1844.

The effects of free trade were uneven and politically contested. Artisans and textile producers in places such as Puebla and highland Ecuador faced competition from imports and pressed for protective duties. Mexico’s tariffs of the late 1820s included prohibitions on certain goods, and in 1830 Lucas Alamán established the Banco de Avío to provide public credit for industrial development, particularly textile mills. Other governments leaned toward open trade, arguing that customs revenue and cheap goods served the public. The disputes brought together merchants, landowners, artisans, and officials in shifting alliances that cut across the divide between liberals and conservatives.

Regional economies recovered in different ways. Mexican silver mining attracted British capital in the 1820s, with Cornish miners and steam engines introduced at Real del Monte, though many ventures failed after the crash of 1825. Chilean exports of copper, silver, and wheat grew after the discovery of rich silver deposits at Chañarcillo in 1832. In the Río de la Plata, estancias expanded across the pampas to produce hides, tallow, and salted meat, and Rivadavia’s emphyteusis law of 1826 leased public land on long terms, a measure that in practice concentrated holdings in a few hands. In Brazil, sugar remained important in the northeast while coffee production, based on enslaved labor, expanded in the Paraíba valley in the 1830s.

Property changed hands but its structure was largely preserved. Confiscated royalist estates and grants and vouchers to patriot officers redistributed some land, though many officers sold their claims at a discount to speculators and merchants. Haciendas, estancias, and plantations continued to dominate the countryside, communal Indigenous lands survived but under pressure, and Church property remained substantial until later reforms. Merchants, officials, and landowners with access to credit and to customs revenue gained influence, and foreign merchant houses mediated trade and finance. The new states thus presided over economies in which opportunity was unevenly distributed and in which the inherited agrarian order remained intact.

Section 58.19

58.19 Territory, Borders, Provincial Centers, and Frontier Authority

Independence required new states to define the territory they governed, and the starting point was the principle that borders followed the colonial jurisdictions of 1810 or the date of independence, later called uti possidetis. It provided a common reference, yet colonial boundaries had often been vague, overlapping, or unmapped, and populations on the margins had loyalties to local leaders and to ecclesiastical, military, and commercial networks that did not match them. States therefore faced disputes with neighbors and challenges within their claimed territory.

Several disputes were settled by force or by local decision. The port of Guayaquil, claimed by Peru and favored for union by some of its elite, was incorporated into Gran Colombia in July 1822 after Bolívar’s pressure, the same month of his meeting there with San Martín. Disputes over Jaén and Maynas contributed to a war between Peru and Gran Colombia in 1828–1829. In Central America, Chiapas declared its adherence to Mexico in 1821 and confirmed it in 1824, while the neighboring district of Soconusco was disputed with Guatemala for decades. In the Río de la Plata, Brazil annexed the Banda Oriental as the Cisplatine province in 1821, a rebellion followed in 1825, and the war between Brazil and the United Provinces ended in 1828 with the creation, under British mediation, of the independent state of Uruguay.

Claimed territory and governed territory were different things. Large areas lay beyond effective state authority: Amazonia and the Orinoco plains, the Mapuche lands south of the Biobío river where Chile negotiated agreements with Indigenous leaders at assemblies such as Tapihue in 1825, the pampas and Patagonia, the Chaco, and the northern Mexican borderlands where Comanche and Apache groups raided settlements and where governments struggled to protect them. Britain maintained settlements and claims on the Caribbean coast of Central America, including Belize and the Mosquito shore. In these zones, sovereignty depended on diplomacy, trade, and negotiation with Indigenous societies and with foreign powers as much as on military force.

Within states, regional capitals, ports, and mining districts dominated the interior, and local strongmen often controlled rural areas. National governments recognized this by negotiating with provincial elites, granting them offices and tolerating their autonomy in exchange for loyalty. The gap between legal claim and effective control explains why borders were redrawn, why provinces seceded or threatened to, and why frontier conflicts were such persistent features of the period. Territorial integration, completed in many places only in the later nineteenth century, was therefore one of the longest tasks of state-building.

Section 58.20

58.20 Reconstructing Administration and Public Institutions

Governing a country required people, records, roads, and money, and all were scarce after the wars. The colonial administrative system had relied on viceroys, intendants, and local magistrates trained in Spain or in the colonies, and most republics replaced them with presidents, ministers, and prefects, governors, or jefes políticos who supervised provinces and districts. Brazil used provincial presidents appointed by the emperor, with provincial councils from 1824 that were replaced by elected provincial assemblies in the Additional Act of 1834. Officials were usually paid irregularly, were drawn from local elites, and depended on those elites to enforce orders, which gave governors little independence from their regions.

The information needed to govern was thin. Census-taking was incomplete: Chile held its first national census in 1835, and Brazil did not conduct one until 1872. Land registries and tax rolls were inaccurate, and in many regions parish priests kept the only continuous records of births, marriages, and deaths. Roads were poor and travel slow, so that orders and money could take weeks to reach distant provinces, and mail services were limited. Ports and customs houses were the best-organized parts of the state because they produced revenue, which meant that administration was strongest where trade was heaviest.

Education was a priority of republican leaders because they believed that citizens had to be educated to sustain self-government. Governments encouraged the Lancasterian system, in which older pupils taught younger ones, to educate large numbers cheaply. Mexico’s Lancasterian Company was founded in 1822, and Joseph Lancaster himself worked in Caracas from the middle of the decade. Santander’s plan of 1826 organized universities at Bogotá, Caracas, and Quito, the University of Buenos Aires was founded in 1821, and Brazil established law schools at São Paulo and Olinda in 1827. These institutions trained the lawyers and officials of the new states, though they served a small elite.

Few of these efforts reached the rural poor. Schools were concentrated in cities, literacy remained low among rural people, Indigenous communities, and women, and teachers were scarce and poorly paid. The institutions that could reach the population, the parish, the town council, and the Church, remained the principal channels of local administration. The pace at which governments extended their capacity to tax, record, educate, and enforce determined how far their authority reached, and it was usually slower than the pace of political change. The deficiencies were not unique to Latin America, but they were intensified by war, distance, and fiscal weakness.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Ordinary People Met the Republic

For most people the republic appeared at the parish or town. An election might mean attending a parish assembly, perhaps pressed by a landlord, priest, or militia officer, and voting aloud or in public before electors chose deputies. The town council set market rules, repaired roads and bridges, and organized the militia, and it was the body to which villagers and artisans addressed petitions. Indigenous communities often kept their own council and officers under colonial-era titles. Surviving election records, council minutes, and petitions allow these procedures to be reconstructed.

Taxes were the most tangible contact with the state. Customs duties raised prices of imported cloth and iron, sales taxes were collected at markets and city gates, and in several states Indigenous adults paid a head tax collected through community officers and local officials, whose collection could determine whether a family kept access to land. Fiscal records show the amounts and administrative procedures, but the burden on households has to be inferred from prices and wages.

Military recruitment affected poor men most directly. Forced levies (levas), militia duty, and the requisitioning of animals and food took labor and goods from villages, and avoiding recruitment by hiding, flight, or the intervention of a patron was common. Courts handled disputes over land, inheritance, debt, and labor contracts, and notarial and judicial records show artisans, widows, free people of color, and enslaved people using them. Religion framed daily life through baptisms, marriages, confraternities, and festivals, and tithe changes affected parishes. Schools reached few children, mostly in towns, and girls’ schools were fewer.

Evidence and inference: the records mostly come from officials, lawyers, and priests, so ordinary people’s own views are known mainly through petitions, testimony, and legal cases that were shaped by those who wrote them down. Statements about daily burdens, such as the weight of conscription or taxes, are inferences from official records and later accounts, and they should be read as reasoned reconstruction instead of direct testimony.

Division V — §§58.21–58.24

Liberalism, Conservatism, Religion, and Public Culture

Division V examines the ideas and institutions through which people argued about what the new states should be. Political language, religious authority, and a growing print culture shaped how those arguments were made and who could join them. The labels “liberal” and “conservative” are useful but must be handled with care, since parties in the modern sense developed mostly after the period covered here.

Section 58.21

58.21 Liberalism and the Promise of Republican Reform

Latin American liberalism drew on several sources: the Spanish liberalism of Cádiz, which had given Americans experience of representation and a free press; French constitutional thought, especially Benjamin Constant, whose idea of a neutral power above the branches of government lay behind the Moderating Power of Brazil’s 1824 charter; British utilitarianism, including Jeremy Bentham, whose writings were read in Mexico, Colombia, and Buenos Aires; and political economy that favored commercial freedom and individual property. Liberals defined their program against the colonial inheritance of absolute monarchy, corporate privilege, and trade restriction, and they argued that constitutional government, legal equality, and education would make the new states prosperous and free.

In practice liberals sought to limit executive power through written constitutions and legislatures, to protect freedom of the press and of commerce, to replace corporate privileges, the fueros of the clergy and army and the separate status of Indigenous communities, with a single law for all citizens, and to promote public education. Moderates accepted property qualifications for voting, a strong role for the state, and cautious change, and in Colombia Santander’s government promoted the study of Bentham in the universities. Radicals, often federalists, pressed for broader suffrage, local autonomy, and sharper limits on the Church. The categories overlapped, and individuals moved between them as circumstances changed.

Buenos Aires under Governor Martín Rodríguez and his minister Bernardino Rivadavia between 1821 and 1824 offers the clearest example of a liberal program. The government suppressed the colonial cabildos in 1821, founded the University of Buenos Aires the same year, extended the vote to a wide group of free adult men through an electoral law, reorganized public education on Lancasterian lines, created a charitable society run by women in 1823, and enacted the ecclesiastical reform of 1822 examined in §58.23. Rivadavia’s later presidency of the United Provinces in 1826–1827 ended in failure when the provinces rejected the centralizing constitution, a sign of the limits of reform imposed from a single city.

Liberal measures had unintended and unequal effects. Removing corporate privilege could also strip Indigenous communities and the Church’s charities of protection, and commercial freedom helped importers more than artisans. Liberals disagreed about slavery, since many were slaveholders or depended on slaveholders’ support, and about how far equality should extend to Indigenous people, women, and the poor. Critics, including conservatives, clergy, and artisans, accused them of imposing foreign ideas without regard to local conditions. The liberal inheritance was nonetheless lasting, as later generations of reformers returned to its themes in the middle of the century.

Section 58.22

58.22 Conservatism, Social Order, and Institutional Continuity

Conservatism in these decades defended continuity: the Catholic Church, property, social hierarchy, and a strong executive. The movement included former royalists who accepted the new order and patriots who feared that rapid change would bring disorder. Its leaders were not opponents of independence or of constitutions. Lucas Alamán, a Mexican minister and historian born in Guanajuato, supported independence and served in republican governments while arguing for centralism, the protection of religion and property, and public credit for industry. Mariano Egaña and Andrés Bello in Chile combined respect for legal tradition and order with commitment to education and constitutional rule.

Their arguments rested on the experience of war and fiscal collapse. They held that weak states needed hierarchy and authority, that religion supplied the moral cement of a society without monarchy, that voting should be limited to men with a stake in the community, and that federal systems invited fragmentation and civil war. Many also feared social upheaval from below, whether by enslaved people, Indigenous communities, or the urban poor, and they pointed to Haiti and to the popular insurgencies of the independence wars. Such concerns made them receptive to presidents with strong powers and to alliances with the clergy and the army.

Chile’s experience under Diego Portales is the best-known example. After the conservative victory at Lircay in April 1830 ended a civil war, Portales, a merchant who served as minister of the interior and of war rather than as president, worked to build an order based on a strong executive, a disciplined army and a civic militia, and an alliance with the Church and landowners. He was killed in 1837 by mutinous soldiers. The constitution of 1833 embodied many of these principles, and the state it created enjoyed unusual stability. The case shows that conservatives could be state-builders as well as defenders of tradition.

The labels remain slippery. Conservatives often adopted liberal institutions such as congresses, written constitutions, and commercial treaties, and liberals sometimes adopted conservative methods, including strong executives. Alignments in the 1820s and 1830s turned frequently on regional interest, personal loyalty, and specific measures, and organized parties in the modern sense emerged later, in Chile in the 1840s and 1850s, in Colombia in 1848–1849, and in Mexico in the 1840s and 1850s. Historians therefore treat conservatism as a set of positions and alliances that took shape in conflict, not as a fixed ideology that existed in advance.

Section 58.23

58.23 The Catholic Church, Patronage, and the Secular State

The Catholic Church was the central institution of colonial society. It owned urban and rural property, made loans secured on mortgages, ran schools, hospitals, and charities, and kept the parish registers on which civil identity depended. Its clergy were subject to their own courts, and the Spanish Crown held the royal patronage (patronato real), which gave it the right to propose bishops and oversee the collection of tithes. Independence raised the question of who inherited these rights, since the republics claimed them as attributes of sovereignty while the papacy, unwilling to offend Spain, withheld recognition for years.

The result was a prolonged conflict. Pius VII’s brief Etsi longissimo terrarum of 30 January 1816 had urged loyalty to Ferdinand VII, and many Spanish-born bishops left or were expelled, leaving dioceses vacant. In the late 1820s the papacy began to appoint bishops to American sees, and in the 1830s it formally recognized several republics. In Brazil the equivalent right, the padroado, passed to the emperor, who accepted Catholicism as the state religion while allowing other faiths private worship. Throughout the region, governments sought to control clerical appointments and finances, and bishops resisted, appealing to the papacy.

Reforms in Buenos Aires in 1822 show how far a government might go. The Law of General Reform of December 1822 abolished the clergy’s personal immunity from civil courts, ended the collection of tithes from 1 January 1823 and replaced it with a state budget for worship, converted the diocesan seminary into a state college, and suppressed Bethlemite religious houses and smaller houses of other orders, while imposing limits on the size and governance of surviving communities. Gran Colombia in 1821 closed small convents to finance colleges. Such measures expressed the regalism of the period, the belief that the state should supervise the Church, and they drew on both Enlightenment criticism of monastic wealth and the practical need for resources.

Responses varied. Some priests supported reform and sat in congresses as liberals, while others defended Church privileges and used pulpits to rally opposition, so the clergy were divided and the conflict cut across social groups. Many people who disliked reforms of tithes or convents remained devout, since religious practice was bound up with local life. Most republics kept Catholicism as the official or only public religion, so the dispute concerned jurisdiction, finance, and education, and not whether the Church should exist. These conflicts would escalate in later decades, particularly in Mexico, Colombia, and Chile, when liberal governments moved against Church property and privileges (Chapter 63).

Section 58.24

58.24 Newspapers, Schools, Associations, and the Formation of Public Opinion

Public opinion became a political force during the independence era because printing expanded rapidly. The Cádiz Cortes decreed freedom of the press in 1810, and although Ferdinand VII later suppressed it, wartime gazettes and pamphlets in Caracas, Buenos Aires, Mexico City, and Lima had already shown how print could mobilize support. In Brazil, press censorship ended in 1821, and many newspapers appeared in Rio de Janeiro and the provinces in the following years. Governments both used and feared the press: they funded official newspapers, such as Chile’s El Araucano, founded in 1830, and prosecuted editors who attacked them.

Newspapers and pamphlets were usually political instruments. In Mexico in the 1820s, rival papers were tied to opposing political groups and Masonic lodges, which functioned as organizations for recruiting and coordinating supporters, a development noted here and taken up in Chapter 59. Editors argued over constitutions, federalism, the Church, and trade, and they publicized scandals, elections, and rebellions. Their circulation was small, but their content reached more readers through reading aloud, lending, and discussion in shops, cafes, taverns, and plazas, which broadened the audience beyond the literate elite.

Associations gave opinion a more durable form. Patriotic societies such as those in Caracas and Buenos Aires in 1810–1811 debated independence, economic societies promoted agriculture and education, literary and debating clubs met in cities, and lodges and religious confraternities provided networks of sociability and support. Petitions from town councils, guilds, Indigenous communities, and groups of citizens were a long-established way of addressing authorities and increased in volume as governments depended on consent. Civic festivals on independence anniversaries, oaths to the constitution, flags, anthems, and portraits of leaders cultivated loyalty among people who could not read.

The public sphere was limited and divided. Women, most Indigenous communities, and the enslaved had little access to the printed press, and rural areas were reached sporadically, so urban lawyers, officials, merchants, and clergy shaped much of the debate. Even so, the arguments circulated widely enough to establish a shared vocabulary of citizens, nation, constitution, and liberty, and they offered competing visions of national identity: a Hispanic and Catholic nation, a civic republic of equal citizens, or a union of regions and peoples. Which of these would prevail was not settled in this period, and the contest continues in the national histories that follow.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: What Independence Transformed and What Survived

What Changed

  • Sovereignty and representation: authority moved from a monarch to constitutions, assemblies, and elections, though in Brazil a crown survived.
  • Legal status: official caste labels and the separate Indigenous republics were abolished in many states, and tribute was cut or renamed.
  • Trade and finance: the commercial monopoly ended, ports opened, and states borrowed abroad.
  • Slavery: gradual or immediate emancipation began in several states.
  • Public life: newspapers, associations, and civic ritual expanded.

What Remained Continuous

  • Law and administration: colonial legal codes, courts, notaries, and many officials continued.
  • Property: haciendas, estancias, and plantations largely survived, as did unequal access to land.
  • Race and status: hierarchies of color and honor persisted informally.
  • The Church: it remained the largest landowner, lender, and registrar, and nearly all states kept Catholicism as the official or only public religion.
  • Municipal politics: town councils remained the main local institution.
  • Slavery and tribute: both continued where economies and treasuries depended on them.

Who experienced change most directly: urban elites and officers, whose careers and offices were transformed, and Indigenous communities, who lost legal protections, and the enslaved in states that adopted free-womb laws, whose change was partial. Greater continuity: rural laborers, tenants, enslaved adults in plantation regions, and women, whose daily conditions changed less than the political order.

Why the pattern matters: the republics were built on colonial foundations. Explaining their difficulties requires attention to inherited structures of land, law, and hierarchy, as well as to new constitutional forms, and it avoids the assumption that independence marked a clean break.

Section 58.25

58.25 Independence Armies and the Militarization of Republican Politics

The wars left the new states with armies larger and more experienced than their treasuries could support. After Ayacucho in 1824 the Colombian and Peruvian armies contained thousands of veterans, mixed with officers who had risen from modest origins through battlefield promotion, and similar forces existed in Mexico, Chile, and the Río de la Plata. These soldiers expected to be paid, pensioned, or rewarded with land, and officers claimed a special status as the founders of the nation. Demobilization was therefore a political as well as financial problem, because disbanding troops risked unrest while keeping them cost money that governments did not have.

Armies operated as political institutions. Officers received appointments in administration and diplomacy as well as in the field, regiments were tied to commanders by loyalty and patronage, and garrisons in provincial towns often exercised more real authority than civil officials. Pay arrears and promotions were constant sources of grievance and gave commanders a motive to intervene in politics. Foreign troops sometimes complicated matters: the Colombian division stationed in Lima became a focus of resentment in Peru, and its mutiny in January 1827 helped bring down the Bolivian constitution in Peru. Governments tried to control their armies through purges, rotation, and the creation of rival forces.

Citizen militias were intended to provide an alternative. Chile reorganized its civic militia, the National Guard, in the early 1830s under Portales, and after the victory at Lircay purged officers loyal to the defeated liberals, replacing them with men loyal to the new order. Brazil created its National Guard in August 1831, open to citizens who met income qualifications, with local officers chosen by the members at first, and used it to maintain order in the provinces without relying wholly on the regular army. Mexican states likewise formed civic militias in the 1820s. These forces gave property-holding citizens a role in defense and policing, but they also gave local notables private armed followings.

The place of the army in the republic was contested. Liberals feared a standing army as a threat to liberty and favored militias, while conservatives argued that professional forces were necessary to deal with rebellion and frontier threats. Civilian ministers tried to subordinate officers to constitutional authority but often depended on them for survival. Officers could justify intervention by claiming to defend the constitution or the nation, which linked military power to the question of legitimacy examined below. For ordinary men the army meant forced recruitment, discipline, and uncertain pay, while for officers it offered a career and a route to political influence.

Section 58.26

58.26 Caudillos, Patronage, and Regional Coalitions

The word caudillo, from the Spanish for leader, was used in this period for a man whose authority rested on personal following. Its sources varied: military reputation won in the independence wars, control of land and cattle, the ability to distribute offices and favors, and the loyalty of militias and rural dependents. A caudillo governed through a network of reciprocal obligations, in which followers gave support in return for protection, employment, land, and recognition, and the leader had to keep supplying benefits to retain loyalty. This made caudillismo a system of negotiation, not simply of command.

Historians distinguish several types. José Antonio Páez built his power in the Venezuelan plains on the loyalty of the llaneros, the cattle-raising horsemen who had been central to the patriot armies, and went on to become the leading figure in Venezuela after its separation from Gran Colombia in 1830. In the Río de la Plata, estate owners such as Juan Manuel de Rosas and provincial leaders such as Facundo Quiroga in La Rioja drew on militias recruited from their rural dependents (Chapter 62). Vicente Guerrero, a leader of mixed ancestry who had fought in the Mexican insurgency, derived his standing from popular support among veterans and became president in 1829 before his overthrow and execution in 1831 (Chapter 59).

Not all strongmen fit the pattern. José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia, a lawyer, ruled Paraguay as Supreme Dictator from 1814 until 1840 through a centralized, isolationist state that controlled trade and curbed the old elite. Diego Portales in Chile held no military command and governed as a minister. Andrés de Santa Cruz in Bolivia was a former officer who ruled through an effective administration and a reorganized fiscal system. These examples show that caudillismo overlapped with state-building: some caudillos built bureaucracies, collected taxes, and enforced order, while others left only personal followings that disappeared with them.

The older view portrayed caudillos as the antithesis of constitutional order, men who replaced laws with personal will, and it linked them to a supposed Hispanic tradition of authoritarianism. More recent scholarship stresses that caudillos worked within and often through institutions, such as legislatures, elections, and courts, and that their power depended on negotiated alliances with landowners, merchants, and the Church. Their relationship to the state changed over time, and their emergence reflected the weakness of central authority, the militarization of rural society, and the importance of regional economic interests. Interpreting them as symptoms of these conditions, instead of as causes of instability, has become the more common approach.

Historical Vocabulary
Caudillismo

Caudillismo is the term historians use for politics organized around caudillos, leaders whose authority rested on personal followings. Their power came from combinations of military reputation, control of land and cattle, patronage, and the loyalty of militias and dependents. The word comes from the Spanish for leader and was used during the nineteenth century, often as a criticism.

Caudillos governed through reciprocal obligations: followers gave support and received protection, employment, land, or recognition. A leader who could no longer provide benefits risked losing the loyalty of his clients. Caudillos varied widely. Some, like Páez in Venezuela or Rosas in Buenos Aires, relied on rural horsemen and regional alliances. Others, like Santa Cruz in Bolivia, built administrations, and the civilian Portales in Chile and the lawyer Francia in Paraguay governed in different ways.

The term is easily misused as a synonym for dictator or warlord. Earlier historians treated caudillos as the opposite of constitutional government, while recent scholarship stresses their relations with legislatures, elections, and the Church and their role in building states. It also should not be read as a uniquely Latin American cultural trait, since it describes conditions of weak central authority and militarized rural societies.

Section 58.27

58.27 Pronunciamientos, Coups, and Contested Political Legitimacy

The pronunciamiento was a declaration, usually by military officers and often joined by civilian notables and municipal councils, announcing that a government had lost legitimacy and calling for a change of leadership or policy. It had Spanish origins in Rafael del Riego’s rising of January 1820, which forced the king to restore the Cádiz Constitution, and Spanish Americans adopted the form quickly. A pronunciamiento was typically issued as a document, a plan or acta, setting out grievances and demands and inviting other garrisons and towns to adhere to it.

The process followed a recognizable pattern. A commander or group of officers in a garrison proclaimed the plan, sought adherence from neighboring units, and publicized the declaration in the press. The government responded with force, negotiation, or concession, and the outcome depended on which units joined each side and on the attitude of provincial elites. Many pronunciamientos produced no change of government, since they ended in a negotiated settlement or collapsed for lack of support. Some historians describe the practice as a form of forceful negotiation in which armed units bargained with governments over policy, appointments, and pay.

Examples show how the justification varied. In Mexico the Plan of Veracruz of December 1822 and the Plan of Casa Mata of February 1823 helped bring down Iturbide’s empire by calling for a restored congress (Chapter 59). In 1828 the disputed election of that year in Mexico led to a pronunciamiento and a riot in the capital, and in Peru and Bolivia repeated pronunciamientos preceded changes of president. Plans claimed to defend the constitution, restore the will of the nation, or end tyranny, and they frequently appealed to popular sovereignty, which had been established as the basis of political legitimacy. Both rebels and defenders of the government accused the other side of violating the constitution.

The term “coup” has a narrower meaning than pronunciamiento and should be used with care. A coup involves the seizure of power by a small group, usually from within the state, whereas pronunciamientos were public acts with a broader claim to representation and could aim at policy change as well as the replacement of rulers. Their frequency reflected the weakness of constitutional mechanisms for resolving disputes, the political role of the army, and the lack of a single accepted source of authority. They also helped sustain a political culture in which both opponents and supporters of governments used constitutional language to justify armed action.

Section 58.28

58.28 Civil Wars, Rural Mobilization, and the Social Costs of State Formation

Civil war was a recurring feature of the early republics. In the Río de la Plata, conflict between supporters of a centralized state, the Unitarians, and provincial leaders, the Federales, began after the failure of the 1826 constitution and continued for decades. In Central America, the federal government and the Guatemalan conservatives fought between 1826 and 1829 until Francisco Morazán’s forces prevailed. Chile’s civil war ended with the conservative victory at Lircay in April 1830. Colombia’s War of the Supremes, beginning in 1839 with a revolt in Pasto over the closure of convents, spread across the country until 1842. Brazil’s provincial revolts of the 1830s, including the Cabanagem and the Farroupilha, are examined in Chapter 61.

These conflicts mobilized a wide range of people. Landowners raised followings from their dependents, and rural horsemen, such as the llaneros and the gauchos, fought as irregulars. Indigenous communities allied with different factions according to local interests: in the southern Andes of Colombia, Pasto’s communities had been royalist and later resisted the republic’s reforms, and royalist bands such as the Pincheira brothers continued to operate on the Chilean frontier until 1832 with the support of some Mapuche groups. Urban artisans, militias, and clergy also took sides. Motivations ranged from patronage and local autonomy to opposition to taxes, conscription, or religious reform.

The costs to civilians were high. Conscription removed workers from farms, armies requisitioned food, livestock, and horses, and forced loans and plunder damaged towns and estates. Cattle economies in the Río de la Plata and the Venezuelan plains lost herds, mines and plantations were disrupted, and families were displaced. Some conflicts, such as the Cabanagem in Brazil’s Pará province, which involved the rural poor, Indigenous people, and free people of color, ended in repression that killed large numbers, with estimates running into the tens of thousands. Such wars reveal the social tensions that political programs overlay, and they often drew on racial and economic resentments.

The wars also contributed to state-building. Victors consolidated their authority, built more reliable armies and fiscal systems, and imposed institutions on defeated regions, as the Portalian government did in Chile after Lircay. In Argentina and Venezuela, regional leaders emerged from the conflicts as national figures. In Colombia the War of the Supremes helped sharpen the identities of the groups that became the Liberal and Conservative parties. At the same time, repeated warfare drained the treasury, strengthened military men, and weakened trust in constitutional procedure, so that civil war was both a symptom of the fragility of the republics and one of the means by which their institutions were forged.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Institutional Failure, Constitutional Experimentation, or Both?

The question: Should the repeated constitutional crises of 1810–1850 be understood as evidence of weak institutions or as a period of active constitutional experimentation?

Interpretation A — Institutional failure: Historians in this tradition point to frequent pronunciamientos, short-lived charters, defaults, and the role of armies, and they explain them by weak central authority, colonial legacies of absolutism and corporate privilege, and fragile economies. On this view, constitutions were largely ineffective, and order depended on strongmen.
Interpretation B — Experimentation: More recent scholarship stresses that the period produced elections, legislatures, a public sphere, codification, and genuine arguments about citizenship and sovereignty. Instability came partly from contested questions that no one could yet settle, and many conflicts took constitutional forms, since actors claimed to defend the constitution.
Interpretation C — Interaction: Many historians now combine the two, arguing that crisis and experimentation reinforced each other. Revisions responded to failures, and failures arose from the experiments. They also point to variation: Chile and Brazil achieved relative stability, while Mexico, Central America, and the Río de la Plata faced repeated challenges.

Evidence: constitutions, assembly debates, electoral records, newspapers, petitions, court records, fiscal accounts, and military correspondence. Each type reflects the interests of its authors, so conclusions depend on which sources are emphasized.

Why it matters: the question affects whether later chapters read the period as a prelude to dictatorship or as the beginning of a long process of state formation. It also warns against teleology, the habit of treating later outcomes as inevitable results of earlier events.

Section 58.29

58.29 Federal Experiments in Mexico, Central America, and Gran Colombia

Mexico, the Central American Federation, and Gran Colombia faced the same four problems: how much autonomy provinces should keep, how they should be represented nationally, who would pay for the central government, and how a large territory could be held together. Their answers differed. Mexico’s constitution of 1824 created a federation of nineteen states and four territories, leaving the status of Tlaxcala to later legislation, each with its own constitution, governor, legislature, and militia, and a national congress in which every state held two Senate seats. The Central American Federation, established by a constitution of November 1824, joined five states, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica, under a federal congress, senate, and president, with its seat at Guatemala City until 1834, when it moved, after a brief interim at Sonsonate, to San Salvador, where a federal district was created in 1835.

Gran Colombia chose the opposite principle. The Cúcuta constitution of 1821 vested authority in the national government, and in 1824 a law divided the republic into twelve departments governed by intendants appointed from Bogotá. Its drafters believed that a country threatened by Spain and divided by distance and war required a single government, and the wartime leadership of Bolívar and the army lent weight to this view. Yet the centralized union faced regional pressures as strong as those in the federal states. Venezuelan resistance to Bogotá, expressed in the movement of 1826 around Páez, and separatist sentiment in Quito and Guayaquil showed that a centralized design did not eliminate demands for local autonomy.

Fiscal weakness afflicted all three. The Mexican federal government depended on customs revenue and on state contributions that states paid late or not at all, and the Central American federal government had even less income because the states controlled the main sources of revenue. Gran Colombia carried the heaviest debt, through its London loans and army costs, and its customs revenue was divided among competing regional ports. In each case, governments found that revenue was the practical test of sovereignty: a state that could not pay its soldiers or officials could not enforce its authority, whatever the constitution said.

The comparison suggests that outcomes depended less on the formal choice of federal or unitary structure than on underlying conditions. Mexico had a dominant central region around the capital and a main port at Veracruz, and its federation lasted until 1835–1836 before giving way to centralism (Chapter 59). Central America had no single economic or demographic center, and rivalry among five provincial capitals and between conservative and liberal factions led to civil war and the dissolution of the federation by 1838–1840 (Chapter 62). Gran Colombia contained three large regions with different economies and military leaders, Páez in Venezuela, Santander in New Granada, and Flores in Quito, and it split in 1830 (Chapter 60). Institutional design interacted with geography, economic structure, and the power of regional leaders, and no design was sufficient alone.

Section 58.30

58.30 Centralized Authority and Constitutional Experiments in the Andes and Southern Cone

Chile, Peru, Bolivia, and the Río de la Plata tried different ways of concentrating authority, and their results illustrate the conditions under which centralization could work. Chile’s constitution of 1833 created a unitary state in which the president appointed provincial intendants and governors, a bicameral congress controlled the budget, and a council of state advised the executive. The system followed several short-lived charters, including a liberal constitution of 1828, and was shaped by the conservative victory at Lircay. It provided a framework that most major political groups accepted for decades, and the presidents who held office under it governed with considerable authority.

Several features of Chile’s situation favored this outcome. Its settled core lay in a compact central valley between Santiago and Valparaíso, a small landowning elite dominated the capital and the countryside, customs revenue from Valparaíso and growing mineral exports gave the government resources, and the army and militia could be brought under executive control. The Mapuche frontier south of the Biobío lay outside the state’s reach, a limit to its claim of authority. The costs of the model were limited political competition, restricted suffrage, and the repression of opponents, including the exile or dismissal of liberal officers and officials after 1830.

The Andean states experimented more widely. Peru adopted a liberal constitution in 1823 that was suspended almost at once, the lifetime-presidency constitution of 1826, which was repudiated within seven weeks and annulled in June 1827, and a more liberal charter in 1828 with prefects and departmental councils. Bolivia, under Santa Cruz, replaced the lifetime presidency of the 1826 text with a fixed-term executive in 1831 and built an administration that relied on the Indigenous tribute for revenue. Neither state achieved the stability of Chile, in part because of regional rivalries within Peru, the dependence on tribute and a fragile mining economy, and the military ambitions of their leaders, which culminated in the Peru-Bolivian Confederation of 1836–1839 (Chapter 60).

In the Río de la Plata, centralizing constitutions in 1819 and 1826 failed because provinces refused to accept them, and the country remained a loose association of provinces bound by treaties such as the Federal Pact of 1831, under which Buenos Aires managed foreign relations for the others. The Argentine case shows that centralization required a capital with the resources and legitimacy to impose its will, which Buenos Aires lacked as long as the interior provinces controlled their own militias and revenue (Chapter 62). Comparing Chile, the Andes, and the Río de la Plata suggests that executive strength worked best where the state had a concentrated territory, a unified elite, and secure revenue, and that attempts to impose it without those conditions produced rebellion.

Section 58.31

58.31 Haiti, Brazil, and the Limits of a Single Republican Model

Haiti, Brazil, and the Spanish Caribbean show that Latin American state formation followed no single formula. Haiti, the first independent state born from a slave revolution, had to build a government while threatened by French reconquest. After the division between Christophe’s north and Pétion’s south, Jean-Pierre Boyer reunified the country in 1820 and extended its authority over the eastern part of Hispaniola in 1822, so that Haiti governed the entire island until 1844. Its leaders kept a large army, restricted white ownership of land, and accepted the French indemnity of 1825, which tied the state to debt. Boyer’s Rural Code of 1826 tried to bind agricultural workers to plantations, which conflicted with the aspirations of many former slaves to farm small plots of their own.

Brazil took a different path. Its constitution of 1824, a charter granted by Pedro I, created a hereditary monarchy with a Moderating Power, life senators, and indirect elections, and it preserved slavery and the territorial unity of Portuguese America. Continuity with the Braganza dynasty gave it legitimacy that Spanish American republics lacked, and the monarchy’s presence helped keep provincial elites within a single state. This stability was relative: Pedro I abdicated in 1831 and the provinces experienced serious revolts in the 1830s, topics for Chapter 61. But the empire’s survival shows that the republican form was not the only path to a viable state.

Cuba and Puerto Rico remained Spanish. After the liberal reforms of the Cádiz period, a royal order of 28 May 1825 gave the captain-general of Cuba the extraordinary powers of a commander of a besieged fortress to deal with the threat of invasion and insurrection, and the Spanish constitution of 1837 provided that the overseas provinces would be governed by special laws, with Cuban and Puerto Rican deputies excluded from the Cortes. Those special laws were not enacted for decades. Both islands developed plantation economies: Cuba’s sugar production expanded rapidly with the labor of enslaved Africans, and Puerto Rico’s coffee and sugar sectors grew with immigration encouraged by the royal decree of 1815. Planters feared that independence might bring a Haitian-style upheaval or lose them the protection of Spanish power, and they stayed loyal for decades (Chapter 67).

A comparison of these cases highlights three variables. The first is the form of government, republic or monarchy, and its relation to legitimacy: Brazil’s monarchy provided one framework for preserving unity despite provincial revolts, while republican and centralized experiments elsewhere produced both consolidation and fragmentation. The second is slavery and its place in the economy and in elite fears, which encouraged conservative solutions in Brazil and the Spanish Caribbean and shaped Haiti’s military and agrarian policies. The third is external position: Haiti faced hostile powers, Brazil won recognition through a payment to Portugal, and Cuba and Puerto Rico remained within an empire. Such differences explain why, by 1850, the region’s states had diverged so widely.

Section 58.32

58.32 Did Independence Create Nations?

It is common to say that independence created the nations of Latin America, but the evidence of this chapter suggests that independence was only the beginning of a longer set of processes. It is useful to distinguish six different achievements. Political independence meant the end of imperial sovereignty. Constitutional legitimacy meant the acceptance of a government’s right to rule. Collective identity meant a shared sense of belonging to a national community. Territorial control meant effective authority over the claimed space. Citizenship meant legal membership with rights and duties. Administrative capacity meant the ability to tax, record, educate, and enforce. States could possess some of these without the others, and they were achieved at different speeds in different places.

Judged by these tests, the states of the 1850s had very different records. Most had achieved political independence, though Spain withheld recognition in some cases for decades, and Cuba and Puerto Rico had not achieved it. Constitutional legitimacy was uneven: Chile and Brazil enjoyed relative stability, while Mexico, Central America, and the Río de la Plata suffered repeated challenges. Territorial control remained partial everywhere, with large frontier zones in Indigenous hands. Citizenship was defined narrowly, excluded women and many poor men, and coexisted with slavery in several states. Administrative capacity was limited by fiscal weakness, poor communications, and dependence on local elites.

Historians disagree about when national identity emerged. Benedict Anderson argued that Creole officials and printers, working within administrative and commercial circuits that crossed colonial territories, developed imagined communities before independence that became the basis of the new states. Other scholars emphasize that regional, local, and corporate loyalties remained stronger than national ones for decades, that the meaning of the nation was contested among elites, Indigenous communities, and Afro-descended groups, and that national identities were built after independence through schools, wars, ritual, and political conflict. The evidence of flags, anthems, festivals, and press discussion suggests that national symbols spread early, but their popular acceptance was gradual and uneven.

The next four chapters follow these processes in detail. Chapter 59 traces Mexico’s path from empire to republic, including federalism and centralism, fiscal weakness, the rise of Santa Anna, and the loss of Texas. Chapter 60 examines Gran Colombia’s collapse and the divergent republics of the Andes. Chapter 61 follows the Brazilian Empire through the abdication of Pedro I, the Regency, and the consolidation of the monarchy under Pedro II. Chapter 62 compares the Southern Cone and Central America, with their civil wars, regional leaders, and gradual consolidation. Together they show that the institutional problems identified here, legitimacy, citizenship, revenue, territory, and force, shaped each national path in different ways, and that state formation remained unfinished in 1850.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Constitutional Systems Faced Repeated Crises

Long-term causes

Colonial regionalism and the autonomy of towns and provinces; fragmented transport and economies; the dependence of government on corporate institutions such as the Church; and the absence of an accepted source of authority once the Crown fell.

Short-term causes

Wartime destruction and empty treasuries; large armies with unpaid officers; contested definitions of popular sovereignty; the collapse of London credit in 1825–1826; and disputes over who would control customs revenue.

Event or transformation

Constitutions were written, suspended, and revised, executives and legislatures clashed, and armed declarations (pronunciamientos) and civil wars became common ways to contest legitimacy.

Immediate effects

Governments relied on emergency powers and forced loans, soldiers and officials went unpaid, provinces withheld revenue, and constitutional language was used by both rebels and governments to justify action.

Long-term consequences

Strong executives in states such as Chile and Brazil; recurring military involvement in politics elsewhere; debt that constrained policy; and a continuing argument over federalism, citizenship, and the role of the Church that shaped Chapters 59–62.

Historical complexity

Institutional design, fiscal weakness, military power, regional rivalry, social conflict, and legitimacy interacted. A weak treasury made armies political, political armies weakened constitutions, and weak constitutions deprived governments of legitimacy needed to raise revenue. Outcomes varied with geography, elites, and resources, so no single cause explains the crises.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that independence did not automatically produce effective nation-states. The end of imperial rule opened a series of separate problems: who held sovereignty, who counted as a citizen, how power would be divided, who would collect revenue, how armies would be controlled, and whether populations accepted the new order. Each was solved at a different speed, and states could achieve some without the others.

The first division showed that the authority of the new governments had to be rebuilt after the Crown was gone, using the surviving machinery of colonial administration. Cádiz gave Americans experience of elections and municipal councils, insurgent charters, Atlantic revolutionary ideas, and the Hispanic doctrine that sovereignty reverts to the community supplied other sources. Constituent assemblies claimed to speak for the people but depended on limited, indirect elections, and their authority was disputed by executives and provinces. Monarchy, republic, federation, and centralism were all open choices.

The second division examined institutions. Constitutions combined declarations of rights with structures of power, and differed most over the executive: Bolívar’s lifetime presidency, Chile’s strong five-year president, Mexico’s state-elected president, and Brazil’s emperor with a Moderating Power. Legislatures controlled budgets and appointments and clashed with executives, as at Ocaña in 1828. Federalism and centralism reflected different views of sovereignty and different interests in revenue. Courts applied inherited law, codification began, and elections and town councils gave participation limited but real political weight.

The third division showed that membership in the new republics was graduated. Nationality, citizenship, and the vote were separate, and qualifications of property, income, occupation, and later literacy narrowed the electorate. Caste labels were abolished in many places while informal hierarchies of color and honor persisted. Emancipation proceeded through free-womb laws and abolition decrees at different speeds, Indigenous communities lost their separate legal status while tribute was reinstated, and women, though excluded from the vote, acted as property holders, petitioners, and wartime participants.

The fourth division traced the material conditions of state-building. Wartime destruction, the end of colonial transfers, and the collapse of mining left treasuries dependent on customs duties and on restored colonial taxes. London loans of 1822–1825 were followed by defaults after the crisis of 1825–1826, and recognition by Britain, the United States, Portugal, and France was tied to commercial and financial terms, most severely in Haiti’s indemnity. Economies recovered unevenly while estates and plantations survived. Borders followed colonial jurisdictions imperfectly, large frontier zones remained outside state control, and administration was limited by scarce officials, records, roads, and schools.

The fifth division examined ideas and public culture. Liberals sought constitutional limits, individual rights, free commerce, and the end of corporate privileges, while conservatives defended religion, property, hierarchy, and strong government; both included supporters of independence, and the labels remained fluid. Conflicts over the patronage, tithes, convents, and education divided the clergy and the public, and the press, associations, and civic ritual created a public sphere in which competing national identities took shape.

The sixth division explained the central role of armed force. Independence armies left veterans with claims on states that could not pay them, and militias offered an alternative that also created local followings. Caudillos built power through land, military reputation, and patronage, and differed widely, from Páez to Francia and Portales. Pronunciamientos were public declarations that claimed to defend the constitution or the nation, and civil wars mobilized landowners, rural horsemen, Indigenous communities, and townspeople at great civilian cost, while helping victors consolidate states.

The seventh division compared outcomes. Mexico, Central America, and Gran Colombia differed in federal and centralized design, yet all faced fiscal weakness and regional rivalry, and geography and the power of regional leaders helped determine their fates. Chile’s strong executive worked where territory was compact, elites unified, and revenue secure, and the Andean and Río de la Plata experiments show what happened where they were not. Haiti’s state born of emancipation, Brazil’s monarchy, and Spanish Cuba and Puerto Rico demonstrate that there was no single republican formula.

The chapter also developed a methodological point. Constitutions are evidence of aspirations and arguments, not of practice; the records of ordinary people survive mainly in courts and petitions; and many claims about money, population, and enforcement rest on incomplete data. Historians therefore combine texts with fiscal, legal, and local records and are cautious about attributing outcomes to a single cause or to national character.

The final lesson is that independence, constitutional legitimacy, collective identity, territorial control, citizenship, and administrative capacity are different achievements. By 1850 few states had achieved all six, and state formation remained an unfinished process. Chapters 59 through 62 follow that process in Mexico, Gran Colombia and the Andes, the Brazilian Empire, and the Southern Cone and Central America.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Popular sovereignty
The doctrine that authority derives from the people or nation; after 1808 it replaced the king as the basis of legitimacy, though “the people” was narrowly defined.
Constituent assembly
A body convened to write a constitution, whose authority depended on who convened it and who accepted its results.
Cádiz Constitution
The Spanish charter of 1812 that established indirect elections, municipal councils, and a broad but racially limited citizenship.
Federalism
A system dividing sovereignty between national and provincial governments, as in Mexico (1824) and Central America (1824).
Centralism
Concentration of authority in the national government, as in Gran Colombia (1821) and Chile (1833).
Caudillo
A leader whose power rested on personal followings built from land, military reputation, and patronage.
Caudillismo
Politics organized around caudillos, now understood as varied and often interacting with state institutions.
Pronunciamiento
A public declaration, usually by officers and joined by civilians, that a government had lost legitimacy and should change.
Patronato (padroado)
The crown’s right to nominate bishops and oversee Church finances; claimed by republics and, in Brazil, held by the emperor.
Ayuntamiento / cabildo
The municipal council, the main local institution and a focus of elections and petitions.
Fueros
Special legal privileges and separate courts of corporations such as the clergy and army.
Nationality
Membership in a state, distinct from the rights of citizenship.
Citizenship
Legal membership with civil and political rights and duties, usually conditional on property, income, or status.
Suffrage
The right to vote, a further privilege attached to some citizens.
Indirect election
A system in which voters choose electors who choose officials.
Free-womb law
A law freeing children born to enslaved mothers, usually after years of service, as in Gran Colombia (1821).
Manumission junta
A local board created in Gran Colombia to free some enslaved people, chronically short of funds.
Libertos
Freed people, whose rights were limited in many constitutions.
Tribute (contribución indigenal)
A head tax on Indigenous people, abolished and then reinstated in several states, and a major revenue source in Bolivia.
Uti possidetis
The principle that new states inherit colonial administrative boundaries.
Moderating Power
The fourth power of Brazil’s 1824 constitution, reserved to the emperor.
Liberalism
An outlook favoring constitutional limits, individual rights, free commerce, and the end of corporate privilege.
Conservatism
An outlook defending religion, property, hierarchy, and strong government, compatible with constitutionalism.
Customs revenue
Duties on trade, the main source of state income in most republics.
Contingentes
Contributions assigned to Mexican states to fund the national government, often unpaid.
Agiotista
A private lender who advanced money to Mexican governments at high interest.
Leva
Forced recruitment, falling mainly on poor and Indigenous men.
National Guard (militia)
Citizen forces created as an alternative to standing armies, as in Chile and Brazil.
Codification
The drafting of unified legal codes, as in Oaxaca, Bolivia, and Brazil.
Pardocracia
A term used by elites for feared dominance of free people of color.
Lancasterian system
A teaching method using older pupils to teach younger ones, adopted to spread schooling cheaply.

Identification & Significance

Simón Bolívar
Liberator of northern South America; drafted Bolivia’s constitution, defended strong executives, and assumed dictatorial powers in 1828.
Francisco de Paula Santander
Leader of constitutional opposition and president of New Granada; promoted education and the 1821 free-womb law’s implementation.
Bernardino Rivadavia
Architect of Buenos Aires’s liberal reforms of 1821–1824, including the ecclesiastical reform of 1822.
Pedro I
Emperor of Brazil who dissolved the assembly in 1823 and granted the 1824 charter.
Alexandre Pétion
President of Haiti’s southern republic whose 1816 revision created a lifetime presidency and banned slavery.
Diego Portales
Chilean minister who shaped the conservative order consolidated by the 1833 constitution.
José Antonio Páez
Venezuelan llanero leader and dominant figure after 1830.
Lucas Alamán
Mexican conservative who promoted industrial credit and centralism.
Andrés de Santa Cruz
Bolivian president who built a fiscal administration and issued a civil code in 1830.
José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia
Paraguay’s Supreme Dictator who ruled through an isolationist centralized state.
Gran Colombia
Union of Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito (1819/1821–1830), a centralist republic that fragmented.
Central American Federation
Federal union of five provinces formed in 1824, dissolved by 1838–1840.
Brazilian Empire
Constitutional monarchy preserving slavery and unity, 1822–1889.
Haiti
The first independent state born of slave emancipation, divided and then reunified under Boyer.
Cuba and Puerto Rico
Spanish colonies that kept plantation economies and stayed loyal until later in the century.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the making of republics was a long contest over authority, not an event. Constitutions, elections, courts, armies, tax systems, and newspapers were all instruments of that contest, and each worked only where governments and communities accepted it. Independence began the process of state formation, and the decisions of the 1820s and 1830s, about executive power, provincial autonomy, citizenship, and the Church, shaped what states could later do.

The chapter also shows continuity beneath political change. Law, landholding, the Church, municipal institutions, and hierarchies of race and status survived the end of empire, and Indigenous, enslaved, free Afro-descended, and female Latin Americans negotiated their position within them. Their petitions, lawsuits, enlistments, and refusals helped define what republican equality meant in practice.

These themes carry into the next four chapters. Mexico, the subject of Chapter 59, shows federalism, fiscal weakness, military politics, and territorial loss. Chapter 60 follows the collapse of Gran Colombia and the divergent republics of the Andes. Chapter 61 examines the Brazilian Empire, whose monarchy preserved unity and slavery, and Chapter 62 compares the Southern Cone and Central America, where civil war and regional leaders shaped consolidation.

Students should carry forward the habit of distinguishing independence from legitimacy, citizenship from the vote, and claimed territory from governed territory. These distinctions help explain why the nineteenth century produced both durable states and recurrent crises, and why the story of nation-building remained unfinished in 1850.
