Iberia on the Eve of American Expansion

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A World Not Yet Called "Spain"

In January 1492, in a camp of tents and improvised stone buildings outside the city of Granada, two monarchs who ruled two separate kingdoms accepted the surrender of the last independent Muslim state in the Iberian Peninsula. Later that same year, in the Castilian port town of Palos de la Frontera, three ships under the command of a Genoese mariner sailed west into the Atlantic on a voyage whose destination its own captain misunderstood. Neither event, considered on its own terms in 1492, announced the birth of a "Spanish Empire." Both were, instead, the culmination of processes that had been unfolding across the Iberian Peninsula and the eastern Atlantic for centuries — processes this chapter must explain before Volume II can responsibly narrate what followed.

Chapter 24 closed Volume I by insisting that the Indigenous societies of the Americas around 1492 were not waiting, static and unchanging, for a European arrival that would give their histories meaning. The Mapuche, the Guaraní, the Charrúa, and the many other peoples of southern South America, like the Taíno of the Caribbean, the Mexica of central Mexico, and the Inca administrators of Tawantinsuyu, were living within their own dynamic political worlds, pursuing their own conflicts and alliances, adapting to their own environments, on the eve of a contact none of them could have anticipated. This chapter performs the same service for the other side of the Atlantic. It asks what kind of political, religious, military, commercial, and maritime world existed on the Iberian Peninsula immediately before Christopher Columbus's first voyage, and it insists on a parallel caution: the Iberian kingdoms of the late fifteenth century were not a finished, unified "Spain" quietly awaiting its imperial destiny. They were plural, contested, evolving societies whose eventual involvement in American conquest and colonization was the product of specific historical developments, not an inevitable outcome written into their medieval past.

Students who arrive at this chapter already knowing how the story ends — with vast Spanish and Portuguese empires stretching from California to Patagonia — face a particular interpretive danger. It is tempting to read every earlier Iberian development as a rehearsal for empire: to see centuries of intermittent warfare between Christian and Muslim states as a single continuous "Reconquista" building inexorably toward 1492, to see Portuguese voyages down the African coast as a deliberate dress rehearsal for the crossing of the Atlantic, and to see the marriage of Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon as the founding moment of a unified Spanish nation-state. Each of these readings contains a kernel of truth and a larger distortion. This chapter is organized to correct the distortion without discarding the truth.

The truth is this: by the final decades of the fifteenth century, the Iberian kingdoms possessed an unusual accumulation of relevant historical experience. Centuries of frontier warfare between Christian and Muslim polities had produced military institutions, land-grant practices, and a culture of conquest and resettlement. Long-standing religious plurality among Christians, Muslims, and Jews had produced both practices of coexistence and periodic episodes of violent coercion, and by 1492 the pressure toward religious uniformity was intensifying dramatically. Iberian merchants, sailors, and monarchs had spent much of the century developing Atlantic navigation, colonizing island societies, and competing for access to African gold and, increasingly, African captives. Castile and Aragon had, through royal marriage, begun coordinating their considerable resources even while remaining legally and institutionally distinct kingdoms. Portugal, for its part, had pursued an independent and in some ways more precocious Atlantic strategy for the better part of a century before Columbus ever proposed his westward voyage to any European court.

The distortion lies in treating this accumulated experience as a plan. No fifteenth-century Castilian monarch, military commander, merchant, or churchman set out in, say, 1450 to design the institutions that would later govern Mexico City or Lima. The frontier practices developed during centuries of warfare in Iberia proper, and the colonial practices developed on Atlantic islands such as the Canaries, provided templates, vocabularies, and expectations that later proved adaptable to American circumstances — but adaptation is not identity, and the fit between Iberian precedent and American reality was never perfect, automatic, or foreordained. Columbus's 1492 voyage was one proposal among several then circulating in European courts, favored by circumstance and by the particular political moment in Castile, not the predetermined result of some deeper Iberian national destiny.

This chapter therefore asks students to hold several ideas in tension simultaneously. Iberia in 1492 was not one country but several: the separate crowns of Castile and Aragon, the independent kingdom of Portugal, the small kingdom of Navarre, and, until January of that year, the Nasrid Emirate of Granada. Iberia was not uniformly Christian in any simple sense; it was a peninsula whose history for centuries had included large Muslim and Jewish populations, whose relationships to the Christian kingdoms ranged from military conflict to diplomatic alliance to legally protected coexistence to, by 1492, violent expulsion. Iberia was not isolated from wider commercial and intellectual currents; Genoese bankers financed Castilian and Portuguese ventures alike, Mediterranean trade networks connected Barcelona and Valencia to Italy and the eastern Mediterranean, and Atlantic navigation drew on generations of accumulated maritime knowledge shared unevenly across European seafaring communities. And Iberia's turn toward the Atlantic was not a sudden 1492 event; it had been building, unevenly and with real setbacks, since at least the Portuguese capture of Ceuta in 1415 and the subsequent Portuguese and Castilian competition over the Canary Islands.

Understanding this world matters for reasons beyond simple background. The institutions, assumptions, and practices that Castilian and Portuguese actors carried across the Atlantic after 1492 — the vocabulary of conquest and reconquest, the practice of granting conquered land and labor to soldiers and settlers, the fusion of religious mission with military and commercial expansion, the treatment of newly subjected populations, the reliance on royal charters and negotiated capitulations, and the deep entanglement of monarchy, church, and private enterprise — did not spring into being in the Caribbean. They had deep roots in the Iberian and eastern Atlantic world this chapter examines, and later chapters will repeatedly return to those roots to explain why Spanish and Portuguese colonization took the specific institutional forms it did. At the same time, this chapter's final section insists that none of this made the specific outcome — a westward voyage, a Caribbean landfall, and the subsequent conquest of vast American empires — inevitable. Understanding the difference between precondition and destiny is itself one of the central historical skills this textbook asks students to develop, and this chapter offers one of its clearest opportunities to practice that skill.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the separate crowns of Castile, Aragon, Portugal, and Navarre, and the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, as distinct political entities on the Iberian Peninsula in the late fifteenth century.
  2. Explain why the marriage of Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon created dynastic coordination rather than a unified Spanish state.
  3. Describe the institutions of Castilian monarchy, nobility, towns, and the Cortes, and explain their relevance to later American colonization.
  4. Explain the Crown of Aragon as a composite monarchy encompassing distinct territories, laws, and institutions.
  5. Trace Portugal's independent Atlantic expansion from the capture of Ceuta in 1415 through the colonization of Atlantic islands and voyages along the West African coast.
  6. Analyze the medieval history conventionally called the Reconquista as a long, uneven, and religiously and politically complex process rather than a single continuous national crusade.
  7. Evaluate the military and political significance of the Granada War of 1482–1492 and the terms of the Capitulations of Granada.
  8. Explain the religious history of medieval and late-fifteenth-century Iberia, including Jewish, Muslim, and Christian communities, the establishment of the Spanish Inquisition in 1478, and the 1492 expulsion of the Jews.
  9. Assess how frontier warfare, land grants, and negotiated settlement in medieval Iberia created institutional precedents later adapted, imperfectly, to American conquest and colonization.
  10. Describe the Mediterranean and Atlantic commercial systems in which Iberian merchants, financiers, and ports participated before 1492.
  11. Explain the significance of the Canary Islands, Madeira, and the Azores as sites of Atlantic colonization, plantation experimentation, and Indigenous conquest preceding the Caribbean encounter.
  12. Evaluate why the historical preconditions examined in this chapter made American expansion possible without making its specific form or timing inevitable.

Historical Context

Volume I traced Indigenous American civilizations on their own terms across twenty-four chapters, from the earliest human migration into the hemisphere through the diverse political worlds of Mesoamerica, the Andes, the Caribbean, the Isthmo-Colombian region, Amazonia, and southern South America on the eve of conquest. Volume II now turns to the other historical trajectory that conquest and colonization would eventually join together: the political, religious, military, and commercial world of late-medieval and early modern Iberia and the eastern Atlantic. Just as Volume I insisted that Indigenous peoples not be treated merely as a passive backdrop for later European action, this chapter insists that Iberian history not be treated merely as an undifferentiated prologue racing toward Columbus.

The Iberian Peninsula in the late fifteenth century occupied a distinctive position at the intersection of the Mediterranean world, the Atlantic world, and continental Europe. It had been shaped for more than seven centuries by the presence of Muslim states following the early-eighth-century conquest of most of the peninsula by Umayyad forces, by the gradual expansion of Christian kingdoms in the north and their long, uneven advance southward, and by centuries of Jewish communal life that predated the Muslim conquest and continued, with major legal and social changes, throughout the medieval period. By 1492, this long history had produced a peninsula divided among several Christian kingdoms — Castile, Aragon, Portugal, and Navarre — and one remaining Muslim state, the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, along with substantial Jewish and Muslim populations living, under changing and increasingly precarious legal conditions, within the Christian kingdoms themselves.

Students should resist two temptations when approaching this material. The first is to imagine medieval Iberia as a simple binary struggle between Christians and Muslims stretching without interruption from 711 to 1492. In reality, Christian kingdoms fought one another as often as they fought Muslim states; Muslim rulers formed alliances with Christian kings against other Muslim rulers; tribute payments, intermarriage, diplomatic marriages, and shared administrative practices crossed religious lines throughout the medieval period; and the political map of the peninsula shifted dramatically and repeatedly across seven centuries rather than moving along a single steady trajectory of Christian advance. The second temptation is to treat "Spain" as an appropriate label for this period. Castile and Aragon remained separate crowns with separate laws, separate representative institutions, separate currencies, and, in Aragon's case, separate constituent territories, throughout the period this chapter examines and for centuries afterward. Portugal was, and remained, an entirely independent kingdom. This chapter uses "Iberia" or "the Iberian Peninsula" to describe the geographic space as a whole and identifies specific kingdoms by name whenever precision matters, reserving "Spain" primarily for later periods when it becomes historically appropriate.

At the same time, several genuine historical developments were converging by the late fifteenth century in ways that would prove significant for what followed. Dynastic marriage was beginning to coordinate the resources of Castile and Aragon without erasing their institutional distinctness. A prolonged and increasingly well-organized military campaign was bringing the Granada War to its conclusion. Religious authorities and monarchs were moving, with increasing urgency and increasing violence, toward a goal of religious uniformity that would culminate in the establishment of the Spanish Inquisition and the 1492 expulsion of Jews from Castile and Aragon. Portuguese and, increasingly, Castilian sailors, merchants, and colonists had spent much of the century developing navigational techniques, colonizing Atlantic islands, and competing for access to West African trade. And accumulated Mediterranean and Atlantic commercial networks, financed substantially by Genoese and other Italian merchant-bankers, connected Iberian ports to wider European and Mediterranean markets. This chapter examines each of these developments in turn, always asking not only what happened but what kind of precedent, institution, or expectation it created for the Atlantic world that Volume II's subsequent chapters will examine.

Section 25.1

Iberia at the End of the Fifteenth Century

A modern political map of the Iberian Peninsula shows two countries: Spain and Portugal. A map of the same peninsula in 1490 would show something considerably more plural: the Crown of Castile, occupying the largest share of the peninsula's territory and population; the Crown of Aragon, itself a composite of several distinct territories along the Mediterranean coast; the independent Kingdom of Portugal, running along the Atlantic seaboard; the small Kingdom of Navarre, wedged against the Pyrenees; and, in the south, the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, the last independent Muslim state on the peninsula, still ruling a wealthy and densely populated territory centered on the city of Granada. No single crown, no single set of laws, and no single institution governed the whole of Iberia. Terms such as Hispania and España existed as geographical and sometimes cultural descriptions of the peninsula as a whole, and had done so since Roman antiquity, but they did not designate a single unified political state. Political allegiance, law, taxation, and government remained rooted in distinct crowns and kingdoms, and it is this political fact — not the absence of any shared geographic vocabulary — that this chapter asks students to keep in view.

This plurality was not merely nominal. Castile and Aragon possessed different legal traditions, different systems of taxation, different representative assemblies, different currencies, and different relationships between monarch and nobility. Portugal had followed its own political trajectory since achieving independence from León in the twelfth century and had, by the late fifteenth century, developed a maritime and commercial orientation notably more advanced than either Castile's or Aragon's. Navarre remained a small but genuinely independent kingdom whose rulers balanced relationships with both Castile and the emerging kingdom of France. Granada, though facing an increasingly precarious military position by the 1480s, remained a functioning state with its own court, administration, fortifications, and international relationships, including periodic diplomatic and commercial contact with North African Muslim states.

The peninsula's geography reinforced this plurality. The Meseta, Iberia's high central plateau, favored the pastoral and grain economies that shaped Castilian society, while Mediterranean Aragon and Atlantic Portugal each developed maritime and commercial orientations suited to their coastlines. Mountain ranges divided regions from one another and complicated overland communication, while river systems and coastal access connected different parts of the peninsula more readily to the wider Mediterranean and Atlantic worlds than to one another. A merchant in Barcelona had more routine commercial contact with Genoa, Naples, or Sicily than with, in many respects, distant Castilian towns; a Portuguese sailor returning from the West African coast disembarked in a kingdom whose maritime institutions and royal patronage of exploration had no close Castilian equivalent until decades later.

This section's central lesson, then, is a corrective one: before this textbook can responsibly discuss Spanish and Portuguese American colonization, it must abandon the anachronistic habit of treating "Spain" as an already-existing unified actor in the late fifteenth century. What existed instead was a set of related but distinct Iberian polities, each with its own institutions and interests, some of which — chiefly Castile, and to a lesser extent Aragon and Portugal — would become deeply entangled in American expansion for reasons this chapter now examines in turn.

Section 25.2

From Medieval Frontiers to the Late Reconquista

The conventional term for the long medieval process by which Christian kingdoms in northern Iberia gradually extended their control over territory previously ruled by Muslim states is the Reconquista, a Spanish and Portuguese word meaning "reconquest." The term is useful as a broad label and will appear throughout this textbook, but students must understand from the outset that it can seriously mislead if taken too literally. It suggests a single, continuous, religiously motivated Christian campaign pursued without interruption from the early eighth century to 1492. The actual medieval history was considerably messier, more contingent, and more religiously and politically complicated than that label implies.

Following the rapid Umayyad conquest of most of the Iberian Peninsula beginning in 711, a small area in the far north, in the mountainous region of Asturias, remained outside Muslim control and became the nucleus from which several Christian kingdoms — eventually including León, Castile, Navarre, Aragon, and, later, an independent Portugal — gradually emerged over subsequent centuries. This process unfolded across more than seven hundred years, involved dramatic reversals as well as advances, and never constituted a single unified campaign directed by a single Christian authority. For long stretches, Christian kingdoms fought one another over succession, territory, and dynastic advantage with at least as much intensity as they fought Muslim states. Christian rulers repeatedly formed alliances with Muslim rulers against rival Christian kingdoms, and Muslim rulers just as often allied with Christian kings against rival Muslim factions, particularly during the period of political fragmentation into smaller successor states known as taifa kingdoms following the collapse of the Umayyad Caliphate of Córdoba in the early eleventh century.

Tribute payments, called parias, flowed in both directions at different periods, as did diplomatic marriages, commercial exchange, and shared administrative and legal practices. Mozarabic Christians lived under Muslim rule for centuries, often retaining their religion, language, and much of their legal status, while Mudéjar Muslims would later live under Christian rule following conquest, similarly retaining, for a time, considerable legal and religious autonomy under negotiated terms. This pattern of negotiated coexistence, however unequal and however precarious, existed alongside recurring episodes of warfare, forced conversion, and violence, and historians continue to debate how to weigh these different dimensions of medieval Iberian society against one another — a debate examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Two developments in particular reshaped the trajectory of this long process. The Christian victory at the Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212, achieved by a coalition of several Christian kingdoms against the Almohad Caliphate, opened the way for major Christian advances across much of southern Iberia over the following decades, including the conquest of the great cities of Córdoba, Seville, and Valencia during the thirteenth century. By the middle of that century, the only substantial Muslim state remaining on the peninsula was the Emirate of Granada, established under the Nasrid dynasty in 1238, which survived for a further two and a half centuries by combining fortified mountainous terrain, periodic tribute payments to Castile, diplomatic maneuvering, and, at various points, alliances with North African Muslim powers.

Understanding this longer medieval history matters directly for the chapter's larger argument. By the fifteenth century, Iberian Christian kingdoms possessed centuries of accumulated experience in frontier warfare, negotiated conquest, the resettlement of conquered territory, and the administration of religiously and culturally diverse populations under Christian rule. These were genuine historical resources that later proved adaptable, in modified form, to American circumstances. But the medieval process that produced them was neither as unified nor as inexorably progressive as the later term "Reconquista" has often suggested. As this chapter's Historical Debate feature discusses further, the modern, unified national meaning of "Reconquista" was consolidated especially through nineteenth-century Spanish historiography and was later amplified in National-Catholic political rhetoric under the Franco dictatorship, even though medieval participants themselves genuinely drew on religious and crusading ideas of their own.

Section 25.3

Castile: Land, Nobility, Towns, and Royal Power

Because the Crown of Castile would become the principal institutional base for most early Spanish American ventures, this chapter devotes particular attention to its internal structure. By the late fifteenth century, Castile occupied the greater part of the Iberian Peninsula by territory and population, encompassing the high central plateau of the Meseta, the fertile river valleys of Andalusia in the south, and the Atlantic coastline of the north. Its population, economy, and institutions were considerably more agrarian and pastoral than those of Aragon or Portugal, though Castile also possessed important commercial centers, particularly the Andalusian ports that would soon become central to Atlantic navigation.

Castilian society was organized around a powerful landed aristocracy, whose great noble houses controlled vast estates, exercised extensive local jurisdiction, and maintained private armed retinues capable of rivaling royal forces in some periods. The Castilian monarchy had spent much of the fifteenth century, particularly during the troubled reign of Isabella's half-brother Henry IV, contending with powerful noble factions who periodically challenged royal authority, supported rival claimants to the throne, and treated royal power as one force among several rather than as an unquestioned sovereign authority. Isabella's own accession in 1474, following a contested succession crisis and a subsequent war against a rival claimant backed by Portugal, illustrates how genuinely uncertain and contested Castilian royal authority remained on the very eve of the period this chapter examines.

Alongside the nobility, Castilian towns and cities possessed significant institutional weight. Municipal councils, staffed by local elites, governed day-to-day town affairs, administered justice, and organized militias. By Isabella’s reign, the regular working core of the Castilian Cortes consisted increasingly of procuradores representing a restricted group of privileged towns — seventeen at her accession, with Granada added after 1492 — convened chiefly to authorize extraordinary taxation and, less frequently, to address broader matters of governance. Clergy and nobility retained real political importance and could participate in Cortes proceedings, but no longer functioned consistently as regular parliamentary estates in the way they had in earlier centuries. The Cortes of Castile never developed the kind of sustained, powerful check on royal authority that some contemporary European assemblies achieved, but it remained a real institutional presence that Castilian monarchs, including Isabella, had to negotiate with rather than simply ignore.

Castile's economy rested substantially on sheep farming, organized through the powerful sheep-owners' guild known as the Mesta, which controlled vast seasonal migratory routes for merino sheep and generated wool exports that fed textile industries in Flanders, Italy, and elsewhere. This pastoral economy coexisted with grain agriculture, viticulture, and, in Andalusian towns such as Seville, increasingly important commercial and maritime activity connecting Castile to Atlantic and Mediterranean trade. Castile's military traditions, forged through centuries of frontier warfare against Muslim states and periodic internal conflict among Christian factions, produced a martial aristocracy accustomed to campaigning, to negotiated settlement of conquered territory, and to the practice of granting land, offices, and revenue rights to soldiers as reward for service — a practice, examined further in Section 25.9, with direct later relevance to American conquest.

Isabella's reign, beginning in 1474, marked a genuine, if incomplete, consolidation of royal authority relative to the aristocracy. Isabella and her advisors worked to bring noble private armies and fortresses under greater royal control, to reform royal finance and justice, and to project royal power more effectively through instruments such as the Hermandad, a royal-sponsored law-enforcement association originally organized by towns. These reforms should not be overstated; Castilian nobles retained enormous wealth, local authority, and social prestige throughout Isabella's reign and long afterward. But the trajectory toward stronger, more assertive royal government, however incomplete, forms an important part of the political backdrop against which the Granada War, the Inquisition, and the authorization of Columbus's voyage should all be understood.

Section 25.4

The Crown of Aragon and the Mediterranean World

If Castile anchors this chapter's account of Iberia's Atlantic-facing kingdom, the Crown of Aragon anchors its account of Iberia's Mediterranean-facing one — and it does so through a political form quite different from Castile's more territorially unified monarchy. The Crown of Aragon was what historians call a composite monarchy: a single ruling dynasty presiding over several distinct territories, each of which retained its own laws, institutions, and representative assemblies, joined together chiefly through the person of a shared monarch rather than through any unified administrative structure.

By 1479, the Crown of Aragon encompassed the Kingdoms of Aragon, Valencia, Majorca, Sardinia, and Sicily, together with the Principality of Catalonia. Alfonso V had also ruled Naples from 1442 to 1458, but after his death Naples passed to a separate Aragonese dynasty descended from his illegitimate son Ferrante, and did not come under Ferdinand II’s direct rule until 1504. Each of these territories possessed its own fueros, or bodies of law and customary privilege, its own Corts or Cortes, and its own relationship with the crown, negotiated separately rather than uniformly. A Catalan merchant, an Aragonese noble, and a Valencian town councilor operated under meaningfully different legal and political systems despite sharing the same monarch, and Ferdinand, upon becoming king of Aragon in 1479, inherited this composite structure rather than a unified kingdom comparable to Castile.

Barcelona, Aragon's principal Mediterranean port, had for centuries served as a major hub of Mediterranean commerce, connecting Catalonia to Sicily, Sardinia, Naples, North Africa, and the eastern Mediterranean trade routes that ultimately reached as far as the Black Sea and the Levant. Catalan and Aragonese merchants, financiers, and sailors participated in a commercial world oriented chiefly toward Mediterranean rather than Atlantic exchange, dealing in textiles, spices, grain, and other Mediterranean commodities, and competing, sometimes violently, with Genoese, Venetian, and other Italian merchant communities for access to these markets.

This Mediterranean orientation carries two important consequences for this chapter's larger argument. First, it demonstrates that "Iberia" cannot be reduced to a single Atlantic-facing story; the Crown of Aragon's principal historical trajectory ran toward the Mediterranean, not the ocean that Columbus would eventually cross, and Aragonese resources and attention remained substantially devoted to Mediterranean affairs — including, during the very years surrounding 1492, Ferdinand's ongoing involvement in Italian dynastic politics — even as Castile moved toward Atlantic engagement. Second, it demonstrates why Ferdinand should not be understood merely as "Isabella's husband," a junior partner whose Aragonese kingdom contributed little to the joint monarchy. Ferdinand brought substantial military experience, diplomatic connections throughout the Mediterranean and broader European world, and a political culture of negotiated, contractual monarchy quite different from Castile's more aristocratic and pastoral political economy. The eventual Spanish state that emerged from the union of Castile and Aragon inherited elements of both political traditions, and Aragon's contractual, composite model would leave its own mark on later Spanish imperial administration, even as Castilian institutions, personnel, and legal traditions came to dominate American colonization specifically.

Section 25.5

Portugal and the Atlantic Turn

No account of Iberia on the eve of American expansion can be complete without Portugal, whose Atlantic orientation developed earlier, and in some respects more systematically, than that of either Castile or Aragon. Portugal had achieved independence from the kingdom of León in the twelfth century under Afonso Henriques, and by the late Middle Ages had consolidated a compact, relatively unified kingdom running along the Atlantic seaboard of the peninsula, with Lisbon as its principal commercial and political center.

The conventional starting point for Portugal's Atlantic and African expansion is the 1415 capture of Ceuta, a Muslim-controlled port on the North African coast opposite Gibraltar, undertaken by King João I and his sons, including the prince later known to history as Henry the Navigator. Ceuta's capture reflected several overlapping Portuguese motivations: continuing crusading and religious ambition directed against Muslim powers, a desire to control North African trade in gold and other goods, and the strategic and commercial interests of Portuguese nobility and merchants seeking new outlets for military service and economic opportunity beyond Portugal's relatively limited internal resources.

Over the following decades, Portuguese mariners, operating under royal patronage centered particularly around Prince Henry's household and later that of King João II, undertook increasingly ambitious voyages down the West African coast, steadily extending Portuguese geographic and commercial reach. These voyages combined several purposes: charting unfamiliar coastlines, establishing trading relationships with West African polities for gold, ivory, and other goods, and, with increasing significance from the 1440s onward, acquiring captive Africans for transport back to Portugal and, eventually, to Atlantic island plantations — a development this chapter introduces only briefly, since the fuller history of African societies, Portuguese slaving, and the developing Atlantic slave economy belongs to Chapter 26.

Alongside African exploration, Portugal colonized the uninhabited Atlantic archipelagos of Madeira, settled from the 1420s, and the Azores, settled from the 1430s, establishing agricultural colonies that, particularly on Madeira, developed early and highly profitable sugar plantations worked increasingly by enslaved African labor. These island colonies functioned as genuine laboratories for practices that would later reappear, in modified form, in the Caribbean: the granting of land under royal charter to private captains and settlers, the establishment of colonial administrative and judicial institutions, the introduction of European crops and livestock to new environments, and the organization of plantation agriculture dependent on coerced labor.

Portuguese royal policy toward this expanding maritime and commercial world was distinctive in its degree of sustained royal patronage and its early development of specialized navigational and cartographic expertise, including the increasing use of caravels — a maneuverable ship type well suited to coastal exploration against contrary winds — and increasingly sophisticated portolan charts and navigational instruments. By the 1480s, Portuguese mariners under royal sponsorship had reached the mouth of the Congo River and were pushing further south along the African coast, a process that would culminate, in 1488, in Bartolomeu Dias's rounding of the southern tip of Africa, opening the prospect of a sea route to Asia that Portugal would pursue to fruition a decade later under Vasco da Gama.

This Portuguese trajectory matters enormously for understanding the specific historical moment in which Columbus made his proposal. By the 1480s, Portugal possessed the most advanced and most royally supported Atlantic navigational program in Europe, along with a coherent, increasingly successful strategy of reaching Asian markets by sailing around Africa. When Columbus first proposed his westward alternative to Portuguese authorities, he was proposing it to precisely the European court best positioned to evaluate its plausibility — and, as Section 25.12 explains, that court's informed rejection of his proposal, rather than any Castilian eagerness, forms an essential part of the specific sequence of events that eventually placed Columbus before Isabella and Ferdinand instead.

Section 25.6

Isabella, Ferdinand, and Dynastic Monarchy

The marriage of Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon, contracted in 1469 while both were still heirs rather than reigning monarchs, is often treated in popular accounts as the founding moment of a unified Spanish nation. This chapter asks students to understand the marriage more precisely, as an important act of dynastic coordination that left the fundamental institutional separateness of Castile and Aragon fully intact.

Isabella's path to the Castilian throne was neither smooth nor uncontested. Her half-brother, Henry IV of Castile, had a daughter, Joanna, whose legitimacy was disputed by factions favoring Isabella's succession instead; upon Henry's death in 1474, Isabella claimed the throne, provoking a war of succession against Joanna's supporters, who received military backing from Portugal. Isabella's eventual victory in this war, concluded by 1479, secured her position as queen of Castile but also demonstrated how genuinely contested and unstable Castilian royal authority remained even in the years immediately preceding the developments this chapter examines. Ferdinand became king of Aragon in his own right in 1479, upon the death of his father, John II.

The resulting arrangement, sometimes loosely called the union of the Catholic Monarchs — a title the papacy formally bestowed on Isabella and Ferdinand only later, in 1496, in recognition of their religious policies — created a powerful working partnership without erasing the separate legal and institutional identities of Castile and Aragon. Isabella governed Castile; Ferdinand governed Aragon; each crown retained its own laws, currency, taxation, and representative institutions; and even significant joint undertakings, including the Granada War and the sponsorship of Columbus's voyage, were financed and administered in ways that reflected this underlying separateness rather than a unified Spanish treasury or Spanish state apparatus. Castilian participation in the Granada War and in the subsequent authorization of Columbus's voyage substantially outweighed Aragonese involvement, reflecting Castile's greater territorial proximity to Granada and its Atlantic geographic orientation, while Aragonese resources and attention remained more heavily engaged with Mediterranean and Italian affairs throughout this period.

What the marriage did accomplish, however, was significant: it ended a long history of periodic warfare and rivalry between Castile and Aragon, allowed the two crowns to coordinate military and diplomatic strategy when their interests aligned, as they did decisively during the Granada War, and created a joint royal authority capable of mobilizing resources, prestige, and legitimacy on a scale that neither crown could easily have achieved alone. This coordinated capacity, rather than any deeper institutional unification, is what allowed Isabella and Ferdinand together to complete the conquest of Granada, to pursue an increasingly aggressive religious policy, and, in April 1492, to authorize and finance Columbus's proposed westward voyage. Students should carry this distinction — dynastic coordination without institutional unification — forward into later chapters, since Castilian and Aragonese institutional differences would continue to shape Spanish imperial administration for generations, including the eventual creation of separate councils and administrative structures for Castilian and Aragonese affairs that persisted well into the early modern period.

Section 25.7

Granada and the End of Muslim Rule, 1482–1492

The Granada War, fought between 1482 and 1492, deserves treatment as a major state-building and military event in its own right, not merely as the symbolic final act of a centuries-long Reconquista. The war was fought by combined Castilian and Aragonese forces against the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, whose internal political divisions — including a succession dispute between the eventual last ruler, Muhammad XII, known to Christian sources as Boabdil, and rival claimants within the Nasrid dynasty — significantly weakened Granadan resistance and were actively exploited by Isabella and Ferdinand's diplomacy.

The war proceeded through a prolonged sequence of sieges rather than a single decisive battle, reflecting Granada's mountainous terrain and extensive network of fortified towns. Castilian and Aragonese forces employed increasingly sophisticated siege artillery, including large bombards capable of breaching previously formidable fortifications, alongside substantial infantry and cavalry forces raised through a combination of noble retinues, royal levies, and, significantly, extraordinary taxation authorized by the Cortes specifically to fund the campaign. The war's financing illustrates the increasing fiscal and administrative capacity of the Castilian monarchy under Isabella, whose government developed more systematic methods of taxation, supply, and military logistics over the course of the decade-long campaign than Castile had typically sustained in earlier medieval conflicts.

Successive Castilian and Aragonese victories progressively reduced Nasrid territory throughout the 1480s, culminating in the siege of the city of Granada itself beginning in 1491. Rather than pursuing the city's destruction through prolonged siege warfare or storming its formidable defenses, Isabella and Ferdinand negotiated terms of surrender with Muhammad XII, producing the Capitulations of Granada, signed in November 1491 and formally implemented with the city's surrender on January 2, 1492. These capitulations, examined in detail in this chapter's Primary Source Window, promised the Muslim population of Granada continued practice of their religion, use of their own legal and judicial customs, retention of property, and various other protections in exchange for peaceful surrender.

The Granada War's significance extends well beyond its religious symbolism. It represented one of the most substantial and sustained mobilizations of Castilian and Aragonese military, fiscal, and administrative resources in the fifteenth century, providing Isabella's government with valuable experience in large-scale campaign logistics, extraordinary taxation, and the coordination of noble military service with royal strategic direction. It also provided a large population of veteran soldiers, commanders, and aristocratic adventurers accustomed to campaigning, siege warfare, and the expectation of reward — land, office, revenue rights, or captives — for successful military service. Many of the individuals who would later participate in Caribbean conquest and, in subsequent decades, in the conquests of Mexico and Peru examined in later chapters of this volume, belonged to social groups shaped directly by the Granada War and the broader culture of frontier conquest this chapter has examined. The war's conclusion in January 1492 freed substantial military, financial, and administrative attention for other royal projects — including, within months, the authorization of Columbus's voyage — a temporal proximity that later observers have sometimes read, too simply, as direct cause and effect, a question this chapter's concluding section addresses more carefully.

Section 25.8

Christianity, Jews, Muslims, and Religious Uniformity

Medieval Iberia's religious history was considerably more complicated than a simple story of Christian, Muslim, and Jewish communities living either in harmonious convivencia or perpetual conflict — two competing popular images that this chapter, and the accompanying Historical Debate feature, ask students to evaluate critically rather than accept uncritically. Jewish communities had lived in Iberia since Roman times and continued to do so under both Muslim and Christian rule throughout the medieval period, often serving important roles in royal administration, finance, medicine, translation, and commerce, while remaining subject to distinct legal status, periodic taxation targeted specifically at Jewish communities, and recurring episodes of violence and restriction that intensified considerably from the late fourteenth century onward.

The year 1391 marked a decisive turning point. Waves of anti-Jewish violence swept across Castile and Aragon, destroying Jewish communities in numerous cities and prompting large numbers of Jews to convert to Christianity, in many cases under direct threat of violence rather than through genuine religious persuasion. These converts, known as conversos, and their descendants formed a substantial population whose sincerity many Old Christians increasingly viewed with suspicion over subsequent decades, particularly as some conversos rose to prominent positions in church, royal administration, and commerce, provoking resentment and, in some cases, violent backlash from those who viewed their advancement as illegitimate given doubts about their religious conviction.

It was substantially to address these anxieties that Isabella and Ferdinand requested, and Pope Sixtus IV authorized in 1478, the establishment of the Spanish Inquisition, a Castilian royal institution — distinct from earlier medieval inquisitions operating under direct papal authority — charged with investigating and prosecuting conversos suspected of secretly continuing Jewish religious practice. The Inquisition's establishment reflected a broader royal and ecclesiastical project of enforcing religious uniformity as a component of political consolidation, treating religious conformity as inseparable from loyal subjecthood. Over subsequent decades, the Inquisition would investigate, try, and in numerous cases execute individuals accused of secret Jewish or, later, secret Muslim practice, employing methods including testimony, confession sometimes obtained under torture, and confiscation of the property of the condemned.

The drive toward religious uniformity reached a further decisive point in 1492. On March 31 of that year, Isabella and Ferdinand issued the Alhambra Decree, ordering all Jews in Castile and Aragon who refused Christian conversion to leave their kingdoms within a matter of months. Estimates of the number of Jews expelled or forced to convert vary considerably among historians, with commonly cited figures ranging from tens of thousands to more than one hundred thousand people, and precise numbers cannot be established with full confidence from surviving records; students should treat any single figure cited in other sources with appropriate caution. Many exiled Jews resettled in Portugal, North Africa, the Ottoman Empire, and various parts of Christian Europe, carrying Iberian Jewish, or Sephardic, culture, language, and religious tradition into new diaspora communities that would persist for centuries.

The treatment of Granada's Muslim population followed a related, though initially more gradual, trajectory. The Capitulations of Granada promised religious toleration and legal autonomy to Granada's Muslims, known after conquest as Mudéjares, but these promises eroded steadily over the following decade under mounting pressure from Castilian religious authorities, particularly after Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, already Archbishop of Toledo since 1495, arrived in Granada in 1499 and pursued a substantially more aggressive policy of forced conversion, overriding the slower, more negotiated approach previously associated with Granada's first archbishop, Hernando de Talavera. Forced conversions, provoked rebellions, and eventually formal edicts requiring conversion or expulsion transformed Granada's Muslim population into nominal Christian converts known as Moriscos over subsequent years — a process whose fuller development belongs substantially to later chapters but whose roots lie directly in the 1492 settlement examined here.

This section's central lesson bears directly on the chapter's larger argument. The very years in which Isabella and Ferdinand were extending unprecedented royal patronage to Atlantic exploration were also years of accelerating religious coercion at home. The same monarchy that negotiated generous surrender terms with Granada in November 1491 ordered the expulsion of Jewish subjects four months later and would, within a decade, begin dismantling the religious protections it had promised Granada's Muslims. Understanding this pattern — negotiated accommodation giving way, often rapidly, to coercive uniformity — matters directly for later chapters examining how Spanish authorities would treat Indigenous and enslaved populations in the Americas, where similar patterns of initial negotiation followed by intensifying coercion would recur in different form.

Section 25.9

Warfare, Frontier Institutions, and the Culture of Conquest

Centuries of frontier warfare across medieval Iberia produced a distinctive set of military institutions, social expectations, and administrative practices that later proved genuinely, if imperfectly, adaptable to American circumstances. Understanding these institutions in their original Iberian context is essential before later chapters can meaningfully explain how they were carried across the Atlantic and transformed by American conditions.

Medieval Iberian warfare relied on a combination of heavy and light cavalry, infantry, and, by the fifteenth century, increasingly effective siege artillery, organized through a mixture of royal forces, noble retinues raised through feudal and quasi-feudal obligation, urban militias mobilized by town councils, and specialized military orders — religious-military brotherhoods such as the Orders of Santiago, Calatrava, and Alcántara — that combined monastic discipline with sustained frontier combat and administered substantial territories granted to them as reward for military service. These orders illustrate a broader pattern central to Iberian frontier society: the close entanglement of military service, religious mission, and material reward, an entanglement that would reappear, transformed, in the American conquest expeditions examined in later chapters.

Frontier warfare in Iberia typically culminated not in the wholesale destruction of conquered populations but in negotiated surrender agreements, comparable in form to the Capitulations of Granada examined in Section 25.7, which specified terms under which conquered populations — whether Muslim, in the case of Christian conquest, or occasionally Christian, in cases of Muslim resurgence during earlier centuries — would retain certain rights, properties, and religious practices in exchange for submission and, typically, tribute payment. Conquered territory was subsequently resettled through royal grants of land, urban charters, and fiscal privileges intended to attract Christian settlers, a process called repoblación, or repopulation, which frequently involved granting substantial estates and jurisdictional authority to nobles, military orders, and the church in exchange for their role in conquest and their ongoing responsibility for defense and administration.

Soldiers who participated successfully in frontier campaigns could expect, as customary reward, grants of land, revenue rights, captives, or municipal privileges, formalized through royal charter. This expectation — that military service against non-Christian populations merited material reward in land, labor, or tribute — represents one of the most direct institutional threads connecting medieval Iberian frontier warfare to later American conquest, where soldiers who participated in the conquests of Mexico, Peru, and other regions would similarly expect, and frequently receive, grants of land and Indigenous labor and tribute through the institution known as encomienda, examined in detail in later chapters of this volume.

Students must exercise real caution here, however, in drawing the connection between Iberian precedent and American practice. The scale, the specific populations involved, the environmental and epidemiological conditions, and the administrative structures of American conquest differed enormously from their Iberian antecedents, and later chapters will show that American institutions such as encomienda, while drawing on Iberian precedent, were adapted, negotiated, and contested in ways that produced genuinely new colonial institutions rather than simple transplantation of medieval Iberian practice. The relationship between Iberian frontier institutions and American colonial institutions is best understood as one of adaptable precedent and cultural vocabulary rather than direct, unmodified transfer — a distinction this chapter's Continuity and Change feature examines further.

Section 25.10

Iberian Commerce and the Mediterranean–Atlantic Economy

Iberia on the eve of American expansion was not an economically isolated or backward periphery of Europe; it was an active participant in extensive Mediterranean and increasingly Atlantic commercial networks, financed substantially by foreign capital and connected to markets across Europe, the Mediterranean, and, indirectly, North Africa and beyond. Understanding this commercial world helps explain both the resources available to fund later American ventures and the personnel — merchants, financiers, sailors, and shipowners — who would participate in them.

Castile's most significant export was wool, produced by vast migratory flocks of merino sheep managed through the Mesta and shipped through northern Castilian ports, particularly Burgos's commercial network, to textile manufacturing centers in Flanders, England, and Italy. This wool trade connected Castilian pastoral society to broader European commercial and financial networks and generated substantial royal revenue through taxation, contributing meaningfully to the fiscal capacity that funded the Granada War and, subsequently, Atlantic ventures.

Andalusian ports, particularly Seville and, to its south, Cádiz and the smaller ports of Palos and Huelva, occupied an increasingly important position bridging Mediterranean, Atlantic, and West African trade routes by the late fifteenth century. Seville, situated on the navigable Guadalquivir River, had grown into a major commercial center following its thirteenth-century conquest, attracting merchant communities from Genoa, Florence, and other Italian commercial centers alongside Castilian, Portuguese, and other traders. These Italian merchant communities, particularly Genoese bankers and merchants, played an outsized role in financing Iberian commercial and exploratory ventures throughout this period, providing capital, credit, and commercial expertise that both Castilian and Portuguese monarchs and private entrepreneurs relied upon extensively — a pattern of Italian financial involvement in Iberian Atlantic expansion that would continue directly into the financing of early Caribbean ventures, including aspects of Columbus's own voyages.

The Crown of Aragon's commercial orientation, centered on Barcelona and the broader Catalan and Valencian mercantile world, remained substantially Mediterranean, connecting Iberia to Sicily, Sardinia, Naples, North Africa, and eastern Mediterranean trade routes reaching toward the Black Sea and the Levant, through which spices, silk, and other luxury goods entered European markets. Catalan merchants competed, often intensely, with Genoese and Venetian rivals for access to these lucrative trade routes, and this competition itself provides useful context for understanding why Genoese capital and expertise, somewhat blocked from full participation in eastern Mediterranean trade by Venetian dominance and periodic conflict, increasingly sought outlets in Atlantic and Iberian ventures instead.

Portugal's commercial orientation, as Section 25.5 examined, increasingly centered on the emerging Atlantic and West African trade in gold, ivory, and, with growing significance, enslaved Africans, alongside continuing participation in broader European and Mediterranean commerce through Lisbon's port. By the 1480s, Lisbon had become one of Europe's most important commercial centers specifically because of its role in this expanding Atlantic trade, attracting merchants, sailors, and capital from across Europe eager to participate in Portugal's evidently profitable African ventures.

Taken together, this commercial picture demonstrates that Iberian kingdoms possessed, by the late fifteenth century, substantial financial capacity, extensive commercial networks, and considerable practical experience in organizing long-distance trade ventures — resources and experience directly relevant to funding and staffing the Atlantic voyages examined in subsequent chapters. It also demonstrates that this capacity was never purely "Iberian" in isolation; Genoese, Florentine, and other foreign merchants and financiers were deeply embedded within Castilian and Portuguese commercial life, a pattern of foreign involvement that would continue directly into the financing and staffing of early American ventures.

Section 25.11

Islands, Ships, Navigation, and Atlantic Experimentation

Of all the developments examined in this chapter, none provides a closer institutional precedent for American colonization than Iberian experience colonizing Atlantic islands over the course of the fifteenth century. This section examines the Canary Islands, Madeira, and the Azores as sites where Iberians developed, tested, and refined practices of conquest, settlement, plantation agriculture, and the subjugation of Indigenous populations well before any European vessel reached the Caribbean.

The Canary Islands, an archipelago off the northwest African coast, were inhabited at the time of European contact by several distinct Indigenous Canarian peoples — including the Guanches, properly the Indigenous inhabitants of Tenerife specifically, alongside related but separate peoples on Gran Canaria, La Palma, and the other islands — whose ancestors are generally understood to have settled the islands from North Africa in antiquity and who had, by the fifteenth century, developed distinct societies on each of the several main islands, practicing agriculture, pastoralism, and, on some islands, forms of social and political organization that included chiefdoms and ritual authorities. European contact with the Canaries dated back intermittently to the fourteenth century, but sustained conquest began in earnest in 1402, pursued initially by privately organized Franco-Norman adventurers operating under Castilian royal authorization, and continuing intermittently, with considerable Indigenous Canarian resistance, for most of the century.

The conquest of the Canary Islands proceeded unevenly, island by island, rather than as a single unified campaign, and it involved genuine and often prolonged Indigenous Canarian military resistance. Gran Canaria was conquered by 1483, while La Palma fell in 1493 and Tenerife, the last and most fiercely defended island, only in 1496. The final phases of the Canarian conquest therefore overlapped the Granada War and continued well beyond Columbus's first Atlantic voyage — a reminder that Atlantic island conquest and early Caribbean expansion were partly contemporaneous processes rather than two neatly sequential episodes. Conquest was followed by the distribution of conquered land to Castilian conquerors and settlers, the introduction of European crops, livestock, and agricultural techniques, the establishment of colonial administrative and ecclesiastical institutions, and, over subsequent decades, the profound demographic transformation and substantial decline of the Indigenous Canarian population through violence, enslavement, and epidemic disease introduced by European contact — a pattern whose parallels to subsequent Caribbean history, examined in Chapter 27, are direct and instructive, even though the scale of Canarian conquest and demographic change was considerably smaller than what followed in the Americas.

The Canaries also became an important site of early plantation agriculture, particularly sugar cultivation introduced from Mediterranean and Atlantic precedents and increasingly worked by a combination of surviving Indigenous Canarian labor, imported European labor, and, over time, enslaved Africans brought via the developing Portuguese slave trade — foreshadowing, in miniature, the plantation labor systems that would later define much of Caribbean and Brazilian colonial economy, examined in Chapters 26, 39, and beyond.

Madeira and the Azores present a different but complementary case: both archipelagos were uninhabited at the time of Portuguese discovery and settlement, beginning in the 1420s and 1430s respectively, meaning that Portuguese colonization there did not involve conquering an existing Indigenous population but did involve establishing new colonial administrative structures, introducing European and, in Madeira's case, imported African enslaved labor, and developing highly profitable export agriculture — most notably Madeira's sugar industry, which by the mid-fifteenth century had made the island one of Europe's most important sugar producers and had established plantation-labor practices that Portuguese colonists would later extend to São Tomé and, eventually, to Brazil.

Alongside island colonization, this period saw substantial advances in ship design and navigational technique. The caravel, a relatively small, highly maneuverable vessel developed and refined chiefly by Portuguese shipbuilders, proved especially well suited to exploratory voyages against contrary winds and currents along unfamiliar coastlines, while portolan charts, the magnetic compass, the astrolabe, and increasingly systematic astronomical navigation techniques helped Iberian pilots estimate latitude, maintain courses, and venture progressively farther from familiar coastal waters with greater confidence, although reliable determination of longitude at sea remained an unsolved problem throughout this period and for long afterward. This accumulated navigational expertise, developed substantially through the practical experience of African coastal exploration and island colonization rather than through purely theoretical innovation, formed an essential technical precondition for the transatlantic voyages examined in Chapter 27.

The lesson of this section, and arguably of the chapter as a whole, is that important precedents and partial templates for later American colonization already existed in the eastern Atlantic before 1492. Conquest of an inhabited territory, negotiated or coerced submission of an Indigenous population, distribution of land to conquerors, introduction of European agriculture and livestock, establishment of colonial administration and church institutions, and the development of plantation agriculture increasingly dependent on coerced labor — all of these had already been rehearsed, however imperfectly and on a considerably smaller scale, in the Canary Islands and Madeira. Columbus's 1492 voyage connected those developing practices to a vastly larger and fundamentally different American context, where they would be adapted, combined, contested, and transformed rather than simply reproduced.

Section 25.12

Iberia in 1492: Preconditions, Choices, and Contingency

This chapter has traced several distinct but converging historical developments: the political plurality of the Iberian kingdoms and their gradual, partial coordination through dynastic marriage; the long, uneven medieval process of Christian territorial expansion and its culmination in the conquest of Granada; an intensifying royal and ecclesiastical drive toward religious uniformity, achieved substantially through coercion; a rich tradition of frontier warfare, negotiated conquest, and resettlement; extensive Mediterranean and Atlantic commercial networks financed significantly by foreign capital; and a full century of Atlantic island colonization and navigational development that had already produced, in miniature, most of the institutional elements later associated with American colonization. By early 1492, all of these developments had reached significant, in several cases decisive, milestones within a remarkably compressed span of months: Granada's surrender in January, the Alhambra Decree in March, and, in April, the signing of the Capitulations of Santa Fe authorizing Christopher Columbus's proposed westward voyage.

It is genuinely tempting, given this compressed timeline, to read these events as a single unfolding national project — to imagine that the conquest of Granada freed Isabella and Ferdinand's attention and resources specifically in order to fund Columbus, or that centuries of Reconquista naturally and inevitably culminated in transatlantic conquest. This chapter's concluding section asks students to resist that temptation while still taking seriously the genuine historical connections involved.

Christopher Columbus was a Genoese mariner, born around 1451, who had gained considerable practical maritime experience through voyages associated with Portuguese Atlantic trade, including likely participation in voyages along the West African coast and to Portuguese Atlantic islands, before developing his proposal to reach Asia by sailing west across the Atlantic rather than around Africa. Columbus's underlying geographical reasoning rested substantially on a significant underestimate of the Earth's circumference and a corresponding overestimate of how far east Asia extended, calculations that most contemporary Portuguese cosmographers and pilots, drawing on more accurate estimates, judged implausible. Columbus first presented his proposal to the Portuguese crown under King João II around 1484, where a royal commission of experienced navigators and cosmographers reviewed and, based substantially on more accurate geographical calculations, rejected it as impractical — a rejection that reflected genuine navigational expertise rather than mere shortsightedness, particularly given Portugal's own well-advanced and increasingly successful strategy of reaching Asia by rounding Africa, a route Bartolomeu Dias would successfully pioneer in 1488.

Rebuffed by Portugal, Columbus subsequently sought patronage elsewhere, including approaches to England, France, and Castile, where his proposal was initially reviewed and likewise rejected by a royal commission around 1490, before Isabella, following further consideration, further advocacy on Columbus's behalf by several influential courtiers and clergy, and the conclusion of the Granada War, ultimately agreed to sponsor the voyage in April 1492. The resulting Capitulations of Santa Fe granted Columbus the hereditary titles of Admiral, Viceroy, and Governor over any lands he might discover, along with a substantial share of resulting revenues. The expedition itself was financed through a mixed arrangement rather than by Columbus alone: the Castilian Crown made a significant financial commitment, the port town of Palos was ordered, under an existing royal judgment against the town, to supply two fully equipped caravels, and private financing associated with Columbus, Andalusian shipowning families such as the Pinzóns, and Genoese and other Italian commercial networks in Seville covered other portions of the expedition's cost. Historians continue to debate the precise proportions of royal, municipal, and private funding, but the documentary record makes clear that the voyage was a composite venture rather than a purely private or purely royal undertaking.

Several genuinely contingent factors, rather than any deep national destiny, shaped this specific outcome. Had Portugal's royal commission reached a different technical judgment about Columbus's geographical calculations, Portugal, not Castile, might have sponsored a westward voyage, and Portuguese rather than Castilian claims might have shaped the subsequent history of the Caribbean and much of the Americas. Had the Granada War concluded differently, or dragged on longer, Isabella's attention and Castile's finances might have remained unavailable for Columbus's proposal for years, or indefinitely. Had Isabella's advisors, several of whom actively lobbied on Columbus's behalf during his years of repeated rejection and disappointment at the Castilian court, not intervened when they did, Columbus might have secured patronage from France or England instead, or not at all. None of the developments this chapter has examined — not the union of Castile and Aragon, not the conquest of Granada, not Iberian religious policy, not accumulated frontier institutions, not Mediterranean and Atlantic commerce, not a century of island colonization and navigational development — made Columbus's specific 1492 voyage, under Castilian sponsorship, an inevitable outcome.

What these developments did accomplish, considered together, was to create a Castilian and broader Iberian world unusually well equipped, institutionally, financially, and culturally, to respond to whatever opportunity such a voyage might present, had it succeeded. Castile possessed, by 1492, a monarchy with growing fiscal and administrative capacity, recent and extensive experience organizing conquest and resettlement, a religious and political culture increasingly oriented toward missionary and territorial expansion, ready access to Genoese and other foreign capital and expertise, a maritime tradition informed by decades of Atlantic navigation and island colonization, and a substantial population of veteran soldiers and ambitious adventurers recently freed from the concluded Granada campaign and eager for further opportunities of conquest and reward. These were the preconditions. What followed from Columbus's 1492 voyage — examined beginning in Chapter 27 — was not their predetermined culmination but their application, adaptation, and transformation under circumstances that no one in Iberia, in 1492, could have anticipated or planned for.

Historical Vocabulary
Composite Monarchy

A composite monarchy is a political structure in which a single ruling dynasty or monarch governs several distinct territories, each of which retains its own laws, institutions, and, frequently, representative assemblies, rather than being administratively unified into a single centralized state. The Crown of Aragon, encompassing Aragon proper, Catalonia, Valencia, and Mediterranean territories including Sicily, exemplifies this pattern: Ferdinand ruled as king of each constituent territory separately, under separate legal traditions, rather than as the sovereign of a single unified Aragonese nation.

The concept of composite monarchy helps students understand why the 1469 marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand did not create a unified Spanish state. Castile and Aragon functioned, after their union, as a composite monarchy at an even higher level: two separate crowns, each itself internally composite in Aragon's case, joined through the shared person of their monarchs rather than through unified institutions. This pattern would persist for centuries, and understanding it helps explain why later Spanish American colonial administration developed distinct institutional arrangements — including separate councils — for Castilian and, more rarely, Aragonese affairs.

Primary Source Window

The Capitulations of Granada, 1491

Creators: Negotiated between representatives of Isabella and Ferdinand and representatives of Muhammad XII (Boabdil) · Date: Signed November 25, 1491; implemented with the surrender of the city of Granada on January 2, 1492 · Type: Negotiated treaty of surrender (capitulación)

Context: Facing an increasingly untenable military position after nearly a decade of war and the loss of most Nasrid territory outside the city of Granada itself, Muhammad XII negotiated surrender terms rather than continuing to resist a siege many of his advisors judged unwinnable. The resulting capitulations followed a well-established Iberian tradition of negotiated conquest, examined in Section 25.9, rather than representing an unusual act of Castilian and Aragonese leniency.

Rather than reproducing the lengthy treaty text verbatim, students should understand its central provisions in summary form: Granada's Muslim population was promised continued free practice of Islam, use of their own mosques, continued application of Islamic law and customary practice in matters among themselves, retention of personal property, freedom from new taxation beyond what had previously been owed, and the right of any who wished to emigrate to North Africa to do so freely, with transport provided, within a specified period. Muhammad XII himself received a grant of territory and revenue in the Alpujarras mountains in exchange for his surrender.

Analysis: The capitulations illustrate the same tradition of negotiated conquest examined in Section 25.9: a defeated population's rights are formally guaranteed in exchange for peaceful submission, an arrangement that served the immediate interests of both parties by avoiding costly further warfare. Yet the subsequent history examined in Section 25.8 — the steady erosion of these promises over the following decade, culminating eventually in forced conversion — illustrates how such negotiated guarantees, however binding or advantageous they appeared at the moment of signing, could be reinterpreted, delayed in implementation, or abandoned outright once the immediate military necessity that had produced them no longer applied. This pattern of negotiated promise followed by later erosion recurs, in different form, in later chapters examining Spanish treatment of Indigenous and enslaved populations in the Americas.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might both Isabella and Ferdinand and Muhammad XII have preferred a negotiated surrender to continued warfare in 1491?
  2. What specific protections did the capitulations promise Granada's Muslim population, and why might these promises have been comparatively generous by the standards of the time?
  3. Why did these promised protections erode over the following decade, and what does that erosion suggest about the relationship between negotiated treaties and long-term political intentions?

Historical Debate

What Did the Reconquista Mean?

The question: Was the medieval history of Christian territorial expansion in Iberia a single, coherent, religiously driven national project — a genuine "Reconquista" — or is that framing substantially a later political and historiographical construction imposed on a messier, more contingent, and more religiously and politically complicated medieval reality?

Interpretation A — The Reconquista reflects genuine medieval religious and political coherence: Proponents of this view note that medieval Christian rulers, chroniclers, and the papacy itself frequently described the expansion of Christian territory in explicitly religious and providential terms, granted crusading indulgences and privileges to participants, and organized military orders specifically around the goal of extending Christian rule at the expense of Muslim states. On this view, however uneven and interrupted the actual military campaigns were, medieval Iberian Christians themselves understood their long struggle as a meaningful, religiously coherent project, and treating this self-understanding as merely retrospective invention risks underestimating genuine medieval religious motivation.
Interpretation B — "Reconquista" is substantially a later national and religious construction: Critics of the traditional framing emphasize that medieval Christian kingdoms fought one another as frequently as they fought Muslim states, that alliances regularly crossed religious lines, that centuries of tribute payment, diplomacy, and negotiated coexistence occurred alongside warfare, and that the term "Reconquista" itself gained its modern, unified national meaning substantially through nineteenth-century Spanish nationalist historiography and, later, through its prominent use in the political rhetoric of the twentieth-century Franco dictatorship, which invoked the Reconquista explicitly to legitimize its own nationalist and Catholic self-image. On this view, applying a unified "Reconquista" narrative to the entire 711–1492 period substantially distorts the genuine complexity, contingency, and cross-confessional interaction of medieval Iberian history.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying medieval record — chronicles, treaties, land grants, tribute agreements, and military campaign records — but differ in how much unifying coherence and self-conscious religious purpose to attribute to a process that in practice extended across many centuries, dozens of rulers, and shifting political circumstances.

Why it matters: This textbook uses "Reconquista" as a conventional label because it remains the standard term in most scholarship and general usage, but it consistently qualifies the term by emphasizing the medieval period's genuine political fragmentation, cross-confessional alliance, and negotiated coexistence, precisely to avoid the anachronistic and politically loaded unified-national-crusade narrative that later political movements constructed from this history.

Student task: Using evidence from Section 25.2 and Section 25.7, construct an argument for each interpretation, then explain which position you find more persuasive and why, paying particular attention to how later political uses of the term "Reconquista" might affect how historians today should use it.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the 1490s: Perspectives on a Changing Iberia

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Castilian noble veteran of the Granada War: A decade of campaigning had brought military experience, expectation of reward in land or office, and a sense that royal service against non-Christian populations was both a religious duty and a path to advancement — an outlook that, with the war concluded, left many such veterans looking for new opportunities for service and reward.
From the perspective of a Granadan Muslim family in early 1492: The negotiated capitulations promised continued practice of Islam, retention of property, and legal autonomy, offering genuine, if uncertain, hope of a tolerable future under Christian rule — hope that would be tested, and substantially betrayed, by the erosion of these promises over the following decade.
From the perspective of a Jewish family in Castile facing the March 1492 Alhambra Decree: Generations of established life, property, and community in Castile confronted a sudden choice between conversion to Christianity, likely under continued suspicion from the Inquisition, or exile to Portugal, North Africa, or elsewhere, carrying only what could be transported within the decree's short timeframe.
From the perspective of a Genoese merchant-financier active in Seville or Lisbon: Iberian Atlantic ventures, whether Portuguese African trade or prospective Castilian westward exploration, represented promising opportunities for capital investment, particularly given Genoa's own difficult commercial competition with Venice in eastern Mediterranean markets, making Iberian ports increasingly attractive outlets for Genoese trade and financial expertise.
From the perspective of a Portuguese royal cosmographer reviewing Columbus's proposal around 1484: Established Portuguese navigational and geographical expertise, developed through decades of practical African exploration, indicated that Columbus's calculations of the distance to Asia were almost certainly too optimistic, making his proposal appear an unnecessary risk when Portugal's own African route toward Asia was already showing real promise.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Frontier Iberia to Atlantic Iberia

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from a fragmented peninsula of several Christian kingdoms and one Muslim emirate toward, by 1492, dynastic coordination between Castile and Aragon and the elimination of independent Muslim rule
  • Religious policy shifted from centuries of uneven, often precarious coexistence among Christians, Muslims, and Jews toward an increasingly coercive drive for uniform Christian conformity
  • Iberian geographic orientation broadened from a chiefly Mediterranean and internal peninsular focus toward increasing Atlantic engagement, driven substantially by Portugal and, by the 1490s, increasingly by Castile as well
  • Military experience shifted from centuries of internal frontier warfare toward its extension, via island colonization, into a genuinely Atlantic and, after 1492, American context

What Stayed the Same

  • Castile and Aragon remained institutionally separate crowns even after 1469, a distinctness that would persist for generations
  • The practice of negotiated conquest followed by later erosion of promised terms recurred across the Granada capitulations and, as later chapters show, American conquest settlements
  • Genoese and other Italian financial and commercial involvement remained a constant feature of Iberian Atlantic ventures throughout this period
  • The expectation that successful military and exploratory service merited reward in land, office, or revenue rights persisted from medieval frontier warfare directly into American conquest

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine transformation of Iberian political and religious life across this period and the underlying continuity of key institutions and expectations helps students understand why American colonization drew so heavily on Iberian precedent while still producing genuinely new colonial institutions shaped by unprecedented American circumstances.

Cause and Effect

Why Iberian Atlantic Expansion Accelerated in the Fifteenth Century

Long-term causes

Centuries of accumulated frontier warfare experience, negotiated conquest practice, and Mediterranean commercial engagement, combined with Iberia's Atlantic-facing geography and its established participation in wider European and Mediterranean financial networks.

Short-term causes

Portugal's 1415 capture of Ceuta and subsequent royal patronage of African exploration; growing Genoese interest in Atlantic ventures amid difficult Mediterranean competition with Venice; advances in ship design and navigation; and the political consolidation achieved through the 1469 Castilian-Aragonese dynastic union and the concluding Granada War.

Effect

By the 1480s and 1490s, Portugal possessed an advanced, systematically pursued African and Atlantic navigational program, while Castile, freed from the Granada War and newly coordinated with Aragon, possessed the fiscal capacity, military manpower, and religious motivation to sponsor Columbus's westward voyage.

Historical significance

This accumulated capacity did not make any specific voyage or outcome inevitable, but it ensured that whichever Iberian kingdom acted first would do so with substantial institutional, financial, and military resources already in place, resources that shaped the specific, and specifically coercive, form that subsequent American conquest and colonization would take.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Iberian Atlantic expansion; dynastic politics, religious motivation, commercial competition, technological development, and individual initiative all interacted, and removing any single factor could plausibly have changed the specific outcome without necessarily preventing some form of European Atlantic expansion altogether.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Iberian Households on the Eve of 1492

The political and military history this chapter has emphasized should not obscure the fact that most Iberians in 1492 experienced these developments through the texture of ordinary life rather than through royal courts and battlefields. A Castilian wool-producing household in the Meseta organized its year around the seasonal migration of merino flocks managed through the Mesta, selling wool to merchants who would ship it north to Flemish and English textile centers, and experiencing royal taxation and occasional military levies as the most direct connections to the larger political world described in this chapter.

A Seville merchant household, by contrast, lived amid the increasingly cosmopolitan commercial world of Andalusia's principal port, doing business with Genoese and Florentine trading houses, handling goods arriving from Mediterranean, Atlantic, and increasingly West African trade, and hearing, well before most of the rest of Castile, rumors and reports of Portuguese voyages, African gold, and new Atlantic possibilities. A Lisbon maritime household, whose men might serve as sailors, pilots, or shipbuilders in Portugal's royally sponsored African voyages, experienced the Atlantic turn examined in Section 25.5 as a matter of direct occupational involvement, risk, and, for some, real material opportunity through royal service and commercial venture.

A Granadan Muslim household in the city of Granada itself, in the weeks before January 1492, faced a far more uncertain future: continued residence in a city about to change rulers, negotiated promises of religious and legal autonomy whose durability could not yet be known, and the difficult individual choice, available to some under the capitulations, of emigration to North Africa rather than remaining under new Christian rule. These four households, each experiencing the same broad historical moment through radically different daily circumstances, illustrate why this textbook consistently emphasizes social and regional variation rather than treating "Iberia in 1492" as a single uniform experience.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Iberian Peninsula and the eastern Atlantic world immediately before the sustained American expansion that Volume II's remaining chapters will trace in detail. It has insisted, from the outset, that "Spain" is an anachronistic and misleading label for the late fifteenth century, replacing it instead with a careful account of the separate crowns of Castile and Aragon, the independent kingdom of Portugal, the small kingdom of Navarre, and the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, each possessing distinct institutions, laws, and historical trajectories.

Students have examined the long, uneven medieval process conventionally called the Reconquista, learning to distinguish its genuine historical substance — centuries of frontier warfare, negotiated conquest, and shifting political fortune — from the later, more unified national narrative that nineteenth-century and twentieth-century political movements constructed around it. They have examined Castile's internal structure of monarchy, nobility, towns, and the Cortes, and Aragon's distinctive composite-monarchy structure spanning Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, and Mediterranean territories, learning why these institutional differences persisted even after the 1469 dynastic union of Isabella and Ferdinand.

The chapter has traced Portugal's independent and, in important respects, more advanced Atlantic and African expansion, from the 1415 capture of Ceuta through decades of West African voyages and the colonization of Madeira and the Azores, establishing Portugal as the European kingdom best positioned, by the 1480s, to evaluate — and ultimately reject — Columbus's proposal. It has examined the Granada War of 1482–1492 as a major state-building and military mobilization in its own right, and it has traced the accelerating drive toward religious uniformity that produced the 1478 establishment of the Spanish Inquisition and the March 1492 expulsion of Jews from Castile and Aragon, a pattern of negotiated accommodation giving way to coercion that would recur, in different form, in later Spanish treatment of Indigenous and enslaved populations in the Americas.

Students have examined the frontier military and administrative institutions developed across centuries of Iberian warfare — negotiated surrender, resettlement, and the expectation of land and reward for military service — as adaptable precedents, though not exact templates, for later American conquest. They have examined Iberia's embedded position within wider Mediterranean and Atlantic commercial networks, financed substantially by Genoese and other Italian capital, and they have examined the Canary Islands, Madeira, and the Azores as the closest and most direct institutional precedents for American colonization, where conquest of an inhabited Indigenous population, in the Canaries' case, or settlement of previously uninhabited land, in Madeira's and the Azores's cases, had already produced plantation agriculture, colonial administration, and coerced labor systems well before 1492.

Finally, the chapter has examined the specific, genuinely contingent sequence of events that led Isabella and Ferdinand, rather than the Portuguese crown or any other European monarchy, to sponsor Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage, emphasizing that the near-simultaneous conclusion of the Granada War, the expulsion of the Jews, and the authorization of Columbus's voyage reflected converging circumstances and individual choices rather than a single unified national project moving inevitably toward American empire.

These themes — political plurality, religious coercion following negotiated accommodation, adaptable frontier institutions, embedded commercial networks, and Atlantic island precedent, all considered alongside genuine historical contingency — form the essential Iberian counterpart to the Indigenous American world examined throughout Volume I. Chapter 26 now turns to the other side of the Atlantic triangle this volume must establish before conquest can be responsibly narrated: West Africa, Portuguese expansion along its coast, and the developing system of Atlantic slavery and commerce that would soon connect Africa, Iberia, and the Americas into a single, tragic, and enduringly consequential historical system.

Key Terms

Reconquista
The conventional term for the long, uneven medieval process by which Christian kingdoms in Iberia gradually extended control over territory previously ruled by Muslim states; a useful but potentially misleading label if treated as a single unified national campaign.
Composite monarchy
A political structure in which a single dynasty governs several distinct territories, each retaining its own laws and institutions, exemplified by the Crown of Aragon.
Crown of Castile
The largest Iberian kingdom by territory and population in the late fifteenth century, whose institutions became the principal base for early Spanish American colonization.
Crown of Aragon
A composite monarchy uniting Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, and Mediterranean territories, oriented chiefly toward Mediterranean rather than Atlantic affairs.
Cortes
Representative assemblies in Castile and the territories of the Crown of Aragon, convened chiefly to authorize extraordinary taxation.
Nasrid dynasty
The ruling dynasty of the Emirate of Granada from 1238 until its 1492 surrender.
Capitulation
A negotiated treaty of surrender specifying terms under which a defeated population would submit to new rule, as in the Capitulations of Granada.
Converso
In late-medieval Iberia, especially a Jew who converted to Christianity, or a descendant of Jewish converts; conversos became major targets of suspicion and Inquisitorial investigation.
Morisco
A baptized Christian of Muslim ancestry, especially the communities created by forced or coerced conversion policies following the conquest of Granada.
Alhambra Decree
The March 1492 royal edict ordering Jews in Castile and Aragon who refused conversion to leave the kingdoms.
Spanish Inquisition
A Castilian royal institution established in 1478 to investigate and prosecute conversos suspected of secretly practicing Judaism.
Military order
A religious-military brotherhood, such as the Orders of Santiago, Calatrava, or Alcántara, combining monastic discipline with frontier warfare and territorial administration.
Fuero
A body of law or customary privilege granted to a particular territory, town, or population in medieval and early modern Iberia.
Repoblación
The medieval Iberian practice of resettling conquered territory through royal grants of land and privilege intended to attract Christian settlers.
Caravel
A small, highly maneuverable ship type developed and refined chiefly by Portuguese shipbuilders, well suited to Atlantic exploration.
Portolan chart
A type of navigational chart, based on compass bearings and coastal observation, used by Mediterranean and Atlantic pilots.
Mesta
The powerful Castilian sheep-owners' guild controlling migratory grazing routes and the wool export economy.
Guanche
Properly, the Indigenous inhabitants of Tenerife specifically; sometimes used more loosely, though imprecisely, for Indigenous Canary Islanders generally. Guanche and related Indigenous Canarian resistance and eventual subjugation prefigured, on a smaller scale, later American conquest.
Dynastic union
The joining of two or more crowns through marriage or inheritance, without necessarily unifying their separate institutions, as in the 1469 marriage of Isabella and Ferdinand.
Papal bull
A formal papal decree, several of which addressed Portuguese and Castilian rights of exploration and conquest in the Atlantic during the fifteenth century.
Contingency
The historical principle that outcomes depend on specific, non-inevitable circumstances and choices rather than predetermined destiny; central to this chapter's treatment of Columbus's 1492 voyage.
Precondition
A circumstance or development that makes a later outcome possible without making it certain, a distinction this chapter applies throughout to Iberian history before 1492.
Capitulations of Santa Fe
The April 1492 agreement between Isabella, Ferdinand, and Columbus authorizing his westward voyage and granting him hereditary titles and revenue rights.
Taifa kingdoms
The smaller successor states into which the Caliphate of Córdoba fragmented in the eleventh century, a period of Iberian Muslim political division.
Mudéjar
A Muslim living under Christian rule in medieval Iberia, typically retaining, for a time, legal and religious autonomy under negotiated terms.
Sephardic Jews
Jews of Iberian origin and descent, including those who formed new diaspora communities following the 1492 expulsion.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity — such as "Spain" as a unified nation-state — to an earlier period in which it did not yet apply in that sense.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look closely at a world usually rushed past on the way to Columbus: the plural, contested, evolving kingdoms of the late-medieval Iberian Peninsula and their gradually deepening engagement with the Atlantic Ocean. Doing so matters for the same reason Volume I insisted on treating Indigenous American civilizations on their own terms rather than as a passive prelude to conquest. Neither side of the Atlantic encounter that Volume II will now trace was standing still, simple, or unified in 1492. Both were dynamic, internally diverse historical worlds whose peoples could not have anticipated how their respective trajectories were about to intersect.

The habits of mind this chapter has modeled — distinguishing Castile from Aragon from Portugal, distinguishing genuine medieval religious motivation from later nationalist historiographical construction, distinguishing negotiated accommodation from its subsequent coercive erosion, and distinguishing historical precondition from historical inevitability — will recur throughout the remainder of this volume. When later chapters examine the conquest of Tenochtitlan, the fall of the Inca, or the establishment of colonial institutions across Spanish and Portuguese America, students should be able to recognize the Iberian institutional vocabulary — capitulation, encomienda, cabildo, repartimiento — this chapter has introduced, while remaining alert to how thoroughly American circumstances transformed that vocabulary's practical meaning.

Above all, this chapter has tried to model intellectual honesty about contingency. It would be simpler, and in some ways more dramatically satisfying, to tell the story of 1492 as the destined culmination of eight centuries of Iberian history — a Reconquista that flowed naturally into a Conquista. But that story would not be true, and this textbook's commitment to historical accuracy requires resisting simpler, more satisfying narratives when the evidence does not support them. Iberia in 1492 possessed real, substantial preconditions for Atlantic expansion. What its people did with those preconditions — and what the peoples of the Americas, examined throughout Volume I, would experience as a result — remained, in early 1492, genuinely undetermined.

Chapter 25 has asked what kind of world produced the ships that sailed from Palos in August 1492. Chapter 26 now turns to West Africa and the developing Atlantic slave trade, the third essential piece of background Volume II requires before Chapter 27 can finally narrate the Caribbean encounter itself — an encounter whose consequences, for both Iberia and the Indigenous Americas, would prove more transformative, and more devastating, than anyone standing in Granada or Palos in 1492 could have foreseen.
