Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the End of Spanish Empire in the Americas

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: 1 January 1899

On 1 January 1899 the Spanish flag came down in Havana, but Cuba was not governed by Cubans. The island passed under U.S. military occupation, while Puerto Rico moved from Spanish colonial rule to American territorial administration.

Cubans had fought for independence since 1868, and Puerto Ricans had risen at Lares in the same year. Spain had lost sovereignty, but neither island became independent in the way the new republics of the 1820s had. This chapter explains why.

It follows the Ten Years' War, abolition in Puerto Rico in 1873 and in Cuba in 1886, the work of José Martí, the war of 1895, the intervention of 1898, and the different settlements for the two islands. It stresses that U.S. entry in 1898 did not originate Cuba's independence struggle and that Puerto Rican history cannot be reduced to the American invasion.

Evidence includes statutes and treaties, proclamations and manifestos, newspapers from exile communities, court records of enslaved people's petitions, and military reports. Most were produced by officials or political leaders; the voices of the enslaved and of rural women survive mainly in fragments.

The chapter completes Part XIV. It connects to Chapter 66 on Brazilian abolition and anticipates Chapter 70 on the longer history of U.S. influence in the hemisphere.

Division 1 · The Spanish Caribbean and the First Independence Struggles
Section 67.1

Cuba, Puerto Rico, Slavery, and Spanish Colonial Rule in the Nineteenth Century

By the 1850s Cuba and Puerto Rico were the last substantial Spanish possessions in the Americas, and Spain held them for reasons mainland Spanish America had not supplied: sugar, slavery, strategic position, and loyalist elites who feared both abolition and race war. Cuba's western plantation districts around Havana and Matanzas made it one of the world's great sugar producers, with an enslaved population on the order of 350,000–400,000 around 1860, alongside tobacco districts and a large commercial sector. Puerto Rico had a smaller slave economy, perhaps 40,000 enslaved people at its peak, and combined sugar on the coast with coffee and tobacco farming in the interior, where free smallholders, many of mixed ancestry, were numerous.

Both islands were governed by captains-general with extraordinary powers, justified as necessary for order and invoked to suppress dissent. Representation in the Spanish Cortes was intermittent and restricted; peninsular-born officials and merchants held privileged positions. Free people of color, large in both societies, faced legal and social limits. Historians draw on censuses, plantation records, notarial and court files, and colonial correspondence, sources that record the enslaved mainly through the eyes of those who owned and counted them.

Spain's ability to retain the two islands after the mainland revolutions was not simply a matter of geography or an undisturbed loyalty to the crown. The islands contained powerful constituencies that calculated the likely costs of separation differently. In Cuba, the expansion of export sugar depended on mills, imported equipment, merchant credit, slaveholding, and naval connections to markets abroad. Political elites in Havana could oppose particular taxes or appointments while fearing a war that might threaten plantation property. The memory of the Haitian Revolution was frequently invoked by colonial officials and slaveholders to defend coercive order; it must be understood as a political argument, not evidence that emancipation would necessarily generate similar events.

The nineteenth-century expansion of Cuban sugar altered the island's social landscape. Improved milling, steam engines, rail connections and expanding cane acreage encouraged the concentration of capital in western districts, particularly Havana, Matanzas and neighboring regions. Sugar and tobacco moved through different labor organizations, however: tobacco cultivation and the cigar trade could provide opportunities for free labor, while western sugar estates remained major slaveholding employers. Eastern Cuba contained plantations but also livestock districts, towns, smaller farms and different networks of landownership. These contrasts help explain the uneven geographical support for the insurgencies of 1868 and 1895 without reducing political choices to a simple economic map.

Puerto Rico followed a related but not identical path. Sugar production occupied coastal plains, while coffee expanded in the central and western highlands, where small and medium producers coexisted with larger estates. The island had its own patterns of enslavement, manumission, free labor, tenancy and coerced work; slavery was central to some districts but was less numerically dominant than in Cuba. Regulations intended to control rural labor, including the mid-century system of compulsory labor documentation, could constrain poor free people as well as the enslaved. For many islanders, the issue was therefore broader than constitutional allegiance: it concerned access to land, local authority, taxes, labor obligations, and the ability to challenge officials without retaliation.

The crown preserved military government through captains-general who possessed extensive emergency powers. Officials distinguished between persons born in Spain and those born in the colonies, while race, property, sex and literacy shaped practical access to political influence. Some merchants benefited from preferential trade and the colonial administrative system; other residents resented tariffs, monopolies and the concentration of offices in peninsular hands. Neither society can be divided neatly into Spaniards versus patriots. Local-born supporters of Spanish rule, mixed families, free Black professionals, enslaved laborers, and immigrants all made decisions under differing constraints. Colonial censuses offer important scale but cannot explain those decisions by themselves.

Historical Vocabulary
Grito

A grito ("cry") is a proclamation of rebellion. The Grito de Lares (1868) and the Grito de Yara (1868) are the founding gestures of Puerto Rican and Cuban insurgency. The term should not be confused with a single battle; each grito began a longer process.

Historical Vocabulary
Patronato

The patronato was Cuba's 1880–1886 transitional labor regime, in which former slaves (patrocinados) owed labor to former owners. It was not full freedom, though it differed from slavery in that patrocinados gained limited legal rights.

Section 67.2

Reformism, Abolitionism, Annexationism, and the Growth of Anticolonial Opposition

Opposition took several forms. Reformists sought representation in Madrid, colonial autonomy, and an end to the slave trade. Some wealthy Cuban slaveholders, anxious about British pressure on slavery and Spanish instability, favored annexation to the United States, where slavery was protected; the filibustering expeditions of Narciso López (1850–1851) drew on that current. Annexationism differed from independence and was never the aim of the later revolutionary mainstream.

Puerto Rican radicals were more often abolitionist. Ramón Emeterio Betances and Segundo Ruiz Belvis, both of whom had studied in Europe, joined Eugenio María de Hostos in advocating abolition and independence, and Hostos envisioned a confederation of the Antilles. Spain convened a reform commission in Madrid in 1866–1867, but its failure to deliver reforms, together with new taxes and repression, pushed many reformers toward revolt.

The goals grouped under reformism were themselves diverse. Assimilationists sought the rights of ordinary Spanish provinces and dependable representation in the Cortes. Autonomists wanted elected insular institutions, control over local budgets and policy, and a substantial reduction in the captain-general's arbitrary powers while retaining a constitutional relationship with Spain. Separatists judged these proposals too weak or too late. Abolitionists might be found in any of these camps, but defenders of slavery could also advocate constitutional changes that protected their property. In Cuba especially, proposals for annexation to the United States were tied in the 1840s and 1850s to the ambitions of some slaveholders and to southern American expansionism; not all annexationists agreed with one another, and annexation was never synonymous with the later independence program.

The Cuban exile Narciso López led failed armed expeditions in 1850 and 1851, drawing on networks in the United States and on opposition to Spanish control. His ventures exposed the political risks of treating outside support as a substitute for broad organization inside the island. Spanish authorities used such attempts to justify surveillance and repression. Reform advocates meanwhile pushed for fiscal equality, a less discriminatory administration and changes in the slave trade. Madrid sometimes called consultative meetings, including the reform discussions of 1866–1867, but the gulf between raised expectations and limited concessions intensified distrust rather than settling political grievances.

In Puerto Rico, Ramón Emeterio Betances and Segundo Ruiz Belvis treated enslavement and political subordination as mutually reinforcing. Betances used medicine, public writing and organizing; Ruiz Belvis worked against bondage and in political networks before his death. Eugenio María de Hostos linked education, civic rights and eventual Caribbean federation to anticolonial reform. Their ideals were not universally accepted on the island. Merchants and conservative landowners often preferred the security of Spanish institutions, and many moderate reformers remained wary of an uprising that might unleash military retaliation or jeopardize trade.

Women played important roles in correspondence, clandestine networks, fund-raising, households and public expression, even when formal politics excluded them. The poetry and activism of Lola Rodríguez de Tió would become especially important in Puerto Rican nationalist memory. Workers and poor farmers were not merely recruited followers of professional revolutionaries: their grievances about wages, taxes, compulsory labor and social status gave these movements different meanings at the local level. The historian must therefore ask both what the manifesto demanded and what each class or community expected political change to accomplish.

Section 67.3

The Grito de Lares and Puerto Rico's Independence Movement, 1868

Betances, working from exile in the Dominican Republic and the Caribbean, helped organize a revolutionary committee, with Ruiz Belvis, whose death in Chile in 1867 deprived the movement of a key figure. Manuel Rojas led the planned rising in the coffee highlands, where debt, forced-labor regulations, and Spanish officialdom had made enemies of small farmers, laborers, and some slaveholders. On 23 September 1868, insurgents took the town of Lares, proclaimed a Republic of Puerto Rico, and promised abolition. Spanish detection of the plot had already disrupted coordination; a day later, the rebels failed at San Sebastián, and the movement collapsed within days.

Military failure did not end its significance. Spanish authorities imprisoned many participants, though amnesties followed, and the Grito became the foundational event of Puerto Rican independence memory, commemorated each September. It must be understood as a Puerto Rican rising, planned independently of Cuba's, and one that linked emancipation to sovereignty.

The immediate setting of Lares was a countryside marked by unequal relations between merchants, producers, laborers and colonial authority. Credit obligations could bind small cultivators to merchants; land and labor rules restricted mobility, and political dissent was met with coercive policing. Revolutionary cells emerged in the west of the island and communicated with expatriate organizers. Betances and his associates prepared proclamations that demanded the abolition of slavery and civil liberties. Their political vision exceeded the removal of a single governor: it called for a republic whose legitimacy would rest on rights denied under colonial rule. Yet the movement lacked the logistics and weapons needed to guarantee that scattered cells would rise together.

The insurrection had been scheduled for later in September. Once officials uncovered preparations, local rebels advanced their plans. Several hundred people assembled near the Rojas family property and entered Lares during the night of September 23. They seized municipal authority, proclaimed a republic, and freed enslaved participants. Francisco Ramírez was associated with the revolutionary government, while Manuel Rojas became a key military leader. They then tried to advance on San Sebastián del Pepino, where local Spanish forces proved capable of resisting and the rebels withdrew. The brevity of the campaign should not obscure the scale of the political risks undertaken by participants who could face imprisonment, execution, or confiscation.

The repression produced arrests and harsh sentences. A subsequent amnesty allowed many prisoners to leave custody, but government surveillance and the disruption of local networks continued. One widely circulated Spanish account written after the uprising was openly hostile to the rebels, and thus supplies information about colonial fears and methods of depiction as well as about events. The Library of Congress's documentary account records secret cells involving landowners, merchants, professionals, peasants and enslaved people; this composition warns against treating Lares as either only an elite conspiracy or a uniform peasant revolt. The precise social motivations of participants require separate evidence.

Lares became a political memory that changed across generations. Independence advocates invoked the revolutionary flag, the proclaimed republic and Betances's abolitionist language; defenders of Spanish rule emphasized disorder and the rebellion's failure. The question for students is not whether the revolt succeeded militarily—it did not—but why a short rebellion could furnish a durable language of self-determination. Its simultaneous appearance with Cuban rebellion in 1868 reveals shared problems of empire without implying a single unified command or an identical island history.

Section 67.4

The Grito de Yara and the Beginning of Cuba's Ten Years' War, 1868

On 10 October 1868, Carlos Manuel de Céspedes, a lawyer and planter, proclaimed rebellion at his estate, La Demajagua, near Yara in eastern Cuba, and freed the enslaved people he held, inviting them to join. Insurgents took Bayamo days later. Eastern Cuba's landowners were less tied to the great sugar plantations than their western counterparts, were resentful of Spanish taxation and of Havana's dominance, and had smaller slave populations. The western elite, whose wealth rested on enslaved labor, largely stayed loyal for fear of social upheaval.

The new army, composed of landowners, free men of color, and formerly enslaved men, came to be known by the name mambí, a label whose etymology remains disputed. Emancipation was proclaimed locally and unevenly; its general application remained a bitter political question.

Céspedes's proclamation at La Demajagua converted years of eastern Cuban frustration into open armed resistance. The description of a plantation owner freeing those he held is important, but the gesture should not be romanticized as though one act ended the institution across the insurgent zone. Enslaved people responded in differing ways, and commanders confronted arguments over whether abolition should be immediate, conditional on military service, or subject to compensation. The first months of revolt brought rapid local successes, including the insurgent occupation of Bayamo, which became an important revolutionary center. Spanish reinforcements later forced the rebels to retreat, and insurgents burned the city rather than leave all its resources to the colonial army.

The geography of eastern Cuba created opportunities for mobile warfare. Hills, wooded areas and agricultural districts offered places to hide and obtain food, while Spanish control of main towns and sea lanes limited the rebels' access to arms. Some proprietors wanted relief from taxes and political exclusion; poor rural participants sought land, family security, or escape from coercive labor. Men of African descent joined military units, and Afro-Cuban officers rose through experience rather than hereditary rank, although social hierarchies and prejudice persisted inside revolutionary institutions. The revolution's ambition to establish a racially inclusive nation was therefore a practical question of recruitment and command as well as a public ideal.

The label *mambí* came to identify the Cuban independence fighter. Historians have proposed several origins, including links to the Dominican conflicts and African-language or colloquial traditions; the Library of Congress notes that no single origin is established. What matters is that a term once used disparagingly became an emblem of patriotism. Its evolving meaning is evidence of how military identities were made in wartime, not proof of one origin story. Combatants' own letters and newspaper accounts should be considered alongside later nationalist recollections when reconstructing their experience.

The start of the Ten Years' War also sharpened the differences between Cuba and Puerto Rico. Spanish authorities could draw forces and revenues from the imperial system against a rebellion whose early geographical center lay far from Havana's richest plantations. Western Cuban elites feared the loss of enslaved property and the consequences of a prolonged social war; some remained committed to Spain while others favored cautious reform. The difficulty of unifying these interests would shape the insurgent constitution of 1869 and the subsequent struggle between civilian and military authority.

Section 67.5

The Ten Years' War, 1868–1878: The Cuban Republic in Arms and Spanish Counterinsurgency

In April 1869 insurgent representatives met at Guáimaro and adopted a constitution creating a republic in arms, with Céspedes as president and a legislature that soon quarreled with the military command. Ignacio Agramonte led cavalry in Camagüey, the Dominican-born veteran Máximo Gómez adopted rapid, destructive raiding, and Antonio Maceo, an Afro-Cuban from Santiago, rose from the ranks. Spain sent large reinforcements, relied on colonial volunteer militias, built fortified lines (trochas), executed prisoners, and confiscated property.

Divisions over strategy, regional rivalries, racial anxieties, and the legislature's deposition of Céspedes in 1873 weakened the insurgents; Agramonte died in 1873 and Céspedes in 1874. The war never decisively reached the western sugar heartland, and by the late 1870s exhaustion on both sides opened the way for negotiation.

At Guáimaro in April 1869, the insurgents attempted to give their war a constitutional framework. Delegates from different rebellious districts affirmed independence, created a chamber of representatives, and placed a president above an army commanded by experienced officers. The arrangement expressed the fear that military necessity might produce dictatorship, but the legislature's intervention in campaigns could also constrain commanders who needed to move quickly. These were not abstract arguments. They determined who could command troops, allocate scarce ammunition, negotiate with foreigners, and speak for communities that had committed themselves to rebellion. Political legitimacy and military effectiveness proved difficult to reconcile during a sustained guerrilla war.

The insurgents faced a much larger Spanish military establishment, reinforced by volunteers loyal to colonial authority. Colonial troops occupied towns, guarded transport corridors, destroyed supplies and punished suspected collaborators. Rebels adopted mobility, sabotage and irregular fighting, drawing food and information from countryside networks. Railway lines and plantations became strategic targets because sugar revenue and troop transport sustained the colonial state. Yet the same destruction that weakened Spanish finances could impoverish workers and small farmers whom the insurgents hoped to recruit. A family's willingness to cooperate might depend as much on protection from retaliation as on support for constitutional independence.

Notable commanders represented different experiences. Ignacio Agramonte was associated with disciplined cavalry and republican constitutionalism; Máximo Gómez brought military knowledge from the Dominican Republic and advocated aggressive operations; Antonio Maceo, a free man of color from eastern Cuba, became an unusually prominent officer whose authority challenged assumptions about race and rank. Their achievements did not eliminate rivalries. Disputes divided eastern and central regions, civilian representatives and field generals, advocates of an early western invasion and those focused on defending established insurgent zones. Céspedes was removed from the presidency in 1873; the deaths of Agramonte that year and Céspedes in 1874 further weakened the movement.

The war's humanitarian consequences reached far beyond battles. Armies consumed harvests, military requisitions burdened communities, and families endured migration, separation and uncertainty about enslaved relatives. The end of large-scale hostilities in 1878 followed strategic fatigue and political fragmentation rather than an unambiguous battlefield victory that settled the underlying issues. Spain retained sovereignty, and many independence advocates refused to accept that constitutional promises or selective manumission were enough. The experience left a body of soldiers, exiles, letters and political grievances that later organizers would inherit; it also offered lessons about the need to coordinate military command, civilian representation, financing and racial inclusion.

Section 67.6

Abolition in Puerto Rico and Gradual Emancipation in Cuba, 1870–1873

Spain's Moret Law of 4 July 1870 declared free children born to enslaved mothers from 17 September 1868 onward, enslaved people over sixty, and specified other groups, with provisions applying to both Cuba and Puerto Rico, but it left most people in bondage and was unevenly enforced; its 'free' children could be held in apprenticeship-like conditions. In Puerto Rico, the National Assembly in Madrid passed abolition on 22 March 1873, with compensation to owners and a requirement that freedpeople contract with their former owners, other employers, or the state for at least three years, while full political rights were delayed for five years. Roughly 29,000 people were freed.

Enslaved people shaped these outcomes by flight, litigation, purchase of freedom, and, in Cuba, by joining insurgent forces. Formal freedom did not bring land, and plantation owners used contract rules and vagrancy regulations to retain labor.

Spain's gradual abolition measures arose from several pressures acting at once: demands from enslaved people for freedom, abolitionist petitions, international diplomacy, colonial warfare and debate in the Spanish legislature after the 1868 revolution. The law commonly called the Moret Law was enacted on July 4, 1870. It applied to Spain's slaveholding Caribbean colonies, not exclusively to Cuba. Its provisions freed specified categories rather than the entire enslaved population. The law treated children born to enslaved mothers after September 17, 1868, as legally free, including a retroactive provision that required compensation arrangements for earlier births within that interval; it also addressed older enslaved people and those connected to service for Spain. Guardianship and labor obligations could restrict what freedom meant in practice.

For Puerto Rico, the path to broader abolition was influenced by a smaller enslaved population and an organized campaign at home and in Spain. Petitioners framed slavery as inconsistent with the rights claimed by a modern constitutional nation. Enslaved people themselves made claims, fled, saved to purchase freedom, and negotiated obligations; they did not wait passively for parliamentary legislation. Nevertheless, the abolition statute passed in Madrid on March 22, 1873, protected former owners through compensation and imposed obligations on the newly freed. Article 2 required labor contracts with their former possessors, other employers or the state for no less than three years; designated protectors were to oversee the contracts. Article 7 delayed the full enjoyment of political rights for five years. Emancipation and enfranchisement were therefore deliberately separated.

The number freed is commonly estimated at nearly thirty thousand, but the social transition was not uniform. Employers sought continuity on sugar estates, while households struggled to retain wages, reunite relatives, establish new marriages and choose where to live. A labor contract negotiated under the threat of official sanction cannot be treated as equivalent to a freely negotiated modern employment agreement. Newly emancipated people nonetheless interpreted the law in their own interest, moving, bargaining, challenging abuse and attempting to build secure family and community lives. These activities are visible unevenly in local notarial, police and legal records.

The comparison with Cuba is instructive. Large western plantations, war in the eastern districts and the weight of slaveholding interests encouraged a longer transition in Cuba. Puerto Rico's 1873 law ended legal slavery sooner, yet constrained labor and political voice; Cuba's 1870 provisions affected some people but left the institution in place. The distinction is not a moral ranking of the islands. It reveals how enslaved populations, reform movements, regional economies and colonial political arrangements shaped different legal solutions to the same injustice.

Section 67.7

The Pact of Zanjón, the Protest of Baraguá, and the Little War, 1878–1880

On 10 February 1878 insurgent leaders and the Spanish general Arsenio Martínez Campos concluded the Pact of Zanjón. It promised amnesty, representation similar to Puerto Rico's, and freedom for slaves and Chinese contract laborers who had fought in the insurgent ranks, but not general abolition or independence. On 15 March, at Baraguá, Antonio Maceo refused to accept it, demanding independence and abolition; his Protest of Baraguá became a symbol of uncompromising resistance, though he lacked the strength to continue and left the island.

The Little War (Guerra Chiquita) of 1879–1880, led by figures including Calixto García, failed for want of resources and organization. It taught the next generation, Martí among them, that a future war would need better planning, unity, and external support.

The negotiations that produced the Pact of Zanjón took place after a decade of destructive campaigning in which the insurgent government had struggled to maintain stable authority. General Arsenio Martínez Campos combined military pressure with an offer of political concessions. The pact of February 10, 1878, promised an amnesty and a form of reform comparable to arrangements in Puerto Rico. It provided freedom for enslaved people and Chinese indentured workers who had served in the insurgency but did not establish immediate general abolition. Many rebels hoped to secure safety for their families and a way back to civilian life; others regarded any settlement short of independence as an abandonment of the war's declared purpose.

Antonio Maceo's meeting with Martínez Campos at Baraguá on March 15 became a defining symbol because Maceo rejected the pact's authority to end the struggle on those terms. Accounts preserve their exchange through participants and later recollections, rather than a single stenographic record beyond dispute. The historical significance lies in the political stance, not in exact speeches repeated in patriotic retellings. Maceo insisted that autonomy and limited emancipation could not substitute for sovereign independence and a broader commitment to freedom. The continuing capacity of his forces was limited, and resistance did not turn the decision into a renewed national campaign; he ultimately left the island.

The Little War of 1879–1880 revealed the organizational weaknesses of exile-led revolutionary efforts. Calixto García and others tried to revive armed resistance, but colonial intelligence, insufficient funds, fragmented support and the inability to coordinate a decisive rising prevented sustained expansion. Some Cubans preferred to test legal reform, and the population had already endured enormous losses. Failed mobilization therefore cannot be explained solely by a lack of patriotism. Warfare had altered households, regional economies and the political calculations of the people from whom the insurgents needed food, money, recruits and shelter.

For José Martí and his associates, these episodes would later furnish lessons about leadership. A renewed war needed institutional mechanisms to prevent rivalry among generals, a civilian organization capable of collecting contributions and communicating across borders, and a public program that did not alienate Black Cubans or Spaniards willing to accept independence. The 1878 settlement also demonstrated that legal abolition and constitutional reform could be advanced by imperial officials without answering the demand for national sovereignty. The period between the two major Cuban wars was not therefore a political vacuum; it was an era of conflict over how to organize for a better-supported campaign.

Section 67.8

The Patronato System and the Final Abolition of Cuban Slavery, 1880–1886

Spain's 1880 law replaced slavery in Cuba with the patronato, under which former slaves, called patrocinados, owed labor to their former owners for up to eight years in return for minimal pay and care. Patrocinados used the courts, self-purchase (coartación), and local officials to challenge unfair treatment, and flight and work stoppages continued. Planters adapted, partly by turning to wage labor and mechanization. A royal decree dated 7 October 1886 ended the patronato upon its promulgation in Cuba later that month, completing legal abolition there without eliminating coercive labor relations.

Emancipation did not eliminate economic dependence, racial discrimination, or poverty. Afro-Cuban journalists and organizers such as Juan Gualberto Gómez pressed for civil equality, and the formerly enslaved became an important constituency for the next independence movement.

The Cuban patronato law of February 13, 1880, sought to extinguish slavery through an eight-year transition while preserving production on large estates. Formerly enslaved people were reclassified as patrocinados and their former owners as patrons. Although the new legal status recognized certain protections and limited remuneration, patrons retained important powers over work and movement. The formal language of guardianship presented continuing compulsion as orderly preparation for freedom. In practice, it gave planters additional time to reorganize labor rather than obliging them to negotiate immediately on equal terms.

People subjected to patronato challenged the system through several methods. They sought documentation, invoked promised wages, disputed misconduct before officials, escaped restrictive arrangements and petitioned for transfers or release. Families attempted to remain together and to secure the status of children born after partial-emancipation laws. Court records, complaints and work arrangements can disclose these initiatives, but the proceedings were generally recorded by literate officials who operated within a colonial administrative hierarchy. It would be misleading either to imagine a complete disappearance of slavery in 1880 or to treat patrocinados as wholly without new legal opportunities.

The end came sooner than the eight-year timetable. A royal decree dated October 7, 1886, stated in Article 1 that patronato would cease when the decree was promulgated in Cuba. It was published in the *Gaceta de Madrid* on October 8 and later promulgated on the island; accounts of the latter report publication in Havana on October 29. This is why historians should distinguish the signing date from local legal implementation. The termination of patronato is commonly used as the marker for the final abolition of Cuban slavery, but the decree did not guarantee land, voting rights, compensation for forced work or the disappearance of racial exclusion.

In the following years, freed families negotiated contracts, moved among plantations and towns, and participated in associational life. The transition occurred alongside sugar mechanization, changes in labor recruitment and volatile international markets. Workers' mobility could strengthen their bargaining position, but access to land and capital remained unequal. Afro-Cuban writers and activists, including Juan Gualberto Gómez, challenged discrimination and argued for equal participation in the public life of the colony. Their efforts linked the promise of emancipation to the question of what kind of independent Cuba could protect civil and political equality.

Section 67.9

Colonial Reform, Exile Communities, and the Political Reorganization of the Spanish Caribbean

After Zanjón, Cuban moderates formed the Liberal Autonomist Party, seeking self-government within the empire, while separatists conspired abroad. Puerto Rican autonomists, led first by Román Baldorioty de Castro and later by Luis Muñoz Rivera, formed a similar movement in 1887; in the repression known as the compontes (1887) authorities jailed and tortured many of its supporters. Spain's refusal of substantial reform strengthened separatists.

Exile communities were vital. Cigar workers in Key West, Tampa, and New York, many Afro-Cuban and Puerto Rican, financed political clubs, published newspapers, and created mutual-aid societies; reading aloud in factories (lectores) spread political ideas. The historian can trace these networks through newspapers, club minutes, and police reports.

Reconstruction of anticolonial networks proceeded differently in the two islands. Cuban autonomists sought practical concessions in administration, taxation and representation, arguing that constitutional change could prevent another ruinous war. Separatists disagreed about the feasibility of insurrection without a disciplined army and dependable resources. The memories of the Ten Years' War gave authority to veterans, but also created rival claims to leadership. Spain's postwar reforms provided political space for newspapers and associations while leaving decisive authority with imperial officials. In Puerto Rico, liberals and autonomists similarly developed organizational networks within a system whose governor could still act coercively.

Puerto Rico's *compontes* of 1887 reveal the fragility of legal opposition. In response to social unrest, commercial boycotts and fears about clandestine activity, colonial police detained activists and subjected detainees to violent interrogations. Some residents advocated autonomy rather than separation and still suffered repression. The episode helped discredit claims that colonial rule could be reformed solely by petitions; it did not create agreement on an independent republic. Newspapers and exile memoirs frequently present the repression through partisan lenses, so their allegations should be compared with administrative files and evidence of physical coercion wherever possible.

Migration created political spaces beyond the captains-general's reach. Cuban cigar workers in Key West and Tampa, and clubs in New York, contributed money and public discussion to the independence cause. Workshops could have *lectores* who read newspapers and speeches aloud, bringing literacy and political debate to workers with varied educational backgrounds. Afro-Cuban and Puerto Rican migrants were among the organizers, and the collection of weekly contributions turned nationalism into repeated organizational work. The communities did not agree on strategy, race or class; indeed, their debates required leaders to persuade rather than simply command.

Exile institutions also had practical weaknesses. Police agents monitored them, funds were precarious, and communication with conspirators in Cuba or Puerto Rico was difficult. Political writing could reach more people than arms, but manifestos did not guarantee that weapons would arrive when a coordinated rising began. Such constraints explain Martí's commitment to a permanent party organization with local clubs, elected delegates and communication between civilians and officers. They also show why the demand for sovereignty was embedded in broader questions of labor rights, racial dignity, popular education and the right to debate public affairs without repression.

Section 67.10

José Martí, the Cuban Revolutionary Party, and the Vision of an Independent Cuba

José Martí, born in Havana in 1853, was imprisoned as a teenager for anti-Spanish activity and exiled to Spain, then lived in Mexico, Guatemala, Venezuela, and, from the 1880s, mainly in New York. A poet, journalist, and organizer, he wrote essays such as 'Nuestra América' (1891) that defended a Latin American identity distinct from European and U.S. models. He founded the Cuban Revolutionary Party in 1892 to unify exiles and prepare a coordinated independence war, publishing the newspaper Patria and working with Puerto Rican independentists, Betances among them, who joined through a Puerto Rican section.

Martí insisted on racial inclusion in the republic, though the promise was not always matched in practice, and he warned of the danger of U.S. expansion in the Caribbean. His political success lay in persuading veterans like Gómez and Maceo to place their military experience under civilian direction.

Martí's life in exile made him unusually attentive to the politics of the whole hemisphere. His imprisonment as a young opponent of Spanish rule and subsequent experience in Spain, Mexico and other American republics exposed him to different constitutional forms and to the vulnerabilities of societies formally independent but economically dependent. From New York, he observed the United States with admiration for some civic institutions and increasing alarm over racial injustice, commercial power and expansionist ambitions. His essay *Nuestra América* urged Americans south of the United States to understand their own societies and to resist the imposition of imported formulas. These arguments shaped his insistence that Cuban independence should be political, social and moral, not merely the replacement of one ruling elite by another.

The Cuban Revolutionary Party was an organizational response to earlier failures. Clubs formed in expatriate communities adopted principles and supported party officials; small contributions collected repeatedly furnished an operational base rather than reliance on a few wealthy patrons. The party's bases and statutes of 1892 identified Cuban independence as the immediate objective and recognized the importance of Puerto Rico's freedom. Martí's newspaper *Patria* gave the movement a public vocabulary and a means to correct rumors, promote solidarity and explain what the new republic should avoid. Its audience was not limited to professionals: cigar workers and mutual-aid associations supplied essential legitimacy and resources.

The relationship between race and republican identity was central to Martí's project. He rejected the claim that racial differences justified permanent political hierarchy and sought common citizenship among Cubans of African and European descent. His language must nevertheless be read critically: a universal republican ideal does not tell us whether all activists agreed on racial discrimination, landownership or representation. Afro-Cuban veterans such as Antonio Maceo possessed their own authority and aspirations, while white elites could endorse equality in public and resist it in practice. Historians therefore contrast Martí's published political program with the actions of officers, club organizers and emerging civilian leaders.

Martí also had to negotiate with experienced commanders whose reputations rested on the earlier war. He persuaded Máximo Gómez to accept leadership and worked to reconcile personalities who had quarreled over strategy or political control. His preparations for a coordinated expedition were disrupted in early 1895 when American authorities seized arms connected with the Fernandina plan. The setback illustrates why financial support, clandestine movement, diplomatic risk and military timing were inseparable. Martí's achievement lay in building an organization capable of proceeding despite such setbacks, and in giving a projected war a language of civic responsibility that extended beyond a military command.

Section 67.11

The Cuban War of Independence Begins, 1895

Uprisings began on 24 February 1895, but those in the west were quickly suppressed. On 25 March Martí and Gómez issued the Montecristi Manifesto in the Dominican Republic, defining the war as one for independence without racial division and promising respect for Spaniards who did not oppose it. Martí landed in eastern Cuba in April and died in a skirmish at Dos Ríos on 19 May; Gómez became general-in-chief, with Maceo as second-in-command.

Revolutionary institutions were created: at Jimaguayú in September a constitution established a government under Salvador Cisneros Betancourt. Martí's death turned him into the leading martyr of Cuban nationalism, a symbol claimed by later governments of differing ideologies.

The February 24 rising was the outcome of years of secret planning rather than a spontaneous island-wide explosion. Organizers wanted several districts to rebel together, but coordination was uneven, and colonial authorities suppressed some conspiracies before they could take root. Eastern Cuba, where earlier military networks remained influential, offered a more favorable environment. Martí and Gómez issued the Montecristi Manifesto from the Dominican Republic on March 25, setting out the political purpose of the renewed campaign. Its declarations about national inclusion and protection of peaceful Spaniards attempted to prevent the war from being depicted as a race conflict or a campaign against every Spanish-born resident. A manifesto announced an intention; it did not ensure uniform conduct by every combatant.

The expeditionary leaders entered Cuba separately. Martí and Gómez landed in the east in April, while Antonio Maceo and other veterans rejoined insurgent networks. Martí, inexperienced in field command relative to his veteran collaborators, insisted on accompanying the army and died at Dos Ríos on May 19. His death deprived the movement of a key organizer and potential mediator between civilian and military authority. His later elevation into a national symbol should not obscure the practical importance of living organizers who continued to finance, communicate and staff revolutionary institutions. Gómez's continued leadership and Maceo's operational ability were critical to keeping the rebellion alive.

At Jimaguayú in September 1895, insurgent representatives adopted a constitutional framework for the Republic in Arms. The constitution attempted to address the earlier war's tension between a deliberative government and military autonomy. Salvador Cisneros Betancourt became president, while military commanders retained the capacity to move rapidly. Revolutionary institutions performed tasks beyond military direction: collecting contributions, assigning officials, maintaining correspondence, determining relations with foreign governments and asserting the legitimacy of the independence project. Their effectiveness varied with the fortunes of the war and the difficulty of governing territory subject to Spanish raids.

The renewed movement did not simply repeat the Ten Years' War. Its preparation through the Revolutionary Party, commitment to a western campaign, broader appeal across racial lines and networks outside Cuba increased its reach. Yet these advantages were vulnerable to shortages, infiltrators, local loyalties and the survival needs of rural people. Cuba's independence war was neither an uninterrupted series of patriotic victories nor merely a prelude to the U.S. intervention of 1898. The violence, political ambitions and social transformations of 1895–1897 are essential to explaining Spain's final crisis.

Section 67.12

The Mambí Armies, Spanish Reconcentration, and the Destruction of the Colonial Countryside, 1895–1897

In late 1895 Gómez and Maceo led a cavalry invasion westward, reaching Pinar del Río by January 1896 and burning cane fields to deprive Spain of revenue. Spain responded by sending Valeriano Weyler, who from October 1896 ordered rural civilians into fortified towns (reconcentración). Hunger and disease followed; scholarly estimates of deaths associated with reconcentration range widely, from roughly 60,000 to nearly 200,000, among a population of approximately 1.6 million; early claims were sometimes much higher, and the underlying records are incomplete.

Maceo was killed in December 1896. Afro-Cubans made up a large share of the rank and file and of the officer corps, though prejudice persisted, and women served as nurses, couriers, and supporters; Mariana Grajales, Maceo's mother, became a symbol. The sugar economy was gravely damaged and the colony was financially drained.

The invasion of western Cuba in late 1895 and early 1896 was among the insurgency's most consequential operational choices. Gómez and Maceo moved mobile columns across the island, crossing or evading defensive barriers and making warfare possible in plantations that had been comparatively protected during the Ten Years' War. Cane fields, milling facilities, transport lines and rural stores became targets because the insurgent leadership hoped to deny the colonial government the export revenue on which it depended. Their strategy created pressure for negotiation but also imposed deep suffering on wage workers, smallholders and families whose livelihoods depended on the crops being destroyed.

Spanish commanders faced a dilemma. Conventional occupation of towns did not secure the countryside, while pursuits of mobile insurgent columns exhausted troops and animals. The state built and strengthened fortified lines called *trochas*, mobilized colonial volunteers and increased pressure on civilians suspected of providing food or intelligence. Valeriano Weyler's reconcentration orders, implemented from 1896, required many rural residents to move toward guarded towns. Officials described the policy as a means of separating fighters from their supply base; its operation deprived families of fields, animals, tools, shelter and reliable food. Towns overwhelmed by newcomers suffered crowding, sanitation failures and epidemic disease.

The scale of deaths is disputed, and students must not confuse displacement with death. Modern researchers using population records and wartime administrative evidence have discussed estimates ranging from about sixty thousand to figures approaching two hundred thousand associated with reconcentration and its conditions; some early publicity figures were far higher. The uncertainty reflects incomplete registration, migration, the difficulty of separating disease and starvation caused by policy from wider wartime mortality, and the political uses of statistics. Scholarship on displaced *guajiros* emphasizes that reconcentrated people were not a passive mass: they sought work, food, protection and ways to recover family property, while officials sometimes treated displaced poverty as criminality.

The war also unsettled racial and gender divisions. Afro-Cuban soldiers and officers were prominent in insurgent forces, and Maceo's death in December 1896 became a major blow to the army and to the promise of interracial patriotism. Women acted as couriers, fund-raisers, nurses, traders and protectors of the vulnerable; families were displaced into new social networks or fractured by combat and reconcentration. Revolutionary proclamations promised equality while social prejudice remained active in towns and camps. A complete military history must therefore explain how the conflict transformed daily life, not only who occupied a particular fortified position.

Section 67.13

Puerto Rican Autonomism and Spain's Colonial Reforms of 1897

Luis Muñoz Rivera allied his autonomist faction with Spain's Liberal leader Práxedes Mateo Sagasta in 1897, and the Liberals, back in power after the assassination of Cánovas, issued the Autonomic Charter in November 1897 for Cuba and Puerto Rico. It created a two-chamber legislature and a cabinet, while Spain kept control of defense, foreign affairs, and a governor-general. An autonomous government was inaugurated in February 1898, with elections in March.

Independentists regarded the charter as insufficient, and Cuban insurgents rejected it as too late. Autonomy in Puerto Rico had barely begun when U.S. forces landed.

Puerto Rican autonomism drew support from people who wanted to end arbitrary colonial government without risking another failed armed revolt. Román Baldorioty de Castro and Luis Muñoz Rivera became central figures in successive phases of the movement, but their political projects were neither identical nor uncontested. Moderate reformers emphasized insular control over budgets, education, public works and local economic policy. Independence advocates viewed these demands as insufficient while Spanish conservatives feared they would fragment the empire. The legacy of Lares and the violence of the compontes gave such disagreements immediate personal significance for families who had experienced police detention or exile.

The 1897 alliance pursued by Muñoz Rivera's supporters with Spanish liberals under Práxedes Mateo Sagasta offered a constitutional route to self-government. The Autonomic Charter of November 25, 1897, provided for an insular legislature and ministers while reserving decisive imperial powers, including major external and military authority, to Madrid and the governor-general. The measure followed the assassination of Spanish conservative premier Antonio Cánovas and renewed pressure from the Cuban war. It must be read as part of Spain's attempt to stabilize both colonies, not simply as evidence that the imperial state willingly relinquished control.

The timing is crucial. An autonomous government was inaugurated in February 1898, with March elections producing a legislature in which Muñoz Rivera's Liberal Autonomists were influential. Local politicians briefly acquired new opportunities to debate policies, but sovereignty remained Spanish, and the institutions had little time to demonstrate their powers before the United States entered the war. Contemporary supporters could reasonably consider autonomy a breakthrough; critics could point out its dependence on Spanish military control and its vulnerability to sudden imperial decisions. Neither judgment can be dismissed by reference to what happened in July.

Puerto Rican society was not politically homogeneous. Some merchants welcomed stability under Spain, while others imagined that U.S. commercial connections might expand trade. Autonomists hoped to preserve or improve self-government, independentists feared merely exchanging one distant ruler for another, and rural residents faced immediate issues of wages, land access and debt regardless of constitutional status. Students should compare election results and party newspapers with accounts by workers and local communities. The American landing later in 1898 cut short an experiment whose long-term results cannot be reconstructed as if it had been allowed to continue.

Section 67.14

The United States, Cuba, the USS Maine, and the Road to War in 1898

U.S. interests included investment in Cuban sugar, strategic concern for the approaches to a future isthmian canal, humanitarian reaction to reconcentration, and the lobbying of Cuban exiles and the Junta. Sensationalist newspapers amplified public sympathy. On 15 February 1898 the battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbor, causing 266 eventual deaths among the crew. Its cause remains disputed: a U.S. Navy inquiry blamed an external mine, while later technical studies suggested an internal explosion, perhaps from a coal fire; the question remains unresolved.

President McKinley pursued diplomacy, but Congress on 19–20 April passed a joint resolution demanding Spanish withdrawal and, through the Teller Amendment, disclaiming any intention to exercise sovereignty over Cuba. The U.S. blockade began, and Congress recognized a state of war retroactive to 21 April.

By the late nineteenth century, Cuba had substantial significance for United States trade, investment, shipping and strategic planning. American merchants bought Cuban sugar and sold goods to the island; investors held property claims whose security deteriorated with the war. Yet economic interest alone cannot explain Washington's decision. Political debates also concerned humanitarian responsibility, the capacity of Spain to govern, party competition, domestic press coverage and the question of whether a European empire should remain in the Caribbean. Cuban exile representatives tried to influence officials and the public, but they were not simply instruments of U.S. policy. Their objective of Cuban independence could conflict with American proposals for a settlement short of sovereignty.

The destruction of the USS *Maine* in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898, deepened the crisis. The Navy records 266 eventual deaths, including six who died later from injuries. A contemporary inquiry attributed the blast to an external mine without identifying a perpetrator; later technical reassessments have disputed the mechanism. It is therefore incorrect to teach Spanish sabotage as an established cause. Spanish rescuers and nearby ships assisted survivors, a fact that complicates newspaper stories portraying a united Spanish enemy. The explosion accelerated military preparations and political polarization at a moment when negotiations over the Cuban war were already unstable.

President William McKinley initially sought diplomatic measures, including relief for suffering civilians and changes in Spanish administration. Congress and public opinion did not speak with one voice. Some favored immediate intervention to end atrocities, others opposed overseas war, and expansionists saw an opportunity to extend American power. The joint resolution of April 20 demanded Spanish withdrawal from Cuba while the Teller Amendment disclaimed any intention to exercise American sovereignty over Cuba except in a temporary pacification role. The resolution represented an official promise about Cuba, not a comparable pledge of independence to Puerto Rico or the Philippines.

The precise declaration chronology matters. The naval blockade began on April 21; Spain declared war on April 23; the United States issued its formal declaration on April 25, retroactive to April 21. The origins of the war must be read alongside the continuing Cuban insurgency. Spain had confronted rebellion since 1895, and the destructive military and social situation shaped what American intervention could achieve. The historian should evaluate congressional resolutions, executive correspondence, Cuban revolutionary statements and naval inquiries separately, because they were produced by actors with different objectives and different access to evidence.

Section 67.15

The Spanish–American War of 1898: Cuba, Santiago, and the Defeat of Spain

The war was global: on 1 May Commodore Dewey destroyed the Spanish squadron at Manila Bay. In Cuba, U.S. troops landed near Santiago in June, with Cuban forces under Calixto García providing scouting and flank support. U.S. units fought at San Juan Heights on 1 July, and the Spanish fleet under Admiral Cervera was destroyed on 3 July when it tried to leave Santiago harbor. Santiago surrendered on 17 July.

Cuban insurgents had weakened Spain across three years, yet they were excluded from the surrender and from Santiago's administration, an early sign that the United States intended to determine the island's political future. Disease killed far more American soldiers than combat did.

The Spanish–American War extended beyond the Caribbean. At Manila Bay on May 1, 1898, Commodore George Dewey defeated Spain's Pacific squadron, opening the way to an American occupation struggle that became entangled with a Filipino independence movement. In the Caribbean, American naval operations sought to isolate Spanish forces and prevent reinforcement. Cuba's coasts and ports were crucial: the siege of Santiago could be undertaken because the fleet controlled maritime approaches and could support landing operations. This wider geographical frame helps explain why the settlement of the war would produce overseas American possessions even though the American public campaign had emphasized Cuba.

U.S. soldiers landed near Santiago in June. Cuban insurgents under Calixto García helped supply intelligence, secure routes and pressure Spanish positions, but the allies' objectives were not identical. American commanders concentrated on taking the city, while Cuban officers expected recognition of their army's political legitimacy and claims to independence. Difficulties of coordination, language, logistics and racial prejudice strained cooperation. At Las Guásimas and in the fighting for El Caney and San Juan Heights, the campaign revealed the danger of tropical disease and inadequate preparation as well as the military advantages of American firepower. The destruction of Admiral Cervera's fleet on July 3 narrowed Spain's options for continuing the defense.

Santiago's surrender on July 17 supplied a moment of victory that different participants interpreted differently. The American command negotiated the arrangements, and Cuban revolutionary leaders were not given equal authority over the occupied city. For insurgents who had fought the empire for years, this exclusion suggested that military assistance might become political supervision. For American leaders, the need to maintain order and protect property supplied an argument for direct control. Historians should distinguish the fact that American naval and military forces were decisive in the final conventional campaign from the separate fact that Cuban insurgents had made the Spanish position difficult over three years of warfare.

The short U.S.–Spanish war should not be confused with a short Cuban war. The fighting of 1898 occurred after years of destroyed livelihoods, forced migration and Spanish counterinsurgency. Disease, rather than battlefield wounds, caused many American military deaths and afflicted Spanish and Cuban troops as well. Contemporary photographs and reports often centered American soldiers, while Cuban revolutionary records preserve a different account of the war's chronology. Neither collection alone is sufficient for a balanced history of how Spain lost Cuba.

Section 67.16

The American Invasion of Puerto Rico and the Collapse of Spanish Rule, 1898

On 25 July 1898 General Nelson Miles landed at Guánica, then advanced toward Ponce and other towns. Spanish forces resisted in a few places, such as Hormigueros and Asomante, but the campaign was short. Reactions varied: some Puerto Ricans, including many autonomists and merchants, welcomed U.S. rule, hoping for greater liberty; others, among them independentists, were wary; and Spanish loyalists felt abandoned.

An armistice was signed on 12 August. Spanish authority formally ended on 18 October 1898, when American officials took over in San Juan. The elected insular parliament had met only briefly, and the transfer ended the autonomy experiment before it could be tested.

The Puerto Rican campaign took place within a conflict whose outcome was already moving against Spain. American naval strength enabled the army to land at Guánica on July 25, 1898, and establish routes toward Ponce and other towns. General Nelson A. Miles presented the expedition as a liberation from Spanish rule, a message that appealed to people tired of the captain-general's authority but did not guarantee an independent political future. Spanish units resisted in several engagements, and difficult mountainous terrain limited movement. The armistice of August 12 interrupted operations before the campaign became a prolonged struggle across the entire island.

Local responses were neither uniform nor fixed. Some Puerto Ricans provided intelligence, supplies or welcomes to U.S. forces, believing that change might improve taxation, civil liberties, trade or the administration of justice. Others defended existing offices, doubted the promises of an invading army, or argued for independence rather than annexation. Autonomist leaders who had invested in the institutions established earlier in 1898 faced the abrupt loss of a framework that they hoped to develop. Rural workers and coffee farmers were often more immediately concerned with harvests, wages, debt and protection from looting than with the precise constitutional terminology used by governors.

The chronology of the handover needs emphasis. The arrival of troops in July established American military control over particular areas; the armistice of August 12 suspended open hostilities; the formal change of government and flag at San Juan on October 18 symbolized the end of Spanish authority across the island. The December Treaty of Paris would supply the international legal cession. These were related steps, but their different dates show why occupation, cessation of war, and sovereignty transfer should not be compressed into a single event.

The island's recent Autonomic Charter gives the invasion special meaning. Puerto Rico was not an administrative blank slate waiting for American institutions. It had political parties, newspapers, elected representatives and public debates about the meaning of self-government. The brief autonomous government had not become sovereign, but its abrupt termination removed an experiment whose supporters had struggled to achieve it. Whether U.S. administration would expand or contract local political authority soon became a central question for Puerto Rican politicians, and it cannot be answered simply by noting the popularity of some American troops upon landing.

Section 67.17

The Treaty of Paris of 1898 and the End of Spain's American Empire

A peace protocol was signed on 12 August 1898, and the Treaty of Paris on 10 December, with the U.S. Senate approving it in February 1899. Spain relinquished sovereignty over Cuba (Article I), ceded Puerto Rico and Guam (Article II), and transferred the Philippines to the United States for $20 million. Cuban representatives were not invited to the negotiations, nor were Puerto Ricans or Filipinos.

Sovereignty over Cuba was thus given up by Spain without being assigned to Cubans: the United States occupied the island from 1 January 1899. The treaty ended four centuries of Spanish rule in the Americas but left competing meanings of liberation and imperial succession.

The United States and Spain negotiated the peace without granting Cuba, Puerto Rico or the Philippines equal representation at the conference. This fact matters because the war was often described in the language of liberation and self-government, yet the final international instrument was written by governments exercising diplomatic power over other peoples. The August 12 protocol suspended hostilities and set preliminary conditions; commissioners then negotiated the formal treaty signed in Paris on December 10, 1898. Ratification proceeded through the American Senate and Spanish authorities in 1899. Military administration on the islands had already begun before all treaty formalities were complete.

The first two articles established a decisive legal contrast. Article I stated that Spain relinquished its claim of sovereignty over Cuba and recognized that the United States would occupy the island, assuming certain international duties during that occupation. Article II ceded Puerto Rico, other Spanish possessions in the West Indies, and Guam to the United States. Article III dealt with the Philippines and provided for the payment of twenty million dollars. Cuba was not ceded to the United States in the same terms as Puerto Rico, nor did the treaty itself establish a fully independent Cuban government. The different verbs and obligations were foundational to the islands' subsequent history.

For Spain, the settlement represented the loss of the last major remnants of its early modern American empire. Debate within Spain concerned imperial responsibility, military failure, finances and the meaning of national regeneration after defeat. For U.S. policymakers, the acquisition of Puerto Rico and the Philippines raised constitutional questions about whether republican institutions could govern people abroad without offering the political rights ordinarily associated with states. Cuban patriots interpreted Spain's departure as a necessary but incomplete victory because the new occupier now determined the timetable and conditions of government.

The treaty should therefore be read as both a legal record and a political artifact. It precisely identifies actions taken by two governments; it cannot by itself reveal popular consent among the inhabitants or settle whether those terms fulfilled the promises made during the war. The absence of Cuban negotiators must be paired with the long pre-1898 history of Cuban revolutionary institutions. Likewise, Puerto Rican representatives had debated autonomy and independence before the American invasion, yet their preferred arrangements were not decisive at Paris. The document is indispensable precisely because it exposes the distance between the formal transfer of sovereignty and the broader struggle for political self-determination.

Section 67.18

The American Occupation of Cuba, 1899–1902, and the Platt Amendment

Under military governors John Brooke and, from late 1899, Leonard Wood, the occupation built schools, reformed sanitation (including the yellow-fever work of Walter Reed and Carlos Finlay's mosquito theory), and disbanded the insurgent army with payments. A constitutional convention met in Havana in late 1900. In March 1901 Congress attached the Platt Amendment to an army appropriations act, and the United States demanded its inclusion in the Cuban constitution as a condition for withdrawal.

The amendment limited Cuba's treaty and debt powers, authorized U.S. intervention to preserve independence and stable government, and provided for naval bases. Cuban delegates debated hotly and accepted it by a 16–11 vote on 12 June 1901; the 1903 treaty added the Guantánamo lease.

The American military occupation of Cuba began in January 1899 after Spanish withdrawal. Governor John R. Brooke and, from late 1899, Leonard Wood governed through military authority, with local institutions operating under American supervision. Their administration reorganized public finance, sanitation, education and municipal arrangements, but the meaning of reform was contested. Public health campaigns associated with Carlos Finlay's mosquito theory and the work of the U.S. Army's yellow-fever researchers improved scientific understanding and urban conditions. Yet praise for those changes should not erase Cuban medical knowledge or the fact that the occupation was not government by an independent Cuban electorate.

The demobilization of the Cuban Liberation Army brought difficult decisions. Payments to soldiers could recognize their wartime service and facilitate civilian recovery, but they also reduced the organized military power of the insurgent movement. War-damaged farms and sugar mills required investment, and American capital gained increased opportunities to purchase or finance assets. Political leaders disagreed about the acceptable level of foreign economic influence. Many Cubans wanted rapid withdrawal and a sovereign constitution; American officials argued that financial stability, protection of investments and civil order justified continued conditions on the prospective republic.

The constitutional convention convened in 1900 to define the new political order. Its delegates faced pressure after the American Congress passed the Platt Amendment in March 1901. The provisions constrained Cuba's ability to make treaties and incur certain debts, recognized a U.S. intervention power in specified circumstances, and required arrangements for naval or coaling stations. Cuban opponents argued that these conditions contradicted the spirit of the Teller Amendment's promise not to annex Cuba. Others, reluctant but convinced that rejection would delay American withdrawal, supported acceptance. On June 12, 1901, the convention accepted the terms by a vote of sixteen to eleven. An imposed choice under occupation should not be narrated as unqualified popular approval.

The amendment's legal path also requires precision. It began as a provision of American legislation, was attached to Cuba's constitutional arrangements under pressure, and was later embodied in the bilateral relations treaty of 1903. The U.S. naval base arrangement at Guantánamo followed separate agreements that year. These instruments established the framework within which the Cuban Republic would operate after May 1902. Military occupation therefore ended while substantial American leverage continued. The chapter should neither deny the reality of Cuban republican institutions nor present the republic as unconstrained sovereignty.

Section 67.19

Puerto Rico under American Rule: Military Government, the Foraker Act, and Territorial Status

Military government lasted until 1900. The Foraker Act took effect on 1 May 1900, providing for a governor and executive council appointed by the U.S. president, an elected House of Delegates, a resident commissioner in Washington, and tariff arrangements. Currency conversion hurt many planters, and the 1899 hurricane San Ciriaco devastated the coffee economy, while sugar companies expanded.

In the Insular Cases of 1901 the Supreme Court held that territories like Puerto Rico were not automatically fully incorporated into the United States and that Congress could decide what constitutional protections applied. Puerto Ricans were not made U.S. citizens until 1917.

American military administrators governed Puerto Rico from the 1898 occupation until the beginning of civilian territorial government in 1900. They inherited a society with Spanish legal and municipal institutions, a significant coffee economy, coastal sugar plantations and debates about the future of autonomy. Military decrees introduced reforms and sometimes disrupted established commercial practices. The exchange rate between the local peso and the dollar, tariff changes, and the reorientation of markets affected borrowers, merchants and rural producers differently. A legal change of sovereign power did not remove unequal access to credit or land.

The hurricane known as San Ciriaco devastated Puerto Rico in August 1899, destroying buildings, crops and transport links. Coffee-growing regions faced especially difficult recovery because trees required years to return to productive yields and finance was scarce. Disaster, changing trade relationships and the new currency system interacted to deepen hardship. Sugar investors with access to American credit and markets sometimes found opportunities unavailable to small coffee growers. These developments are part of the social history of territorial rule, but broad claims about who gained or lost need to be tested against local property, banking and agricultural records.

The Foraker Act, signed April 12, 1900, took effect with the beginning of civilian government on May 1. It established an American-appointed governor and executive council alongside an elected House of Delegates, a resident commissioner, and a judicial framework. The elected chamber provided a forum for Puerto Rican political action but lacked the powers of a sovereign legislature: federal authority and appointed officials retained substantial control. The act created a form of Puerto Rican citizenship rather than collectively making island residents citizens of the United States. It did not settle the island's eventual constitutional relationship with the Union.

The Supreme Court's Insular Cases, beginning in 1901, supplied a contested legal doctrine under which overseas territories could belong to the United States without being incorporated into it for every constitutional purpose. The decisions were divided and drew criticism, particularly from jurists who regarded the approach as incompatible with equal constitutional government. Puerto Rican political leaders developed multiple responses—statehood, independence, stronger self-government and changing arrangements within territorial rule. U.S. citizenship would be conferred collectively in 1917, beyond this chapter's principal time frame. Puerto Rico should not be described as politically inert under occupation: its newspapers, elected delegates and civic associations debated these alternatives from the beginning.

Section 67.20

Cuban Independence in 1902 and the Comparative Legacy of Spain's Departure

Tomás Estrada Palma, a long-time exile and Martí's successor at the Cuban Revolutionary Party, was inaugurated as president on 20 May 1902, and U.S. troops withdrew. The new republic was legally independent but constrained by the Platt Amendment, dependent on the U.S. market, and marked by foreign ownership of sugar land and racial inequality; Afro-Cuban veterans found political and economic space limited, a tension that erupted later in the century's first decades.

Puerto Rico instead remained a territory, where status questions of statehood, independence, and enhanced autonomy structured politics. Whether 1898 represents decolonization, imperial succession, or a combination remains open. Cuba became sovereign but constrained; Puerto Rico moved from one imperial sovereignty to another. Chapter 70 takes up U.S. hegemony in the hemisphere.

On May 20, 1902, the transfer of authority marked the inauguration of Cuba's republic under President Tomás Estrada Palma. The ceremony fulfilled an aspiration that Cuban revolutionaries had pursued through different organizations and wars since 1868. Former insurgents, journalists and expatriate organizers could see the formation of a Cuban government as a genuine achievement. At the same time, the Platt Amendment placed boundaries around the republic's treaty-making, finance and security decisions, and American commercial influence weighed heavily on the sugar economy. Political independence was therefore real but qualified, a formulation more precise than claiming that Cuba had either full autonomy or no sovereignty at all.

The republic inherited unresolved social conflicts. Emancipation had ended legal enslavement in 1886 but had not equalized landownership, education or access to political power. Afro-Cuban veterans could point to their central service in the independence movement, yet many faced discrimination and exclusion from patronage or offices. Rural Cubans struggled to recover farms after reconcentration and devastation. Women who had organized relief, correspondence and support networks did not receive political equality simply because the new state identified itself as republican. These contradictions invite students to contrast independence declarations with the economic and civic circumstances of the people who had sustained the war.

Puerto Rico entered the twentieth century under a separate constitutional arrangement. The Foraker Act created local electoral activity within a framework controlled by Washington, and the island's inhabitants debated how to obtain fuller rights or independence. Those debates were shaped not only by abstract political philosophy but by the experience of the 1897 autonomy charter, military occupation, the hurricane, the reorientation of the coffee and sugar economies, and new court decisions about territory and citizenship. Puerto Rican nationalism also survived in cultural and political forms, and support for different status arrangements changed across groups and generations.

The two outcomes must be understood against the older history shared by the islands: racial slavery, pressure for emancipation, imperial military government, exile communities, and experiments in self-government. They also diverged because the Cuban revolutionary army had sustained an extended independence war while U.S. policymakers chose a different legal disposition for Puerto Rico. Comparison should not imply that Cuba's people agreed unanimously on the Platt Amendment or that Puerto Ricans were united about U.S. rule. The chapter ends by identifying a larger change in hemispheric history: Spain's authority receded, but U.S. strategic power gained new legal and military foundations. Chapter 70 will examine that wider order in more detail; the present chapter closes with the voices and choices of the two Caribbean societies themselves.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: 1868–1902

What changed? Slavery ended, Spain left, and a Cuban republic was created. What remained continuous? Sugar dependence, racial inequality, and outside influence on sovereignty. Who experienced change most directly? The formerly enslaved and rural civilians. Who experienced greater continuity? Large landowners. Why it matters: Formal change did not remove structural dependence.

Continuity was not an absence of change: enslaved people obtained legal freedom while labor control persisted in new forms; Spanish officials departed but forms of unequal representation endured. Comparison should weigh changes in legal sovereignty against persistence in racial hierarchy, wealth distribution and dependence on global sugar markets.

Chapter Summary

Cuba and Puerto Rico remained under Spanish rule after the mainland independence revolutions because of military geography, lucrative Caribbean trade, institutions tied to the crown, and elites who feared the disruption of slavery and property. This did not mean colonial consensus: free and enslaved residents organized for reform, emancipation, autonomy and independence, often pursuing those aims through different methods.

The simultaneous revolts of 1868 emerged from distinct local networks. At Lares Puerto Rican revolutionaries declared a short-lived republic and attacked slavery; in eastern Cuba Céspedes launched a longer uprising that developed into the Ten Years’ War. Military failure did not render these movements historically irrelevant because they created organizations, symbols and political commitments inherited by later activists.

Cuba’s Ten Years’ War mixed constitutional ambitions with factional disputes and irregular warfare. Guáimaro established a republic in arms, while commanders and civilian representatives disagreed over military strategy. The Pact of Zanjón ended the principal fighting in 1878 without independence, and Maceo’s protest at Baraguá and the Little War sustained the argument that limited reform did not satisfy Cuban aspirations.

Emancipation proceeded unevenly under Spanish law. The Moret Law of 1870 freed defined categories on both islands. Puerto Rico abolished slavery in 1873, but compelled transitional labor contracts and deferred political rights. Cuba replaced slavery with patronato in 1880; its decree of October 1886 ended the system on promulgation in the island. Neither legal transition automatically equalized landownership, wages or citizenship.

The renewal of the Cuban campaign reflected the political work of exile communities, veterans and José Martí’s Cuban Revolutionary Party. Martí and Gómez articulated a vision of national independence linked to civic equality, while the 1895 insurgency expanded warfare westward and imposed new pressure on the sugar economy. Spanish reconcentration, insurgent destruction and years of fighting caused immense human suffering whose mortality remains difficult to calculate precisely.

Puerto Rican autonomism provided a separate trajectory: repression in 1887 intensified demands for change, and Spain’s November 1897 charter briefly established elected institutions in 1898. The U.S. invasion interrupted that experiment. The explosion of the USS Maine contributed to American entry into war, but the cause was not conclusively established and U.S. intervention followed years of Cuban insurgency.

The 1898 Treaty of Paris legally separated the islands’ destinies. Spain relinquished sovereignty over Cuba, which the United States occupied, while it ceded Puerto Rico to the United States. Cuban representatives did not control the treaty negotiations. Acknowledging American military success must not erase Cuban contributions to the destruction of Spanish rule or the political claims of Puerto Rican inhabitants.

Cuba became a republic in May 1902 under the limitations imposed through the Platt framework. Puerto Rico instead received territorial civilian administration under the Foraker Act and entered a contested constitutional relationship elaborated by the Insular Cases. The end of Spanish empire thus combined liberation, constrained sovereignty and the transfer of imperial rule, inaugurating new debates in Caribbean and hemispheric history.

Chapter Reflection

The last major Spanish colonies in the Americas did not become independent on the same timetable as the mainland. Their political histories were shaped by slavery, trade, military government, exile, anticolonial organization and the different possibilities of reform. The Gritos of 1868, the Ten Years’ War, Puerto Rican abolition in 1873, Cuban abolition in 1886 and the war begun in 1895 show that the end of empire was a prolonged transformation, not a single American battlefield victory.

The settlement of 1898 created new political distinctions. Spain relinquished sovereignty over Cuba while ceding Puerto Rico to the United States. Under occupation, Cubans argued over constitutional conditions imposed by Washington; Puerto Ricans debated the limited self-government of a new territorial arrangement. Cuba established a republic in 1902, but the Platt framework constrained its decisions; Puerto Rico remained under American sovereignty. The two experiences reveal that ending Spanish rule did not alone determine the degree of freedom, citizenship or control over national resources that followed.

The chapter ends Part XIV by asking students to judge political change in more than one dimension. Sovereignty is not identical to social equality, legal emancipation is not the same as economic independence, and the presence of an elected assembly does not by itself establish democratic control. Chapter 68 begins Part XV with export economies and global capital; Chapter 70 will return to U.S. hemispheric power. Both depend on the contested Caribbean transitions explained here.
