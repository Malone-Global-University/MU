Chile, Peru, Bolivia, and the War of the Pacific

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Landing at Antofagasta

On 14 February 1879, a few hundred Chilean soldiers came ashore at Antofagasta, a Bolivian port in the Atacama Desert. Bolivian officials had scheduled that same day to auction the property of a Chilean-owned nitrate company. Within hours the town was in Chilean hands, and a dispute over a tax had become a dispute over who ruled the coast.

The landing met little resistance. Antofagasta was a young settlement, and its working population was overwhelmingly Chilean-born, drawn north by silver strikes and by the nitrate enterprise whose assets the Bolivian government now proposed to sell. The Bolivian authorities had little military force on the coast. Chile's leaders presented the occupation as the lawful response to a Bolivian breach of the boundary treaty of 1874. Bolivia's leaders presented it as the seizure of sovereign territory. Both claims rested on documents, and both documents could be read in more than one way. That ambiguity is where this chapter begins.

The occupation would grow into the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), the largest interstate conflict in western South America since independence. It pitted Chile against an alliance of Peru and Bolivia, and it unfolded across some of the driest terrain on earth. Its first phase was naval, because the desert coast could not be supplied overland. Its middle phase was a series of Chilean amphibious landings and desert battles that destroyed the allied armies in the south. Its final phase was an occupation of Peru's capital and a long, dispersed resistance in the Andean highlands, a phase that made the war a struggle involving rural communities as well as armies.

The stakes were material. Guano had paid for much of Peru's state in the mid-nineteenth century, and by the 1870s sodium nitrate, a fertilizer and explosive ingredient in demand across the Atlantic world, had become the new mineral frontier. Nitrate lay in Peru's province of Tarapacá and in the Bolivian and disputed zones to its south. Whoever taxed its export controlled a large and growing source of revenue. But the war was not only about minerals. It was also about treaty obligations, fiscal weakness, military credibility, domestic politics in three capitals, and competing ideas of what each republic owed its citizens and territory.

The chapter takes care to represent all three belligerents. Chile's victory was real and decisive, and it rested on naval power, logistics, and political cohesion. But Peru's long resistance, the collapse and reconstitution of Peruvian authority, the role of Andean peasants, and Bolivia's experience of losing its entire coastline are equally part of the history. So is the war's human cost: conscripts, women who followed the armies, Chinese and Afro-Peruvian workers, and civilians in occupied towns.

Evidence needs special care, because few modern wars have been so thoroughly wrapped in patriotic memory. Official dispatches, soldiers' memoirs, newspaper reports, treaty texts, diplomatic correspondence, and later commemorations all survive in quantity. Each of the three nations built a national pantheon from the war: Arturo Prat and the heroes of La Concepción in Chile, Miguel Grau, Francisco Bolognesi, and Andrés Avelino Cáceres in Peru, and Eduardo Abaroa in Bolivia. This chapter treats those traditions as historical evidence about memory, and separates them where possible from what documents establish. It also does not rely on any single country's national historiography.

The chapter belongs to Volume III and to Part XIV, which examines how war, reform, and consolidation reshaped nineteenth-century states. Chapter 64 examined the devastation of Paraguay and the realignment of the Río de la Plata. Chapter 65 turns to a different pattern: a resource-rich frontier becoming the center of interstate war. Chapter 66 will turn to Brazil's abolition and the fall of its monarchy. The controlling argument here is that the War of the Pacific converted mineral-rich frontier disputes into a lasting reorganization of territory, economic power, and national identity.

Historical Context

The three republics emerged from different parts of the Spanish empire. Chile had been a peripheral captaincy general, and after independence it developed a comparatively centralized state under a political elite that valued fiscal order, as Chapter 62 described. Peru had been the core of Spanish rule in South America and entered independence with a divided society, a deep Indigenous majority in the highlands, and an elite concentrated in Lima. Bolivia was formed in 1825 from Upper Peru, a highland region whose wealth had rested on Potosí's silver, and it entered the nineteenth century with a large Indigenous (Aymara and Quechua) population, a small and fractious elite, and a very thin coastal outlet at the Pacific desert port region called the Litoral.

Their relationships were not fixed. In the 1830s Chile had fought and defeated the Peru–Bolivian Confederation of Andrés de Santa Cruz, a conflict that shaped Chilean suspicion of any combination of its northern neighbors. In 1864–1866, however, Chile and Peru were allies against Spain in the Chincha Islands War, and a Peruvian and Chilean fleet defended against Spanish naval pressure. Alliances in the region shifted with circumstance, which is why historians resist reading the 1879 coalition back into earlier decades.

The desert itself defined the political problem. The Atacama is among the driest places in the world, with almost no rainfall and little surface water, and it separated the populated heartlands of central Chile and Peru. Its coast held guano on offshore islands and, inland, the nitrate beds, but boundaries across it had been drawn on maps by officials who had rarely seen it. Colonial jurisdictions had been vague, and after independence each republic cited a different colonial division as its source of title. The principle at stake, uti possidetis, meant that new states inherited the boundaries of the colonial units they replaced. In an unexplored desert, that principle could support several incompatible readings.

Chapters 62, 63, and 64 have shown how mid-century wars and civil conflicts shaped state capacity across the region. Part XIV's theme is that warfare and territorial conflict were also tools of consolidation. The War of the Pacific fits this pattern but with a distinct feature: the contested territory was valuable not for population or agriculture but for what lay beneath its surface and for the customs revenue that moved through its ports. The chapter therefore pays equal attention to the international commodity market, to state finance, and to the people who actually lived and worked in the desert.

Section 65.1

Guano, Nitrates, and the Economic Transformation of the Pacific Coast

The first great Pacific export boom was guano, the accumulated seabird droppings of Peru's offshore islands, especially the Chincha Islands. From the 1840s, European and North American farmers demanded it as fertilizer, and Peru's government, which owned the deposits, leased their marketing to foreign merchant houses under consignment contracts. The revenue transformed the Peruvian state. It financed railways, urban improvement, the abolition of the Indigenous tribute that had long supported the treasury, and a standing bureaucracy, and it also supported heavy foreign borrowing. Guano was dug by a labor force that included many indentured Chinese workers brought to Peru between 1849 and 1874, whose conditions were often brutal, and it left a state dependent on a single exhaustible resource.

By the 1860s and 1870s the richest guano was running down, and attention shifted to nitrate. Sodium nitrate, called salitre in Spanish, formed in beds in the desert inland from the coast. It was refined in plants called oficinas, and it was wanted for fertilizer and for the manufacture of explosives. The richest beds lay in Tarapacá, then a Peruvian province, and in the zone around Antofagasta and the Salar del Carmen, which Bolivia governed but where Chilean capital and labor were heavily invested. Chilean entrepreneurs, later joined by British investors, financed nitrate works, ports, and railways. The Compañía de Salitres y Ferrocarril de Antofagasta, formed in 1872 with Chilean and British capital, held a concession in Bolivian territory and soon employed thousands of workers.

The desert posed physical challenges that shaped every economic decision. Water had to be brought in or distilled, fuel carried from afar, and workers fed from distant markets. Ports like Iquique and Pisagua had no hinterland of their own. They existed because railways and mule trains linked them to the beds, and because steamships took the product to Europe. Labor came largely from central Chile and from Andean communities in Peru and Bolivia, and many workers were paid in a mixture of wages and company credit, as the system of company stores and tokens developed in the following decades. The nitrate economy was therefore integrated into global markets in a way that made the desert coast, though almost empty, one of the most valuable frontiers in the hemisphere.

Silver added to the pressure. The discovery of rich silver at Caracoles in 1870, in Bolivian territory south of Antofagasta, drew thousands of Chilean miners into a zone Bolivia could scarcely administer. By the middle of the decade, Chileans formed a large majority in the Bolivian coastal settlements. Chile also experienced a sharp economic downturn in the late 1870s as silver, copper, and wheat prices fell and a banking crisis developed, which increased the government's interest in the revenue nitrate could provide. Peru, for its part, faced a fiscal crisis as guano declined and its external debt payments became unmanageable, and it defaulted in 1876. The coast's wealth was therefore matched by pressure on every state that claimed a share of it.

Historical Vocabulary
Nitrate and Guano

Guano is accumulated seabird excrement, rich in nitrogen and phosphates, and in the mid-nineteenth century it was Peru's principal export. Nitrate (salitre, sodium nitrate) is a mineral deposit found in the Atacama Desert, used for fertilizer and explosives and refined in oficinas.

Both were bulk commodities whose value came from distant demand. Neither resource implies a conspiracy. Historians treat the two as successive extractive booms that made fiscal revenue depend on foreign markets, and the nitrate boom as the one that created a contested international frontier. Students should avoid reading "nitrate" as a single cause of war: it created the conditions in which disputes over taxes and boundaries became dangerous.

Section 65.2

The Atacama Boundary Disputes and the Treaties of 1866 and 1874

The dispute between Chile and Bolivia over the Atacama coast was old. Chile's early constitutions treated the Atacama desert as its northern limit, while Bolivia claimed a coastline extending well south of the 23rd parallel, and each cited colonial jurisdictions in support. The Spanish colonial divisions, which had separated the Captaincy General of Chile from the Audiencia of Charcas, were ambiguous on this point, and after independence there was no survey. In 1842 a Chilean law declared the guano deposits south of Mejillones to be national property, Bolivia protested, and the issue festered for decades, flaring each time a new resource was found.

The treaty of 1866 attempted a compromise. It fixed the boundary at the 24th parallel south and created a shared-revenue zone between the 23rd and 25th parallels: each state would divide the export duties collected on minerals taken from that zone. The agreement acknowledged that the region's real wealth lay in places neither government could police alone, and it joined their fiscal interests. But it also built in friction. Revenue sharing required customs administration, and each side accused the other of undercounting. Bolivian officials resented Chilean customs agents at Mejillones, and Chilean entrepreneurs resented duties collected by a government that provided few services.

In 1874 the two governments replaced the disputed revenue-sharing arrangement. Signed at Sucre on 6 August 1874, after Peru and Bolivia had already concluded their secret defensive alliance in February 1873, the treaty confirmed the 24th parallel as the boundary. The broad sharing of mineral-export receipts under the 1866 settlement ceased, but it is incorrect to say that all resource sharing vanished: Article III retained arrangements for sharing guano deposits, later clarified by a complementary protocol concluded on 21 July 1875 to concern the zone from 23° to 25° south. Articles II and IV chiefly concerned the Bolivian mineral zone between the 23rd and 24th parallels, not an undifferentiated 23rd–25th mineral-tax band. Article IV limited additional export duties and provided that Chilean persons, industries, and capital would not be subject to new contributions beyond those then existing for twenty-five years. This was a significant constraint on Bolivian fiscal authority in its own territory, negotiated in return for a more predictable boundary and the removal of an unstable revenue-sharing system.

The treaty's wording lies at the heart of the later conflict. Bolivian governments argued that the tax associated with a concession approved by the legislature was a matter of domestic law and that the 1874 protection did not remove all legislative authority over concessions. Chile argued that a new levy on the Antofagasta nitrate company violated the twenty-five-year undertaking. The 1875 complementary protocol explicitly provided for arbitration over interpretation and execution of the 1874 treaty; Chile appealed to this mechanism, while Bolivia emphasized its jurisdiction over the concession. The documents identify the points of legal disagreement, but they do not by themselves establish that either state had exhausted every opportunity for peaceful settlement.

The Chile–Bolivia Boundary Treaty of 1874

Creators: Plenipotentiaries of Chile and Bolivia · Date: 1874 · Type: Bilateral boundary treaty, published in official collections of both states

Context: The treaty was signed at Sucre on 6 August 1874, replacing the mineral-duty provisions of 1866. Peru and Bolivia had already signed their secret defensive treaty on 6 February 1873. The later complementary instrument of 21 July 1875 clarified guano-sharing geography and arbitration.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Article I fixes the frontier at the 24th parallel; Article II addresses identifying mineral deposits in the 23rd–24th parallel band; Article III preserves the joint treatment of guano; Article IV caps applicable mineral-export duties and protects Chilean persons, industries, and capital against additional contributions for twenty-five years. A complementary agreement of 21 July 1875 clarifies guano sharing across 23°–25° south and submits disputes about the treaty's interpretation and implementation to arbitration.

Historical Vocabulary
Territorial Sovereignty and Uti Possidetis

Territorial sovereignty is a state's supreme authority over a defined territory. Uti possidetis was the principle, adopted by many Spanish American states, that each inherited the colonial boundaries existing in 1810.

In places like the Atacama, colonial boundaries were unclear, so the principle supported competing claims. The term matters because both Chile and Bolivia justified their positions in its terms, and because later settlements replaced inherited boundaries with treaty lines. Students should not equate sovereignty with effective administration: in the desert, the two diverged for decades.

Section 65.3

Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and the Secret Defensive Alliance of 1873

Peru's position on the nitrate question was shaped by fiscal desperation. Its governments in the early 1870s, notably that of President Manuel Pardo, a civilian reformer, inherited a treasury that had counted on guano and now faced debt service it could not meet. Peru legislated a nitrate sales monopoly (estanco) on 18 January 1873, seeking to acquire the product for resale by the state. That experiment encountered resistance and implementation difficulties. In 1875 the government shifted toward expropriating or purchasing privately owned nitrate oficinas, compensating owners with government certificates. The purpose was to control supply, stabilize prices, and capture revenue. The program was costly and administratively difficult, and Peru found it hard to finance the purchases, but it left the Peruvian state as owner or regulator of much of the Tarapacá industry and tied its fortunes to the nitrate market. The policy also irritated foreign and Chilean investors with interests in the province.

Bolivia's problem was weakness. Its government collected little revenue, controlled the coast through a small administration, and faced a population in the Litoral that was largely Chilean. A string of military coups, including the one that brought Hilarión Daza to power in 1876, kept policy unstable. The mining economy of the highlands, notably the silver of Huanchaca, was gaining, but the state's reach into the desert was minimal. Bolivian leaders worried that Chile's economic dominance of the coast could become political control, and they saw an alliance with Peru as insurance.

On 6 February 1873, Peru and Bolivia signed a defensive treaty in Lima. It committed each to defend the other's independence and territorial integrity against external aggression, provided for arbitration of disputes, and was kept secret. The treaty invited Argentina to adhere. Argentina, at odds with Chile over Patagonia and the Strait of Magellan, negotiated, but its Congress did not approve joining, and Argentina remained outside. Historians stress this point: an anti-Chilean coalition was a possibility rather than a settled alignment, and Argentine participation never materialized in 1879. Chile signed a boundary treaty with Argentina in 1881 that reduced the risk of a second front.

The motives for the alliance are debated. Peruvian authors have presented it as a defensive hedge against Chile's naval and commercial rise, while Chilean authors have presented it as evidence of hostile intent toward Chile, and the treaty's secrecy fed Chilean suspicion. Chile's government learned of its existence and obtained confirmation of its terms in early 1879, shortly before the war, and its leaders cited the treaty in demanding that Peru declare neutrality. The treaty was a defensive instrument by its terms, and whether the casus foederis, the circumstance that triggers the alliance, applied to the Antofagasta dispute was itself contested. Peru's decision to honor it, rather than the treaty alone, would turn a bilateral dispute into a regional war.

Section 65.4

The Antofagasta Tax Crisis and the Outbreak of War, 1878–1879

In February 1878 the Bolivian legislature approved legislation concerning an earlier concession to the Antofagasta company and imposed a tax of ten centavos on each quintal (approximately 46 kilograms) of nitrate exported. Bolivian officials presented it as a condition of confirming the concession, and as a fiscal measure for a treasury in need. The company, supported by the Chilean government, argued that the tax violated Article 4 of the 1874 treaty and refused to pay. Chile protested and proposed arbitration, as the treaty seemed to provide. The Bolivian government replied that the matter concerned a private company subject to Bolivian law, and that Bolivian courts would decide it.

Negotiations dragged through 1878. Daza's government, facing fiscal pressure and political weakness, hardened its position, and in December demanded back payment. In early February 1879 it rescinded the company's concession and ordered the seizure of its assets, with an auction set for 14 February. The Chilean government, under President Aníbal Pinto and facing economic downturn at home, judged that the Bolivian action was a treaty violation that jeopardized Chilean property and Chile's wider position on the coast. It ordered its navy and a small military force to Antofagasta, and Chilean troops landed on the morning of 14 February, taking the town and, soon after, advancing to the 23rd parallel. Chile declared that the Bolivian breach had voided the 1874 treaty and restored its earlier claims.

Bolivia declared a state of war on 1 March. In late March, Chilean troops took Calama, where a small Bolivian force under local leaders resisted. Eduardo Abaroa, a civil engineer who joined the defense, was killed at the Topáter bridge on 23 March and became Bolivia's central war martyr, and the date is now commemorated in Bolivia as the Day of the Sea. Meanwhile Peru sent an envoy, José Antonio de Lavalle, to Santiago to mediate. Chile's government pressed him to say whether Peru would remain neutral, and Peru, which had a treaty with Bolivia and domestic pressure to honor it, would not give the assurance Chile wanted. Chile declared war on Peru and Bolivia on 5 April 1879. Peru's President Mariano Ignacio Prado formally declared that the casus foederis, the triggering circumstance of the 1873 defensive alliance, had arisen on 6 April. These were separate diplomatic acts, not simultaneous declarations by all three governments.

The chain of decisions shows why monocausal accounts fail. Bolivia's tax decision was driven by fiscal need and by a view of its sovereign authority. Chile's reaction reflected its investors' interests, a legal reading of the treaty, and a government's decision that arbitration had failed. Peru's choice reflected treaty obligations, a belief in the alliance, and the fear that a Chilean victory would endanger Tarapacá. Each choice could have been different, and several leaders on all sides later regretted them. The war began as a limited local seizure and escalated through diplomatic refusals within seven weeks.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Nitrate Taxation, Diplomatic Escalation, and Territorial Conquest

Long-term causes

Vague colonial boundaries; the guano-to-nitrate economic shift; Chilean economic expansion into Bolivian territory; Peruvian and Bolivian fiscal weakness; regional rivalry dating from the 1830s Confederation.

Short-term causes

Bolivia's 1878 tax; the company's refusal to pay; the Chilean demand for arbitration; Daza's seizure order; the Peru–Bolivia treaty of 1873.

Event or transformation

Chile's occupation of Antofagasta on 14 February 1879, followed by declarations of war in March and April.

Immediate effects

Bolivian loss of Antofagasta and Calama; Peruvian involvement under the 1873 treaty; mobilization in all three states; the beginning of a naval war.

Long-term consequences

Chilean annexation of the nitrate zone; Peruvian loss of Tarapacá; Bolivian loss of its coast; a new regional balance of power.

Historical complexity

No single actor or motive explains the war. Treaty interpretation, domestic politics, investor interests, and alliance obligations combined, and the war's aims expanded as it progressed.
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did the War Begin?

The question: Was the war primarily a result of Chilean economic expansionism and elite interests in nitrate, or of treaty disputes and the Peru–Bolivia alliance, or of the interaction of both?

Interpretation A — Economic expansion and elite interest: Some historians emphasize that Chilean and British capital had built the Antofagasta enterprise, that the Chilean elite and government were closely tied to nitrate investors, and that the Chilean economic crisis of the late 1870s created pressure to acquire new revenue. On this view, treaty arguments were a legal cover for a strategic acquisition of nitrate territory.
Interpretation B — Treaty breach, alliance politics, and miscalculation: Other historians emphasize that the immediate cause was Bolivia's tax decision, that Chile's government sought arbitration before occupying the port, that the secret Peru–Bolivia alliance made Peruvian involvement likely, and that all three governments made choices under domestic political pressure. On this view, the nitrate economy created the conditions, but diplomacy and political decisions made the war.

Evidence: Both readings draw on treaty texts, diplomatic correspondence, company records, and congressional debates in all three countries. Each national historiography has tended to emphasize the other side's responsibility. The two interpretations overlap: most scholars accept that economic interests mattered and that the legal dispute was genuine. Claims that British investors engineered the war are not supported by the diplomatic record, although British capital was a major beneficiary of the outcome.

Why it matters: The question affects how the later territorial settlements are judged. If the war was mainly an economic seizure, the settlements look like spoils. If it was a legal and diplomatic escalation, the settlements look like the consequences of a conflict no one fully controlled. National memory in each country has chosen different emphases.

Division II
Naval Warfare and the Struggle for the Pacific Coast
Sections 65.5–65.8 · 1879
Section 65.5

Naval Power, Ironclads, Coastal Geography, and Military Logistics

The coast's geography made the sea the decisive medium. The Atacama had no navigable rivers and almost no roads, and armies could not live off the land. Any force that wanted to move north or south had to carry food, water, fuel, and ammunition, usually by ship. The side that controlled the sea could land troops at will, cut its enemy's supply lines, and blockade ports whose customs revenue funded the war. Whoever lost the sea would depend on slow desert marches and fixed railways.

At the outset the naval balance was closer than the eventual outcome suggests. Peru's fleet centered on the Huáscar, a turret ship built in Britain in the 1860s, and the Independencia, an ironclad frigate, supported by wooden corvettes and two older river monitors that were poorly suited to the open ocean. Chile's fleet centered on the ironclads Almirante Cochrane and Blanco Encalada, built in Britain in the mid-1870s with heavier armor and more modern guns, and included the wooden corvette Esmeralda, the O'Higgins, and the captured gunboat Covadonga. Chile's two main ironclads were individually stronger than Peru's two, but Peru's ships were faster and more experienced, and its officers, including Miguel Grau, were skilled. Bolivia had no navy to speak of.

Logistics were a second battlefield. Chile had a merchant marine of steamers, and it requisitioned and chartered transports, relying on coal from its southern mines. Peru had a smaller steamer fleet and fewer ports connected to its inland resources. Armies in the desert needed water, and the only reliable supply came from coastal condensers or limited inland sources. Railways, such as those linking nitrate fields to Iquique and Pisagua, mattered less as lines of advance than as sources of supply and as prizes. The scale of the problem helps explain why Chile spent the first six months of the war fighting at sea before attempting a serious land campaign.

The armies were also unequal in organization, though not as decisively as popular accounts imply. Chile began with a small regular army but mobilized volunteers and National Guard units quickly, and its government, though pressed by debt, kept a relatively coherent chain of command and could fund the war through loans and, later, nitrate duties. Peru had more men in principle but a fragmented officer corps, mixed rifle types that complicated supply, and political divisions. Bolivia's army included numerous Aymara and Quechua soldiers and was poorly supplied. Bolivia did possess arduous routes from its highlands through its own Litoral to the Pacific before the coastal occupation; the difficulty was not an absolute absence of access through Bolivian territory. Once Chile seized the coastal ports, the main Bolivian field forces operated in conjunction with the Peruvian front through Tacna and Arica, and water, transport, fragmented command, and supply severely constrained their options. Naval power helped determine where sustained combined operations were feasible, but land routes and local decisions continued to matter.

Section 65.6

Iquique, Punta Gruesa, Arturo Prat, and Miguel Grau

Chile began the war by blockading Iquique, Peru's principal nitrate port, in April 1879. The blockade was meant to deny Peru customs revenue and to pressure it politically. In mid-May the main Chilean fleet under Rear Admiral Juan Williams Rebolledo sailed north toward Callao, hoping to find the Peruvian fleet, and left the wooden Esmeralda under Captain Arturo Prat and the Covadonga under Captain Carlos Condell to maintain the blockade. The Peruvian fleet, meanwhile, had left Callao southward. The two fleets passed each other, and on the morning of 21 May 1879 the Peruvian Huáscar, under Captain Miguel Grau, and the Independencia arrived off Iquique.

Two separate engagements followed. At Iquique, the Huáscar engaged the Esmeralda, a much weaker ship, and after a prolonged action rammed it repeatedly. Prat leapt aboard the Huáscar during the action and was killed, and the Esmeralda sank with heavy loss of life. Peruvian crews rescued survivors. To the south, the Covadonga had slipped away, and the Independencia pursued it close inshore along a rocky coast. The shallow-draft Chilean ship passed through shoals, and the larger Independencia ran aground at Punta Gruesa and was lost. In a single day, Chile had lost its weaker ship and Peru its second ironclad, a trade that Peru could less afford.

Both countries immediately turned the day into national myth. For Chile, Prat became the figure of heroic sacrifice, and 21 May is now Navy Day. Chilean tradition credits him with defiant words as he boarded, but their exact wording is traditional and not verifiable from contemporaneous evidence. For Peru, the day was a victory at Iquique that preceded the shock of Punta Gruesa, and the figure of Grau grew in importance. Historians point out that these national memories have different emphases and that the engagements need to be read as separate actions with distinct results. The sinking of the Esmeralda was a tactical Peruvian victory, and the loss of the Independencia a strategic setback that helped tilt the naval balance.

Section 65.7

The Campaign of the Huáscar and the Battle of Angamos

After Iquique, the Huáscar became the center of the naval war. Under Grau's command it raided Chilean shipping and coastal points between June and October 1879, bombarded ports and shore installations, sank or captured merchant vessels, and forced the Chilean fleet to chase it. Its most consequential success came on 23 July, when it captured the steamer Rímac, which was carrying a cavalry regiment, horses, and supplies north. The loss was a political shock in Chile, exposing the weakness of its naval strategy and the vulnerability of its sea lanes.

The raids exposed command problems on the Chilean side. Williams Rebolledo, the fleet commander, was criticized for failing to catch the Peruvian ship and for a cautious approach, and the government in Santiago, led by Pinto, with political figures like Rafael Sotomayor, restructured the command. Galvarino Riveros replaced Williams, a more aggressive plan was adopted, and the Chilean fleet was reorganized into divisions to hunt the Huáscar and its companion, the corvette Unión. Meanwhile Peru's strategy was constrained: it could not replace losses, and its ships could harass but not change the balance of power.

On 8 October 1879, off Punta Angamos, near Mejillones, Chilean divisions caught the Huáscar and the Unión. The Unión escaped. The Huáscar faced both Chilean ironclads, and after a prolonged action in which Grau and most of his senior officers were killed, the ship was boarded and captured. Chile repaired it and used it in the rest of the war. The Huáscar survives today as a museum ship in Chile. Peru's remaining fleet was reduced to supporting roles and coastal defense, and Chile held command of the sea for the rest of the war.

The strategic result was decisive, but its interpretation is shaped by memory. Chile celebrates Angamos as the victory that made the land campaign possible, and Peru commemorates the same day as the death of Grau, the "Caballero de los Mares," the knight of the seas, honoring him for professional skill and conduct. Both uses are historically meaningful, but they are not evidence of the action itself. Historians treat Angamos as the turning point after which Chile could choose where to land, whom to supply, and which ports to blockade, while Peru and Bolivia could not.

Section 65.8

Pisagua and the Opening of the Land Campaign

With the sea secured, Chile's leaders turned to the land. They chose Tarapacá, the Peruvian nitrate province, because it held the revenue that sustained Peru, and because capturing it would give Chile the economic prize that financed the war. The army, which had grown through mobilization to roughly ten thousand men, was gathered at Antofagasta and prepared for an amphibious assault on the port of Pisagua. The planning relied on transports provided by the merchant fleet, on naval gunfire support, and on secrecy about the landing site. The Minister of War in the field, Rafael Sotomayor, and General Erasmo Escala shared command of the expedition.

On 2 November 1879, Chilean warships bombarded Pisagua and landing parties went ashore in boats under fire from the Peruvian and Bolivian defenders, who were fewer than the attackers but held entrenchments above the beach. The landing succeeded after heavy fighting, and the port was taken within hours. The defenders included Bolivian units, which fought and suffered losses. Chile then advanced inland and seized the railway and nearby water supplies, essential for any further movement. The operation was among the first significant amphibious landings of the modern era in the region and demonstrated the practical value of command of the sea.

The landing also made the war a land war. The Allied armies in the south, scattered across Tarapacá and the Arica–Tacna region, now faced an invading army with secure supply, and their leaders had to decide where to concentrate. Water became the decisive constraint. Control of coastal condensers, of the railway, and of the few inland springs determined where armies could move, and Chilean engineers worked to extend a water-supply line. The choice of Pisagua, a port near the nitrate railways, was therefore as much a logistical decision as a tactical one.

Naval superiority was a necessary condition for these operations, but it was not a sufficient one. A fleet could land an army, but it could not march it across the desert, defeat an enemy field army, or compel a government to make peace. The next phase of the war showed that Chile still had to win battles on land against armies that were willing to fight, and that Peru could and did win some of them. It also showed that even a victory ashore could not be exploited unless supply lines held.

Division III
The Desert Campaigns and the Defeat of the Peru–Bolivia Alliance
Sections 65.9–65.12 · 1879–1880
Section 65.9

Dolores, Tarapacá, and the Campaign of 1879

After Pisagua, the Allied command in Tarapacá, under the Peruvian general Juan Buendía, concentrated forces in the interior. On 19 November 1879, at San Francisco (also called Dolores), Buendía's Peruvian-Bolivian forces attacked Chilean positions on the heights covering the wells at Dolores. Chilean artillery and infantry repelled repeated assaults, and the Allied formation withdrew, leaving the Chileans in control of the critical water supply. The Allied forces suffered serious casualties; figures vary among the opposing reports, and elements of the beaten army withdrew toward the south and toward Tarapacá. The Peruvian garrison at Iquique, cut off and judging its position hopeless, evacuated the port, and Chile occupied it on 23 November. Chile now held the center of Tarapacá's export economy.

The subsequent battle began with a Chilean initiative, not a fresh Peruvian offensive. After Dolores, Peru's surviving formations, including troops led by Colonel Andrés Avelino Cáceres, regrouped near Tarapacá village. Chilean forces under Colonel Luis Arteaga advanced to attack them on 27 November, but the defenders fought back, counterattacked across difficult ravine terrain, and inflicted a costly tactical defeat on the Chilean columns. The Chilean force was caught in difficult terrain, suffered heavy casualties, and withdrew. It was the only major Peruvian victory on land in the southern campaign. Sources differ on casualty figures, and Chilean losses were severe enough that the defeat is remembered in Chile as a costly reverse.

Yet Peru abandoned Tarapacá anyway. The victorious Peruvian force was short of water, food, and ammunition, far from its base, and exposed to a Chilean army that would soon be reinforced and resupplied by sea. Its commanders decided to retreat north toward Arica through harsh desert terrain, a march that cost many men through exhaustion and thirst. The province's nitrate fields and ports thus passed to Chile without a decisive final battle. The episode illustrates a central lesson of the campaign: tactical success without logistics could not hold ground.

The consequences were large. Chile now collected export duties on Tarapacá's nitrate, which gave it revenue to finance the rest of the war, and Peru lost the revenue it needed. In Lima the loss damaged the government's credibility. Peruvian citizens and soldiers, who had borne the shock of Iquique and Angamos, now faced the loss of the nitrate province itself. The political fallout, described in the next section, would reshape Peru and Bolivia within weeks.

At Dolores, control of the well field was operationally decisive. A mounted unit could move faster across the open plain than a thirsty infantry column could stay in action; artillery positioned above the wells made an Allied assault against the slopes costly. Reports of the fighting differ in the precise distribution of troops and losses, but both traditions place the battle within the campaign for the desert's few reliable sources of drinking water. The Chilean victory did not mean Peru's defenders ceased to fight: Tarapacá a week later showed the limits of inferring morale or combat ability from the collapse of a coordinated field position.

Section 65.10

Hilarión Daza, Bolivian Mobilization, and the Crisis of Allied Command

Bolivia had mobilized an army after 1 March 1879, composed largely of Aymara and Quechua conscripts and led by officers loyal to President Daza, whose personal guard, the "Colorados," was among its best units. But getting this army to the coast meant crossing hundreds of kilometers of highland and desert, and Bolivia had no railways to the front. Much of the army remained in the highlands during the months of naval war, and elements reached the Peruvian south only gradually, mostly through Tacna and Arica.

In November 1879, Daza led a force from Tacna toward the coast to join the Allied army in Tarapacá. At Camarones, after learning of the loss at Dolores and under pressure from his own officers, he turned back toward Arica rather than continue. Peruvian and Bolivian accounts differ on the reasons: Peruvians often described it as an abandonment, and Bolivian defenders have said Daza made a realistic judgment about the army's condition. Regardless of motive, the effect was to deny the Allied army reinforcements and to deepen mistrust. Daza's authority in Bolivia, already weak, collapsed. On 27 December 1879 he was overthrown, and Narciso Campero became president in January 1880.

Peru's government faced its own crisis. President Mariano Ignacio Prado had left the country in December 1879 on a mission to purchase arms in Europe, and critics accused him of abandoning his post, a charge he and his defenders rejected. On 23 December Nicolás de Piérola, a political rival, seized power in Lima and declared himself dictator. The result was that in the weeks after Tarapacá, both Allied states underwent coups, and each new government had to reorganize its army and its command.

Allied cooperation was complicated by language, military culture, geography, and mistrust. The two armies fought under different commanders without a unified chain of command, used different equipment and organization, and had to coordinate across great distances. Historians argue that the Allied defeat in the south cannot be explained by a single failure: political instability, logistical limits, loss of sea control, and divided command interacted, and each worsened the others.

Section 65.11

The Campaign of Tacna and the Defeat of the Allied Army, 1880

In early 1880 Chile prepared its next offensive, against the remaining Allied positions in southern Peru. Using the fleet, it landed an army of roughly twelve thousand men near Ilo, at Pacocha, in late February, and advanced inland to Moquegua. On 22 March, at Los Ángeles, a Peruvian force blocked the road but was defeated, and the Chilean army secured the Moquegua valley and the approaches to Tacna. These operations cut the Allied army at Tacna off from the coast and from supplies.

The Allied army, assembled around Tacna under Peruvian and Bolivian command, numbered in the low-to-mid teens of thousands by most estimates, including Bolivian forces under Campero and Peruvian forces under Admiral Lizardo Montero. It took up a position on the plateau of Intiorko, known as the Campo de la Alianza. On 26 May 1880, the Chilean army under General Manuel Baquedano attacked. The battle lasted several hours, with heavy casualties on both sides, and ended in Chilean victory. Estimates of dead and wounded vary, but the losses were in the thousands for both armies. The Allied army broke and retreated, and Chile occupied Tacna.

For Bolivia the battle was effectively the end of the war in the field. Campero withdrew the surviving Bolivian forces to the highlands, and Bolivia did not again send a major army against Chile. This did not end Bolivia's formal participation in the war, which continued until the truce of 1884, but it marked a fundamental asymmetry: Peru would continue fighting for another three years, while Bolivia's war was effectively over. The distinction is essential, because Bolivia's territorial loss came through Chilean occupation of the Litoral, not through a decisive defeat of a Bolivian army on its own territory.

The costs were immense and unevenly shared. The battle shattered both the Peruvian and Bolivian field armies in the south, and Peru lost many officers and soldiers who could not be replaced. Chilean casualties were also heavy. The battle also left Tacna, an important Peruvian city, in Chilean hands, a fact whose consequences would last for decades through the unresolved plebiscite provision of the Treaty of Ancón.

Section 65.12

The Battle of Arica and the Collapse of Southern Peruvian Defenses

Arica, a port at the foot of a steep headland called El Morro, was the last major Peruvian position in the south. Its garrison, commanded by Colonel Francisco Bolognesi, numbered perhaps around two thousand men, with fortifications, mines, and artillery. Its capture would give Chile complete control of the southern approaches and open a rail connection to Tacna. Chile's army, after Tacna, pivoted to Arica, and the Chilean commander Baquedano demanded a surrender, which Bolognesi rejected. Tradition attributes defiant words to him, but the precise phrasing is traditional and is not documented in contemporary sources.

On the morning of 7 June 1880, Chilean infantry stormed the Morro in a rapid assault that lasted little more than an hour. The Peruvian garrison, outnumbered and with fortifications that could not be fully manned, was largely destroyed or captured. Bolognesi was killed, along with most of his senior officers. Peruvian losses were severe, and Chilean losses, while smaller, were also significant. The commemorative tradition in Peru also includes the story of Colonel Alfonso Ugarte, who is said to have ridden his horse off the Morro rather than surrender, a narrative that historians treat with caution as legend.

With Arica taken, the Allied armies in the south had ceased to exist as organized forces. The Chilean navy, which had blockaded the Peruvian coast since 1879, now held the southern ports, and Chile's army held the interior south of Lima. The political result was that Peru's government in Lima, under Piérola, had to decide whether to seek peace or to continue the war. The decision would be made in the context of American diplomatic mediation and of the continued Chilean advance, described in the next division.

The battle belonged to the aftermath of Tacna, not to a new independent campaign. Chile had deprived the Allied army of its main field position, while Arica still offered a defended harbor, supplies, and a communications link to the interior. Colonel Pedro Lagos directed the Chilean attacking formations under the overall command of General Manuel Baquedano. Assault parties moved against several fortified positions before turning toward the Morro; because the headland dominated the harbor, the fighting there carried significance beyond its limited acreage. Peruvian defenders included regulars and local volunteers, and their artillery and mines imposed a serious danger despite Chilean numerical superiority. The exact strength and casualty totals vary with national military records and later commemorations and should not be treated as established simply because a traditional figure is frequently repeated.

The death of Bolognesi and the collapse of his garrison became especially important in Peruvian commemorative culture, but the historical question is broader than individual martyrdom. Accounts of the final assault include allegations of mistreatment or killing of men after resistance had collapsed, and should be evaluated against contemporary testimony from both sides rather than accepted indiscriminately as a single narrative. For Chile, the immediate military result was possession of a strategic port and fortification; for Peru, it was the loss of another coastal base, though not the disappearance of its government, forces elsewhere, or capacity for continued resistance. The distinction helps explain why the later campaign against Lima and the prolonged occupation were necessary to Chile's political objectives.

Division IV
The Fall of Lima, Occupation, and Peruvian Resistance
Sections 65.13–65.16 · 1880–1883
Section 65.13

The Road to Lima: Diplomacy, Raids, and Chile's Strategic Decision

After Arica, the United States offered to mediate. In October 1880, delegations from Chile, Peru, and Bolivia met aboard the USS Lackawanna, anchored off Arica, with American ministers to the three countries presiding. The Chilean delegates laid out seven conditions in the protocol of 22 October: cession of specified Peruvian and Bolivian territories south of the Camarones line and west of a proposed Andean boundary; a joint indemnity of twenty million pesos, with four million payable immediately; restitution of certain Chilean property; return of the captured transport Rímac; abrogation of the secret Peru–Bolivia alliance; Chilean retention of Moquegua, Tacna, and Arica until the stipulated obligations were met; and a restriction on future Peruvian fortification of Arica. These were Chilean demands, not terms accepted by Peru and Bolivia. Peru and Bolivia refused territorial cession, and the conference ended after a few days without agreement. The episode showed how far apart the parties were: Chile, having won on the battlefield, wanted a territorial settlement, while Peru, whose capital was still unoccupied, would not accept one.

The United States was an intermediary, not an architect of the outcome. American diplomacy in this period was inconsistent. Some ministers favored Peru, and in 1881 Secretary of State James Blaine pursued a policy sympathetic to Peru that annoyed Chile, but his successor abandoned it after Blaine left office. The Chilean government, aware of its military advantage, resisted outside pressure. Britain and France, whose investors had an interest in the outcome, also watched events but did not intervene.

While diplomacy failed, Chile conducted raids along the Peruvian northern coast. In 1880 a naval squadron under Patricio Lynch, carrying troops, struck coastal towns and sugar estates, demanding forced contributions and destroying property where they were not paid. The raids damaged the coastal economy and heightened social tensions on estates with Chinese indentured labor. They also taught Chilean commanders about the coast and strengthened a faction that wanted a decisive blow against Lima rather than a prolonged occupation of the south.

Chile's government decided to attack Lima. In December 1880, an expeditionary army of roughly twenty-five thousand men landed south of the capital near Pisco and Curayacu. Peru's dictator Piérola prepared the defense of Lima with two lines of fortifications south of the city, at Chorrillos and Miraflores, and mobilized reserves from the capital's population, including many men with little training. Peruvian political divisions persisted: Piérola's regime had critics among the civilian elite who thought the war unwinnable. The stage was set for the largest battles of the war.

The protocol is useful because it demonstrates the distance between battlefield success and a negotiated settlement. Chile did not merely request security guarantees or compensation for previous losses; its representatives demanded strategically significant territory as the price of peace. Peruvian and Bolivian negotiators could view that demand as a violation of their territorial integrity even while recognizing their weakened position in the war. Conversely, Chilean leaders argued that territory was an essential guarantee after expensive campaigns. The presence of American representatives provided a diplomatic forum but did not give Washington the authority to impose an agreement. The conference's failure intensified the argument within Chile for bringing further military pressure to bear on Lima.

The published conference protocols (Chilean National Congress Library) identify the delegates, dates, and demands; the documentary record therefore supports more than a generic claim that peace talks failed. It also shows that American diplomatic presence did not mean the United States authored Chile's conditions. For students, comparing this written proposal with the eventual treaty of 1883 is a way to see how wartime claims changed in response to continued fighting and Peruvian political fragmentation.

Section 65.14

Chorrillos, Miraflores, and the Occupation of Lima, 1881

On 13 January 1881, the Chilean army attacked the Peruvian first line at Chorrillos, a seaside town south of Lima, in a battle that lasted most of the day and ended in Chilean victory. The Peruvian line was dispersed. The fighting was costly and bloody. In the town of Chorrillos, which had been a resort for wealthy Lima families, the fighting and the aftermath involved burning and looting, and the nearby resort of Barranco suffered damage as well. Responsibility for the destruction is debated: contemporary accounts blame Chilean troops, and Chilean sources point to the conditions of combat and the behavior of some defenders.

On 15 January, Chilean forces attacked the second Peruvian line at Miraflores, again defeating a defense made up of regular units and civilian reserves. Casualties in the two battles were in the thousands for both armies, and Peruvian dead included many men of the Lima reserve. Foreign residents in Lima, including volunteer fire companies drawn from the immigrant communities, had formed part of the city's defense and suffered losses. The capital's authorities had little choice but to seek terms.

Chilean forces entered Lima on 17 January 1881. Piérola left the capital with part of his forces and went into the interior, while Peru's civil authorities in the capital, who wanted peace, set up a provisional government at La Magdalena under Francisco García Calderón. The Chilean occupation authorities, led by Patricio Lynch, who was named commander of the occupation, pressed García Calderón to accept the cession of Tarapacá. When he refused, they arrested him and sent him to Chile in late 1881, an act that deepened Peruvian resentment. Piérola resigned his dictatorship later in 1881, and authority in Peru fragmented among competing claimants.

The occupation of the capital did not end the war. Peru had lost its political center, but it had no single authority that could sign a treaty, and the leaders who held out, including Admiral Montero in Arequipa and Cáceres in the central highlands, declined to accept terms. The Chilean government faced a dilemma: it had won the capital but could not compel a peace, and it now needed to garrison a large and hostile country far from home.

Section 65.15

Occupied Peru: Civilians, Cultural Institutions, and the Human Cost of War

The Chilean occupation of Lima and the coast lasted until 1884. The occupation authorities administered the city, collected customs and taxes, and levied contributions on residents and estates to support the army. Requisitions and forced contributions fell heavily on landowners, merchants, and ordinary households, and sugar and cotton estates on the coast suffered damage and loss of labor. The occupation was also a period of uncertainty about law and authority, since Peru's own state existed in fragments.

Cultural institutions became a controversy. The National Library of Peru in Lima lost thousands of volumes to Chilean removal, and the collections of other institutions, including the University of San Marcos and museums, were also affected. Chilean authorities argued that the material was war booty, and Peruvian authors regarded it as plunder. The writer Ricardo Palma, who later led the effort to rebuild the library, appealed to donors across the world for books, and the library reopened in 1884. Partial returns of volumes from Chile have occurred in the early twenty-first century, and the episode remains part of the memory of the war in Peru.

The social effects of the war varied across Peru's heterogeneous society. On the coast, Chinese indentured laborers, who had endured harsh conditions on estates, took advantage of the confusion: some rose against their employers, and some cooperated with Chilean forces, and Chinese communities then suffered reprisals from Peruvians who accused them of collaboration. In Lima, crowds attacked Chinese businesses around the time of the capital's fall. Afro-Peruvians, many of whom worked as urban laborers and soldiers, served in the armies and were also blamed by elites for disorder. Historians caution that these episodes reflect existing racial tensions as much as wartime choices.

Women's roles were essential and often invisible in official records. The Peruvian and Bolivian armies were accompanied by women called rabonas, who carried supplies, cooked, nursed the wounded, and sometimes fought, and the Chilean army had its own cantineras and nurses. Women in occupied towns managed households, provided food and shelter to soldiers, and faced shortages and displacement. Memory has recorded a few names, but most women's experiences are known only in outline.

Occupation altered daily choices in ways that formal military histories rarely record. Families needed to secure food and fuel while prices and employment fluctuated; merchants decided whether to trade with occupying soldiers; municipal officials had to administer sanitation, markets, policing, and poor relief under competing orders. For workers on sugar and cotton estates, the collapse of employers' authority could sometimes create opportunities to leave coercive labor arrangements, but it could also expose them to retaliation or the loss of their livelihoods. The experiences of Chinese migrants were especially varied, and participation by some individuals in resistance to former employers cannot be extrapolated to all Chinese Peruvians. Likewise, not every act of disorder in occupied Lima should be treated as an expression of the same political allegiance.

Cultural losses require equally careful source handling. Ricardo Palma's appeals described an institution in ruin, yet his correspondence was also a purposeful effort to obtain donations. The work of rebuilding the National Library included books collected locally, books returned by individuals or governments, and donations solicited abroad; a library could be reconstructed materially even while arguments about the legitimacy of wartime seizures persisted. Recent scholarship from Peru's National Library has emphasized that displaced volumes circulated through several channels, not only official removal to Chile. The destruction or dislocation of books and archives also affected later generations' capacity to investigate the war itself.

Everyday Life and Social History

Conscripts, Camp Followers, Workers, and Displaced Families

Chile's volunteers and conscripts came largely from central and southern Chile, including rural laborers and artisans, and they marched through a desert where water and fresh food were scarce and disease was a major killer. In Bolivia and Peru, Aymara and Quechua men were conscripted from highland communities, often with little notice and with limited training, and many never returned. Their families lost labor at planting and harvest, and the burden of supporting the army, through food and animals, often fell on village communities.

Nitrate workers lived in the desert under harsh conditions: the oficinas were isolated, dependent on company supplies, and subject to the discipline of employers. When fighting reached Tarapacá, many workers fled, joined armies, or changed employers, and after the war the province's population shifted toward Chilean migrants. In the highlands, villages hosted guerrillas, fed them, and bore Chilean reprisals. After the war, many soldiers returned to poverty, and widows and orphans sought pensions that were slow or unavailable. The war's human cost is recorded in memoirs, parish records, and petitions, but most of its victims left few written traces.

Section 65.16

Andrés Avelino Cáceres and the Campaign of the Breña, 1881–1883

After the fall of Lima, resistance moved to the central highlands. Andrés Avelino Cáceres, an officer who had fought at Tarapacá and Miraflores, escaped to Ayacucho and organized an army of regular soldiers and local volunteers. The highlands of Junín and the Mantaro valley, a region of Andean peasant communities and haciendas, became the main theater. The Chilean garrisons there were small and far from supply, and they faced a rugged landscape in which local knowledge was decisive. The term Breña, meaning a rough, rugged terrain, came to name the campaign.

The war here took a different form. Guerrilla bands called montoneras, organized by communities and local leaders, harassed Chilean columns, cut communications, and provided intelligence and supplies to Cáceres. Chilean forces responded by requisitioning food and punishing villages they believed hostile, which increased local support for resistance. In July 1882, at La Concepción, a Chilean garrison of about eighty men was surrounded and destroyed by Peruvian forces, including many local fighters. The episode is a central memory in Chile as an act of heroic sacrifice, and in Peru it is remembered as evidence of local resistance. The Chilean response involved the burning of villages.

Peru's resistance was not unified. Cáceres relied on peasants but also on landowners and officers, and relations between them were tense, since communities had grievances that predated the war. Some historians, such as Nelson Manrique, emphasized peasant nationalism, and others, such as Florencia Mallon, emphasized the way class and ethnic conflict shaped the resistance, with communities fighting for local goals as much as for the Peruvian state. In 1883, Chilean forces under Colonel Alejandro Gorostiaga defeated Cáceres at Huamachuco on 10 July, a battle that ended the effective capacity of his army to confront Chile.

Parallel to the military resistance was a political split. General Miguel Iglesias, in northern Peru, issued the Grito de Montán in August 1882 calling for peace even at the cost of cession, and he was recognized by Chile as the Peruvian authority willing to negotiate. Cáceres and others denounced him. In 1883, with the resistance exhausted and the country devastated, Iglesias's government signed the Treaty of Ancón. The settlement opened a civil war in Peru between Iglesias and Cáceres, which Cáceres won in 1885, and he later became president.

Section 65.17

The Treaty of Ancón and the Territorial Settlement with Peru, 1883

The Treaty of Ancón was signed on 20 October 1883 at the town of Ancón, near Lima, by Chile's plenipotentiary Jovino Novoa and Peru's plenipotentiaries José Antonio de Lavalle and Mariano Castro Zaldívar, representing the Iglesias government. Its first article restored peace. Its second article provided for Peru's cession to Chile, perpetually and unconditionally, of Tarapacá. Its third article placed the provinces of Tacna and Arica under Chilean control for ten years, after which a plebiscite would decide which country would hold them, with the winner paying the other a sum of ten million pesos. Other provisions dealt with guano revenue and with Peruvian obligations.

The treaty's legal character matters. Tarapacá was ceded permanently, while Tacna and Arica were held conditionally. The plebiscite provision created an unresolved question: who would vote, under what rules, and under whose administration? Chile governed the two provinces and pursued policies of Chilenization, including closing Peruvian schools and pressuring Peruvian clergy and institutions, and Peru contended that the conditions for a fair vote did not exist. The plebiscite was never held, and the two governments disputed the question for four decades, through failed negotiations, an arbitration by the United States president in the 1920s, and a plebiscite commission that could not function.

The matter was settled by the Treaty of Lima on 3 June 1929. Tacna returned to Peru, and Arica stayed under Chilean sovereignty. Under Article VI Chile undertook to pay Peru six million United States dollars and to transfer specified public works and state-owned property in the territory restored to Peru. Article V required Chile to construct facilities in Arica for Peru's commerce, including a wharf, customs agency, and terminal for the Tacna railway, with special transit arrangements. A supplementary protocol restricted either country's cession of the relevant territories to a third state without the other's prior agreement, a provision with implications for later Bolivian corridor proposals. The settlement closed the last territorial question between Chile and Peru, though the memory of the war persisted.

The delayed plebiscite also exposed the difficulty of turning a promise of popular sovereignty into an administrable international procedure. Who qualified as a voter after years of Chilean rule, schooling, migration, and policing? What institutions could ensure campaigning and voting without coercion? The 1920s commission associated with United States good offices encountered severe obstacles, and the two governments ultimately chose diplomatic partition over the ballot envisaged in 1883. Tacna and Arica were consequently governed for decades under arrangements different from those originally anticipated. That history illustrates the distinction between an agreement's legal formula and its implementation on the ground.

The 1883 treaty also involved economic claims arising from the guano deposits and Peru's creditors. Those questions mattered because the governments and foreign bondholders understood control over exports as a means of servicing debt. A settlement written as a border agreement could therefore reorganize customs flows, property rights, and international financial claims at the same time. The durability of the Tarapacá cession did not depend on the ultimately unfulfilled Tacna–Arica plebiscite; the two arrangements were placed in different articles and carried different legal consequences.

Historical Vocabulary
Cession, Plebiscite, and Truce

Cession is the formal transfer of territory by treaty. A plebiscite is a direct vote by a population on a question such as sovereignty. A truce suspends hostilities without settling their cause.

The three terms distinguish the settlements in this chapter: Tarapacá was ceded in 1883, Tacna and Arica were left to a plebiscite that never took place, and Bolivia's coast was held under a truce in 1884 before cession in 1904. Treating all outcomes as "annexation" hides these legal differences.

Section 65.18

Bolivia's Loss of Its Coast: The 1884 Truce and the Treaty of 1904

Bolivia's settlement followed a different path from Peru's. After Tacna, Bolivia was effectively out of the war, but it had not signed a peace. On 4 April 1884, at Valparaíso, Bolivia and Chile signed a truce. It established an indefinite truce and expressly declared the state of war terminated, while reserving the possibility of a future definitive peace treaty; the text was therefore neither a simple battlefield ceasefire nor a formal territorial cession. Chile retained control and administration of the Litoral, with the right to govern the territory and its resources, and the truce set out arrangements for customs and commerce. It did not formally cede the territory, and it left open the final terms of peace. For Bolivia, the difference between occupation and cession mattered, since it left room to negotiate.

The negotiation took twenty years. During that time, Bolivian governments, mostly conservative and mining-oriented, explored different possibilities, including a transfer of Tacna and Arica, in discussions that came to nothing. Chile built the railway from Antofagasta to Bolivian mining centers, and the two states debated the meaning of commercial access. Bolivia also faced other territorial pressures, including the loss of the Acre territory to Brazil in 1903. In this context, the Bolivian government concluded that a formal settlement with Chile was preferable to continued uncertainty.

The Treaty of Peace and Friendship was signed on 20 October 1904. Bolivia recognized Chilean sovereignty over the Litoral in perpetuity. In exchange, Article III committed Chile to construct the Arica–La Paz railway and made additional commitments concerning Bolivian railway development; Article IV required payment of £300,000 in two installments; Article V allocated separate funds to specified Bolivian debt and compensation obligations; and Article VI recognized Bolivia's permanent and broad right of commercial transit through Chilean territory and Pacific ports. Article VII addressed Bolivian customs agencies in designated ports, initially Antofagasta and Arica. The railway was completed in 1913. The treaty gave Bolivia transit rights and infrastructure but not sovereign access to the sea. Bolivian governments later argued that Chile had promised more in subsequent discussions, and Chilean governments argued the treaty settled the issue.

The 1904 document is especially important because it distinguished possession, sovereignty, and commercial access. Article II recognized Chile's permanent title over territories it had administered under the truce, and the new boundary was described in extensive topographic detail. Free transit meant that Bolivian merchandise could use designated routes and port arrangements without Bolivia owning a sovereign seacoast. That distinction made the treaty workable for trade while leaving a powerful symbol of territorial loss in Bolivian political life. It is inaccurate to treat the railway and cash obligations as equivalent to the return of a port: they were infrastructure and financial commitments within an agreement that accepted Chilean sovereignty.

The railway commitment had practical consequences for mining districts, merchants, and communities far from the diplomatic negotiations. Lower-cost connections could help highland exporters reach external markets, while routing a country's commerce through another state's territory created persistent questions about tariffs, customs operations, and transit administration. The completed Arica–La Paz link in 1913 formed part of this new network. Over the following decades, Bolivian appeals for sovereign access and Chilean insistence on the binding nature of the settlement became enduring features of bilateral diplomacy. These later controversies should be traced without retroactively changing what the 1884 and 1904 texts actually provided.

Section 65.19

Nitrate Wealth, State Reconstruction, Labor, and the New Pacific Economy

For Chile, victory brought a nitrate boom. Export duties on nitrate became a very large share of state revenue, by many estimates around half of ordinary income in the 1890s, which allowed the government to lower other taxes, build railways and schools, and modernize the army, including with German instructors. Yet the industry itself passed largely into foreign hands. After the war, Chile decided to honor the certificates Peru had issued when it bought nitrate works, returning properties to certificate holders. Investors, notably British entrepreneurs such as John Thomas North, who had bought certificates cheaply, gained control of many works. Historians therefore describe the nitrate economy as a Chilean fiscal windfall combined with foreign ownership of much of the industry, not as a simple transfer of wealth to Chilean elites.

The nitrate zone's labor system was harsh. Workers lived in company camps in the desert, were often paid in tokens redeemable at company stores, and had limited legal protection. Labor organizing grew, and strikes were met with repression. The most notorious episode was the massacre at the Santa María school in Iquique in December 1907, when troops fired on striking workers and their families; the official January 1908 investigation recorded 126 dead and 135 injured, while other contemporary testimony, later scholarship, and commemorative tradition have offered substantially higher figures. The especially prominent figure of 3,600 belongs to later cultural memory and is not an independently verified death toll. The industry declined in the twentieth century with the development of synthetic nitrogen, and the boom ended in the 1920s and 1930s, leaving ghost towns in the desert.

Peru's recovery was slow. The war left the state bankrupt, the coastal economy damaged, and the political system divided. Under Cáceres and later governments, Peru restructured its debts. In 1889 the Grace Contract transferred control of its railways to British bondholders in exchange for cancellation of the external debt, and the resulting Peruvian Corporation operated them for decades. Peru shifted its economy toward sugar, cotton, and mining, and a civilian oligarchy dominated politics after the 1890s. The highlands, where the war's rural costs were heaviest, saw rising tension between landowners and communities.

Bolivia's economy was reshaped differently. Without a coast, it relied on rail connections through Chile and Peru, and its mining sector changed from silver to tin by the early twentieth century. The conservative oligarchy that governed in the 1880s and 1890s gave way to the Liberal revolution of 1899, which moved the seat of government toward La Paz. Landlocked status shaped trade costs and diplomacy. The war's economic benefits, in short, were uneven: Chile gained revenue, foreign investors gained assets, Peru lost its nitrate province and rebuilt slowly, and Bolivia lost its coast.

The costs and gains from nitrate taxation were not distributed evenly. Chilean state revenue benefited from export duties even where the oficinas themselves were privately or foreign owned. Investors could profit from concessions, rail transport, and international sales, whereas workers bore the physical burden of desert extraction. Their bargaining position depended on isolation, access to company stores, and the ability to organize collectively. This economic distinction is necessary for understanding why Chile's victorious state could become wealthier while its northern mining camps remained settings of acute labor conflict. The Santa María violence of 1907 belongs to the later labor history of the nitrate region, not to the military war of 1879–1884, but it illuminates the social order created after conquest.

Section 65.20

The War's Legacy: National Memory, Maritime Claims, and the Pacific Balance of Power

Each country built a national memory of the war. In Chile, the war is remembered as a defensive and ultimately victorious struggle, with Prat, the heroes of La Concepción, and the army as central symbols, and 21 May is a national holiday. In Peru, the war is remembered as a national catastrophe met with heroism, with Grau, Bolognesi, and Cáceres as exemplary figures, and 8 October, the day of Angamos, is a naval commemoration. In Bolivia, the war is remembered above all as the loss of the sea, with Abaroa as the emblem of resistance, and 23 March is the Day of the Sea. These traditions are valuable historical evidence about identity, but they often rely on legendary elements, such as famous last words, that cannot be verified.

The territorial settlements continued to generate disputes. Chile's occupation of Tarapacá and its retention of Arica shaped Peru's politics for decades. Bolivia's demand for sovereign access to the sea became a standing foreign-policy goal, and in 2013 Bolivia brought a case against Chile to the International Court of Justice, arguing that Chile was obliged to negotiate. In October 2018, the Court ruled that Chile had no such obligation. In 2014, the same court had settled a maritime boundary dispute between Peru and Chile. These cases show that questions rooted in the war remained legally active well into the twenty-first century, and that the court's decisions did not erase the memory of the war.

The war also reshaped the regional balance. Chile emerged as the strongest naval and military power on the Pacific coast, and its army and navy modernized. With the northern frontier secured, its government turned to the southern frontier, completing the occupation of the Araucanía, the Mapuche homeland, by 1883, an outcome Chapter 38 and later chapters place in a longer history of Mapuche resistance and dispossession. Peru and Bolivia rebuilt more slowly. Argentina, which had stayed out of the war, concluded boundary treaties with Chile and later competed with it in naval armament. The balance of power along the Pacific changed in ways that affected diplomacy for generations.

Returning to the map shows the chapter's argument. The war began as a dispute over a tax and a boundary in a desert. It ended with Chile extended north to include Antofagasta and Tarapacá, Peru reduced and recovering Tacna only in 1929, and Bolivia without a coast. The change came from combinations of mineral wealth, treaty ambiguity, naval power, occupation, and political fragmentation. It also left a long inheritance of grievance and legal claim, which is why a war fought between 1879 and 1884 remains a living part of the politics of the Pacific coast.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the War of the Pacific transformed mineral-rich frontier disputes into a lasting reorganization of territory, economic power, and national identity. It began with a landing at Antofagasta on 14 February 1879 and ended, in legal terms, only with the Treaty of Lima in 1929, and it involved three states, each with its own political crises and priorities.

The economic background was the shift from guano to nitrate. Guano had financed the Peruvian state in the mid-nineteenth century and left it dependent and indebted. Nitrate, found in Tarapacá and the Antofagasta zone, became the next great export, drawing Chilean and British capital and Chilean labor into territory that Peru and Bolivia claimed but poorly administered. Silver strikes at Caracoles added to Chilean migration into the Bolivian Litoral.

Boundary treaties tried to manage these pressures. The treaty of 1866 divided the territory at the 24th parallel and shared revenue between the 23rd and 25th. The treaty of 1874 ended revenue sharing and barred new Bolivian taxes on Chilean capital for twenty-five years. Its wording left room for conflicting readings. Meanwhile Peru and Bolivia signed a secret defensive alliance in 1873, and Peru adopted a nitrate monopoly that strained its finances.

Bolivia's 1878 tax on nitrate exports brought these tensions to a head. The company refused to pay, Chile demanded arbitration, Bolivia seized the company's assets, and Chile occupied Antofagasta. Peru's decision to honor its treaty obligation brought a declaration of war in April 1879. Historians reject explanations based on any single cause, including conspiracy by British investors, and emphasize the interaction of treaty interpretation, fiscal need, economic interest, and domestic politics.

The war's first phase was naval. Command of the sea was essential because the desert could not be crossed on foot. The events of 21 May 1879 at Iquique and Punta Gruesa, the raids of the Huáscar, and its capture at Angamos on 8 October gave Chile naval supremacy. The landing at Pisagua on 2 November opened the land campaign, but naval power did not by itself defeat the Allied armies.

In the desert campaigns, Chile won at Dolores, lost at Tarapacá, and took Tarapacá anyway because Peru could not sustain its victory. Political crises in Bolivia and Peru, including the overthrow of Daza and Piérola's coup, weakened Allied command. In 1880, Chile's victories at Tacna and Arica destroyed Allied forces in the south and effectively removed Bolivia from active fighting, though its formal war lasted until 1884.

The fall of Lima in January 1881 did not end the war. Peru's government fragmented, Chile's occupation brought requisitions, cultural losses, and social tensions involving Chinese, Afro-Peruvian, and Indigenous communities, and women's labor sustained armies and households. In the central highlands, Cáceres and rural communities carried on a guerrilla campaign, producing events like La Concepción and Huamachuco, and revealing both national feeling and local conflicts among Peruvians.

The war's settlements differed in legal form. The Treaty of Ancón in 1883 ceded Tarapacá and deferred Tacna and Arica to a plebiscite that never occurred, until the Treaty of Lima in 1929 returned Tacna to Peru and left Arica with Chile. Bolivia's truce of 1884 preserved occupation without cession, and the treaty of 1904 formalized Chile's sovereignty in exchange for transit rights and a railway.

Economically, Chile gained a nitrate-based fiscal windfall, though foreign investors owned much of the industry. Peru rebuilt slowly, restructured debt, and shifted toward other exports, while Bolivia, landlocked, moved from silver to tin. Nitrate workers faced harsh labor conditions and repression, and the industry declined in the twentieth century. National memories in all three countries, with their heroes and commemorations, preserved the war as a defining experience, and legal disputes including the International Court of Justice cases of 2014 and 2018 show how long its consequences lasted.

Comparing National Memories of the War of the Pacific

This project asks you to compare how Chile, Peru, and Bolivia remember the war and to separate documentary evidence from patriotic tradition.

  1. Select an episode. Choose Iquique, Angamos, Arica, La Concepción, or Topáter.
  2. Gather contemporary evidence. Locate official reports, newspaper accounts, and soldiers' memoirs from the relevant side or sides.
  3. Gather memorial traditions. Locate school textbooks, commemorations, monuments, and popular accounts from each country.
  4. Compare. Identify agreements, contradictions, and legendary elements, such as famous last words, that lack contemporary documentation.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what can be reconstructed with confidence, what reflects later memory, and how each nation's use of the episode serves present identity.

Chapter Reflection

The War of the Pacific shows how a remote desert frontier could become the center of regional politics when global demand made its resources valuable and when weak treaties left its sovereignty unclear. The conflict was not inevitable, but it grew from decisions made under fiscal pressure, political rivalry, and mutual suspicion in three capitals.

The chapter also shows how war reshapes states differently. Chile consolidated its position and funded its state through nitrate. Peru experienced collapse, occupation, and a rural resistance that exposed both national feeling and deep social divisions. Bolivia lost its coastline without a decisive national defeat and has lived with that loss ever since. Indigenous communities, women, workers, and migrants, whose experiences are less visible in official records, carried much of the war's burden.

Its legacies connect forward. Nitrate and the labor system it produced fed the labor movements of northern Chile, and the debt, railway, and export arrangements of the 1880s and 1890s belong to the export economies examined in Chapter 68. The Araucanía's final occupation links to Indigenous dispossession traced in Chapters 38 and 69. The consolidation of Chilean power also frames the hemispheric order of Chapter 70.

Chapter 66 turns to Brazil, where abolition and the fall of the monarchy reshaped a very different society. Students should carry forward the lesson that nineteenth-century states were remade by war, resources, and law together, and that settlements on paper could leave questions open for generations.
