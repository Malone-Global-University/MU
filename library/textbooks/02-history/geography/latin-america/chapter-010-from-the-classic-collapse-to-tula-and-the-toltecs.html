From the Classic Collapse to Tula and the Toltecs

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A World Reorganized, Not a World Destroyed

Beginning by roughly the fifth century CE, communities occupied the low rise above the Tula River, in what is today the Mexican state of Hidalgo; by about 600–650 CE they were constructing the monumental center now called Tula Chico, and over succeeding generations that settlement grew into one of the largest and most influential cities Mesoamerica had yet produced. They were not founding a city in an empty landscape, and they were not the first or only people responding to the transformed political world left behind by the decline of Teotihuacan's centuries-long dominance over central Mexico. They were building within, and adding to, a Mesoamerica already reorganizing itself in dozens of places at once.

Chapter 8 examined Teotihuacan at the height of its influence, and Chapter 9 traced the prolonged, regionally uneven process by which Classic Maya dynastic kingship broke down across the southern lowlands between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries. Both chapters resisted a single dramatic word — "collapse" — as an adequate description of what actually happened, insisting instead on migration, warfare, drought, dynastic failure, and political fragmentation operating differently from city to city and region to region. This chapter continues that same historical discipline while shifting attention to a different, though overlapping, transformation: the reorganization of central Mexico and much of highland Mesoamerica during the centuries archaeologists generally call the Epiclassic and Early Postclassic periods, roughly the seventh through twelfth centuries CE.

At the center of this chapter stands the city of Tula, in the modern state of Hidalgo, capital of the people later Nahua tradition would remember as the Toltecs. Tula's monumental architecture — its stepped pyramids, its colonnaded halls, and above all its famous carved stone Atlantean warrior columns atop Pyramid B — has fascinated visitors since the sixteenth century, when Spanish chroniclers recording Nahua oral tradition first wrote down elaborate accounts of a legendary city called Tollan, ruled by a wise and eventually exiled priest-king named Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, whose Toltec subjects were remembered as the inventors of nearly every worthwhile art and craft in Mesoamerica. Those later accounts are themselves a form of historical evidence, and this chapter takes them seriously as such. But they are not a substitute for archaeology, and one of this chapter's central tasks is to keep the excavated, physically documented city of Tula analytically separate from the legendary Tollan of later Nahua memory, even while explaining why the two became so thoroughly intertwined in both ancient and modern imagination.

This chapter also asks students to resist a second, equally tempting oversimplification: the idea of a unified "Toltec Empire" that conquered much of Mesoamerica, imposed its artistic style wherever its armies marched, and single-handedly exported Feathered Serpent religion, warrior imagery, and urban planning across the region. The archaeological evidence for Tula's political and military reach is considerably more modest, and considerably more ambiguous, than this popular image suggests. Shared architectural forms, shared iconography, and shared ritual practices across Epiclassic and Early Postclassic Mesoamerica are real and important, but they do not automatically prove conquest, tribute, or centralized political control. This chapter will repeatedly ask a question that recurs throughout Mesoamerican archaeology: when two distant places share a symbol, a building style, or a technology, does that similarity reflect migration, diplomacy, pilgrimage, trade, shared religious participation, direct political domination, or some combination of these processes that leaves no single unambiguous archaeological signature? Learning to hold that question open, rather than reaching automatically for the most dramatic answer, is one of this chapter's central goals.

The centuries this chapter examines were not, in any single Mesoamerican region, centuries of simple decline. Teotihuacan's diminished political centrality did not mean the disappearance of the people, agricultural systems, religious traditions, or trade networks of central Mexico; it meant a redistribution of population and power among a larger number of smaller, often fortified, and sometimes newly founded centers. Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, and Cholula — examined in this chapter alongside Tula — each demonstrate a different facet of this reorganized political landscape: defensive planning and hilltop fortification at Xochicalco, hybrid artistic vocabularies bridging Maya and central Mexican traditions at Cacaxtla, and remarkable religious continuity and long-term importance at Cholula. Tula did not simply replace Teotihuacan as the region's dominant power; it developed as one major center within a landscape already characterized by multiple competing and cooperating polities, migration, and cultural exchange.

This chapter also introduces students to one of Mesoamerican archaeology's more difficult interpretive problems: the relationship between changes in material culture — particularly a distinctive style of pottery archaeologists call Coyotlatelco ware — and the movement of actual human populations. For much of the twentieth century, the appearance of Coyotlatelco pottery across central Mexico was read as straightforward evidence that a new ethnic group, sometimes identified with later "Chichimec" or northern peoples, had migrated into the region only after Teotihuacan's political collapse, displacing or absorbing the area's earlier Teotihuacan-affiliated inhabitants. Revised radiocarbon chronology, discussed in detail later in this chapter, overturns a key part of this picture: the earliest Coyotlatelco-related settlement at Tula now appears to date to roughly the fifth century CE, a period when Teotihuacan still dominated much of the surrounding region and centuries before that city's political decline. This overlapping, rather than sequential, chronology suggests ongoing population movement, local continuity, and gradual reorganization occurring well before, during, and after Teotihuacan's decline, rather than a single decisive invasion arriving into a post-collapse vacuum.

Finally, this chapter situates Tula's rise and fall within a chronological framework that continues to be actively refined by archaeologists. Tula did not spring into existence as a fully formed imperial capital; the earliest settlement at the site dates to roughly the fifth century CE, and monumental construction at an earlier center called Tula Chico began around 600–650 CE, continuing for roughly two centuries before the city's focus shifted to the much larger complex known as Tula Grande, which reached its greatest size and influence during what archaeologists call the Tollan phase, roughly from the tenth through the mid-twelfth centuries CE. Its decline, generally dated to around 1150 CE, did not occur through a single dramatic invasion documented in the archaeological record, whatever later legendary accounts may suggest; abandonment and the later burning of some of the city's principal monuments now appear, on current evidence, to be separable events rather than a single catastrophic episode. Understanding this chronology carefully — and understanding what it does and does not establish — is essential preparation for Chapter 11's turn to late Oaxaca, Chapter 12's examination of the Postclassic Maya world (including Chichén Itzá, whose relationship to Tula this chapter can only introduce), and Chapter 13's account of the Mexica migration into the Basin of Mexico, a migration that Mexica tradition would later frame, in part, as an inheritance of Toltec civilization itself.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Describe the political and demographic changes in central Mexico following the decline of Teotihuacan's centralized authority.
  3. Compare Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, and Cholula as distinct Epiclassic centers illustrating regionalization, fortification, artistic hybridity, and religious continuity.
  4. Evaluate the relationship between changes in material culture, such as Coyotlatelco pottery, and claims about migration and ethnic identity.
  5. Trace Tula's development from the earlier Tula Chico settlement to the larger Tollan-phase city of Tula Grande.

  7. Analyze the political and military symbolism embodied in Tula's Atlantean warrior columns without treating that imagery as literal historical portraiture or automatic proof of conquest.
  8. Explain the Feathered Serpent religious tradition as a broader Epiclassic and Early Postclassic Mesoamerican phenomenon rather than a Tula invention exported by conquest.
  9. Assess Tula's participation in long-distance exchange networks, including obsidian sourcing and connections to western Mexico and more distant regions.
  10. Distinguish the archaeologically documented city of Tula from later Nahua traditions concerning Tollan, the Toltecs, and Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the architectural and iconographic similarities between Tula and Chichén Itzá without assuming a single settled answer.
  12. Explain the decline of Tula around 1150 CE as a complex process rather than the result of a single documented invasion.

Historical Context

This chapter builds directly on the two chapters that precede it in Part II. Chapter 8 examined Teotihuacan as the dominant urban and political center of central Mexico during the Classic period, a city whose multiethnic neighborhoods, extensive obsidian industries, and far-reaching commercial and religious influence shaped developments across much of Mesoamerica for centuries. Chapter 9 examined the Classic Maya world of the southern lowlands and traced the gradual breakdown of divine kingship, monument dedication, and large-scale political organization across that region between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries CE, a process this textbook described as regionally uneven rather than simultaneous, and as involving warfare, drought, and dynastic failure operating differently from city to city.

This chapter turns to a related but geographically distinct transformation: the reorganization of central Mexico and adjoining highland regions following the decline of Teotihuacan's political centrality, a decline that most current archaeological chronology places considerably earlier than the southern Maya lowland breakdown examined in Chapter 9. Teotihuacan's loss of regional dominance appears to have begun by around the sixth or seventh century CE, evidenced by the burning of major monuments along the city's principal avenue and a subsequent, gradual reduction in the city's population and political reach. This did not mean the immediate disappearance of Teotihuacan as a settlement — people continued to live at the site for centuries afterward — but it did mean the end of the centralized political and economic dominance that had structured much of central Mexican life for the preceding several hundred years.

Archaeologists generally use the term Epiclassic to describe the period immediately following Teotihuacan's political decline, roughly the seventh through ninth or tenth centuries CE in central Mexico, a period characterized by the proliferation of smaller, often fortified regional centers competing for regional influence in the absence of a single dominant power. The subsequent period, from roughly the tenth through the twelfth centuries CE, is generally called the Early Postclassic, and it is during this period that Tula rose to its greatest size and influence. Students should understand that these period labels, like "Classic" and "Postclassic" themselves, are modern scholarly conventions imposed for the purpose of historical organization; the people living through these centuries did not experience their own lives as belonging to a period called "Epiclassic" any more than the Maya rulers examined in Chapter 9 understood themselves to be living through a "collapse."

This chapter also depends on a body of archaeological scholarship that has substantially revised earlier twentieth-century narratives about Tula and the Toltecs. Much of that earlier narrative derived heavily, and sometimes uncritically, from colonial-era Nahua accounts recorded by Spanish chroniclers many centuries after the events they purported to describe — accounts that this chapter examines directly in Section 10.10, and that must be read as historical memory shaped by later political and religious purposes rather than as contemporary documentation of Tula's Early Postclassic history. Modern excavation, radiocarbon dating, ceramic analysis, obsidian sourcing, and settlement-pattern survey now provide a substantially different, more archaeologically grounded picture of Tula's development — one that this chapter presents as the primary basis for understanding the city, while still explaining why later legendary accounts of Tollan mattered enormously to the societies, including the Mexica, that inherited and reinterpreted them.

Section 10.1

From the Classic to the Postclassic: Collapse or Reorganization?

The word "collapse" appears throughout popular writing about ancient Mesoamerica, and it is not entirely without justification: significant political systems did break down, monumental construction did slow or stop in many places, and populations did decline or relocate in some regions during the centuries this chapter examines. But as Chapter 9 argued at length regarding the Classic Maya lowlands, "collapse" becomes historically misleading the moment it implies a single event, a single cause, or a single timetable applying uniformly across Mesoamerica. This chapter extends that same caution to central Mexico, where the relevant transformation — the decline of Teotihuacan's centralized political and economic dominance — began considerably earlier than the Maya lowland breakdown and unfolded according to its own distinct chronology and causes.

Archaeologists conventionally divide the centuries between the decline of Teotihuacan's dominance and the rise of the Mexica-led Triple Alliance, examined in Chapters 13 and 14, into two broad periods: the Epiclassic, extending roughly from the seventh through the ninth or tenth centuries CE, and the Early Postclassic, extending roughly from the tenth through the twelfth centuries CE. These labels are useful organizing conventions, not descriptions of unified political eras. During the Epiclassic, no single center replaced Teotihuacan's former dominance; instead, multiple competing regional centers — including Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, and Cholula, examined in Section 10.3 — developed political, military, and religious institutions independently, often incorporating new defensive architecture and new artistic influences from neighboring regions, including the Gulf Coast, Oaxaca, and the Maya area. Only later, during the Early Postclassic, did Tula emerge as one of the region's largest and most influential cities, though even then it did not achieve anything resembling the singular political dominance Teotihuacan had once held over central Mexico.

Students should therefore approach this chapter's title with appropriate skepticism toward its own shorthand. "The Classic collapse" is a convenient label for a complex, multi-causal, multi-regional, multi-century historical process — one involving political fragmentation, population movement, changing trade networks, environmental pressures in some regions, warfare, and religious innovation, unfolding at different paces in different places. Tula's rise should be understood not as the moment this process ended, but as one particularly well-documented and historically consequential development within a much longer and more geographically extensive transformation of Mesoamerican political life.

Section 10.2

Central Mexico after Teotihuacan

The decline of Teotihuacan's political centrality did not mean the disappearance of central Mexican civilization, nor did it create an empty landscape awaiting new occupants. The Basin of Mexico and surrounding valleys remained densely populated, agriculturally productive, and religiously significant throughout the Epiclassic period. What changed was the region's political structure: rather than a single dominant city commanding tribute, trade, and religious authority across a wide hinterland, central Mexico fragmented into a larger number of smaller, more regionally focused polities, many of which competed actively with one another for territory, trade routes, and prestige.

This fragmentation had significant consequences for how power was organized and displayed. Where Teotihuacan's rulers had emphasized anonymous, collective religious and civic imagery — a striking feature of the city's art examined in Chapter 8 — many Epiclassic centers instead developed more personalized, militarized forms of political display, including depictions of individual warriors, captives, and rulers associated with specific lineages or dynasties. This shift should not be read as evidence that warfare or personal rulership were entirely new to Mesoamerica; both had long histories in the region, including among the Classic Maya examined in Chapter 9. Rather, it reflects a changing political environment in which smaller, competing polities had greater need to assert and legitimate their authority through visible displays of military capacity and dynastic identity, in the absence of a single unquestioned regional hegemon.

Agricultural systems, household economies, craft production, and long-distance trade all continued through this period of political reorganization, often adapting to new patterns of regional competition and alliance. Obsidian working, an industry that had been central to Teotihuacan's economy, continued at various centers throughout the Epiclassic and into the Early Postclassic, though the specific sources exploited and the networks through which finished goods traveled shifted over time, a theme developed further in Section 10.9. Understanding this continuity alongside political change is essential to avoiding the common error of treating the "post-Teotihuacan" centuries as a dark or empty interval separating two more impressive eras.

Section 10.3

Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, Cholula, and the Epiclassic Political World

Three Epiclassic centers illustrate the diversity of political and cultural life in the centuries before Tula's rise to prominence, and each deserves attention in its own right rather than serving merely as a prelude to Tula.

Xochicalco, located on a defensible hilltop in the modern state of Morelos, developed as a fortified regional center whose architecture reflects a deliberate concern with defense and internal political segmentation. The site's builders modified the natural hilltop with terraces, walls, and moats, creating a settlement that could be defended against attack and that physically separated different zones of activity, likely reflecting distinctions of status, function, or corporate group membership among the city's population. Xochicalco's famous Pyramid of the Feathered Serpent, carved with imagery combining central Mexican, Maya, and other regional artistic influences, demonstrates that the city participated actively in long-distance networks of religious and artistic exchange even as its architecture emphasized military preparedness. Xochicalco's combination of fortification and cosmopolitan artistic influence exemplifies a broader Epiclassic pattern: political fragmentation and competition did not prevent, and may even have encouraged, active interregional contact, as competing centers sought prestige, allies, and access to valuable materials and ideas from beyond their immediate region.

Cacaxtla, in the modern state of Tlaxcala, is best known for a series of remarkably well-preserved polychrome murals depicting warriors, deities, and battle scenes rendered in a hybrid artistic style that combines central Mexican conventions with techniques and iconographic elements more commonly associated with Maya art of the same general period. Scholars continue to debate the precise historical circumstances that produced this hybridity — whether through the presence of Maya-affiliated merchants or elites at the site, through the deliberate adoption of prestigious foreign artistic conventions by local rulers, or through some combination of population movement and cultural borrowing. Whatever the precise mechanism, Cacaxtla demonstrates that the political fragmentation of the Epiclassic period coexisted with, and did not prevent, sustained artistic and cultural connections spanning hundreds of kilometers across culturally and linguistically diverse regions of Mesoamerica.

Cholula, in the modern state of Puebla, offers a different lesson: continuity rather than rupture. The site's Great Pyramid, one of the largest pre-Columbian structures in the Americas by volume, had already been the object of continuous religious and architectural investment for many centuries before the Epiclassic period, and it would remain a major religious center for centuries afterward, continuing in importance through the Early Postclassic and into the era of Mexica dominance examined in Part III, and still functioning as a significant religious site at the time of the Spanish invasion examined in Volume II. Cholula's long-term religious significance, largely unbroken across the political transformations that reorganized so much of the rest of central Mexico, is an important reminder that not every aspect of Mesoamerican life was equally affected by the political fragmentation following Teotihuacan's decline, and that some forms of continuity — particularly religious and pilgrimage traditions — could persist across many centuries of surrounding political change.

Read together, Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, and Cholula demonstrate that the Epiclassic political landscape of central Mexico was neither a simple power vacuum nor a uniform pattern of decline. It was a genuinely multipolar world of competing, interacting, and in some respects cooperating centers, each developing its own distinctive political, military, and religious strategies. Tula's later rise to prominence, examined beginning in Section 10.5, should be understood as a development within this already complex and interconnected world, not as its starting point.

Section 10.4

Migration, Coyotlatelco, and New Communities

No topic in this chapter requires more methodological caution than the relationship between changing material culture and the movement of actual human populations. Archaeologists working in central Mexico have long noted the appearance, beginning in the Epiclassic period, of a distinctive pottery tradition known as Coyotlatelco ware, characterized by red-on-buff decoration and forms that differ noticeably from the ceramic traditions associated with Teotihuacan's Classic-period apogee. For much of the twentieth century, the spread of Coyotlatelco pottery across central Mexico was interpreted as straightforward evidence that a new population — sometimes loosely associated with later Nahua traditions about northern "Chichimec" peoples — had migrated into the region, and in some accounts, had actively displaced or conquered the area's earlier Teotihuacan-affiliated inhabitants.

Current archaeological scholarship treats this narrative with considerably more caution, for reasons that illustrate a broader interpretive principle relevant throughout Mesoamerican archaeology: a change in pottery style does not, by itself, prove the arrival of a new ethnic group, and the arrival of a new population does not, by itself, prove conquest or displacement. Ceramic styles can spread through trade, imitation, intermarriage, and the gradual adoption of new techniques and tastes by existing populations, without requiring any large-scale population replacement at all. Even where genuine population movement did occur, migrating groups often settled alongside, intermarried with, and gradually blended with existing communities, rather than simply replacing them outright.

Revised radiocarbon chronology at Tula itself has produced a genuinely significant finding that overturns the older sequential narrative entirely, rather than merely complicating it. The earliest Coyotlatelco-related settlement identified at the Tula site, sometimes called the Early Corral settlement, now appears to date to roughly the fifth century CE — a period when Teotihuacan still dominated much of the surrounding region and centuries before that city's political decline examined in Section 10.1. This finding means that Coyotlatelco-associated settlement at Tula did not follow Teotihuacan's collapse at all; it developed alongside, and largely contemporaneously with, the tail end of Teotihuacan's regional dominance. Some degree of population movement into the Tula area likely did occur during this long span, but the evidence points toward a gradual, centuries-long process of settlement, growth, and cultural change unfolding well before, during, and after Teotihuacan's decline — not a single migration event timed to a post-collapse vacuum, and certainly not evidence for a "Toltec" population arriving suddenly to occupy an emptied landscape.

Students should take from this section a broader lesson about archaeological reasoning that will recur throughout this textbook: material culture, however informative, requires careful and contextualized interpretation rather than direct translation into ethnic labels, political events, or population movements. The safest historical statement available from current evidence is that the Tula region experienced meaningful population change and cultural transformation during the Epiclassic and Early Postclassic periods, likely involving some genuine in-migration, but that the details of this process — how many people moved, from where, over what period of time, and under what circumstances — remain considerably less certain than older, more confident narratives once suggested.

Evidence & Interpretation

What Does Coyotlatelco Pottery Tell Us About Migration?

The evidence: Coyotlatelco ware, a red-on-buff decorated ceramic tradition, is strongly associated with Epiclassic central Mexico generally. At Tula specifically, however, revised chronology pushes the earliest Coyotlatelco-related occupation back into the fifth century CE, contemporaneous with continuing Teotihuacan dominance rather than following its decline.

The older interpretation: Twentieth-century archaeologists often treated the spread of Coyotlatelco pottery as direct evidence of a migrating population, sometimes associated with later Nahua traditions about northern peoples, arriving in central Mexico after Teotihuacan's collapse and displacing or absorbing the region's earlier inhabitants.

The revised interpretation: Current scholarship treats Coyotlatelco pottery as evidence of significant cultural and possibly demographic change, but resists translating a ceramic style directly into a named ethnic group, a specific migration route, or a conquest narrative. The overlapping chronology with continuing Teotihuacan influence suggests a more gradual, layered process of population movement, local adaptation, and cultural blending rather than a single decisive invasion.

Why the distinction matters: Treating pottery style as a direct proxy for ethnicity or conquest has, in other contexts, been used to construct simplistic and sometimes politically motivated historical narratives. Careful archaeological reasoning requires distinguishing what material evidence can support — changes in production, style, and possibly population — from what it cannot, by itself, establish, such as language, self-identification, or the specific political events that accompanied cultural change.

Student task: Identify what additional kinds of evidence (skeletal, genetic, architectural, or textual) might help archaeologists distinguish between population migration, trade-driven stylistic adoption, and gradual internal cultural change at a site such as Tula.

Section 10.5

From Tula Chico to Tula Grande

Tula's rise to prominence was not instantaneous, and the archaeological record demonstrates a clear developmental sequence rather than the sudden appearance of a fully formed imperial capital. The earliest substantial monumental construction at the Tula site is generally referred to as Tula Chico, a complex of plazas, platforms, and ceremonial structures that developed during the Epiclassic period, overlapping chronologically with the earliest Coyotlatelco-related settlement discussed in Section 10.4. Tula Chico functioned as a genuine monumental and likely political center in its own right, not merely as a preliminary sketch for the later city.

Sometime around the ninth or tenth century CE, the focus of monumental construction and, apparently, political authority shifted from Tula Chico to a new complex approximately one kilometer away, generally known as Tula Grande. This shift accompanied substantial population growth and marks the beginning of what archaeologists call the Tollan phase, the period of Tula's greatest size and influence, extending from roughly the tenth through the mid-twelfth centuries CE. Importantly, current scholarship emphasizes considerable continuity between Tula Chico and Tula Grande in architectural layout and sculptural conventions, suggesting that the shift represented a reorganization and expansion of an existing political and religious tradition rather than a rupture imposed by a wholly new population or political order.

This developmental sequence carries an important interpretive lesson. Popular accounts of Tula have sometimes presented the city as springing into being abruptly, fully formed, in the immediate aftermath of Teotihuacan's decline — an image that conveniently supports a simple narrative in which a new "Toltec" people arrived to fill a political vacuum. The actual sequence, from an earlier Epiclassic monumental center at Tula Chico to the later, larger complex of Tula Grande, demonstrates a more gradual process of political consolidation and urban growth extending across several centuries, paralleling the layered and non-instantaneous character of nearly every other transformation examined in this chapter.

Section 10.6

Tula: City, Neighborhoods, Production, and Everyday Life

Popular imagination often reduces Tula to its most dramatic monumental features: the Atlantean warrior columns, the pyramids, and the ballcourts of Tula Grande. Archaeological survey demonstrates, however, that Tula during its Tollan-phase apogee was a large, densely populated city extending well beyond its monumental civic-ceremonial core. Revised settlement estimates place Tollan-phase Tula at roughly sixteen square kilometers or somewhat more of continuous or near-continuous settlement, encompassing a substantial residential population organized into distinct neighborhoods, or barrios, many of which have been identified and partially excavated by archaeological survey.

These residential barrios contained households of varying size, construction quality, and material wealth, indicating meaningful social and economic differentiation among Tula's population rather than a uniform urban population living under a single undifferentiated standard of living. Some households show evidence of specialized craft production, including the working of obsidian, ceramics, and other materials, suggesting that particular neighborhoods or family groups specialized in particular economic activities, integrated into the city's broader economy and, as Section 10.9 discusses, into long-distance exchange networks extending well beyond the Tula valley itself.

This evidence should reshape how students think about Tula's population as a whole. The city's residents were not simply warriors, priests, and rulers; they were farmers, artisans, traders, household heads, children, and elderly residents living ordinary daily lives organized around household economies, neighborhood social networks, agricultural cycles, craft specialization, and religious practice, in addition to whatever military and political institutions the city's rulers maintained. Understanding Tula as a genuine city, with the social complexity that term implies, is essential to avoiding a reductive picture in which Tula appears merely as a stage set for warrior imagery and legendary kingship.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household in a Tula Barrio

Archaeological excavation of residential compounds in Tula's barrios reveals households organized around small interior courtyards, with multiple rooms serving different domestic functions, hearths for cooking, and storage facilities for maize and other agricultural products. Grinding stones (metates and manos) recovered from many households indicate the ongoing labor, likely performed primarily by women, of processing maize into the flour used for tortillas and other staple foods, a labor-intensive daily task central to household subsistence across ancient and colonial-era Mesoamerica alike.

Evidence of craft production, including obsidian debris, ceramic wasters, and, in some households, tools associated with textile production, suggests that many Tula households combined agricultural subsistence with craft specialization, producing goods for household use, local exchange, or wider trade networks. Variation in the size, construction quality, and material assemblages of different households across Tula's barrios indicates real social and economic inequality within the city's population, a pattern consistent with what archaeologists have documented at other large Mesoamerican urban centers, including Teotihuacan, examined in Chapter 8.

Because Tula's residents left no alphabetic written records of their own daily lives, historians and archaeologists must reconstruct household experience primarily from material remains: the layout of domestic space, the tools and vessels recovered from household contexts, and the food remains preserved in trash deposits. This evidence cannot capture individual voices or specific life stories, but it does allow a reasonably confident reconstruction of the general shape of household economy, labor, and social differentiation among Tula's ordinary residents, a valuable complement to the monumental, elite-focused evidence examined in the sections that follow.

Section 10.7

Warriors, Rulers, and Political Authority

Tula Grande's civic-ceremonial core includes some of the most widely recognized monumental sculpture in Mesoamerican archaeology: the so-called Atlantean columns atop Pyramid B, four (originally more) monumental basalt figures carved in the form of armed warriors, standing over four meters tall, that once supported the roof of a temple structure at the pyramid's summit. These figures, along with smaller pillars carved with additional warrior imagery, ballcourts, a colonnaded hall likely used for gatherings of the city's elite, and reclining stone figures known as chacmools — sculptures whose exact ritual function remains debated but which are generally associated with offerings and sacrificial practice — together constitute a public architectural and sculptural vocabulary emphasizing military identity, elite gathering, and sacred authority.

This concentration of military imagery has led some scholars and popular accounts to characterize the Early Postclassic period generally, and Tula specifically, as an era in which Mesoamerican political culture became newly and distinctively militaristic, in supposed contrast to an earlier, more peaceful Classic period. This characterization should be firmly rejected. Chapter 9 has already demonstrated that Classic Maya political culture, spanning many centuries before Tula's rise, was thoroughly organized around warfare, captive-taking, and the political display of military success in monumental inscriptions and imagery. What changed at Tula was not the introduction of warfare into Mesoamerican political life, but rather the particular prominence given to standardized, repeated warrior imagery — arranged in orderly rows of processing figures rather than individualized portraits of specific historical rulers — within the city's most important public and religious architecture.

Students should also resist a second common misreading: treating the Atlantean columns and associated warrior imagery as portraits of specific, named historical individuals whose biographies can be reconstructed from their appearance. Unlike some Classic Maya monuments, which combine royal portraiture with hieroglyphic texts identifying specific rulers, events, and dates, Tula's warrior imagery is generally understood by archaeologists as depicting an idealized, standardized warrior identity — likely connected to specific military or religious orders — rather than individualized political biography. This distinction matters considerably for how the imagery should be interpreted: it more plausibly reflects the importance of warrior status and military institutions within Tula's broader political and religious order than it documents the specific military campaigns, conquests, or territorial claims of any named Tula ruler.

Finally, students should be cautious about converting Tula's prominent warrior imagery directly into evidence of extensive territorial conquest or a centralized "Toltec Empire" administering distant tributary provinces. While Tula certainly participated in warfare, as most Mesoamerican polities did, the specific archaeological evidence for a large, centrally administered Tula empire — comparable in documented scale to the later Mexica imperial system examined in Chapter 14 — remains considerably weaker than the popular image of "Toltec conquest" often suggests. This question recurs with particular force in Section 10.11's discussion of Tula's relationship with Chichén Itzá.

Section 10.8

Religious Change and the Feathered Serpent Tradition

Alongside its prominent warrior imagery, Tula's religious architecture and iconography participated in a broader Mesoamerican tradition centered on the Feathered Serpent, a deity and symbolic complex generally associated in later Nahua tradition with the god Quetzalcoatl, but whose iconographic roots extend back through the Classic period, including at Teotihuacan and, as Section 10.3 noted, at Xochicalco's Epiclassic Pyramid of the Feathered Serpent. Feathered Serpent imagery at Tula appears prominently in architectural sculpture, including carved serpent forms integrated into pyramid facades, reflecting the deity's continuing importance within the city's religious life.

A crucial interpretive point follows from the wider distribution of Feathered Serpent imagery across multiple Epiclassic and Early Postclassic centers, rather than its exclusive association with Tula alone. Scholars have argued for the existence of a broad, interregional Feathered Serpent religious network spanning much of Mesoamerica during this period, connecting centers as geographically distant as Xochicalco, Cholula, Tula, and, as Section 10.11 discusses, potentially Chichén Itzá. This interpretation suggests that shared Feathered Serpent iconography across these sites more plausibly reflects participation in a broader tradition transmitted through multiple, overlapping mechanisms — pilgrimage, the exchange of religious specialists and ideas, political alliance, population movement, and possibly the movement of specialists or mercenaries — than it reflects direct political domination by any single center, including Tula, over the others.

This religious tradition connected to broader Mesoamerican understandings of sacred rulership, in which political authority derived legitimacy partly from association with specific deities and cosmological narratives. Chacmool sculptures, reclining stone figures holding a flat receptacle on their abdomen, appear at Tula and at other Early Postclassic sites, including Chichén Itzá, and are generally interpreted as receptacles for offerings connected to sacrificial ritual, though their precise religious meaning and origin remain subjects of ongoing scholarly discussion. As with the warrior imagery discussed in Section 10.7, students should resist assuming that the presence of chacmools or Feathered Serpent imagery at a distant site automatically proves that Tula exported these forms through military conquest, rather than through the less dramatic, but historically well-documented, mechanisms of religious exchange, pilgrimage, and the voluntary adoption of prestigious foreign symbols by local elites.

Section 10.9

Obsidian, Trade, and the Early Postclassic Exchange World

Tula's economic significance extended well beyond its immediate agricultural hinterland, connecting the city to exchange networks spanning much of Mesoamerica and, in some respects, regions beyond. Obsidian, the volcanic glass used throughout ancient Mesoamerica for cutting tools, projectile points, and ritual objects, provides particularly clear evidence of these connections because different obsidian sources have distinct, scientifically identifiable chemical signatures, allowing archaeologists to trace finished obsidian artifacts back to their geological point of origin.

During Tula's Tollan-phase apogee, the city's obsidian procurement shifted dramatically toward green obsidian from the Sierra de las Navajas source, located within the broader Basin of Mexico region: Sierra de las Navajas material rises from under ten percent of earlier Tula-area obsidian assemblages to roughly sixty to eighty-five percent of assemblages at Early Postclassic Tula. This dramatic shift demonstrates intensified access to and procurement from this particular source, integrated into the city's craft-production economy discussed in Section 10.6 and into wider exchange networks extending outward from Tula itself. It is important to be precise about what this evidence does and does not establish: chemical sourcing can demonstrate where obsidian originated and how procurement patterns changed over time, but it cannot, by itself, determine whether that access was commercial, diplomatic, or backed by direct political or military control of the source itself. Green obsidian tools and blades associated with Tula-related material culture have been identified at sites considerably distant from the Tula valley, indicating that the city functioned as a significant node within a broader Early Postclassic exchange system.

Tula's exchange connections extended toward western Mexico, a region associated with distinctive metallurgical traditions — copper and, somewhat later, other metals — that were relatively new to Mesoamerica during this period, as well as marine shell and other prestige materials. Evidence also points toward exchange connections reaching into more distant northern frontier zones, potentially including networks extending toward the northern Mexican deserts and, in some interpretations, connections reaching indirectly toward parts of the present-day American Southwest, though the precise extent and mechanisms of these more distant connections remain subjects of ongoing research and should not be overstated as evidence of direct political or military control.

These exchange networks should be understood as involving several distinct kinds of interaction that must be analytically separated rather than merged into a single undifferentiated category of "contact." Trade involved the exchange of goods, sometimes over very long distances, without necessarily implying any political relationship between the parties involved. Diplomacy involved formal political relationships between polities, potentially including marriage alliances, gift exchange, or coordinated action, without necessarily implying subordination. Migration involved the movement of people, who might carry goods, technologies, and religious ideas with them without representing formal political or economic institutions at all. Conquest involved the use of organized military force to establish political control, often followed by the extraction of tribute. Evidence for Tula's obsidian trade and broader material exchange supports confident conclusions about extensive economic connection; it does not, by itself, establish which of these several distinct kinds of relationship — trade, diplomacy, migration, or conquest — best explains any particular connection between Tula and a distant site, a distinction of considerable importance for the debate examined in Section 10.11.

Section 10.10

What Did "Toltec" Mean? Tollan, Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, and Historical Memory

No section of this chapter requires more careful historical discipline than this one, because it addresses a body of evidence fundamentally different in kind from the archaeological material examined in the preceding sections. Beginning in the sixteenth century, Spanish missionaries and chroniclers, working with Nahua informants and, in some cases, Indigenous co-authors, recorded extensive oral traditions concerning a legendary city called Tollan, ruled by a wise, pious, and artistically accomplished people called the Toltecs, whose eventual ruler, a priest-king named Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, was remembered as having presided over a golden age of peace, prosperity, and artistic and religious achievement before being driven from the city by rival factions or supernatural adversaries, after which he was said to have traveled eastward toward the seacoast; surviving traditions differ considerably about his ultimate fate, with the Anales de Cuauhtitlan, a Nahuatl-language chronicle completed around 1570, describing his transformation into the Morning Star after cremating himself upon reaching the coast, while other accounts vary in their description of his destination and fate.

These accounts are genuinely valuable historical evidence, but they must be understood correctly as evidence of what later Nahua societies, writing and speaking many centuries after Tula's Tollan-phase apogee, believed and wished to communicate about their own civilizational inheritance — not as contemporary documentation of Tula's actual Early Postclassic history. By the time these traditions were recorded in the sixteenth century, "Toltec" had become, for the Mexica and other Nahua-speaking peoples examined in Chapter 13, less a precise ethnic or historical designation than a term connoting exceptional artistic skill, wisdom, legitimate rulership, and civilizational achievement generally — Mexica rulers and nobles frequently claimed descent from, or continuity with, Toltec ancestors as a way of asserting political legitimacy, regardless of the precise historical accuracy of such claims.

This creates a genuine interpretive challenge that this chapter resolves through explicit separation rather than synthesis. On one hand, archaeology documents a real city at Tula, with a real chronological development from Tula Chico through Tula Grande, a real residential population organized into barrios, real evidence of craft production and long-distance exchange, and real monumental architecture including the Atlantean columns and Feathered Serpent imagery examined in Sections 10.7 and 10.8. On the other hand, later Nahua tradition preserves an elaborate, richly detailed narrative about a legendary Tollan and its ruler Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl that cannot be verified against contemporary Tula-period evidence and that shows clear signs of having been shaped by centuries of subsequent religious and political reinterpretation, including probable conflation with earlier Teotihuacan traditions, with the god Quetzalcoatl himself, and with the political needs of later Nahua societies seeking to claim legitimate descent from a prestigious civilizational ancestor.

Students should therefore treat claims about Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl's specific biography — his exact reign dates, the precise circumstances of his political rivalry and exile, and his specific itinerary after leaving Tollan — as later legendary tradition rather than secure historical fact, while still recognizing that this tradition may preserve some genuine historical kernel, perhaps reflecting an actual political crisis or succession dispute at Tula whose specific details have been irretrievably transformed by centuries of subsequent retelling. The responsible historical position is neither to dismiss the Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl tradition as pure invention nor to treat it as a reliable historical chronicle, but to present it explicitly as valuable evidence of later historical memory and political ideology, analytically distinct from, though clearly related to, the archaeologically documented city examined throughout the rest of this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Tollan / Toltec

Tollan (sometimes rendered Tullan) is the Nahuatl term generally translated as "place of the reeds," used in later Nahua tradition to refer to a legendary city of exceptional civilizational achievement. Scholars generally identify archaeological Tula, examined throughout this chapter, as the most probable historical referent for at least some layers of the Tollan tradition, though "Tollan" also functioned in Nahua thought as something closer to an idealized archetype of a great capital city, occasionally applied to other important centers, including, in some usages, Teotihuacan itself and, later, the Mexica capital of Tenochtitlan.

Toltec (Toltecatl in Nahuatl, generally understood as meaning something like "person from Tollan" or, by extension, "skilled artisan") referred, in later Nahua usage, both to the specific historical or legendary population associated with Tollan/Tula and, more broadly, to a general concept of exceptional artistic and civilizational skill. Students must distinguish this later Nahua usage from the modern archaeological convention of using "Toltec" as a scholarly label for the material culture associated with Tula's Tollan-phase apogee — a modern convention that should not be mistaken for a precise, stable ethnic category used consistently across all periods and sources.

Section 10.11

Tula and Chichén Itzá: Conquest, Migration, or Interaction?

One of the most striking and most debated problems in Mesoamerican archaeology concerns the extraordinary architectural and iconographic similarities between Tula, in central Mexico, and Chichén Itzá, a major center in the northern Maya lowlands examined in full in Chapter 12. Both sites feature closely comparable colonnaded halls, warrior columns, chacmool sculptures, Feathered Serpent imagery, and other shared architectural and artistic conventions, similarities too extensive and too specific to be coincidental. The historical relationship producing these similarities, however, remains a genuinely unresolved scholarly question, and this chapter presents that question honestly rather than adopting the older, more confident narrative that once dominated popular accounts.

That older narrative, drawing partly on later Maya chronicles describing the arrival of foreign "Itza" people at Chichén Itzá, proposed that Toltec warriors from Tula conquered or otherwise politically dominated Chichén Itzá, imposing their architectural and religious conventions on the Maya city and installing themselves as a ruling elite. This narrative offered a clean, causally satisfying explanation for the shared iconography, and it fit comfortably with the general image of an expansive "Toltec Empire" that this chapter has repeatedly cautioned against.

Current scholarship treats this conquest narrative as considerably less certain than it once appeared, for several converging reasons. Chronological research has produced conflicting proposals regarding the relative dating of Tula's and Chichén Itzá's architectural florescence, with some research suggesting that significant elements of Chichén Itzá's "Toltec-style" architecture may have developed earlier than, or roughly contemporaneously with, rather than strictly after, comparable developments at Tula Grande. Complicating the picture further, evidence from Tula Chico, discussed in Section 10.5, indicates that some "Toltec-style" imagery existed at Tula earlier than once assumed, suggesting a longer and more gradually developing shared artistic tradition rather than a single, datable moment of conquest and imposition originating from a fully mature Tula Grande.

Given this genuinely unresolved chronology, much current scholarship emphasizes explanations centered on intensive, sustained interaction — through trade, diplomacy, shared religious participation in the broader Feathered Serpent tradition discussed in Section 10.8, elite intermarriage, or the movement of specific groups of merchants, religious specialists, or political refugees — as at least as plausible as, and possibly more plausible than, a simple narrative of one-directional military conquest. This does not mean that military conflict, political alliance, or even some form of shared or overlapping elite governance can be entirely ruled out; it means that the specific political mechanism connecting Tula and Chichén Itzá remains an open and actively debated question, one that this chapter can only introduce, since Chichén Itzá itself, its Maya political context, and the broader northern Maya lowland world belong properly to Chapter 12.

Historical Debate

Did a Toltec Empire Exist?

The question: Do the archaeological, art-historical, and textual evidence discussed throughout this chapter support the existence of a centralized "Toltec Empire" exercising political and military domination over a wide territory, including distant centers such as Chichén Itzá, or do they instead support a more limited, regionally focused Tula whose broader influence spread primarily through trade, religion, and prestige rather than conquest?

Interpretation A — Tula exercised substantial military and political reach, including over Chichén Itzá: This view draws on the striking specificity of shared architectural and iconographic forms between Tula and Chichén Itzá, on later Maya chronicles describing the arrival of foreign conquerors associated with Feathered Serpent worship, and on Tula's clearly militarized public art discussed in Section 10.7, to argue that Tula's rulers or a closely affiliated military elite achieved genuine political influence, and possibly direct control, over Chichén Itzá and other distant centers, constituting a loosely organized sphere of Toltec political and religious dominance extending well beyond the Tula valley itself.
Interpretation B — Shared forms reflect interaction, exchange, and a common religious tradition rather than centralized conquest: This view emphasizes the unresolved and in some respects conflicting chronological evidence for the relative dating of Tula's and Chichén Itzá's architectural florescence, the evidence from Tula Chico suggesting an earlier and more gradually developing shared artistic tradition, and the broader Epiclassic and Early Postclassic Feathered Serpent pilgrimage network discussed in Section 10.8, to argue that intensive trade, diplomacy, elite intermarriage, and shared religious participation offer a better-supported explanation for the similarities than a specific narrative of Toltec military conquest.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of architectural and iconographic comparison between the two sites, but differ in how they weigh contested chronological evidence, later Maya textual traditions of uncertain historical reliability, and the broader regional pattern of religious and artistic exchange documented elsewhere in Epiclassic and Early Postclassic Mesoamerica.

Why it matters: The resolution of this question would significantly affect how historians understand the geographic and political reach of Tula's power, and it bears directly on the broader question, addressed throughout this chapter, of whether a centralized "Toltec Empire" existed as a coherent political entity. This textbook does not resolve the debate, because the current archaeological evidence does not allow for a confident resolution; it presents both interpretations to illustrate a genuinely open historical question rather than manufacturing false certainty in either direction.

Student task: Drawing on the chronological evidence discussed in Sections 10.5, 10.8, and 10.11, explain what kind of additional archaeological evidence would most effectively help resolve this debate, and evaluate whether the specificity of the architectural similarities between Tula and Chichén Itzá makes a purely non-political explanation (trade and religion alone, without any political relationship at all) fully satisfying.

Section 10.12

The End of Tula and the Making of a Toltec Legacy

Current chronometric evidence places the end of Tula's major Tollan-phase occupation at approximately 1150 CE, marking the conclusion of the city's roughly two-century apogee as one of Mesoamerica's largest and most influential urban centers. As with so much else in this chapter, however, the precise mechanism and sequence of Tula's decline resist reduction to a single, simple explanation, and students should be especially cautious about older popular narratives attributing the city's end to a single dramatic invasion.

Archaeological excavation at Tula Grande has documented evidence of deliberate burning at several of the site's principal monumental structures, a pattern that has understandably invited speculation about violent conquest. Careful stratigraphic analysis, however, indicates that the burning of specific monuments and the broader abandonment of the city as a major population center are not necessarily part of a single, simultaneous event; the burning appears to follow closely upon the end of the Tollan-phase occupation, but who carried it out, and precisely when relative to abandonment, remains unknown on current evidence. This distinction matters considerably: it separates the question of why Tula's population and political importance diminished from the separate, unresolved question of who burned specific monuments and why. Excavators emphasize that no archaeological evidence currently ties this burning to the later Aztec II occupation of the site, and any explanation of who was responsible should be treated as an open question rather than a demonstrated fact.

Excavators working at the site have explicitly cautioned against reviving an older narrative, associated with some earlier twentieth-century interpretations, that attributed Tula's destruction to invasion by later Nahua-speaking "Aztec II" groups migrating into central Mexico. Current evidence does not support treating this specific invasion narrative as an established archaeological fact. Several factors have been proposed as contributors to Tula's decline, and these should be understood as hypotheses rather than demonstrated causes: political fragmentation or succession crisis among Tula's ruling elite; disruption to the long-distance exchange networks examined in Section 10.9, on which the city's economy substantially depended; and a severe regional drought conventionally dated to roughly 1149–1167 CE, a chronology suggestively close to Tula's decline but not sufficient by itself to demonstrate that drought caused the city's collapse. The gradual reorientation of political and demographic weight toward other, competing centers in central Mexico during the twelfth century set the stage for the migrations and political reorganization examined in Chapter 13, regardless of which specific combination of these proposed factors proves most important.

The chapter's final and in some ways most important observation concerns the striking contrast between Tula's declining political power and its dramatically increasing symbolic and religious prestige in the centuries following the Tollan phase's end. Rather than being forgotten, Tula's ruins became an object of active pilgrimage, veneration, and political appropriation by later central Mexican societies, most importantly the Mexica examined in Chapters 13 and 14. Mexica rulers deliberately excavated and removed artifacts from Tula's ruins, incorporated them into their own religious and political displays in Tenochtitlan, and cultivated genealogical and cultural claims of descent from, or continuity with, the prestigious Toltec civilization discussed in Section 10.10 — claims that served to legitimate Mexica political authority by connecting it to an already ancient and widely respected civilizational inheritance. Tula's Tollan-phase political and demographic apogee ended around 1150 CE, but the site continued to see later occupation, offerings, and burials, and its cultural and ideological legacy continued to shape Mesoamerican political life for centuries afterward, extending directly into the world of the Mexica Triple Alliance examined later in this volume.

Historical Vocabulary
Epiclassic

The Epiclassic is a modern archaeological period label applied to central Mexico and adjoining regions of Mesoamerica, extending roughly from the seventh through the ninth or tenth centuries CE. It describes the interval between the decline of Teotihuacan's centralized political dominance and the rise of Tula as a major Early Postclassic center, characterized by the proliferation of smaller, often fortified, competing regional polities such as Xochicalco, Cacaxtla, and, in a different sense, the continuously important center of Cholula.

As with other period labels used throughout this textbook, "Epiclassic" is a scholarly convention for organizing historical analysis, not a category that the people living through this period would have recognized or used themselves. Students should understand the term as referring to a a general span of time and a loosely shared set of political circumstances — fragmentation, competition, and regional reorganization — rather than a perfectly uniform historical era experienced identically across every region of Mesoamerica.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Tula from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across evidence. They are not quotations from any specific named historical individual.

From the perspective of a Tula ruler or member of the city's political elite: Authority rested on a combination of military prestige, association with warrior identity and orders displayed publicly at Pyramid B, participation in the broader Feathered Serpent religious tradition connecting Tula to other important Mesoamerican centers, and intensified access to valuable resources and exchange networks, including green obsidian from the Sierra de las Navajas — though sourcing evidence alone cannot establish whether that access rested on direct political control of the source itself.
From the perspective of an artisan or farmer living in a Tula barrio: Daily life centered on household labor, agricultural cycles, craft production, and neighborhood social ties, only some of which intersected directly with the political and military display concentrated at the city's monumental civic-ceremonial core; the household economy, examined in Section 10.6, mattered as much to ordinary residents as the warrior imagery that later observers would find most striking.
From the perspective of a long-distance trader connecting Tula to western Mexico or the Gulf Coast: Tula was one significant node within a much wider Mesoamerican and, in some respects, greater exchange world, connected through material goods, including obsidian and, increasingly during this period, metal objects from western Mexico, moving along routes that predated and would outlast any single political center.
From the perspective of a later Nahua person, centuries after Tula's decline, recalling Tollan and the Toltecs: Tollan represented a golden age of civilizational achievement and legitimate rulership, embodied in the figure of Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, whose inheritance offered later rulers, including the Mexica examined in Chapter 13, a valuable and actively cultivated claim to political and cultural legitimacy, regardless of how precisely that memory corresponded to the historical realities of Tula's Tollan-phase apogee.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Teotihuacan to Tula

What Changed

  • Political organization shifted from a single dominant regional power (Teotihuacan) to a fragmented, competitive landscape of Epiclassic centers, and later to Tula as one major, though not singularly dominant, Early Postclassic city
  • Public political imagery shifted toward more personalized, standardized warrior identity and dynastic display, in contrast to Teotihuacan's more anonymous, collective civic-religious art
  • Obsidian procurement patterns shifted toward the Sierra de las Navajas source during Tula's Tollan-phase apogee
  • Scholarly understanding of Tula's chronology and political reach has itself changed substantially, from an older narrative of a centralized "Toltec Empire" to a more cautious, evidence-based account of gradual development and contested regional influence

What Stayed the Same

  • Feathered Serpent religious traditions and other core elements of Mesoamerican cosmology persisted and evolved across the Classic-to-Postclassic transition, rather than being replaced wholesale
  • Long-distance exchange networks connecting central Mexico to western Mexico, the Gulf Coast, and other regions continued, even as the specific centers controlling or participating in those networks changed
  • Household-level agricultural and craft economies continued largely uninterrupted beneath the level of shifting elite political competition
  • Later societies, including the Mexica, continued to draw on and reinterpret earlier central Mexican civilizational achievements, including those of both Teotihuacan and Tula, for their own legitimating purposes

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents students from imagining Mesoamerican history as a series of disconnected civilizations replacing one another, and instead reveals an ongoing, evolving tradition in which later societies actively built upon, reinterpreted, and sometimes physically appropriated the material and symbolic inheritance of their predecessors.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Tula Become a Major Center?

Long-term causes

The gradual decline of Teotihuacan's centralized authority created a reorganized central Mexican political landscape in which no single power dominated, and Tula's location offered favorable access to the Sierra de las Navajas obsidian source and to trade routes connecting central Mexico with western Mexico and the northern frontier.

Short-term causes

Political and demographic consolidation at the Tula site during the ninth and tenth centuries CE, building on the earlier monumental foundation of Tula Chico, concentrated population, craft production, and political authority at the emerging complex of Tula Grande.

Effect

Tula developed into one of the largest and most influential cities of Early Postclassic Mesoamerica, participating in long-distance exchange networks, developing a distinctive public political and religious architectural vocabulary, and eventually acquiring immense symbolic prestige among later Mesoamerican societies.

Historical significance

Tula's political and religious traditions, reinterpreted through later Nahua historical memory, provided an important legitimating civilizational inheritance for subsequent central Mexican states, including the Mexica examined in Chapters 13 and 14.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Tula's rise; geography, prior political fragmentation, gradual institutional development, economic advantage, and religious and cultural factors all interacted to produce the city's Early Postclassic prominence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the transformation of central Mexico and adjoining highland Mesoamerica during the centuries following the decline of Teotihuacan's centralized political dominance, a process this chapter has consistently described as regionally uneven political reorganization rather than a single synchronized "collapse." The Epiclassic period, extending roughly from the seventh through the ninth or tenth centuries CE, produced a genuinely multipolar political landscape, illustrated in this chapter through the fortified hilltop planning of Xochicalco, the hybrid artistic vocabulary of Cacaxtla, and the long-term religious continuity of Cholula.

The chapter examined, with particular methodological care, the relationship between the spread of Coyotlatelco pottery and claims about migration and ethnic identity, concluding that changes in material culture require cautious, contextualized interpretation rather than direct translation into population movements or conquest narratives. Revised chronological evidence placing Tula's earliest Coyotlatelco-related settlement alongside continuing Teotihuacan influence in the surrounding region further complicates any simple narrative of a new population arriving to fill a post-collapse vacuum.

Students have traced Tula's development from the earlier monumental center of Tula Chico to the larger Tollan-phase complex of Tula Grande, a gradual process of political consolidation and urban growth rather than a sudden appearance. The chapter examined Tula as a genuine city of significant size, encompassing residential barrios, socially differentiated households, and specialized craft production, in addition to its famous monumental civic-ceremonial core.

The chapter analyzed Tula's Atlantean warrior columns and associated military imagery as evidence of the prominence of warrior identity within the city's public political and religious culture, while cautioning against reading this imagery as either a documented historical chronicle of specific rulers or automatic proof of extensive territorial conquest. It examined Feathered Serpent religious traditions and chacmool sculpture as elements of a broader interregional Epiclassic and Early Postclassic religious network, rather than as inventions exported exclusively by Tula through military force.

Tula's participation in long-distance exchange, particularly its shift toward Sierra de las Navajas green obsidian and its connections to western Mexico and more distant regions, demonstrated the city's significant economic reach, while the chapter insisted on distinguishing trade, diplomacy, migration, and conquest as separate and non-interchangeable explanations for shared material culture across distant sites.

This distinction proved especially important in the chapter's treatment of Tula's relationship with Chichén Itzá, examined as a genuinely unresolved historical debate rather than a settled conquest narrative, given the conflicting chronological evidence for the relative dating of the two sites' architectural florescence and the broader regional Feathered Serpent tradition that offers an alternative explanation for their shared iconography.

The chapter devoted sustained attention to separating the archaeologically documented city of Tula from later Nahua traditions concerning Tollan, the Toltecs, and Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, treating those traditions as valuable evidence of later historical memory and political legitimation rather than as reliable contemporary chronicles of Tula's Tollan-phase history.

Finally, the chapter examined Tula's decline around 1150 CE as a complex process involving political fragmentation, disrupted exchange networks, and possible environmental pressures, explicitly rejecting older narratives attributing the city's end to a single documented invasion, while emphasizing the striking paradox that Tula's political power declined even as its cultural and religious prestige grew, culminating in deliberate Mexica efforts to excavate Tula's ruins and claim Toltec ancestry as a source of political legitimacy.

Chapter 10 has asked how Mesoamerica reorganized itself after the political fragmentation following Teotihuacan's decline, and how Tula rose to become one of the most historically significant centers of that reorganized world. Chapter 11 turns southward to Oaxaca, tracing the distinct Mixtec and Zapotec dynastic worlds of the same general period, before Chapter 12 examines the Postclassic Maya world, including Chichén Itzá, and Chapter 13 returns to central Mexico to trace the Mexica migration into the Basin of Mexico and the founding of Tenochtitlan — a migration that Mexica tradition would frame, in significant part, as an inheritance of the Toltec civilizational legacy examined in this chapter.

Key Terms

Epiclassic
The period, roughly seventh through ninth or tenth centuries CE, following Teotihuacan's political decline and characterized by fragmented, competing regional centers.
Early Postclassic
The period, roughly tenth through twelfth centuries CE, during which Tula rose to its greatest size and influence.
Tollan
The Nahuatl term for a legendary city of civilizational achievement in later Nahua tradition, generally, though not exclusively, identified with archaeological Tula.
Toltec
A term used in later Nahua tradition for the people of Tollan and, more broadly, for exceptional artistic and civilizational skill; also used by modern archaeologists as a scholarly label for material culture associated with Tula's Tollan phase.
Tula Chico
The earlier monumental complex at the Tula site, developing during the Epiclassic period before the later complex of Tula Grande.
Tula Grande
The larger monumental complex associated with Tula's Tollan-phase apogee, including Pyramid B and its Atlantean columns.
Coyotlatelco ware
A red-on-buff Epiclassic pottery tradition whose spread across central Mexico has long been, and continues to be cautiously, associated with population movement.
Atlantean columns
Monumental basalt warrior sculptures atop Pyramid B at Tula Grande, generally interpreted as depicting idealized warrior identity rather than specific historical individuals.
Chacmool
A reclining stone sculpture holding a flat receptacle, associated with offerings and sacrificial ritual, found at Tula and other Early Postclassic sites including Chichén Itzá.
Feathered Serpent tradition
A widespread religious and iconographic complex transmitted through multiple forms of interregional interaction, including pilgrimage, exchange, political alliances, population movement, and possibly military activity; shared imagery across sites does not, by itself, establish political domination by Tula or any other single center.
Barrio
A residential neighborhood within a Mesoamerican city such as Tula, often associated with particular economic specializations or social groups.
Obsidian sourcing
The scientific analysis of the chemical composition of obsidian artifacts to determine their geological point of origin, used to trace ancient exchange networks.
Sierra de las Navajas
A green obsidian source in the Basin of Mexico region that became increasingly important to Tula's Early Postclassic economy.
Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl
A priest-ruler figure central to later Nahua traditions about Tollan, whose specific biography cannot be verified against contemporary evidence.
Xochicalco
A fortified, internally segmented Epiclassic hilltop center in Morelos illustrating defensive planning and cosmopolitan artistic influence.
Cacaxtla
An Epiclassic center in Tlaxcala known for murals combining central Mexican and Maya-influenced artistic conventions.
Cholula
A long-lived religious and political center in Puebla demonstrating continuity across the Classic-to-Postclassic transition.
Ethnogenesis
The historical process by which a group comes to understand itself, or be understood by others, as a distinct people or ethnicity, often through gradual social and cultural processes rather than a single defining event.
Historical memory
The way later communities remember, reinterpret, and use the past, often for present political or religious purposes, as distinct from contemporary historical documentation.
Interregional exchange
Trade and interaction spanning multiple distinct cultural or political regions, documented at Tula through obsidian sourcing and connections to western Mexico and beyond.
Dynastic legitimation
The use of ancestry claims, religious association, or inherited symbols to justify a ruler's or dynasty's right to authority, illustrated by later Mexica claims to Toltec ancestry.
Ceramic seriation
An archaeological dating method based on changes in pottery style and form over time, central to identifying and dating traditions such as Coyotlatelco ware.
Settlement survey
Archaeological research mapping the extent and density of ancient habitation across a landscape, used to estimate the size and population of cities such as Tula.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply, relevant to distinguishing archaeological Tula from later Toltec legend.
Skull rack (tzompantli)
A ritual structure for the public display of skulls associated with sacrifice, appearing in Postclassic Mesoamerican religious and political architecture.
Pilgrimage network
A pattern of religious travel connecting distant sites through shared ritual practice, offered in this chapter as an alternative explanation to conquest for shared religious iconography.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to think carefully about how Mesoamerican societies reorganized themselves after the decline of Teotihuacan's centralized authority, and about how one particular city, Tula, rose to become one of the most historically consequential — and most historically misunderstood — centers of the Early Postclassic world. The habits of mind this chapter has emphasized, including resisting simple collapse narratives, distinguishing material culture from ethnicity and migration, separating archaeological evidence from later legendary tradition, and refusing to treat shared iconography as automatic proof of conquest, are not unique to the study of Tula. They will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook, whenever students encounter dramatic, tidy historical narratives that oversimplify genuinely complex and only partially resolved evidence.

Tula's story also illustrates a theme central to this textbook's treatment of Indigenous history: civilizations did not develop in isolation, replace one another in clean succession, or vanish without leaving lasting influence. Teotihuacan's decline opened space for a genuinely multipolar Epiclassic world; that world, in turn, produced Tula as one significant center among several; and Tula's own decline did not erase its significance, but instead transformed it into a source of civilizational prestige actively claimed and reinterpreted by later societies, including the Mexica who will occupy much of the remainder of Part III.

Understanding Tula accurately — as a real city with real households, real craft production, real participation in long-distance exchange, and a real, if imperfectly understood, political and military culture, rather than as the capital of a legendary and centrally administered "Toltec Empire" — equips students to approach the chapters that follow with appropriate historical caution. Chapter 11 turns to Oaxaca's own Mixtec and Zapotec dynastic worlds, tracing a parallel but distinct process of Postclassic political development. Chapter 12 examines the Postclassic Maya world, including Chichén Itzá and the unresolved questions about its relationship to Tula raised in this chapter. Chapter 13 then returns to central Mexico to trace the Mexica migration into the Basin of Mexico, a migration whose protagonists would look back to Tollan and the Toltecs, examined in this chapter, as the source of their own claim to legitimate and civilized rule.
