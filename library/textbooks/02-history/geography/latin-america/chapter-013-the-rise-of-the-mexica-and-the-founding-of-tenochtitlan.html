The Rise of the Mexica and the Founding of Tenochtitlan

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Late-Arriving People in a Crowded Basin

When a small, relatively obscure migrant group first appeared on the shores of Lake Texcoco sometime in the thirteenth century, no one — least of all the migrants themselves — could have known that their descendants would one day rule an empire stretching across much of Mesoamerica. The Basin of Mexico they entered was not empty land waiting to be claimed. It was already crowded with cities, dynasties, and rival powers that had existed, in some cases, for centuries.

It is tempting, knowing how the story ends, to read the Mexica migration as though it were the opening chapter of an inevitable rise to empire — as though a chosen people, guided by prophecy, marched steadily and confidently toward the destiny of Tenochtitlan's later greatness. This chapter asks students to resist that temptation entirely. The people who would eventually call themselves Mexica arrived in the Basin of Mexico as outsiders: latecomers to an already dense political landscape, without significant military power and without secure land of their own. Their rise from this marginal position — without a locally recognized ruling dynasty of their own — to become, by the early fifteenth century, an increasingly important Basin polity was neither quick nor guaranteed. It unfolded across roughly a century through migration, subordination, military service to more powerful neighbors, dynastic marriage, ritual and genealogical claim-making, and the slow, uneven construction of an island city that had to be defended, built, and legitimated almost from nothing.

This chapter traces that process from its beginnings in the Mexica migration traditions, through settlement in the Basin of Mexico, the foundation of Mexico-Tenochtitlan and its sister city Tlatelolco, the installation of the first dynastic ruler Acamapichtli, and nearly half a century of Mexica political life conducted under the shadow of a more powerful neighboring state, the Tepanec kingdom centered at Azcapotzalco. It ends at a genuine turning point — the crisis that followed the death of the Tepanec ruler Tezozomoc and the war that brought Tepanec hegemony to an end around 1428 — but it deliberately stops there. The mature imperial system that Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan built together in the years following 1428, including the mechanics of tribute collection, chinampa agriculture, imperial markets, and territorial expansion, is the subject of the next chapter. This chapter's task is different and, in some ways, more difficult: to explain how a marginal community became a serious Basin power in the first place, without smuggling in the empire before its time.

Understanding this earlier, less triumphant period requires unusual care with evidence. Almost everything historians know about the Mexica migration, the foundation of Tenochtitlan, and the community's earliest political history comes from sources produced generations after the events they describe — pictorial histories and alphabetic chronicles created in the colonial period by Nahua and mestizo authors working from oral tradition, dynastic memory, and earlier pictorial records that have not themselves survived. These sources are neither simple fabrications nor transparent windows onto the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. They are political and sacred narratives, shaped by later dynasties' interests in explaining and justifying their own legitimacy, and historians must learn to read them as such.

This chapter also asks students to set aside a common visual shorthand: the image, familiar from the modern Mexican flag, of an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a serpent. That precise combination of symbols was not necessarily part of the earliest foundational tradition, and later chroniclers and artists appear to have standardized and elaborated the image well after the fact. Learning to distinguish an early, more variable oral and pictorial tradition from its later, more fixed retellings is itself one of this chapter's central historical skills.

Finally, this chapter insists on a distinction that matters throughout Mesoamerican history: Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco were not, at first, one city. They were two related but politically separate Mexica communities, founded within a few years of each other on adjoining islands, whose rulers pursued distinct dynastic strategies and whose political unification lay generations in the future. Holding this distinction in view — along with the equally important fact that Tenochtitlan spent roughly its first half-century as a subordinate ally within someone else's empire, not as an independent power in its own right — is essential to understanding how the Mexica rise to prominence actually happened, rather than how later, imperial-era chroniclers preferred to remember it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the terms Mexica, Tenochca, Tlatelolca, Nahua, and Aztec and explain why this textbook prefers "Mexica" for the peoples of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco.
  2. Describe the Mexica migration tradition, including Aztlan, Huitzilopochtli, and the successive stopping places recorded in later pictorial histories.
  3. Evaluate the evidentiary status of the migration narrative, distinguishing remembered history, political genealogy, sacred narrative, and later colonial-era reconstruction.
  4. Explain why the Mexica entered a Basin of Mexico already occupied by established and powerful altepetl, including Culhua, Tepanec, Acolhua, Xochimilca, and Chalca polities.
  5. Trace Mexica traditions concerning settlement at Chapultepec, conflict, expulsion, and subordination to Culhuacan.
  6. Analyze the traditional foundation narrative of Mexico-Tenochtitlan, including the conventional date of 1325 and the eagle-and-nopal sign, while distinguishing tradition from archaeologically confirmed fact.
  7. Distinguish Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco as two separate, related Mexica altepetl with distinct early histories.
  8. Explain how Acamapichtli's installation as the first Tenochca tlatoani constructed dynastic legitimacy through Culhua-Toltec genealogy.
  9. Describe Tenochtitlan's roughly half-century of subordination within the Tepanec hegemonic system centered at Azcapotzalco.
  10. Assess how military service, marriage alliance, and ritual prestige allowed Tenochtitlan to accumulate resources and status while still politically subordinate.
  11. Trace the succession crisis following the death of Tezozomoc and the war that ended Tepanec hegemony around 1428.
  12. Explain why the rise of the Mexica should not be read as an inevitable prelude to empire, and why Chapter 14 rather than this chapter addresses the Triple Alliance's mature imperial system.

Historical Context

Chapter 12 examined the Postclassic Maya world and the political fragmentation that characterized much of Mesoamerica in the centuries following the Classic collapse discussed in Part III. This chapter turns to a parallel process of political reorganization far to the north and west, in the Basin of Mexico, where Tula's decline as a major and highly influential regional center, and the political fragmentation that followed among the Toltec-affiliated populations discussed in Chapter 10, had left a landscape of competing city-states, or altepetl, none of which possessed clearly dominant regional authority over the others.

By the thirteenth century, the Basin of Mexico — a large, roughly circular highland valley enclosed by mountains and centered on a chain of interconnected shallow lakes, chief among them Lake Texcoco — supported a dense mosaic of Nahuatl-speaking and other communities. Some of these altepetl, such as Culhuacan on the southern lakeshore, claimed direct descent from the prestigious Toltec dynasty of Tula and used that claim to assert superior status among their neighbors. Others, including the growing Tepanec confederation centered at Azcapotzalco on the western shore, were building military and economic power through conquest, alliance, and control of lake-based trade and agriculture. Still others, including Acolhua communities on the eastern shore centered eventually at Texcoco, Xochimilca communities to the south, and Chalca communities in the southeastern Basin, maintained their own dynasties, temples, and territorial claims. This was the crowded, competitive, already ancient political world that Mexica migrants entered — not an empty frontier, but one of the most politically dense regions in all of Mesoamerica.

Historians describe the political units that structured this world using the Nahuatl term altepetl, discussed further in the Historical Vocabulary feature below. Each altepetl was typically organized around a ruling dynasty headed by a tlatoani, a term meaning roughly "he who speaks" and used for a dynastic ruler, normally selected from among eligible members of a recognized royal lineage rather than through simple father-to-son succession, who claimed legitimate authority over a defined territory and population, often subdivided into constituent wards or lineage groups. Political status among altepetl depended heavily on dynastic genealogy: rulers who could claim descent from the prestigious Toltec dynasty of Tula, or who could marry into such lineages, possessed a form of legitimacy that pure military strength alone could not provide. This emphasis on genealogical prestige matters enormously for understanding the political strategies the Mexica would eventually pursue, since a people with no established dynasty and no ancestral claim to the Basin needed to construct legitimacy through other means.

It is important to state plainly, before this chapter's narrative begins, that the Mexica were not a single, ethnically distinct people arriving from an entirely foreign world. Like most of their eventual neighbors and rivals, they spoke Nahuatl, participated in a broadly shared Mesoamerican religious and calendrical worldview, and drew on migration and origin traditions similar in structure, though not identical in content, to those of other Nahua-speaking groups who had also migrated into central Mexico following the Toltec collapse. What distinguished the Mexica migration story, as later chroniclers told it, was not that they came from somewhere entirely alien, but that they arrived comparatively late, without established territory, and had to build their political fortunes almost entirely from negotiation, service, and strategic marriage rather than from inherited status.

Section 13.1

Who Were the Mexica? Names, Identities, and Evidence

Before tracing the Mexica migration and rise, students must master a set of overlapping and often loosely used terms. In much popular usage, "Aztec" serves as a catch-all label for the people who built Tenochtitlan and the empire that grew from it. The term has genuine historical roots — some later Nahua sources associate it with the place-name Aztlan — but its modern usage is considerably broader and less precise than the historical actors themselves would likely have recognized. Present-day specialists increasingly follow a more careful convention: using Mexica specifically for the closely related peoples of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco, whose own name for themselves this word most directly reflects, while treating "Aztec" as a modern umbrella term whose scope varies from author to author and should be used with explicit definition rather than assumed to be self-evident.

This textbook follows that more precise convention. It uses Mexica for the people of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco specifically, Tenochca when referring specifically to the Tenochtitlan branch of that community, and Tlatelolca when referring specifically to the Tlatelolco branch. It uses Nahua as the broader term for Nahuatl-speaking peoples generally, a category that includes the Mexica but also many other altepetl throughout central Mexico who were neither Mexica nor part of the later empire. "Aztec" appears in this textbook chiefly when referring to conventional modern usage or to the empire as popularly known, with the more specific Mexica terminology preferred wherever precision matters.

This terminological care matters because it connects directly to a second, equally important methodological point: nearly everything historians know about the Mexica migration and early settlement comes not from documents created during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries themselves, but from pictorial manuscripts and alphabetic historical narratives produced during the sixteenth century, after the Spanish conquest examined in later chapters. These sources were created by Nahua and mestizo authors working from oral tradition, inherited pictorial records that have not themselves survived, and the political interests of specific noble lineages seeking to establish or defend their status under new colonial conditions. This does not mean the traditions are worthless as historical evidence — far from it — but it does mean that students must learn to read them critically, asking who preserved a given tradition, for what audience, and in whose political interest, before treating any specific narrative detail as an established fact about the thirteenth century.

Section 13.2

Aztlan and the Mexica Migration Tradition

According to the migration traditions recorded in later Mexica sources, the ancestors of the Tenochca and Tlatelolca people originated at a place called Aztlan, often described as an island or lake settlement, before departing under the guidance of their patron deity Huitzilopochtli on a long journey that would eventually bring them to the Basin of Mexico. The traditions describe the migrants as one among several migrant peoples whom later central Mexican traditions classified as Chichimeca, leaving a series of ancestral or semi-legendary places of origin during a period of broader population movement following Tula's decline, discussed in Chapter 10. Students should understand "Chichimeca" not as a single ethnicity or linguistic family but as a broad, context-dependent cultural category that later Nahua traditions applied to various ancestral or frontier populations associated with a less urbanized way of life; many groups so classified were Nahuatl-speaking, but the term itself was not a linguistic or ethnic designation.

Students should resist two opposite temptations when encountering Aztlan. The first temptation is to treat it as a purely legendary invention with no relationship to any real historical experience of migration. The second, equally mistaken, temptation is to treat Aztlan as a securely identified geographic location that modern archaeology or geography could, in principle, pinpoint on a map. Neither extreme reflects the actual state of the evidence. Aztlan's precise geographic status — whether it refers to an actual, locatable place of origin, a symbolic or composite tradition drawing on several earlier community memories, or primarily a sacred rather than geographic concept — remains genuinely unresolved among specialists, and proposed locations have varied widely, from sites in western Mexico to more symbolic interpretations that treat Aztlan primarily as a narrative device establishing the community's origins outside the Basin of Mexico rather than as a specific, recoverable place.

What matters more than Aztlan's exact location is the political and religious work the migration tradition performed for later generations of Mexica. The narrative established that the community's presence in the Basin of Mexico was neither accidental nor illegitimate, but the outcome of a long, divinely guided journey undertaken at the direction of Huitzilopochtli, who promised the migrants a homeland marked by a specific sign — most famously, an eagle perched upon a nopal cactus, discussed further in Section 13.6. The migration story thus functioned simultaneously as remembered history, sacred narrative, and a foundational claim to legitimate belonging in a Basin already occupied by older, more established powers. Later Mexica rulers and their chroniclers had every reason to preserve, elaborate, and politically shape this narrative, which is precisely why historians must read it with methodological care rather than at face value.

Section 13.3

Reading the Migration Sources

The single most important surviving source for the Mexica migration tradition is the pictorial manuscript known as the Codex Boturini, or Tira de la Peregrinación ("Strip of the Pilgrimage"), examined in detail in the Primary Source Window below. Painted on amate bark paper, probably around the middle of the sixteenth century, the codex depicts the migration as a sequence of stopping places, marked by footprints, place-glyphs, and figures representing migration leaders and the sacred bundle associated with Huitzilopochtli. Significantly, the surviving manuscript ends before the foundation of Tenochtitlan itself, meaning that it documents the journey but not, in its present form, the arrival and settlement that form the second half of this chapter's narrative.

Other colonial-era sources — including later alphabetic histories composed by Nahua and mestizo chroniclers drawing on Mexica dynastic memory, as well as additional pictorial manuscripts — supplement and, in places, complicate the Boturini tradition, offering different sequences of place-names, different emphases, and different levels of detail. Historians studying these sources must therefore practice a specific form of source criticism: distinguishing what appears to be genuinely old material, transmitted across generations through oral tradition and pictorial convention, from later colonial-era elaboration, harmonization, or invention introduced to serve specific political, religious, or genealogical purposes under Spanish rule.

A useful way to organize this critical reading is to recognize that the migration tradition, as it survives, layers together at least four distinct kinds of material: remembered history (genuine, if imperfectly preserved, memory of population movement and settlement); political genealogy (claims about descent and status designed to support specific dynastic lineages); sacred narrative (religious meaning attached to the community's relationship with Huitzilopochtli and its ultimate destiny); and later reconstruction (colonial-era systematization, translation, and, in places, invention shaped by the needs of Nahua elites negotiating their status within a new colonial order). Students should approach every specific claim in the migration tradition by asking which of these four layers it most plausibly represents, rather than assuming that pictorial antiquity alone guarantees literal historical accuracy.

Section 13.4

Entering a Crowded Basin of Mexico

Whatever the precise route and timing of their migration, the Mexica who eventually arrived in the Basin of Mexico entered a landscape that had been intensively settled, farmed, and politically organized for centuries. The Basin's chain of shallow interconnected lakes — dominated by the brackish waters of Lake Texcoco, with the somewhat fresher Lake Xochimilco and Lake Chalco to the south — supported a dense network of shoreline and island settlements connected by causeways, canals, and canoe traffic. Surrounding uplands supported rain-fed agriculture, while lakeshore and eventually lake-based chinampa cultivation, discussed more fully in Chapter 14, offered exceptional agricultural productivity to those altepetl that controlled it.

By the time Mexica migrants arrived, this Basin was divided among numerous established altepetl, each with its own tlatoani, temple precinct, and territorial claims. Culhuacan, on the southern shore, held particular prestige as a settlement whose ruling dynasty traced descent from Tula and the Toltecs, discussed in Chapter 10, giving Culhua rulers a claim to legitimacy that newly arrived groups without such ancestry could not match. To the west, the Tepanec confederation, increasingly centered on Azcapotzalco, was expanding its military and political influence. To the east, Acolhua communities maintained their own dynastic traditions, eventually consolidating around Texcoco. Xochimilca and Chalca polities controlled significant portions of the southern lake system, and numerous smaller altepetl dotted the shoreline and surrounding hills.

Within this crowded field, the Mexica possessed almost none of the advantages that established altepetl could claim. They had no ancestral territory within the Basin, no inherited dynasty descended from Tula, and, in the earliest period of their presence, no secure military position. What they did possess, according to later tradition, was a strong sense of shared religious and migratory identity centered on Huitzilopochtli, and, evidently, a willingness to offer military service, labor, and tribute to more established powers in exchange for toleration, protection, or land. This section's central point, one that recurs throughout the remainder of the chapter, is that Mexica political success was built almost entirely through negotiation, subordination, and strategic accommodation with existing powers, not through the immediate assertion of independent strength.

Section 13.5

Chapultepec, Conflict, and Culhuacan

Later traditions describe an early Mexica settlement at Chapultepec ("Grasshopper Hill"), a naturally defensible hill on the western shore of Lake Texcoco with access to fresh spring water — an important asset, given the largely brackish character of the lake itself. According to these traditions, the Mexica presence at Chapultepec provoked hostility from neighboring altepetl, who regarded the newcomers as unwelcome or threatening, and the Mexica were eventually defeated and driven from the site through a coalition of hostile neighbors, including forces associated with Culhuacan and Xochimilco.

Following this expulsion, tradition holds that the surviving Mexica sought refuge under the authority of Culhuacan, the prestigious Toltec-descended altepetl on the southern lakeshore, accepting a subordinate position that allowed them to settle nearby and, according to some accounts, to serve Culhua rulers militarily. This period of dependency is significant precisely because it shows the Mexica accepting a position of clear political inferiority in exchange for a secure, if limited, place within the Basin's existing political order.

The most famous — and most historiographically delicate — episode from this period concerns a tradition involving the daughter of a Culhua ruler. According to later accounts, the Mexica requested a Culhua noblewoman, in some versions explicitly a ruler's daughter, for purposes connected to marriage alliance or ritual elevation of their patron deity's cult; the outcome, in the surviving tradition, involved the young woman's death and, in some versions, subsequent ritual treatment understood by the Culhua as a profound violation, provoking renewed conflict and the Mexica's eventual expulsion from Culhua territory as well. This textbook presents this episode explicitly as an ethnohistorical tradition — a story preserved and shaped by later Mexica chroniclers to explain, among other things, the community's eventual break with Culhuacan and its search for independent territory — rather than as an independently verified sequence of events. Students should note that the story appears in variant forms across different colonial-era sources, a pattern consistent with orally transmitted political narrative rather than a single fixed eyewitness account.

Whatever the precise historical reality underlying these traditions, their cumulative effect within the migration narrative is clear: they establish that the Mexica experienced repeated conflict, expulsion, and renegotiation before finally securing a place of their own, reinforcing the chapter's broader argument that Mexica political success was hard-won rather than smoothly inherited or divinely guaranteed in any simple sense.

Section 13.6

The Foundation of Mexico-Tenochtitlan

Following their departure or expulsion from Culhua territory, later tradition holds that the Mexica migrants withdrew into a marshy island zone within a lake basin already claimed by surrounding polities and increasingly subject to Tepanec power, eventually settling on a modest island or cluster of islets where, according to the most famous version of the foundation narrative, they observed the promised sign of an eagle — in some tellings perched upon a nopal cactus, and in some earlier or variant traditions simply an eagle or bird figure without the fully elaborated cactus-and-serpent imagery familiar from later, more standardized retellings — confirming that this location was the homeland Huitzilopochtli had promised them. This settlement became Mexico-Tenochtitlan.

The conventional date given for this foundation is 1325, a date repeated across numerous colonial-era sources and widely used in modern scholarship and popular commemoration alike. Students should understand, however, that this date functions as a conventional anchor point within the historical tradition rather than as a precisely fixed fact independently confirmed by contemporary documentation or unambiguous archaeological dating. Specialists continue to debate both the exact year and, more fundamentally, what "foundation" actually meant in practice: whether it refers to a single, dramatic founding moment, a specific ritual and calendrical event, or a more gradual process of settlement that later tradition compressed into a single symbolic date. Some scholarship has proposed calendrical or astronomical reasoning behind the specific date recorded in tradition, suggesting that the founding narrative may have been constructed, at least in part, to align with a significant seasonal or ritual moment rather than simply recording an observed historical fact.

Similarly, students should not assume that the fully standardized modern image of an eagle devouring a serpent while perched on a cactus — the image now printed on the Mexican national flag — necessarily reflects the earliest form of the foundational tradition. Some earlier or variant colonial pictorial traditions depict the foundational sign with an eagle or bird alone, without the serpent, suggesting that later chroniclers and artists progressively elaborated and standardized the image over time, eventually fixing it into the now-familiar national symbol. This is an important lesson in how national and civic symbolism can retroactively simplify and harmonize a more variable historical tradition.

What can be said with somewhat greater confidence is that Mexica settlement of this island location placed the community in an ecologically marginal but strategically significant position: surrounded by water, difficult for hostile neighbors to attack directly, and situated near the center of the Basin's lake system, with future access to multiple shorelines. This location, initially a refuge born of necessity rather than choice, would eventually become one of the primary sources of Tenochtitlan's later strength, though realizing that potential required decades of subsequent building, negotiation, and political consolidation examined in the following sections.

Section 13.7

Building an Island Altepetl

Transforming a modest, resource-poor island settlement into a functioning altepetl required sustained effort over multiple generations. Early Mexica settlers needed reliable fresh water, arable or reclaimable land, building materials, and secure means of travel and communication with the mainland — none of which the island offered in abundance at the outset. Tradition and later archaeological evidence together suggest that early Tenochtitlan developed gradually: modest dwellings and a central temple precinct were established first, with subsequent generations expanding settled area through land reclamation at the island's marshy margins, construction of causeways linking the island to the mainland, and development of canoe-based transport across the connecting lake system.

Religious life centered from an early period on a temple precinct dedicated to Huitzilopochtli, reflecting the deity's central role in the migration and foundation traditions examined above; this precinct would, across subsequent centuries, grow into the Templo Mayor examined further in the Material Source feature below and in Chapter 14. Political organization in this early period likely rested on the kinds of kin-based and ward-level social units, sometimes described using the Nahuatl term calpolli, that structured many Nahua altepetl, though detailed evidence for Tenochtitlan's specific early internal organization remains limited for this period.

It is important to note what this section deliberately does not attempt: a full account of chinampa agricultural technology, urban infrastructure at its mature scale, or the elaborate markets and administrative institutions that later characterized imperial Tenochtitlan. Those developments belong properly to Chapter 14's treatment of the mature Triple Alliance imperial system, after Tenochtitlan's political independence and growing wealth allowed for large-scale infrastructure investment. In this earlier period, survival, basic settlement, and the establishment of a defensible, viable island community — one that nonetheless remained economically dependent on wider Basin networks for many resources — were the primary achievements, laying essential groundwork without yet constituting anything resembling an imperial capital.

Section 13.8

Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco: Two Mexica Cities

A detail frequently lost in popular retellings, but essential to an accurate understanding of Mexica political history, is that Tenochtitlan was not the community's only settlement in this period. According to tradition, a dissenting faction of Mexica settlers, following internal disagreement, separated from the main Tenochca group and established a second, related altepetl on a neighboring island a short distance to the north, traditionally dated to approximately 1337 to 1338. This second community became Tlatelolco, and its people are accordingly distinguished as the Tlatelolca, as opposed to the Tenochca of Tenochtitlan proper.

Tlatelolco developed its own ruling dynasty, pursuing a genealogical strategy distinct from Tenochtitlan's: rather than drawing primarily on Culhua-Toltec ancestry, as Tenochtitlan's dynasty would do beginning with Acamapichtli, discussed in Section 13.9, Tlatelolco's ruling house established a marriage connection directly with the Tepanec dynasty of Azcapotzalco, receiving a Tepanec noble as their ruler and thereby linking Tlatelolco's legitimacy to the dominant regional power of the period rather than to the older Culhua-Toltec prestige tradition. This divergence in dynastic strategy underscores an important general point: Mexica political communities pursued multiple, sometimes competing, paths toward legitimacy and security within the Basin's crowded political field, rather than following one single, unified Mexica strategy.

Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco remained politically separate — sometimes cooperative, sometimes rivalrous — altepetl throughout the period this chapter examines and for more than a century afterward. Tlatelolco appears to have begun developing significant commercial specialization, including an early marketplace and long-distance merchants, or pochteca, while it was still an independent altepetl closely tied to Azcapotzalco, well before the political events discussed later in this chapter. Its political incorporation into Tenochtitlan's sphere did not occur until 1473, well beyond the chronological scope of this chapter, and the mature, imperial-scale marketplace for which Tlatelolco later became famous belongs properly to the account of the empire developed in Chapter 14 and beyond. Students should therefore resist any impulse to imagine Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco as a single unified city from the outset, or to import Tlatelolco's later, better-known role as a great imperial marketplace back into this earlier, formative period.

Section 13.9

Acamapichtli and the Construction of Dynastic Legitimacy

For roughly its first half-century, Tenochtitlan appears to have lacked a formally installed dynastic tlatoani in the manner of established Basin altepetl. The precise nature of community leadership during this earlier period is not fully reconstructable from the surviving evidence, though later tradition suggests some form of collective or informally recognized leadership prior to the installation of a proper dynastic ruler. This changed, according to tradition, sometime around the 1370s, when Tenochca leaders sought to install a ruler drawn from a recognized dynastic lineage, a decision that plausibly reflected both internal political considerations and the broader Basin political culture in which genealogical legitimacy mattered greatly to an altepetl's standing among its neighbors.

The individual selected, Acamapichtli, possessed precisely the kind of genealogical credential that the Mexica themselves lacked: descent connecting him to the prestigious Culhua-Toltec lineage, combined, according to tradition, with Mexica ancestry that made him an acceptable choice for the Tenochca community specifically. By installing a ruler whose lineage bridged Culhua-Toltec prestige and Mexica identity, Tenochtitlan constructed, or substantially strengthened, a form of recognized dynastic legitimacy that the community could not otherwise claim through simple ancestral right. This should be understood as a deliberate and sophisticated act of political construction rather than a simple continuation of pre-existing dynastic tradition, since the Mexica, as newcomers to the Basin, had no ancestral dynasty of their own to draw upon.

Scholarly estimates for Acamapichtli's installation vary somewhat, with some placing the event around 1376 and others offering slightly different years; this variation itself illustrates the imprecision inherent in reconstructing exact dates from later tradition. What matters more than pinning down an exact year is recognizing the strategic logic behind the choice: a recently arrived, dynastically disadvantaged community used marriage and genealogical claim-making to acquire, within a single generation, a form of political legitimacy that other altepetl had built up over centuries. This strategy of legitimacy through genealogical construction, rather than through military conquest alone, would remain a recurring feature of Mexica political culture even as the community's military and economic power grew in subsequent generations.

Section 13.10

Under Tepanec Hegemony: Azcapotzalco and Tezozomoc

It is essential for students to understand that Tenochtitlan's early dynastic history, spanning the reigns of Acamapichtli and his two immediate successors, Huitzilihuitl and Chimalpopoca, unfolded almost entirely within a political order dominated by the Tepanec kingdom centered at Azcapotzalco, under the long and increasingly powerful rule of Tezozomoc. Rather than existing as an independent state pursuing its own foreign policy, Tenochtitlan functioned during this period as a subordinate ally within a larger Tepanec hegemonic system, providing military service in campaigns that expanded and consolidated Tepanec regional dominance, and in turn receiving a measure of protection, status, and access to resources that an independent, isolated Tenochtitlan could not have secured on its own.

Archaeological evidence from the Templo Mayor precinct, discussed further in the Material Source feature below, associates the construction phase known as Stage II broadly with this same period — the reigns of Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca, spanning roughly 1376 through 1427 — providing physical, datable evidence of the community's growing investment in its religious and civic center even while remaining politically subordinate to Azcapotzalco. The oldest Mexica object yet recovered from this same archaeological context has been dated to approximately 1350, slightly predating Stage II itself. This combination of subordinate political status and simultaneous local development is important: Tenochtitlan's growing prosperity and religious investment during this period should not be mistaken for growing political independence, since the two developed along different, only partially related, tracks.

Tenochtitlan's marriage alliances during this period reinforced its position within, rather than outside, the Tepanec order. Tlatelolco, as discussed in Section 13.8, secured a Tepanec prince as its own ruler, directly linking that altepetl's dynasty to Azcapotzalco. Tenochtitlan's rulers likewise cultivated marriage and political ties with Tepanec and other established Basin lineages, using the same genealogical strategy that had installed Acamapichtli to further embed the young dynasty within networks of legitimacy and obligation controlled by more established powers. Students should therefore understand the phrase "rise of the Mexica," as used in this chapter's title, as referring to a gradual accumulation of status, wealth, and military experience achieved from within a subordinate position, not as a story of independent expansion pursued from the very beginning of Tenochtitlan's existence.

Section 13.11

Warfare, Marriage, and Political Consolidation

Across the reigns of Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca, several interlocking mechanisms allowed Tenochtitlan to strengthen its position steadily even while remaining formally subordinate to Azcapotzalco. Military participation in Tepanec campaigns gave Tenochca warriors combat experience, opportunities to demonstrate valor and capture prisoners for ritual purposes, and a share, however modest at first, in the spoils and territorial gains that accompanied successful campaigns. Dynastic marriage, as discussed above, connected Tenochtitlan's ruling house to prestigious lineages both within and beyond the Tepanec sphere, gradually building a web of kinship obligations that could translate into political influence. Control over the island altepetl's own developing resources — including whatever agricultural land its early inhabitants had reclaimed from the lake margins, and whatever tribute or labor obligations it could extract from any dependent communities of its own — provided a modest but growing economic base independent of, though still operating within, the larger Tepanec system.

Ritual and religious prestige, centered on the growing Templo Mayor precinct and the cult of Huitzilopochtli, offered a further avenue for status that did not depend directly on Azcapotzalco's approval, allowing Tenochtitlan to cultivate a distinct communal identity and religious significance even while politically subordinate. Taken together, these mechanisms meant that by the time of Chimalpopoca's reign in the 1420s, Tenochtitlan was a considerably more substantial, better-resourced, and more experienced military power than the small migrant settlement that had established itself on the island a century earlier — even though it remained, in formal political terms, a subordinate ally rather than an independent state.

Section 13.12

Crisis in Tepanec Power and the Road to 1428

The long-standing Tepanec order began to unravel with the death of Tezozomoc, Azcapotzalco's dominant ruler, around 1426, after a reign that later tradition remembered as exceptionally long and politically formidable. Tezozomoc's death opened a succession crisis within the Tepanec ruling house, as rival claimants contested control of Azcapotzalco's hegemonic position. Among these claimants, Maxtla emerged as a particularly aggressive figure, whose actions rapidly destabilized relations between Azcapotzalco and its subordinate allies, including Tenochtitlan.

According to tradition, this deteriorating relationship culminated in the death of the Tenochca ruler Chimalpopoca under circumstances connected to the succession conflict, variously described across different sources as execution, assassination, or death under Tepanec pressure. Whatever the precise sequence of events, Chimalpopoca's death removed Tenochtitlan's existing leadership at a moment of acute regional crisis, and the community turned to Itzcoatl, who assumed the position of tlatoani around 1427, to navigate the confrontation with an increasingly hostile Tepanec regime under Maxtla.

Under Itzcoatl's leadership, Tenochtitlan entered into alliance with other Basin powers similarly aggrieved by, or opportunistically positioned against, Maxtla's assertion of Tepanec authority — most significantly the Acolhua leader Nezahualcoyotl and his allies at Texcoco. Surviving accounts are not fully consistent about the exact composition of the wartime coalition, but they agree that Tenochtitlan and the Acolhua forces of Texcoco were its core, and that this alliance, together with other anti-Tepanec forces, defeated Azcapotzalco, conventionally dated to 1428. Tlacopan, a Tepanec-affiliated altepetl, became the third partner in the postwar political settlement that developed, in the years immediately following the war, into the arrangement historians call the Triple Alliance. Students should understand this postwar three-way settlement as the outcome of a process completed shortly after 1428 rather than as a coalition whose precise three-city membership was already fixed before the fighting began.

This chapter deliberately concludes at this juncture. The war of 1428 marks a genuine and important turning point — the moment at which Tenochtitlan, alongside its Acolhua and other allies, ceased to be a subordinate participant within someone else's hegemonic system and became, instead, one of the architects of a new political order. But the detailed workings of that new order — the precise institutional formation and formal workings of the Triple Alliance among Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan, the mechanics of tribute extraction from newly subordinated territories, the development of chinampa agriculture at imperial scale, the growth of the great market at Tlatelolco, and the systematic territorial expansion that transformed a regional alliance into an empire spanning much of Mesoamerica — belong to Chapter 14. This chapter's task has been to explain how Tenochtitlan reached the threshold of that transformation; Chapter 14 explains what the Mexica and their allies built once they crossed it.

Historical Vocabulary
Altepetl

An altepetl (plural altepeme), literally "water-hill" in Nahuatl, was the fundamental sociopolitical unit of Nahua central Mexico: a local community or polity possessing its own collective identity, ruling institutions, jurisdiction over a defined population and territory, and, typically, a dynastic ruling house and patron deity. Most altepetl were internally subdivided into constituent wards or lineage groups. The term should be understood as a rich sociopolitical concept describing sovereignty and community identity, not merely as a description of physical landscape features such as water sources or defensible terrain.

The Basin of Mexico that the Mexica entered was organized into dozens of altepetl of varying size and power, including Culhuacan, Azcapotzalco, Texcoco, Xochimilco, and Chalco, each with its own dynasty, temple precinct, and territorial claims. Understanding the Basin as a landscape of many competing altepetl, rather than as an empty stage awaiting Mexica arrival, is essential to this chapter's central argument.

Historical Vocabulary
Tlatoani

A tlatoani (plural tlatoque), literally "he who speaks," was the ruler of an altepetl, normally selected from among eligible members of a recognized royal lineage by a council of elite electors rather than automatically inheriting office through simple primogeniture. Genealogy established a candidate's eligibility and legitimacy, but it did not by itself determine who would actually be chosen to rule. Among the most prestigious lineages in the Basin of Mexico were those claiming descent from the Toltec dynasty of Tula, discussed in Chapter 10, whose association with earlier Mesoamerican political and religious authority conferred exceptional status on ruling houses that could plausibly claim it.

Because the Mexica arrived in the Basin without an established dynasty of their own, the installation of Acamapichtli as Tenochtitlan's first tlatoani, examined in Section 13.9, represented a significant political achievement: the successful construction of dynastic legitimacy through genealogical connection and political marriage rather than through inherited right alone.

Primary Source Window

The Codex Boturini (Tira de la Peregrinación)

Creator: Unknown Nahua painter-scribe(s) · Date: Probably created around the middle of the sixteenth century, several decades after the Spanish conquest · Type: Pictorial manuscript (amate bark paper), depicting the Mexica migration

Context: The Codex Boturini presents the Mexica migration as a continuous pictorial strip, using footprints, place-glyphs, year-signs, and figures to depict the community's departure from Aztlan and its movement through numerous stopping places under the guidance of Huitzilopochtli's sacred bundle. Significantly, the surviving manuscript ends before reaching the foundation of Tenochtitlan, leaving the culminating episode of the narrative to other sources.

Rather than reproducing the manuscript's pictorial content in narrative prose, students should understand its central features in summary form: a sequence of place-glyphs connected by footprint paths, each associated with a year-sign, tracing the migrants' progress; recurring depictions of the sacred bundle and its bearers; and episodes of conflict, division, and hardship along the route, consistent with independent alphabetic traditions describing a difficult and contested journey rather than an easy or triumphant one.

Analysis: The codex illustrates the layered character of Mexica historical memory discussed in Section 13.3: it preserves what appears to be genuinely old pictorial convention and place-name sequencing, while its precise content and emphases reflect choices made by a mid-sixteenth-century Nahua painter working within a colonial context, quite possibly for an audience — Indigenous, Spanish, or both — with specific interests in seeing Mexica origins presented in a particular way. The manuscript's abrupt ending before the foundation of Tenochtitlan is itself instructive, reminding students that surviving sources are often incomplete or were originally intended to be read alongside other, no longer extant, materials.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a painter working decades after the conquest have chosen to depict the migration using year-signs and place-glyphs rather than in a different format?
  2. What does the manuscript's incompleteness — its ending before the foundation of Tenochtitlan — suggest about how historians should treat any single surviving source?
  3. How might the codex's audience, whether Indigenous nobility, colonial officials, or both, have shaped what the painter chose to include or emphasize?
  4. Why is a pictorial source like this one neither pure invention nor a simple historical transcript?
Material Source Window

Early Templo Mayor Archaeology

Location: The ceremonial precinct of ancient Tenochtitlan, beneath modern central Mexico City · Relevant period: Construction phase generally identified as Stage II, associated with the reigns of Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca, roughly the 1370s to 1420s · Type: Archaeological excavation of successive temple construction layers and associated offerings

Context: Archaeologists excavating the Templo Mayor have identified multiple successive construction stages, each built atop earlier phases as Tenochtitlan's temple precinct grew across the fourteenth, fifteenth, and early sixteenth centuries. The construction phase generally identified as Stage II corresponds broadly to the reigns of Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca, roughly 1376 to 1427. Within this same archaeological context, the oldest Mexica object yet recovered at the site — a carved stone head — has been dated to approximately 1350, somewhat earlier than the Stage II construction itself. Together, this material provides physical, datable evidence roughly contemporary with the community's early urban development and considerably closer in time to the events themselves than the sixteenth-century pictorial and alphabetic traditions examined elsewhere in this chapter.

Analysis: Material evidence of this kind offers historians a valuable, if limited, check on the later textual and pictorial traditions: it can confirm that substantial construction and religious activity were indeed underway during the period tradition assigns to Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca, even though it cannot, by itself, confirm specific narrative details such as the eagle-and-nopal sign or the exact date of 1325. Combining archaeological and textual evidence in this way — using each to test and contextualize the other rather than treating either alone as sufficient — models the kind of layered historical reasoning this chapter has emphasized throughout.

  3. Why is it useful to have both textual and archaeological evidence for the same general period, even when neither is fully conclusive on its own?
Historical Debate

What Can the Mexica Migration Traditions Tell Us?

The question: Given that the surviving migration narratives were recorded generations after the events they describe, how much historical weight can they bear, and what kind of historical knowledge, if any, can historians responsibly claim to derive from them?

Interpretation A — The traditions substantially preserve real historical memory of population movement: Proponents of this view argue that recurring elements across multiple surviving traditions — including shared place-names and shared episodes such as conflict at Chapultepec and subordination to Culhuacan, some of which may derive from common earlier sources now lost — suggest an underlying core of genuinely remembered historical experience, transmitted through oral tradition and pictorial convention across many generations before being fixed in the surviving sixteenth-century manuscripts. Aztlan's precise location and the migration's exact route remain unresolved, but the recurrence of specific named stopping places and episodes across independent later retellings is, on this view, difficult to explain as pure invention.
Interpretation B — The traditions are primarily political and sacred narrative, not reliable migration history: Proponents of this view emphasize that the migration narrative's structure closely serves later Mexica political interests — legitimizing the community's presence in the Basin, explaining its relationship to more prestigious neighbors, and establishing a sacred mandate for eventual greatness — and argue that this narrative function should make historians deeply cautious about extracting specific, reliable facts about thirteenth-century population movement from a source shaped so thoroughly by later political and religious concerns.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of pictorial and alphabetic sources, comparative evidence from the migration traditions of other Nahua-speaking groups, and, to a limited degree, archaeological and linguistic evidence bearing on population movement in central Mexico during this period.

Why it matters: This textbook does not resolve the debate by declaring one interpretation correct. Instead, it treats the migration tradition as valuable evidence for how the Mexica understood and constructed their own identity and legitimacy, while remaining appropriately cautious about extracting a precise, literal migration history from sources compiled generations after the fact.

Student task: Using the layered-reading framework introduced in Section 13.3 — remembered history, political genealogy, sacred narrative, and later reconstruction — identify which layer or layers seem most plausible for specific episodes discussed in this chapter, such as the departure from Aztlan, the conflict at Chapultepec, and the tradition concerning the Culhua ruler's daughter.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Migrants to Altepetl

What Changed

  • The Mexica moved from a mobile, dependent migrant community to a settled, dynastically legitimated altepetl with its own temple precinct and ruling house
  • Political status shifted from repeated subordination and expulsion under Chapultepec and Culhuacan to formal, if still subordinate, alliance within the Tepanec hegemonic system
  • Military experience and resources accumulated steadily across the reigns of Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca
  • Political status shifted decisively with the war of 1428, transforming Tenochtitlan from a subordinate ally into a co-architect of a new regional order

What Stayed the Same

  • Religious devotion to Huitzilopochtli and the sense of a divinely guided community identity remained constant from the migration tradition through the founding of Tenochtitlan and beyond
  • The strategy of constructing legitimacy through genealogy and marriage alliance, first used to install Acamapichtli, would remain a recurring feature of Mexica political culture
  • Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco remained distinct, separately ruled altepetl throughout this entire period
  • The Basin of Mexico's underlying political structure, organized around competing altepetl and shifting hegemonic alliances, persisted even as specific powers rose and fell

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic change in Mexica political fortunes and the underlying continuities in religious identity and political strategy helps students understand the 1428 turning point as the culmination of a long process rather than a sudden, unexplained transformation.

Cause and Effect

Subordination and Opportunity: Tenochtitlan under Tepanec Hegemony

Long-term causes

The Mexica's late arrival in an already densely settled Basin of Mexico, their lack of an inherited dynasty, and the region's broader political culture in which genealogical prestige and hegemonic alliance systems structured relations among altepetl.

Short-term causes

Tenochtitlan's need for security and resources following its precarious founding, and Azcapotzalco's need for reliable military allies as Tepanec power expanded under Tezozomoc.

Effect

Tenochtitlan accumulated military experience, marriage connections, and religious prestige across nearly half a century of formally subordinate alliance, even as it remained politically dependent on Azcapotzalco.

Historical significance

This accumulated strength positioned Tenochtitlan, alongside its Acolhua allies at Texcoco, to convert the succession crisis following Tezozomoc's death into an opportunity to overturn Tepanec hegemony entirely in 1428, with Tlacopan joining the resulting political settlement soon afterward.

Historical complexity: Subordination and opportunity were not opposites in this period; Tenochtitlan's dependent status under Azcapotzalco simultaneously constrained its independence and provided the military and political experience that made its eventual rise possible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in a Community under Construction

Behind the political narrative of migration, subordination, and dynastic maneuvering examined in this chapter lay the daily realities of building and sustaining a community on a marshy island with limited natural resources. Early Tenochca and Tlatelolca households likely depended on fishing, waterfowl hunting, and the gathering of lake plants and insects to supplement whatever agricultural land could be reclaimed or accessed on the mainland, since large-scale chinampa cultivation, examined fully in Chapter 14, developed only gradually and reached its greatest extent after this chapter's chronological endpoint.

Household and ward-level social organization, likely structured through calpolli-style kin and neighborhood groupings common among Nahua communities, would have governed labor obligations, including construction of temples, causeways, and canals, as well as military service owed first to Culhuacan and later to Azcapotzalco. Religious practice, centered on the cult of Huitzilopochtli but likely incorporating broader Nahua ritual and calendrical traditions as well, structured community life through recurring festivals, offerings, and the gradual expansion of the central temple precinct. Although detailed evidence for gender roles, childhood, and household organization in this specific early period remains limited, later, better-documented Mexica social patterns, examined in Chapter 15, likely had roots extending back into this formative century of settlement and consolidation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the rise of the Mexica from a marginal, late-arriving migrant community to an increasingly significant Basin of Mexico polity, culminating in the war of 1428 that ended Tepanec hegemony and opened the way for the imperial system examined in Chapter 14. Throughout, the chapter has insisted on a central corrective to popular retellings: the Mexica rise was neither inevitable nor the simple fulfillment of prophecy, but the outcome of a century of migration, negotiation, subordination, military service, dynastic marriage, and strategic legitimacy-building conducted within an already crowded and competitive political landscape.

Students have examined the terminological distinctions between Mexica, Tenochca, Tlatelolca, Nahua, and Aztec, and have learned to treat the migration tradition — centered on Aztlan, Huitzilopochtli, and a sequence of stopping places recorded chiefly in the sixteenth-century Codex Boturini and related sources — as a layered historical document combining remembered history, political genealogy, sacred narrative, and later colonial-era reconstruction, rather than as a simple, literal historical record.

The chapter has traced Mexica traditions of settlement, conflict, and expulsion at Chapultepec, subordination to and conflict with Culhuacan, and the eventual founding of Mexico-Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco, conventionally dated to 1325 but understood as a conventional rather than precisely fixed date. It has emphasized that Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco developed as two separate, related Mexica altepetl, each pursuing its own dynastic strategy, and that Tlatelolco's later incorporation into Tenochtitlan's sphere lay well beyond this chapter's chronological scope.

Students have examined how Tenochtitlan constructed dynastic legitimacy through the installation of Acamapichtli, whose Culhua-Toltec ancestry combined with Mexica identity provided the newly settled community with a form of legitimacy it could not otherwise claim. The chapter has stressed that Tenochtitlan's subsequent political history, spanning the reigns of Acamapichtli, Huitzilihuitl, and Chimalpopoca, unfolded within, not independent of, the Tepanec hegemonic system centered at Azcapotzalco under Tezozomoc, and that Tenochtitlan's accumulating strength during this period took the form of growing wealth, military experience, and religious prestige acquired from a position of formal subordination.

Finally, the chapter has traced the succession crisis that followed Tezozomoc's death around 1426, the conflict involving Maxtla and the death of Chimalpopoca, and the emergence of Itzcoatl's leadership, culminating in the alliance of Tenochtitlan and its Acolhua partners at Texcoco, together with other anti-Tepanec forces, that defeated Azcapotzalco and ended Tepanec hegemony in 1428, with Tlacopan joining the resulting postwar settlement as its third partner shortly afterward. This chapter has deliberately stopped at that threshold, leaving the mature imperial system — the formal Triple Alliance, tribute administration, chinampa agriculture at scale, the great market of Tlatelolco, and territorial expansion — to Chapter 14.

Chapter 13 has asked how a marginal migrant community became a serious Basin of Mexico power. Chapter 14 turns to the question of what that power became once Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan joined together in the Triple Alliance to build an imperial system of their own.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to set aside the comfortable, retrospective certainty that comes from knowing how the Mexica story eventually turned out, and instead to see the community's rise as its own participants might have experienced it: as a long, uncertain, and often precarious process of migration, negotiation, subordination, and gradual accumulation of status and strength. Nothing about the small migrant community that settled a marshy island around 1325 guaranteed that its descendants would one day command an empire.

The habits of mind this chapter has introduced — reading migration and foundation traditions as layered historical documents rather than literal transcripts, distinguishing conventional dates from precisely established facts, resisting the retroactive imposition of later national symbolism onto earlier and more variable traditions, and recognizing subordination and opportunity as intertwined rather than opposed — will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook's treatment of Indigenous political history.

By ending at the threshold of 1428 rather than continuing into the imperial period, this chapter has also modeled an important structural principle of the larger textbook: significant historical transformations deserve their own careful treatment, rather than being folded prematurely into the more dramatic and better-documented history that follows. Tenochtitlan in 1428 was neither the marginal migrant settlement of a century earlier nor yet the imperial capital it would become. It stood, instead, at a genuine hinge point in its own history — newly free of Tepanec hegemony, allied with Texcoco and soon joined by Tlacopan, and possessed of the accumulated military experience, dynastic legitimacy, and religious prestige that would make the achievements described in Chapter 14 possible.

Understanding this earlier, more uncertain history equips students to read the empire's later grandeur not as a natural or inevitable outcome, but as the product of specific, contingent, and often difficult choices made by a community that had once possessed nothing more than a migration story, a patron deity, and a determination to find a permanent home.
