Resistance and Survival under Colonial Rule

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: Authority That Had to Be Negotiated

In August 1680, Pueblo communities spread across hundreds of miles of New Mexico drove their Spanish colonizers out and held the province for twelve years. In the same century, Mapuche leaders in southern Chile bargained with Spanish governors from positions of strength, escaped enslaved people in Alagoas built a network of settlements that Portuguese forces needed decades to destroy, and Indigenous villagers across New Spain and Peru sued colonial officials in royal courts. These were not the same kind of act, and understanding the differences is the work of this chapter.

Colonial rule in Latin America was real, violent, and durable, and it reorganized the lives of millions of people. It was also never absolute. Crowns in Madrid and Lisbon governed across oceans with small bureaucracies and limited revenue. Viceroys, governors, judges, priests, mine owners, and planters depended on the labor, knowledge, and at least partial cooperation of the people they ruled. That dependence gave Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, free people of African descent, and mixed communities openings to bargain, evade, appeal, and fight. This chapter examines how they used those openings between roughly 1500 and 1800, and what they gained and lost.

The chapter's first argument is that resistance should not be equated with rebellion. Armed uprisings were consequential and are given sustained treatment here, but they were the rarest and most dangerous option. More common were petitions to royal courts, quiet avoidance of tribute and labor drafts, flight, the maintenance of village government and landholding, and the continued use of Indigenous and African languages and kinship systems. An Indigenous community defending its land through litigation, an enslaved family planning an escape, a Mapuche lonko negotiating with a governor, and the residents of Palmares fortifying a stockade pursued different strategies under different constraints. Historians have to ask what each strategy could and could not accomplish.

The second argument concerns outcomes. Some movements were crushed, sometimes with great brutality. Others forced lasting concessions. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 expelled the Spanish for twelve years, and the colonial order that returned after 1692 was not the one that had been overthrown. Mapuche communities south of the Biobío River were never conquered during the colonial period and shaped Spanish policy through repeated parleys. Juan Santos Atahualpa's movement from 1742 closed part of Peru's central forest to Spanish control for roughly a century. Maroon communities in Colombia, Mexico, and Brazil won recognition, were destroyed, or negotiated uneasy compromises. The chapter treats these outcomes as results of particular balances of power, not as expressions of a single pattern.

The third argument is a caution against teleology. It is tempting to read these movements as early chapters of independence, but most of their leaders were responding to local grievances and aiming at local outcomes: the end of a labor draft, the expulsion of a priest or magistrate, freedom for a community, the defense of a territory. Some invoked kings, saints, or Inca ancestors in ways that do not fit later national categories. Independence in the early nineteenth century, treated in Volume III, grew out of different circumstances, and many of the communities discussed here took royalist as well as insurgent positions when that time came.

The evidence demands attention from the start. Most surviving records of resistance were produced by those being resisted. Spanish and Portuguese military reports, court proceedings, chronicles, and governors' letters describe rebels as idolaters, fugitives, or criminals, and they were written to justify repression, request resources, or protect careers. Indigenous voices survive in petitions, notarial records, community land titles, and a small number of Indigenous-authored texts, and enslaved people's voices survive mainly through interrogations conducted under duress. Oral tradition, archaeology, and later memory, including nationalist memory in Brazil, Colombia, Mexico, and the United States, add further layers that must be distinguished from contemporary evidence.

This chapter completes Volume II and Part X. Chapter 44 described slavery and the making of Afro-Latin America, Chapter 45 the hierarchies of race, family, and gender, and Chapter 46 Christianity, missions, and colonial culture. Chapter 47 examines how people living inside those systems contested and endured them. It begins with the many forms resistance took (Division I), turns to Indigenous uprisings and autonomous frontiers (Division II) and to maroon communities, including Palmares (Division III), and closes with colonial responses and the limits of domination (Division IV). The great rebellions of Túpac Amaru II and Túpac Katari, the imperial reforms of Chapter 48, and the Haitian Revolution of Chapter 50 belong to later chapters, though the conclusion shows how this chapter prepares for them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish accommodation, negotiation, evasion, flight, armed rebellion, and territorial autonomy, and explain why resistance and rebellion are not synonyms.
  2. Explain how Indigenous communities used royal courts, petitions, and legal representatives, and assess the limits of litigation as a form of defense.
  3. Analyze how tribute, labor demands, and local abuses produced everyday conflict that could remain local or escalate into collective confrontation.
  4. Compare the causes, leadership, coalitions, and outcomes of the Mixtón War, the Tepehuán uprising, the Maya risings of 1546 and 1712, and Juan Santos Atahualpa's movement.
  5. Evaluate the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 as a coordinated Indigenous coalition, and explain how the Spanish return after 1692 differed from the earlier colonial regime.
  6. Interpret Mapuche warfare and the parlamento system, including the 1641 Quilín negotiations, without reducing them to either Spanish victory or modern treaty diplomacy.
  7. Explain why some Indigenous societies, including the Itza, Amazonian peoples, and Chaco peoples, retained autonomy, with reference to ecology, distance, and colonial resource limits.
  8. Distinguish maroonage, flight, military resistance, settlement, and negotiated recognition, and trace these processes in New Granada, New Spain, and Brazil.
  9. Analyze Palmares as a network of settlements with internal political disagreement, using hostile colonial records critically.
  10. Assess how women, freedpeople, and Indigenous intermediaries shaped resisting communities, and how colonial authorities combined repression with negotiation.
  11. Evaluate historians' debates over what counts as resistance and the claim that colonial rule's reach was continually shaped by those who lived under it.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How can historians identify resistance when most surviving records were written by the authorities it challenged?
  2. Why did some communities choose litigation, others flight, and others armed rebellion, and what conditions made each choice viable?
  3. Why were some uprisings crushed while others, such as the Pueblo Revolt or Mapuche resistance, produced lasting changes in colonial policy?
  4. Why did Spanish and Portuguese officials sometimes negotiate with communities they described as rebels or fugitives, and what did each side hope to gain?
  5. How did geography, including forests, mountains, swamps, deserts, and distance from colonial centers, shape the possibility of autonomy?
  6. What internal divisions, over strategy, leadership, or religion, appeared within resisting communities, and how did they affect outcomes?
  7. In what ways did women, Indigenous intermediaries, and people of mixed ancestry shape resistance and negotiation?
  8. Is it historically useful to treat cultural persistence as resistance, and where does that framing mislead?

Historical Context

Chapters 33 through 43 described how Spanish and Portuguese authority was built: encomienda and tribute, the repúblicas de indios, viceroyalties and audiencias, silver mining and the mit'a, haciendas and plantations, and the cities that linked them to Atlantic markets. Chapter 44 traced the arrival of millions of enslaved Africans, and Chapter 46 showed how missions and parish institutions brought Christianity into Indigenous and African communities. All of these systems rested on demands for labor, goods, and obedience. All of them also created points of friction, and the arrangements that resulted were negotiated, contested, and renegotiated across generations.

The reach of colonial government varied enormously. Core regions such as the Valley of Mexico, the central Andes, and Brazil's sugar coast experienced dense administration, resident clergy, and constant labor demands. Beyond them stretched frontiers, from northern New Mexico and the Chichimeca borderlands to the Mapuche country south of the Biobío, the Maya lowlands of Petén, the Amazon basin, and the Chaco, where Spanish and Portuguese claims outran their capacity to enforce them. Chapters 34 through 39 explained how conquest in many of these regions was incomplete from the start. Chapter 47 follows the same regions into the long era of colonial rule and asks what incomplete control meant for the people who lived there.

Colonial law itself shaped the forms resistance took. Spanish legal tradition treated the crown as the ultimate source of justice and allowed vassals to petition against abuses by local officials, and Indigenous people were legally defined as vassals with rights and obligations, including access to special courts and protectors. Law did not guarantee enforcement, but it gave Indigenous litigants a language of appeal. The legal position of enslaved people was different, since enslavement placed them under the authority of an owner, though Iberian law also recognized some rights, including limited avenues for appeal over cruelty and for purchasing freedom. These legal frameworks, together with the economic power of mine owners, planters, and ranchers, defined the terrain on which resistance operated.

The chapter's chronology extends from the sixteenth century to the late eighteenth. The Mixtón War of 1540–1542 and the Maya rising of 1546 belong to the conquest generations. The Tepehuán revolt, Palmares, the Pueblo Revolt, and the Quilín negotiations belong to the seventeenth century. The Chiapas rebellion of 1712 and Juan Santos Atahualpa's movement belong to the eighteenth. The Bourbon and Pombaline reforms that began to reshape imperial rule in the middle of the eighteenth century, and the great Andean rebellions of the 1780s, are reserved for Chapters 48 and 49.

Section 47.1

Colonial Authority and the Many Forms of Resistance

Colonial authority was layered, and each layer had its own interests. The crown issued laws and demanded revenue. Viceroys and governors tried to maintain order and satisfy competing groups. Judges of the audiencias heard appeals. Priests and religious orders sought converts and parish income. Encomenderos, mine owners, hacendados, and planters wanted labor. Local magistrates, whether corregidores or alcaldes mayores, often earned their living by trading with and extracting from the communities they governed. Indigenous caciques and curacas stood between these demands and their own people. Because these layers did not always agree, the people below them could play one off against another, and they often did.

It follows that resistance covered a wide spectrum. At one end lay accommodation: accepting colonial institutions while shaping how they worked, such as an Indigenous council that administered tribute efficiently to keep outsiders away from village affairs. Next came negotiation, in which communities bargained over the quantity and timing of tribute or labor. Evasion included hiding population from tribute counts, avoiding drafts, or working slowly. Legal action meant petitioning or suing. Flight meant leaving a plantation, mine, mission, or tribute community, temporarily or permanently. Armed rebellion meant organized violence against colonial authority. At the far end stood territorial autonomy, the maintenance or creation of communities that colonial authorities could not govern. Individuals and communities moved among these options as circumstances changed, and a village that litigated one decade might rebel the next.

The line between individual action and coordinated movement is also important. A single enslaved man who ran away for a week to visit relatives was acting differently from a group of fugitives who built a fortified settlement. A single Indigenous litigant arguing a boundary dispute was acting differently from a council of communities pursuing a shared legal campaign. Historians distinguish these cases because they involved different risks, required different resources, and had different political consequences, even though all of them reveal limits on colonial control.

The evidence complicates the picture. Colonial records rarely used the word resistance. They used terms such as alboroto and tumulto (tumult, riot), idolatría, alzamiento (uprising), cimarrón, or, in Portuguese, quilombo, which criminalized or pathologized the behavior they described. A village protest over a corrupt official might be recorded as a riot incited by troublemakers. Historians reading these sources must separate the officials' categories from the participants' aims, and they must remember that records survive disproportionately when something went wrong for the authorities, meaning that successful evasion often leaves no trace.

Several cautions follow. Resistance should not be romanticized. Indigenous communities were not unified, and many fought on the Spanish side, as Tlaxcalan and Mexica auxiliaries did in the Mixtón War (section 47.6). Resistance leaders could be coercive toward their own followers, and rebellions sometimes killed other Indigenous people and enslaved people. Nor should every act of survival be treated as conscious political protest; section 47.5 and the Historical Debate in section 47.18 return to this problem. The chapter uses resistance to mean actions that limited, redirected, or defied colonial demands, whatever their motives, and it asks in each case what evidence supports the claim.

Historical Vocabulary
Resistance, Accommodation, and Autonomy

Resistance refers here to actions that limited, redirected, or defied colonial demands, from litigation and evasion to armed rebellion. Accommodation means working within colonial institutions, often to protect community interests. Autonomy means practical self-government free of effective colonial control, whether in a settlement beyond the frontier or within a recognized community with negotiated privileges.

The terms overlap in practice. A community could accommodate Spanish law to defend its land, and a negotiated agreement could preserve autonomy at the price of obligations to the crown. Modern equivalents can mislead: resistance did not always mean opposition to colonial rule in principle, and autonomy did not mean independence in the modern national sense.

Section 47.2

Petitions, Litigation, and the Defense of Indigenous Communities

Spanish legal culture offered subjects a recognized route to protest. The crown was understood as the source of justice, and vassals could petition it against abuses by local officials. The formula se obedece pero no se cumple, "it is obeyed but not carried out," allowed colonial officials to acknowledge a royal order while suspending its application until the crown was informed of local objections. The practice gave local elites room to resist unwelcome policy, and it also reflected a system in which law and enforcement were separate. Indigenous communities learned to work within this culture, and they became extensive users of colonial courts.

In New Spain, Indigenous litigation expanded after the Juzgado General de Indios was established in the 1590s to hear Indigenous complaints. Communities hired lawyers and agents, paid for them from community funds, and pursued cases before local magistrates, audiencias, and the viceroy. They sued over land boundaries, the seizure of community property by haciendas, excessive tribute, forced labor, abuses by priests and officials, and the election of local officers. Many communities produced land titles, including the títulos primordiales, which blended Indigenous memory of territory with Spanish legal forms and, in some cases, were composed or reworked in the seventeenth century to meet legal needs. The documents illustrate how communities adapted their own historical memory to a colonial evidentiary system.

The Andes show a similar pattern with different institutions. Curacas and communities litigated over mit'a quotas, tribute assessments, the encroachment of estates on community lands, and the status of people absent from their villages. After the land-regularization measures of the 1590s, known as composición, communities could purchase confirmation of their holdings and many did. The Andean chronicler Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala, writing around 1615, addressed a long illustrated letter to the king cataloguing abuses by priests, magistrates, and mine owners, a petition on a very large scale and one that never reached the royal court as intended. Indigenous petitioning could also build on earlier agreements. Tlaxcala, discussed in Chapter 31, invoked the memory of its 1519 alliance for generations to secure tax exemptions and privileges from the crown.

Litigation had serious limits. Cases were expensive and slow. Communities depended on lawyers, scribes, and translators who might exploit them. Powerful opponents could bribe officials, delay proceedings, or ignore judgments, and a favorable ruling was not self-enforcing. Litigation could also expose internal divisions, because many suits were brought by one Indigenous community against another over boundaries, or by commoners against their own caciques. By using colonial courts, communities also strengthened the legitimacy of the institutions that were taking their land and labor. Historians therefore read litigation as both a tool of defense and a form of integration into colonial structures, and they do not treat the two as mutually exclusive.

Black litigants also used the courts. Enslaved people in Spanish America brought suits to complain of cruelty, to enforce promises of freedom, or to arrange the purchase of their freedom through coartación, an institution that Chapter 44 discussed. Their access was more constrained and their risks greater than those of Indigenous litigants, but the pattern is similar: law gave subordinated people a language of claims, and the outcomes depended on resources, witnesses, and local power. The records these suits generated, known as expedientes, are among the most valuable sources for the chapter's themes, since they preserve testimony, however mediated, from people who rarely wrote their own histories.

Section 47.3

Tribute, Labor, and Everyday Negotiation with Colonial Power

Tribute and labor were the daily points of contact between colonial government and Indigenous communities. Tribute was assessed by periodic counts, and communities responded in predictable ways: understating their population, hiding households, delaying payment, or disputing assessments after epidemics had reduced their numbers. Collective complaints to magistrates or viceroys were common, and village councils sometimes sent delegations to the capital. In the Andes, the mit'a and similar labor drafts generated constant bargaining over who would go, for how long, and with what compensation. Communities lobbied to replace men who died, to exempt specific households, or to commute service into cash payments.

Work itself could be a site of negotiation. Indigenous laborers in mines and textile workshops (obrajes) slowed their pace, left without permission, or demanded better wages and food. In the seventeenth century, many Spanish officials in New Spain and elsewhere supplemented their incomes through repartimientos de mercancías, forcing Indigenous communities to buy goods, often overpriced, on credit, or to produce cloth or dye at set prices. The practice generated resentment and frequent protest, and historians identify it as a major grievance behind several of the uprisings discussed later in this chapter. In Portuguese Brazil, Indigenous people living in missionary and royal villages, aldeias, were subject to labor demands by colonists and officials, and they responded by negotiation, litigation, and flight.

Local authorities were central to the story. Caciques and curacas who collected tribute and supplied labor gained status and sometimes wealth, but they were squeezed between Spanish demands and community expectations. Some enriched themselves at their communities' expense, and others defended their people vigorously and faced Spanish retaliation. Communities that believed their leaders had failed them could depose them, petition against them, or simply stop cooperating. Women participated in these struggles as petitioners, as informal leaders, and as participants in crowd actions, and in some regions as cacicas holding authority in their own right.

Conflicts could stay local or escalate. The great majority of disputes ended in compromise, delay, or exhaustion. Some, however, grew into collective violence when a particular grievance combined with a trigger such as a harvest failure, an epidemic, or a new demand. In 1660, residents of the Tehuantepec region in Oaxaca killed a local magistrate in a rising linked to forced trade and tribute. In June 1692, a grain shortage in Mexico City led a crowd of Indigenous people and other poor residents to burn parts of the viceregal palace and city hall, an urban riot that shows how the same grievances could unite groups colonial categories separated. Such events were followed by punishment, but also, often, by investigation of the abuses that had provoked them, an uneven feedback that section 47.17 examines.

Section 47.4

Flight, Relocation, and the Search for Freedom

Flight was among the most widely available forms of resistance because it required no organization and little confrontation. Enslaved people fled plantations, mines, urban households, and ranches. Indigenous laborers left haciendas, mines, and missions. Tribute-paying villagers abandoned their communities. In the Andes, so many people left their home communities that the colonial population divided into originarios, who remained and bore the full tribute and mit'a burden, and forasteros, who migrated and, at least for a time, escaped it. Authorities responded by revising tribute rules to capture forasteros, and the contest between flight and administrative adjustment continued for generations.

For enslaved people, historians distinguish between kinds of flight. Petit marronage means temporary absence, often to visit family, protest conditions, or negotiate with an owner by withdrawing labor. Large-scale or permanent flight, sometimes called grand marronage, meant leaving for good, which created the conditions for maroon communities. Temporary flight was far more common and usually less punished, and owners often tolerated it as a recognized form of negotiation. Permanent flight was treated as a serious threat to property and public order, and it provoked pursuit, severe punishment, and in some cases military expeditions.

Flight depended on geography and social networks. Fugitives sought places where pursuit was difficult: swamps, mountains, dense forests, and remote frontier ranches. They also relied on people. Family members, free people of color, Indigenous communities, itinerant traders, and sympathetic or indifferent townspeople provided food, information, shelter, and false identities. Cities offered anonymity, and many fugitives passed as free in urban neighborhoods. Others sought out Indigenous communities beyond the frontier. Fugitives who could take up skills useful to their hosts, such as metalworking or knowledge of Spanish customs, had better prospects, and some groups of fugitives became important trading partners as well as military allies.

Obstacles were severe. Slave catchers, known as rancheadores in Spanish America and capitães-do-mato ("bush captains") in Brazil, made a living returning fugitives, and punishments for those caught included whipping, mutilation, and execution. Hunger, disease, and exposure killed many. Families complicated flight, because those unwilling to leave children or elderly relatives behind faced agonizing choices. Evidence suggests men fled more often than women in many regions, though records are incomplete. A temporary escape became a lasting community only when conditions allowed: a defensible site, reliable food, enough people for defense, leaders who could hold the group together, and some means of securing trade and information. Section 47.12 returns to these conditions.

Section 47.5

Cultural Persistence, Kinship, and Community Survival

The most widespread form of survival was the continuation of community life. Indigenous languages persisted: Nahuatl, Maya languages, Quechua, Aymara, Mapudungun, and many others remained the everyday languages of most Indigenous people throughout the colonial period, often alongside Spanish or Portuguese. Communal landholding, village government, and obligations of mutual aid continued, shaped by Spanish institutions such as the cabildo but frequently rooted in older forms such as the Andean ayllu or the Nahua altepetl. Kinship networks, including ritual kinship (compadrazgo, godparenthood), provided economic support and political alliance. In African-descended communities, memory of homelands, music, language, and religious practices was carried into new settings and transformed, often within the lay brotherhoods (cofradías) discussed in Chapter 46.

Written and oral traditions also persisted. Maya scribes produced the Books of Chilam Balam, which preserved prophecy, calendar knowledge, and historical memory in Maya language written in Latin letters. A Quechua-language manuscript compiled in the Huarochirí region around 1608 recorded Andean religious traditions at the behest of an extirpating priest, a reminder that preservation itself could occur through colonial channels. Nahua annalists wrote histories in Nahuatl. Notaries in Nahua communities recorded wills and land transfers in Nahuatl for generations, a body of evidence that has transformed understanding of Indigenous life. Such documents show communities adopting European writing for their own purposes.

Whether any of this counts as resistance is a real question. In some cases the answer is clearly yes. The Andean movement known as Taki Onqoy, which emerged in the Huamanga region in the 1560s, urged Andean people to reject Christianity and return to the worship of Andean sacred places, and colonial authorities treated it as a serious threat and suppressed it through extirpation campaigns. Historians still debate its scale, its ties to the Neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba (Chapter 36), and its long-term significance. In other cases, continuity reflected habit, preference, and the practical impossibility of replacing a language or kinship system, with no oppositional intent. Indigenous people also actively adopted Spanish goods, animals, legal forms, and sometimes religious practices, and what persisted was usually transformed. Cultural persistence is best understood as survival and adaptation, and as resistance only when evidence shows that people used it to defy colonial demands.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Language, and the Work of Community Continuity

Community survival rested on the daily work of households. Women taught children the household language, prepared food according to local custom, wove textiles that carried meaning and circulated in exchange and tribute, and, in many communities, served as healers and ritual specialists. Among the Mapuche, machi, religious specialists who were often women, played central roles in healing and ceremony. Men and women arranged marriages that sustained kin networks and land rights, and they chose godparents who linked families across community lines. Village officers organized the labor of repairing churches, roads, and irrigation systems, and they managed the cofradía funds that paid for festivals, burials, and shared obligations.

Enslaved families maintained connections under constant threat of sale. Kin ties across plantations, urban households, and nearby free communities were sources of support and, as the previous section showed, of escape networks. Hidden in this ordinary life are the strategies that made survival possible: sharing food in lean years, giving shelter to an exhausted traveler, hiding a relative from a tribute collector. The sources record these acts unevenly, since officials noticed disorder, not the routines that prevented it, and absence from the record is not absence from history.

Section 47.6

Indigenous Revolts in Central and Northern New Spain

The Mixtón War of 1540–1542 broke out in Nueva Galicia, the northwestern region conquered in the 1530s by Nuño de Guzmán, whose campaigns were notorious for violence, enslavement, and forced labor. The Caxcanes and neighboring peoples, including Zacatecos and Tecuexes, rose against the colonizers, drawing on grievances over tribute, labor, and the seizure of people. The revolt was serious enough that Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza led a major campaign himself, and the Spaniards suffered notable setbacks, including the death of Pedro de Alvarado in 1541 during a retreat near Nochistlán. The sources describe fortified hilltop strongholds, called peñoles, where rebels held out, and religious language that urged a return to older gods.

Spanish victory depended on Indigenous allies. Mendoza's forces included tens of thousands of auxiliaries from central Mexico, among them Nahua and Tlaxcalan soldiers, a pattern that follows directly from the alliances described in Chapter 31. The auxiliaries fought for their own reasons, including status, plunder, and the wish to preserve privileges earned in the conquest of the Mexica. After the war, many captives were enslaved or forced into labor, and the region's Indigenous population declined sharply. Its leader Tenamaxtli survived for years, and in the mid-1550s presented a legal complaint in Spain with the support of Bartolomé de las Casas, a reminder that even defeated rebels could use the language of royal justice. The war is often cited as a turning point in the northern frontier, where the combination of silver discoveries, Indigenous resistance, and the later Chichimeca War shaped Spanish policy for decades. By 1591, the viceroy was settling several hundred Tlaxcalan families in northern frontier towns under negotiated privileges, an example of the empire relying on one Indigenous community to hold another's territory.

The Tepehuán uprising began in November 1616 in Nueva Vizcaya, in the mountains and valleys of present-day Durango. The Tepehuanes had endured disease, mining labor demands, encomienda, and the pressure of Jesuit and Franciscan missions, and prophetic leaders, known in Spanish sources through figures such as Gogojito, urged them to purge the Spanish presence. In the first days of the rising, Tepehuán fighters killed missionaries, colonists, and their dependents in scattered settlements, and Spanish sources report that several hundred people died. The rising spread to neighboring peoples, though not all joined, and some groups fought with the Spanish.

The Spanish response was a prolonged campaign under the governor of Nueva Vizcaya and reinforcements from other regions. The war lasted until about 1620 and ended in the defeat of the major concentrations of rebels, followed by punishment, enslavement of captives, and further depopulation. Historians stress that the Tepehuán rising was not a general Indigenous revolt. It arose from specific local conditions, involved particular communities, and met opposition from other Indigenous groups. Neither the Mixtón War nor the Tepehuán uprising should be taken as representative of all Indigenous societies. Both show, however, that Spanish control on the northern frontier depended on a precarious balance of mining wealth, missions, and military force, and that Indigenous communities could and did threaten it.

Section 47.7

Maya Resistance in Yucatán, Guatemala, and Chiapas

Chapter 34 described the long Spanish conquest of the Maya world. In eastern Yucatán in 1546, a coordinated rising among several Maya polities, including Cupul, challenged the new Spanish settlements, and the rebels attacked colonists and also Maya who had cooperated with them. The Spanish, led by Francisco de Montejo the Younger, defeated the rising with the help of Maya allies, among them leaders of Maní, the Xiu, whose political rivalries with other Maya lords shaped the alliances. The suppression was harsh, and afterward Spanish authorities consolidated their control over northern Yucatán, but Maya town government, known as the cah, and local leadership persisted and later adapted to colonial institutions.

Spanish authority was weaker in the southern lowlands and forested interior. Some Maya people moved into forest zones beyond the missions and colonial towns, and others lived in communities with only nominal Spanish presence. The Itza kingdom at Nojpetén, treated in section 47.11, remained independent until 1697. In the highlands of Guatemala and Chiapas, colonial authority was stronger, with Dominican and other missions, labor demands, and the tribute and trade systems of the colonial economy. Maya communities there used litigation, flight, and negotiation, and a few rose in revolt.

The best-known uprising is the rebellion of 1712 in the highlands of Chiapas, conventionally called the Tzeltal Revolt but involving Tzeltal-, Tzotzil-, and Ch'ol-speaking communities, and not only the Tzeltal. It had antecedents in earlier unrest, including episodes in the 1690s, and grew from grievances over increased tribute, forced purchases imposed by local officials, and demands made by the Church. In the town of Cancuc, a young woman reported visions of the Virgin Mary and became the center of a new cult. Local leaders organized a parallel structure of church and civil authority, naming their own officers and priests, and declared that Spanish rule and Spanish priests were no longer legitimate. Colonial sources describe a rapid spread to dozens of communities, an attack toward Ciudad Real (present-day San Cristóbal de las Casas), and the killing of Spanish residents, priests, and Maya who refused to join.

Spanish reinforcements arrived from Guatemala and other provinces and suppressed the movement by late 1712 and 1713. Cancuc was taken, many people were executed, and punishment fell heavily on leaders and communities. The rebellion's religious dimension is important: the Virgin gave a Christian form to a movement that rejected Spanish clergy, showing that Indigenous communities adopted Christianity on their own terms. It was a multi-community rising, though not unanimous, and it reflected shared grievances more than a single ethnic identity. Historians distinguish between such short-lived revolts, which the Spanish could defeat with outside forces, and long-term autonomy, which required geography, demographic strength, and diplomacy of the kind that the Mapuche and the Itza possessed.

Section 47.8

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 and the Northern Frontier

New Mexico in 1680 was a province of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, not part of any later nation, and it stood at the far northern end of a long supply line from Mexico City. Spanish settlement, begun in 1598 and concentrated around Santa Fe, rested on tribute and labor obligations imposed on the Pueblo peoples, the Indigenous farming communities who lived in dozens of towns along the Rio Grande and in surrounding regions. The Pueblos spoke several distinct languages, including Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, and Keres, and the Zuni and Hopi spoke others. They had no common government and had a long history of local rivalries. Perhaps 2,000 to 2,500 colonists and dependents, a group that included many people of Indigenous, African, and mixed descent, lived among them, along with a small number of Franciscan friars.

Pressures accumulated through the 1670s. Franciscans campaigned against Pueblo ceremonial life, destroying ritual objects and punishing religious leaders. In 1675, colonial authorities arrested dozens of Pueblo religious leaders on charges of sorcery, hanged some, and flogged others, among them Po'pay of Ohkay Owingeh (San Juan Pueblo). Drought and famine weakened communities, epidemics reduced population, and Apache and Navajo raids put pressure on both the Pueblos and the colonists, and Spanish forces proved unable to protect settlements. Encomienda tribute and labor demands continued, and Spanish officials were unable or unwilling to remedy abuses.

Po'pay and other leaders spent the years after 1675 building a coalition. Spanish interrogations conducted in 1681 record that runners carried knotted cords to communities, which were used to count down days to a shared date for the rising. The date was moved forward when Spanish officials learned of the plot, and the revolt began on 10 August 1680. Pueblo fighters killed roughly 400 Spaniards, including 21 of the 33 Franciscans then in the province, and besieged Santa Fe, where Governor Antonio de Otermín and survivors eventually broke out and retreated south toward El Paso del Norte. Some Pueblo people, particularly Piro and Tiwa communities, accompanied them, and their descendants later founded settlements in the El Paso area, including Ysleta del Sur.

For twelve years the Pueblos governed themselves. Po'pay's authority was contested, communities pursued their own interests, and some of the unity of 1680 dissolved under pressure from drought and from conflict with Apaches and Navajos. A Spanish attempt to retake the province in 1681–82 failed. Spanish authorities returned under Diego de Vargas, who entered Santa Fe in 1692 with a small force and secured a largely negotiated submission, then met stronger resistance during the resettlement of 1693–94. A second revolt in 1696 killed several missionaries and settlers and was suppressed, though the Hopi remained outside Spanish control. The Spanish settlers who returned did not restore the earlier regime, since the encomienda was not revived, Franciscans allowed Pueblo ceremonial life more latitude, and Spanish recognition of Pueblo communal landholding developed through colonial legal practice in the following decades, with long consequences. The documentation of particular grants is disputed: the purported 1689 grants attributed to Governor Domingo Jironza Petriz de Cruzate are questioned, and several surviving versions have been identified as spurious, so the recognition should not be treated as a single act of reconquest. Pueblo men also served as auxiliaries against Apache and, later, Comanche raiders, and the colonial relationship became more reciprocal and more dependent on negotiation.

Evidence for the revolt includes Spanish letters and official testimony, notably the 1680–82 interrogations, along with Pueblo oral histories, archaeological studies of Pueblo and Spanish sites, and Pueblo memory carried through generations. Po'pay has become a central figure in Pueblo and New Mexican memory, and a statue of him was placed in the United States Capitol in 2005. Historians caution that the extent of his personal control and the details of his religious claims cannot be reconstructed with confidence from Spanish accounts.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 Occurred and What Changed Afterward

Long-term causes

Encomienda tribute and labor obligations since 1598, Franciscan suppression of Pueblo ceremonial life, decades of epidemic mortality, and the distance of New Mexico from Spanish support.

Short-term causes

The 1675 arrests and punishment of Pueblo religious leaders, drought and famine in the 1670s, escalating Apache and Navajo raids, and Spanish failures to protect or relieve the Pueblos.

Event or transformation

A coalition of Pueblo communities, organized across language boundaries and coordinated by runners, rose on 10 August 1680 and drove the colonists south.

Immediate effects

Roughly 400 Spaniards killed, the siege of Santa Fe, the flight of survivors to El Paso del Norte, destruction of mission churches, and twelve years of Pueblo self-rule.

Long-term consequences

A Spanish return from 1692 on altered terms: no revived encomienda, more latitude for Pueblo ceremonies, evolving Spanish recognition of Pueblo communal lands (the documentation of specific grants is disputed), and Pueblo military service as auxiliaries.

Historical complexity

Unity was temporary and incomplete; the Hopi stayed outside Spanish rule; and the Spanish return still reasserted colonial authority. The revolt was a success in 1680 and an incomplete one by 1700.
Section 47.9

Andean Resistance and the Movement of Juan Santos Atahualpa

Andean communities pursued resistance through litigation, flight, and negotiation throughout the colonial period, and episodically through revolt. The labor and tribute system created by the viceroy Francisco de Toledo in the 1570s, including the resettlement of Indigenous communities into planned towns, reducciones, and the mit'a for Potosí and Huancavelica (Chapter 42), generated generations of conflict. Curacas defended community interests in court and sometimes led collective protests. Local risings occurred periodically, and mestizo and Indigenous crowds rose together in 1730 in Cochabamba against new fiscal demands. The most consequential Andean uprisings, those associated with Túpac Amaru II and Túpac Katari in the 1780s, belong to Chapter 49 and are not treated here.

Juan Santos Atahualpa's movement, beginning in 1742, arose in a different setting. Its center was the central forest region of Peru, the eastern slopes and lowlands east of Tarma, including the Chanchamayo valley and the Gran Pajonal. Franciscan missions had operated there for decades, along with a few Spanish and mestizo settlements engaged in trade and agriculture. The peoples of the region, the Asháninka (called Campa in colonial sources), Yanesha (Amuesha), and others, had complex relations with the missions: some sought iron tools and trade goods, and others resented the demands, the labor, and the epidemics that followed contact. Juan Santos appeared in 1742 claiming descent from the last Inca, Atahualpa, and the title of Apu Inca. Colonial sources describe him as educated, as having spent time in the highlands and perhaps beyond Peru, and as speaking Quechua and Spanish, though details of his early life are uncertain.

His coalition drew chiefly on forest peoples, with some support from Quechua-speaking migrants and others. He called for the expulsion of the Spanish and the restoration of Inca rule, and, according to colonial reports, spoke of marching to Lima. The sources are not consistent about his religious claims: some report that he accepted aspects of Christianity while rejecting Spanish priests, and others emphasize his appeals to Inca restoration. He drove the Franciscans from the region in 1742 and resisted Spanish military expeditions through the 1740s, which suffered setbacks in the difficult terrain. The last major rebel actions occurred around 1752. His fate is unknown, since he disappeared from the record, and the Spanish did not reconquer the area. The central forest remained largely closed to colonial control for roughly a century.

The movement shows how Inca symbolism could organize Indigenous politics in the eighteenth century, as Chapter 36 showed for the earlier Neo-Inca state, and it also shows how limited Spanish power was in the eastern forest. It was a movement of forest peoples with their own grievances, notably against the missions, and it should not be understood simply as another highland protest against tribute. Its leader's claims should be treated cautiously, since nearly all our descriptions come from Spanish officials and missionaries who were frightened of him, and the Indigenous political calculations that sustained the movement are mostly invisible.

Section 47.10

Mapuche Autonomy, Warfare, and Diplomacy

Chapter 38 described Spanish expansion into Chile and Mapuche resistance. In the century that followed, the Mapuche, whose communities were organized in local kin-based groups under leaders called lonkos, with war leaders (toquis) chosen for campaigns, built a system of regional alliance that no Spanish governor could defeat. The Mapuche victory at Curalaba in 1598, in which the governor was killed, led to the destruction or abandonment of the Spanish towns south of the Biobío River. The Mapuche adopted horses and Spanish weapons, developed new tactics, and built fortifications. They took captives and captured livestock, and they raided Spanish settlements while retaining the ability to disperse when pressed. Spanish response was a permanent army on the frontier, financed by silver from Peru in a subsidy known as the situado, and a policy of enslaving captured Mapuche that the crown authorized in 1608 and ended formally in 1674.

Spanish policy shifted among strategies. The "defensive war" promoted by the Jesuit Luis de Valdivia from 1612 sought to limit offensive campaigns and rely on missionary work and negotiation, but it was abandoned by 1626 after criticism from Spanish soldiers and settlers who wanted slaves and land. The Mapuche, meanwhile, formed alliances among communities and adapted to the diplomacy of the frontier. One of the most important institutions that resulted was the parlamento, a formal meeting between Spanish officials and Mapuche leaders, shaped by Mapuche assembly traditions, in which both sides negotiated trade, captives, missionary access, and boundaries. Assemblies between Spanish officials and Mapuche leaders are documented from 1593, including at Quilacoya, Rere, Taruchina, and La Imperial, and dozens were held into the eighteenth century; they became the principal means by which the Spanish crown managed relations with the Mapuche.

The parley held in January 1641 at Quilín, in the Araucanía, convened by Governor Francisco López de Zúñiga, marqués de Baides, is among the best known, and a landmark in the consolidation of this practice. Spanish writers, notably the Jesuit Alonso de Ovalle and later Diego de Rosales, described it as a major diplomatic event, and subsequent writers and Mapuche organizations have treated it as a foundational moment. What was actually agreed is contested. Rosales, whose account long served as the standard narrative, records six provisions, including Mapuche acceptance of missionaries. The earliest known testimony records only five and has no missionary provision, and Francis Goicovich, who published it in 2022, finds no hint of an idea or offer of independence for the Indigenous world in the conditions set out by the lonkos. He therefore cautions against reading Quilín as a recognition of Mapuche sovereignty. Spanish officials read agreements as acts of vassalage, and Mapuche participants may have understood them as agreements among parties with their own authority. Modern scholars warn against reading Quilín either as a complete Spanish victory or as an unambiguous treaty between sovereign states, and the Primary Source Window below shows why.

Quilín did not end the war. A great Mapuche rising in 1655 destroyed much of Spanish settlement on the frontier, and fighting, raids, and parleys continued through the eighteenth century, alongside trade at frontier markets and the missionary activity of the Jesuits. The Mapuche were never defeated in the colonial period. They shaped Spanish policy by war and diplomacy, preserved their land and institutions south of the Biobío, and adapted to a mixed economy of agriculture, livestock, and trade. Mapuche women, as weavers, farmers, and ritual specialists, sustained the economy that made this autonomy possible. Chile's Mapuche remained independent until the military campaigns of the late nineteenth century, a history Volume III will take up.

Section 47.11

Autonomy Beyond the Colonial Frontier: Amazonia, the Chaco, and the Maya Lowlands

Large parts of the continent lay outside effective colonial control for the entire colonial period. Chapter 23 described the Indigenous societies of Amazonia, and the Spanish and Portuguese presence there remained thin and contested. In 1599, the Shuar (called Jívaro in colonial sources) rose against the Spanish town of Logroño de los Caballeros in the eastern Andean foothills, killed the governor, and ended Spanish settlement in the area. Jesuit missions in the upper Amazon, the Maynas missions begun in 1638, operated amid epidemics and the slaving raids of Portuguese expeditions and local intermediaries, and Indigenous communities used flight and negotiation to survive. In the lower Amazon and the Rio Negro, Portuguese forces and missionaries pursued a mixture of enslavement and settlement. In the 1720s, Ajuricaba, a leader of the Manao people, resisted Portuguese expansion and slaving on the Rio Negro and was captured and sent toward Belém, dying on the way.

The Gran Chaco, the lowland plain of present-day Paraguay, Argentina, and Bolivia, presents another pattern. Peoples such as the Abipón, Mocoví, and Mbayá-Guaycurú adopted the horse and developed mounted warfare and raiding that threatened Spanish settlements on the borders of Paraguay, Tucumán, and Santa Fe. Spanish expeditions into the Chaco repeatedly failed, and Jesuit missions in the eighteenth century reached only some communities. The Chaco was not brought under national control until military campaigns in the late nineteenth century. Spanish settlers on the periphery paid for this independence with fortifications and constant insecurity.

The Maya lowlands of Petén provide the clearest case of an organized Indigenous state surviving into the late colonial period. The Itza capital, Nojpetén, stood on an island in Lake Petén Itzá and was ruled by a king, Kan Ek'. Spaniards had passed nearby since Cortés crossed the region in 1525, and Franciscan friars visited in 1618 and 1619 and were received with a mixture of courtesy and suspicion. The Itza continued to trade and negotiate with colonial neighbors and to shelter people fleeing Spanish rule. In the 1690s, the Spaniards of Guatemala and Yucatán, anxious to link the two provinces by road, resumed attempts to subdue the region, and on 13 March 1697 Martín de Urzúa y Arizmendi attacked Nojpetén. The Itza capital fell. The conquest was a Spanish military achievement against a state that had maintained its independence for 175 years after Cortés. Scholars including Grant Jones have also noted that Maya calendrical expectations shaped Itza decisions during the negotiations that preceded the attack, though the extent of that influence is debated.

Why did some societies keep their independence? Geography mattered: dense forest, swamp, and distance made campaigns costly, and Spanish and Portuguese authorities, who depended on limited revenue, rarely judged the expense worthwhile unless gold or silver was at stake. Indigenous societies also adapted. They acquired horses, iron tools, and sometimes firearms, kept their political structures, and negotiated or fought as circumstances required. Missionaries and traders crossed frontiers and served as intermediaries, and Indigenous leaders learned to use the competing interests of Spanish, Portuguese, and other authorities. Autonomy did not mean isolation: these societies were integrated into long-distance trade, and they were often changed by epidemics and exchange, even where they remained politically independent.

Section 47.12

Maroonage and the Geography of Freedom

Maroon communities appeared wherever slavery did. The Spanish word cimarrón, originally applied to domestic animals gone wild, was applied to escaped Indigenous and African captives from the early sixteenth century on. Spanish America used palenque (literally a palisade or stockade) for fugitive settlements, and Venezuelans also used cumbe. Portuguese America used mocambo and quilombo, words drawn from Central African languages. The different words reflect different regions and legal contexts, and they should not be treated as interchangeable. Maroonage began in the first decades of Caribbean slavery: in Hispaniola, the Taíno leader Enriquillo led a long guerrilla resistance in the mountains from about 1519 and negotiated a settlement with the Spanish in 1533, and African maroons were already present on the island by then.

Maroonage should be distinguished from manumission, treated in Chapter 44, which was a legal release granted by an owner or purchased by the enslaved person. Maroonage was an act of seizing freedom, and it could lead to several different outcomes: the quiet absorption of fugitives into free populations, the founding of a settlement that fought colonial forces, a negotiated recognition of the settlement, or destruction. These outcomes did not follow one another in a fixed order. Some communities were recognized, then attacked; others were attacked for decades without ever being subdued; many were small and survived only because they were overlooked.

Geography shaped where maroon communities formed. Fugitives chose mountains, swamps, dense forest, and frontier lands between colonial jurisdictions, places where pursuit was hard and where Spanish or Portuguese settlement was thin. They secured food by farming small plots, hunting, fishing, and raiding. They kept links with the plantations they had left, through kin who remained enslaved, through trade with free people and townspeople, and through information networks. Maroons traded forest products and crops for tools, cloth, salt, and weapons. They organized defense by building stockades, ditches, and hidden entrances, and by sending out scouts. Many communities also depended on alliances with Indigenous neighbors.

Authorities responded with expeditions, bounties, and a system of local slave-catchers, but also, as later sections show, with negotiation. Maroon communities rarely if ever sought to overturn slavery as an institution; their aim was to establish freedom for themselves and their dependents, and some returned captured fugitives as a condition of recognition. Their existence was a standing demonstration that colonial control had limits. The Caribbean and Atlantic tradition of marronage, including in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, forms part of the background to the revolution discussed in Chapter 50.

Historical Vocabulary
Cimarrón, Palenque, and Maroonage

A cimarrón was an escaped enslaved person, or, in the early sixteenth century, an escaped Indigenous captive; the word's root meaning is "wild" or "untamed." A palenque was a fugitive settlement, from the Spanish word for stockade. Maroonage (or marronage) is the English and French term for the process of escape and the creation of fugitive communities; maroon derives from the same Spanish root.

The terms were applied by slaveholding authorities, and they carried judgments. People in these communities used their own names for themselves and their settlements, and the colonial vocabulary should be treated as evidence of colonial attitudes as much as a neutral description.

Section 47.13

Palenques in New Granada, New Spain, and the Caribbean

Near Cartagena de Indias, a major port of the slave trade, fugitive communities grew in the hills of the Montes de María. Their best-known leader, Benkos Biohó (also Domingo Bioho), is said in the sources to have come from the Bissagos Islands region of West Africa, to have escaped from his owner around 1599, and to have established a palenque and a network of settlements. Spanish forces attacked repeatedly, and the maroons repelled them. According to traditional accounts, in about 1605 the governor of Cartagena recognized the freedom of the palenque in an agreement that required Biohó to stop admitting new fugitives. Later, Biohó was captured and executed in 1619, which shows that recognition did not end hostility, and that accommodation could be reversed by new governors or new fears.

Palenques persisted in the region after his death. The community now known as San Basilio de Palenque emerged in the same hinterland, and the history of its foundation is complicated. Tradition makes Biohó its founder, but documents record continuing conflicts with Spanish authorities through the seventeenth century, followed by negotiations. Pardons and recognition are associated with the late seventeenth century, particularly 1691, and with further recognition in 1713. The community's people developed Palenquero, a Spanish-based creole language with African influences, and funerary and kin traditions that continue today. Women of the community sold produce in Cartagena's markets, a link between palenque and city. The history of San Basilio therefore illustrates the need to separate the earliest maroon resistance from later formal recognition, and not to assign a single uncontested foundation date to a long process.

In New Spain, Gaspar Yanga, identified in Spanish records as an African of the Bran people, led maroons in the highlands near Orizaba and Veracruz, who attacked travelers on the road between Veracruz and Mexico City. In 1609, the viceroy sent an expedition under Pedro González de Herrera, and the Spanish sources describe skirmishes, then negotiation. According to the Spanish record, Yanga's terms included freedom for his community, a town with its own government, and a priest, and the Spanish demanded that the community return later fugitives and pay tribute. Agreements were reached around 1618, and the town of San Lorenzo de los Negros was formally founded in 1630. It later became the town of Yanga. The bargain gave the community legal standing and security at the cost of becoming part of the colonial order's policing of slavery.

Other cases show the range. In Esmeraldas, on the coast of present-day Ecuador, Africans who survived a shipwreck in the 1550s allied with local Indigenous peoples and established a polity led by Alonso de Illescas and later by his descendants. In 1599, the Quito painter Andrés Sánchez Gallque, an Indigenous artist, produced a portrait of three leaders of the community, Francisco de Arobe and his sons, dressed in a combination of Spanish and Indigenous finery, sent to the king as evidence of the community's negotiated submission. In Panama, a maroon leader named Bayano led a large community in the 1550s before being defeated and sent to Spain. In Venezuela, a leader called King Miguel led a rising around the Buría mines in 1552. Cuba and other Caribbean islands also saw palenques, and authorities relied on slave-catchers and local militias to suppress them.

These cases show that negotiation was a recurring outcome, although never a safe one. Colonial officials negotiated when military campaigns were expensive and unsuccessful, when roads or trade needed to be secured, and when recognition offered a way to turn fugitives into allies. Maroon leaders negotiated to secure freedom for their dependents. The conditions colonial officials imposed, including the return of new fugitives, reveal the compromises involved.

Section 47.14

Quilombos and Autonomous Black Communities in Portuguese Brazil

In Portuguese America, fugitive communities appeared across the colony, from the sugar coast of Pernambuco and Bahia to the gold districts of Minas Gerais, the interior of Mato Grosso, and the Amazon. The words mocambo and quilombo came from Central African languages, and the Portuguese applied them to settlements of fugitives. In 1740, Portugal's Overseas Council defined a quilombo, in effect, as a settlement of more than five fugitives in an uninhabited place, even without built dwellings, a definition that shows how broad the colonial category was. Many such communities were small, short-lived, and tied closely to the plantations or towns around them.

Quilombos varied. Some were tiny hideouts occupied for a season. Others were villages with farms, houses, and hundreds of residents. Subsistence farming was usual: maize, manioc, beans, and other crops. Many traded with nearby free people, tavern keepers, and enslaved workers. Some raided for supplies, and others produced crafts and goods for exchange. Their populations varied in composition, with people of African birth, Brazil-born people of African descent, Indigenous people, and in some cases people of mixed ancestry or poor free people. Family life was central to many settlements, and leadership might rest on military skill, spiritual authority, or the ability to negotiate with outsiders.

Several cases beyond Palmares are well documented. In Minas Gerais, the Quilombo do Ambrósio, one of the largest of the region, was attacked and destroyed in 1746 by forces organized by colonial authorities. In Mato Grosso, the Quariterê quilombo, led by a woman named Tereza de Benguela, held out in the eighteenth century until colonial forces destroyed it in the 1770s. Her leadership is one of the best-documented cases of a woman leading a maroon community, and later movements have honored her memory. In the Amazon region, communities of fugitives, later known by names such as the Trombetas mocambos, developed networks of trade with Indigenous peoples and local traders. Each community had its own relationships with its neighbors, and Portuguese expeditions, led by local militia, bandeirantes, or soldiers, attacked those that threatened settlement or trade.

Historians caution against portraying every quilombo as equally large, independent, or isolated. Many depended on markets and on connections to people who remained enslaved or free in the surrounding society. Some were tolerated by local landowners who benefited from trade. Some were destroyed within months. Evidence comes mainly from colonial reports, legal records, and increasingly from archaeology, and it tells us more about the colonial reaction than about the daily life inside the communities. Quilombos nevertheless made fugitives' freedom a durable feature of the colonial landscape, and some descendants of such communities remain in Brazil today.

Historical Vocabulary
Quilombo and Mocambo

In colonial Brazil, mocambo and quilombo were Portuguese uses of words from Central African languages, applied to settlements of people who had escaped slavery. Mocambo was more common in the early period and in the northeast and Amazon, and quilombo became the standard term in the eighteenth century, when the Overseas Council gave it a legal definition. Capitão-do-mato, "bush captain," was the term for a slave-catcher.

Some scholars connect quilombo to Central African institutions of military and political organization, though the connection is debated. The modern Brazilian word quilombola now refers to residents of communities recognized as descended from such settlements, a legal category created in the late twentieth century that is beyond this chapter.

Section 47.15

Palmares: Ganga Zumba, Zumbi, and the Defense of Freedom

Palmares, in the hills of the Serra da Barriga in what is now Alagoas, then part of the captaincy of Pernambuco, was the largest and longest-lived maroon network in colonial Brazil. It took shape in the early seventeenth century, as people escaped the sugar plantations of the coast, and expanded during the disruption of Dutch rule in Pernambuco between 1630 and 1654. Dutch expeditions in 1644 and 1645 described large settlements, including what they called Old and New Palmares, and reported populations in the thousands. Later Portuguese writers gave far larger figures, sometimes in the tens of thousands, which historians treat as exaggerated. Palmares is better understood as a network of settlements than as a single town, with residents numbering in the thousands at its height.

The community's economy depended on farming maize, beans, manioc, sweet potatoes, and bananas, along with hunting and gathering palm products, from which the name Palmares comes. Its people also traded with neighboring settlers and exchanged goods, and they raided plantations for tools, weapons, and people. Its residents included Africans of different origins, Brazil-born people of African descent, and Indigenous people. Political organization had Central African features: sources describe a king, Ganga Zumba, a title suggesting "great lord," and subordinate chiefs who governed particular settlements. The principal royal seat, Macaco, was fortified with palisades. Military organization relied on stockades, lookouts, and a capacity for mobilization. Evidence suggests that the community also maintained hierarchy and, in some accounts, enslavement of captives, which complicates any idealized image.

Portuguese authorities attacked Palmares repeatedly through the seventeenth century, with limited success. In 1678, Governor Pedro de Almeida negotiated with Ganga Zumba, who accepted an agreement offering freedom and land at a place called Cucaú to those born in Palmares, in exchange for submission to the crown, while those who had recently escaped were to be returned. Ganga Zumba moved to Cucaú with followers. The agreement divided the community. Zumbi, associated in later tradition as Ganga Zumba's nephew and as a war leader, rejected the terms and continued the struggle. The tradition that Ganga Zumba was later poisoned by opponents around 1680 is reported but cannot be verified. The disagreement shows that Palmares was not unanimous and that negotiation and war were competing strategies under military pressure, with uncertain prospects for any negotiated freedom, as the fate of other agreements showed.

The crown eventually turned to the Paulistas, frontier fighters from São Paulo known as bandeirantes. Domingos Jorge Velho, contracted in the late 1680s, led a large force with artillery reinforcements from Pernambuco against Macaco, which fell in early February 1694 after a siege. Portuguese forces hunted the survivors, and Zumbi was killed on 20 November 1695, reportedly after betrayal, and his head was displayed in Recife as a warning. Many residents were killed, sold into slavery, or dispersed. Palmares had lasted for most of a century against repeated attacks.

Reconstructing Palmares means working from hostile sources. The principal documents are Portuguese and Dutch military reports, an anonymous account of the wars of the 1670s, letters of governors, and later histories such as that of Sebastião da Rocha Pita (1730). No source from within the community survives. Archaeological excavations at the Serra da Barriga since the 1990s have identified settlement remains and material evidence of trade and local production, and they have added information that the texts omit. Zumbi has become an important symbol in modern Brazil, where 20 November is observed as a day of Black consciousness and was made a national holiday by federal law in 2023. Historians distinguish the documented Zumbi from the memory that has grown around him, and they caution against treating Ganga Zumba and Zumbi as simple opposites.

Section 47.16

Freedom Claims, Gender, and Alliances Across Colonial Society

Women took part in every form of resistance discussed in this chapter, though the sources record them unevenly. They appeared as litigants and petitioners, as cacicas who held authority in their own right, as market sellers who connected maroon communities to towns, and as religious specialists. In the Chiapas rising of 1712, a young woman's reported visions of the Virgin gave the movement its center. Tereza de Benguela led the Quariterê quilombo in Mato Grosso. Mapuche machi played ritual roles in communities that sustained war and diplomacy. Later tradition attaches women's names, such as Dandara, to Palmares, but the historical evidence for them is thin and late, and students should distinguish documented figures from legendary ones.

Gender shaped the possibilities of flight and settlement. Maroon communities often had more men than women, particularly in their early years, and raids sometimes included the capture of women. Historians debate how far such captures were coerced, and how women in maroon communities experienced them. Women also bore the work of farming and food preparation that made autonomy possible, and household labor sustained both Indigenous villages and fugitive settlements. In petitions, women spoke as wives, mothers, and widows, adopting roles colonial law recognized, and they sometimes won cases that men might have lost. Resistance communities could reproduce hierarchies of gender and status, and their internal conflicts were real.

Resistance also created alliances across colonial boundaries. Maroon communities in Brazil and Spanish America traded and sometimes allied with Indigenous neighbors. Juan Santos Atahualpa's movement joined forest peoples with Quechua-speaking migrants. Pueblo communities that spoke different languages coordinated a rising. In Esmeraldas, Africans and Indigenous people formed a single polity. Mixed communities of Indigenous, African, and Spanish descent took part in urban riots such as the Mexico City grain riot of 1692. Colonial authorities worried about such alliances, and sometimes they invented them: an alleged conspiracy of Black men and women in Mexico City in 1612 led to executions on evidence extracted under pressure, which historians treat cautiously.

Alliances were never automatic. Indigenous communities sometimes raided maroons, and maroons sometimes took Indigenous captives. Free people of color served in militias that hunted fugitives, and Indigenous auxiliaries served on every side. Interpreters and intermediaries, Indigenous, African, and mixed, could be indispensable to negotiation and also suspected by both sides. The pattern shows that resistance was shaped by relationships and local interests, not by a unified opposition of colonized people to colonizers. The chapter focuses on collective agency, leaving the classification systems of Chapter 45 aside, and notes only that categories mattered when they shaped who could petition, who could be enslaved, and who could bear arms.

Section 47.17

Repression, Negotiation, Recognition, and Colonial Adaptation

Colonial authorities had a repertoire of responses. Military suppression was the most visible, and it often involved collective punishment: the burning of villages and crops, the enslavement of captives, as in Chile until 1674 and in the Tepehuán war, and the public execution of leaders, as in Chiapas in 1712 and Palmares in 1695. Deportation and forced relocation separated rebels from their communities. Colonial forces used divide-and-rule tactics, recruiting Indigenous auxiliaries, as in the Mixtón War, free Black militias, and contracted frontier fighters such as the bandeirantes. The brutality was meant to deter, and it did succeed in ending many movements.

Authorities also negotiated, and the reasons were practical. Wars on distant frontiers were expensive, and officials wanted them ended. Some campaigns failed repeatedly, as in Chile, the central forest of Peru, and the Chaco. Officials wanted peace for trade, for the safety of roads, or to turn former enemies into auxiliaries. Parleys in Chile, the agreement with Yanga, and the 1678 offer to Ganga Zumba were negotiated settlements of this kind. Amnesties and pardons were offered after rebellions to encourage surrender, sometimes followed by punishment of leaders. Many agreements were unequal or temporary. They could be reversed by a new governor, ignored by settlers, or abandoned when the military balance shifted, as the history of Biohó's palenque and Palmares show.

Resistance changed colonial practice. After 1680, Spanish authorities in New Mexico reconsidered the encomienda and moderated their campaign against Pueblo religion. Frontier policy in Chile came to rest on parleys and a permanent army. Rebellions led to investigations of local abuses, as after Chiapas and the earlier uprisings against repartimientos de mercancías, and to reforms of tribute collection, though reforms were often partial. Some communities obtained recognition: evolving legal recognition of Pueblo communal lands, the founding of San Lorenzo de los Negros, and the privileges of Tlaxcalan colonists in the north. Colonial government adapted by making room for these negotiated exceptions while maintaining the larger structure.

The result was a pattern of recurring conflict, repression, and adjustment. Colonial rule survived, and it was repeatedly reshaped by the people it governed. Authorities learned that some forms of control were too costly, and subjects learned which forms of protest the system would tolerate. Historians see this negotiation as essential to the stability of Spanish and Portuguese rule over three centuries, and they also see its limits, since concessions rarely ended the underlying inequalities of land, labor, and legal status.

Section 47.18

Resistance and Survival by 1800: Achievements, Limits, and Historical Legacies

Comparing outcomes shows how much the circumstances mattered. The Pueblo Revolt succeeded in expelling the colonists and in forcing a changed regime on their return. Mapuche resistance achieved the most lasting result, preserving territory and political institutions for most of the colonial period through a combination of war and parleys. The Itza kingdom lasted until 1697, and Juan Santos's movement closed a forest region for a century. Maroon communities achieved recognition in a few cases, but many were destroyed, and Palmares fell after most of a century of defense. Rebellions such as the Mixtón War, the Tepehuán uprising, and the Chiapas rising of 1712 were suppressed, with heavy loss of life. Litigation protected land and rights for many communities, though unevenly, and everyday negotiation and cultural persistence shaped what colonial rule meant in practice.

The limits were equally clear. Colonial coercion remained powerful, backed by silver, militias, and a legal system that legitimated slavery and forced labor. Most resistance failed to overturn the system it challenged, and communities that won concessions often paid with obligations or divisions. Colonialism was not weak: it endured for three centuries and transformed the continent. Its reach was nonetheless negotiated, and its operation was shaped by the daily decisions of the people it ruled. To say that is not to suggest that independence was inevitable. Most of the movements in this chapter had local aims, and in the early nineteenth century many of the communities involved took royalist or neutral positions as well as insurgent ones.

The chapter prepares the ground for what follows. The Bourbon and Pombaline reforms of Chapter 48 increased fiscal and administrative pressure on communities that had learned to negotiate with the older system, and they provoked resistance in turn. The great rebellions of Túpac Amaru II, Micaela Bastidas, Túpac Katari, and Bartolina Sisa, treated in Chapter 49, drew on Andean traditions of litigation, community defense, and Inca symbolism that this chapter has traced. The Haitian Revolution of Chapter 50 grew from an Atlantic tradition of marronage and from a slave society with exceptional demographic and political pressures. Together these developments ended Volume II's social history of colonial rule and opened Volume III's account of its collapse.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: What Counts as Resistance?

The question: Should historians reserve the term resistance for armed uprisings and autonomous communities, or should they extend it to litigation, labor disputes, negotiation, and cultural persistence?

Interpretation A — Resistance as organized, political opposition: Scholars who emphasize uprisings and autonomous communities argue that these actions posed direct challenges to colonial authority, produced distinct political programs, and left clear evidence. They caution that a very broad definition makes the term meaningless, since nearly any act of survival could be called resistance.
Interpretation B — Resistance as everyday and adaptive: Others, building on work such as James C. Scott's studies of everyday forms of peasant resistance and Steve J. Stern's work on adaptive resistance among Andean communities, argue that litigation, evasion, negotiation, and the defense of community institutions limited colonial power and shaped colonial society, often more than rare uprisings did. They stress that apparently accommodating acts, such as legal petitions, could challenge colonial authority in significant ways.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same records. Court files, tribute records, and military reports document petitions, evasion, and revolt, but they rarely reveal motives. The evidence for armed rebellion is more visible, because authorities recorded it, while evidence for everyday acts survives unevenly and requires inference about intent.

Why it matters: The definition shapes whose actions count in the historical record. A narrow definition emphasizes leaders and revolts and risks suggesting that most people were passive. A very broad definition risks reading intentions into customs and habits. Most historians now combine approaches, treating resistance as dependent on circumstances and evidence: not every act of cultural continuity was an intentional protest, while an apparently modest legal petition might significantly challenge colonial authority.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Colonial Domination and Community Autonomy, c. 1500–1800

What Changed

  • Early revolts against conquest, such as the Mixtón War, gave way to more institutionalized relationships, including parleys, negotiated recognitions, and litigation
  • Autonomous Indigenous states, such as the Itza kingdom, disappeared, while maroon communities appeared and became recognized in some cases
  • Colonial authorities adapted policies, ending encomienda in New Mexico, shifting to parleys in Chile, and recognizing selected communities
  • Religious language changed, from a return to older gods to the Christian forms used in 1712 and Inca restoration claims in 1742

What Stayed the Same

  • Tribute, labor demands, and local official abuses remained constant sources of conflict
  • Flight, litigation, and negotiation remained available throughout
  • Colonial authorities continued to describe resisters as criminals and idolaters
  • The underlying inequalities of land, labor, and legal status persisted

Who experienced change most directly: Communities that rebelled and were defeated, such as the Tepehuanes and the people of Palmares, faced the most direct consequences. Greater continuity: Villages that litigated and negotiated across generations experienced more continuity, often protecting land and institutions at the cost of obligations. Why the pattern matters: It shows that colonial rule was both durable and negotiated, and that the people it governed shaped its operation without overturning it, a balance that the reforms and rebellions of the late eighteenth century would disturb.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that colonial authority in Latin America was never absolute. Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, free people of African descent, and mixed communities challenged, negotiated, evaded, and sometimes defeated colonial power between roughly 1500 and 1800. Their strategies ranged from litigation and quiet evasion to flight, armed rebellion, and the creation of autonomous communities, and the chapter has insisted that resistance and rebellion are not synonyms.

Division I established the foundation. Colonial authority was layered and dependent on cooperation, and resistance covered a spectrum from accommodation and negotiation to flight and revolt. Indigenous communities used royal courts, hiring agents, producing land titles, and invoking royal justice, though cases were costly and enforcement uncertain. Tribute and labor demands generated daily bargaining, and local abuses, especially forced trade, could provoke collective violence, as in Tehuantepec in 1660 and Mexico City in 1692. Flight took many forms, from temporary absence to permanent escape, and it depended on geography and social networks.

Cultural persistence, the final topic of Division I, was the most widespread form of survival. Languages, kinship networks, communal landholding, village government, and written and oral traditions continued, shaped by colonial institutions. The chapter has cautioned that such continuity should be called resistance only when evidence shows defiance of colonial demands, since habit and adaptation were also at work.

Division II turned to armed uprisings and autonomous frontiers. The Mixtón War and the Tepehuán uprising arose from local pressures on the northern frontier and were suppressed with Indigenous auxiliaries and heavy loss of life. Maya communities rose in Yucatán in 1546 and in Chiapas in 1712, the latter a multi-community movement that combined political and religious claims, and neither should be seen as representative of all Maya or all Indigenous societies.

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 was a coordinated coalition of communities speaking different languages, organized over several years and carried out on 10 August 1680. It expelled the Spanish from New Mexico for twelve years. When the Spanish returned from 1692, they did not restore the earlier regime: the encomienda was not revived, Pueblo ceremonial life was treated more tolerantly, and Spanish recognition of Pueblo communal lands developed over time, although the documentation of particular grants, including the purported 1689 grants, is disputed. Juan Santos Atahualpa's movement, beginning in 1742, drew on forest peoples and Inca symbolism and left the central forest of Peru closed to Spanish control for roughly a century.

Mapuche communities preserved their territory through warfare and the parlamento system. Quilín in 1641 was a landmark among dozens of parleys, and its terms remain disputed, since both Spanish officials and Mapuche leaders may have understood it differently. The 2022 publication of an early testimony shows how sources can complicate the established account. Other societies, including the Itza of Petén, Amazonian peoples, and peoples of the Chaco, kept their independence because of geography, distance, adaptation, and the limits of colonial resources.

Division III examined maroon communities. Maroonage was an act of seizing freedom, distinct from manumission, and could lead to quiet absorption, settlement, negotiated recognition, or destruction. Benkos Biohó's palenque and San Basilio de Palenque near Cartagena, Yanga's San Lorenzo de los Negros in New Spain, and the polity of Esmeraldas show that negotiation recurred but was never safe. In Brazil, quilombos varied from small hideouts to large villages, and most depended on ties to surrounding society.

Palmares was the largest and longest-lived maroon network in colonial Brazil, a network of settlements that survived most of a century. Its leadership disagreed in 1678 over Governor Pedro de Almeida's offer, and Zumbi's continuing resistance contrasted with Ganga Zumba's acceptance. The Paulista commander Domingos Jorge Velho destroyed the principal settlement in 1694, and Zumbi was killed in 1695. All the surviving sources are hostile or external, and Palmares has become a powerful symbol in modern Brazil.

Division IV considered participants and responses. Women appeared as litigants, leaders, market traders, and ritual specialists, and resistance created alliances across colonial categories while also reproducing hierarchies and conflicts. Authorities combined repression, including collective punishment and enslavement of captives, with negotiation, amnesty, and recognition when campaigns proved costly. Resistance changed colonial practice, leading to parleys, investigations, and negotiated exceptions, while colonial rule survived.

The chapter's conclusion is that colonial rule rested on unequal power, but its actual reach and consequences were continuously shaped by the people who lived under it. This should not be mistaken for the claim that colonialism was weak or that independence was inevitable. The movements studied here had local aims, and their significance is best understood in their own contexts and as the background to the reforms and rebellions that follow in Volume III.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Negotiated Settlements: Yanga, Biohó, Ganga Zumba, and the Mapuche Parleys

This project asks you to compare how colonial authorities and resisting communities negotiated settlements, and to evaluate what each case can and cannot show.

  1. Select two cases. Choose two from: the agreement with Yanga's community, Biohó's palenque, the 1678 offer to Ganga Zumba, or a Mapuche parlamento.
  2. Locate the sources. Using translated documents, scholarly editions, and library databases, identify the main sources for each case and who produced them.
  3. Summarize the terms. Record what each side offered and demanded, and what obligations were imposed on the community.
  4. Compare. Identify similarities and differences in motives, conditions, and outcomes, and note how long each settlement lasted.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what can be reconstructed with confidence, what remains uncertain, whose voices are missing, and what additional evidence would help.

Library resources to use: Translated collections of colonial documents, scholarly studies of maroonage and the Mapuche frontier, journal articles such as Goicovich's 2022 study of the Quilín testimony, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on colonial Latin America.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that colonial rule was a relationship, not only an imposition. Crowns and their officials held great power, but they depended on the labor, knowledge, and partial cooperation of the people they ruled, and those people used every opening available to them. The range of their strategies, from lawsuits and flight to parleys, stockades, and coordinated uprisings, shows why the history of colonial Latin America cannot be told only from the viewpoint of governors and conquerors.

The chapter also shows how unevenly the results were distributed. Some communities preserved land, language, and institutions for centuries, and a few won recognition or independence from colonial control for long periods. Others were destroyed. The difference lay in geography, military balance, leadership, and the interests of neighbors and authorities, and none of it was predetermined. Learning to read hostile sources critically, and to ask whose voices are missing, is a skill that matters throughout the rest of the textbook.

These patterns carry forward. Community litigation, Inca symbolism, maroon traditions, and frontier diplomacy shaped how people experienced the reforms of the eighteenth century and the wars of independence. Mapuche autonomy persisted into the nineteenth century, and the memory of Po'pay, Zumbi, and Palenque communities remains active in modern politics and culture. Afro-descended and Indigenous communities did not disappear from Latin American history after the colonial period, and Volume III will follow them through independence, abolition, and the making of nations.

Chapter 48 turns to the Bourbon and Pombaline reforms, which sought to tighten imperial control over communities long accustomed to negotiation, and Chapter 49 to the rebellions that followed. Students should carry forward the view that the late colonial crisis grew from an unstable balance between domination and negotiation that this chapter has traced, and that Volume II ends not with a settled colonial order but with one whose limits had been tested for three centuries.
