Gran Colombia and the Fragmentation of the Andes

Latin American History: Indigenous Civilizations, Colonization, Independence, and Modern Nations

Chapter Opening: A Republic That Outlived Its Constitution by Months

In January 1830, Simón Bolívar, the man whose name was attached to the Republic of Colombia, told its Congress that he wished to leave office. By the end of that year Venezuela and Ecuador had declared themselves separate states, the general who had won the battle of Pichincha had been murdered on a mountain road, and Bolívar was dead. The republic that had stretched from the Orinoco delta to the Gulf of Guayaquil, and from the Caribbean coast to the Amazonian frontier, was in the process of becoming three states with no agreed rules for dividing its debts, its borders, or its memory.

Historians call that republic Gran Colombia, a label coined later to distinguish it from the modern Republic of Colombia, which descends from its New Granadan core. Its contemporaries simply called it Colombia. It existed as a legal entity from the Fundamental Law of December 1819, was given a constitution at Cúcuta in 1821, absorbed Quito and Guayaquil in 1822, and fragmented in 1829 to 1831. Within those dozen years it fought wars, borrowed in London, hosted a diplomatic congress at Panama, passed a free-womb law, and governed perhaps two and a half to three million people across some of the most difficult terrain in the Americas.

This chapter has two central questions. Why did a union created by a victorious revolutionary leadership fail to become a durable state? And why did the republics that emerged from its wreckage, together with Peru and Bolivia, follow such different political paths? The answers cannot be reduced to Bolívar's ambition, Santander's legalism, or Páez's regional pride. They lie in distances and fiscal burdens, in competing definitions of sovereignty, in the military patronage created by war, in commercial interests that pulled coasts and highlands in different directions, and in rival ideas about what a republic was for.

An essential distinction organizes the chapter. Peru and Bolivia were never constituent parts of Gran Colombia. They belong here because Bolívar, Sucre, and Santa Cruz moved between their politics and Colombia's, because the Colombian-Peruvian war of 1828 and 1829 was a direct consequence of unfinished independence, and because their rival constitutional experiments, culminating in the Peru-Bolivian Confederation of 1836 to 1839, were part of the same Andean struggle over how large a state could be and who should govern it. Chapter 53 told how the armies of Bolívar won independence in the north, and Chapter 55 told how the Andean war ended; this chapter studies the political order that victory produced and could not hold.

The chapter also insists on social history. Constitutional texts declared citizens equal before the law, yet enslaved people in the Cauca valley and on the Caracas coast, Indigenous tribute-payers in the Ecuadorian sierra and the Bolivian altiplano, women denied the vote, and Afro-descended soldiers like Admiral José Prudencio Padilla experienced the republic very differently from the lawyers who drafted its charters. Rural people supplied recruits and taxes, filed petitions, defended communal lands, and sometimes rose in arms. Their choices, not only those of presidents and generals, shaped how far republican institutions could reach.

The evidence is rich but partisan. Constitutions, congressional journals, and proclamations survive in published collections; Bolívar's voluminous correspondence has been edited many times; memoirs by participants such as José Manuel Restrepo, Daniel O'Leary, and José Antonio Páez are valuable and self-serving. Contemporary newspapers reflect the factions that sponsored them. Nationalist historiography in each successor state later reread the dissolution to serve its own founding story. Students should treat every account of 1826 to 1830 as an argument. This chapter follows the outline's boundaries: it begins with the Colombian union in 1819, studies five republics to roughly 1850, and leaves the War of the Pacific to Chapter 65 and Panama's 1903 separation to Chapter 70.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the strategic and political reasons for uniting Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito, and distinguish revolutionary aspiration from effective sovereignty.
  2. Describe the constitutional framework of 1819 and 1821 and distinguish the centralist republic of Cúcuta from the federalism that later became a rival demand.
  3. Analyze how distance, public finance, foreign debt, and administrative geography strained the union between 1821 and 1826.
  4. Trace the sequence from La Cosiata through the Convention of Ocaña to Bolívar's dictatorship, and assess these as connected developments.
  5. Evaluate the war with Peru and the Battle of Tarqui as part of the militarization of Andean politics.
  6. Explain why the end of Gran Colombia was a process stretching from 1829 to 1832 rather than a single proclamation.
  7. Compare the constitutional and political histories of Venezuela, New Granada, and Ecuador through about 1850.
  8. Compare Peru and Bolivia, and interpret the Peru-Bolivian Confederation as a rival Andean union.
  9. Analyze how slavery, Indigenous tribute, women's legal status, and racial hierarchy shaped the unequal experience of republican citizenship.
  10. Interpret competing historical explanations for the failure of Gran Colombia and evaluate the evidence for each.
  11. Distinguish constitutional declarations from separations and from effective control on the ground.
  12. Connect the chapter to Part XIII's argument that independence, republican form, union, and state-building were separate achievements.

Historical Context

Chapter 48 explained how Bourbon reforms strained the Spanish Atlantic empire, Chapters 51 through 55 traced the collapse of imperial legitimacy and the wars that followed, and Chapter 58 introduced the institutional problems facing new republics: sovereignty, citizenship, regionalism, the Church, the army, and fiscal weakness. This chapter applies those general problems to the Andean and Caribbean-facing regions of northern South America. Before 1810 these territories were administered separately: the Captaincy General of Venezuela, the Viceroyalty of New Granada with its capital at Bogotá, and the Audiencia of Quito, itself attached to New Granada after 1739. Each had its own commerce, elites, and Indigenous and African-descended populations.

Independence itself was fought region by region. Venezuelan juntas of 1810 and the first republics of 1811 to 1814 collapsed amid war, earthquake, and social conflict. New Granada's provinces formed rival juntas, and the quarrel between centralist Cundinamarca and federalist provinces in 1812 to 1816, remembered as the patria boba, allowed Spanish reconquest. Quito's 1809 junta was suppressed, and Pasto stayed fiercely royalist. By the time Bolívar proposed union in 1819, the territory had experienced a decade of requisition, conscription, and massacre. Venezuela had lost a considerable share of its population, and military leaders held authority that civilians had not been able to establish.

Colonial institutions remained in place, often modified only in name. Indigenous tribute, the alcabala sales tax, the tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, the tithe, ecclesiastical and military fueros, and slavery all survived into the republic. Cabildos, which had provided local self-government, were reshaped into municipalities. Law books remained largely Castilian. The new state needed revenue from a society impoverished by war and still organized around mining, haciendas, plantations, cattle ranching, small farming, and Indigenous village agriculture. The Atlantic economy, now open to British and North American traders, offered opportunity and competition.

Peru and Upper Peru were different cases. Lima had been the viceregal capital and royalist stronghold; its independence came through the intervention of San Martín, then Bolívar and Sucre, and the final victories at Junín and Ayacucho in 1824 (Chapter 55). Bolivia was created in 1825 from Upper Peru. These countries had larger Indigenous majorities, heavier dependence on tribute and silver, and strong local identities in Lima, Cusco, Arequipa, Chuquisaca, La Paz, and Potosí. Chapter 59 showed Mexico's parallel struggle between federalism and centralism; students should keep it in mind as a comparison, since this chapter does not retell Mexican history. For Brazil and the Southern Cone, see Chapters 61 and 62.

Section 60.1

The Vision of an Andean Republic

Bolívar did not always believe that Venezuela and New Granada belonged in a single state. In the Jamaica Letter of 1815 he had imagined the former Spanish empire dividing into several republics shaped by geography and custom, and he doubted that two populations so different in history and temperament could be governed together. What changed his mind was the war. By mid-1819 the patriot position in Venezuela rested on the Orinoco basin, the Guayana missions, and the cavalry of the plains, a base that could hold territory but could not by itself defeat the royalist army in the coastal cordillera. The crossing of the Andes from the Casanare plains to the highlands of New Granada, and the victory at Boyacá on 7 August 1819, opened a different resource base. Bogotá fell to the patriots within days, the viceroy fled, and the patriots suddenly controlled the principal administrative city of the old viceroyalty, though not Caracas, Quito, or the Caribbean ports.

The case for union was therefore strategic before it was constitutional. Venezuela offered veteran officers, horsemen, and the political prestige of the first republic; New Granada offered a larger population, a more defensible interior, provisions, and the possibility of taxes and recruits on a scale the Orinoco could never supply. A government that could move men and credit across both regions could threaten the Spanish position in Quito and Peru. Bolívar's Angostura address of February 1819 had already argued that republican states needed strong executives and stable institutions, and by the end of the year he and his advisers, among them Francisco Antonio Zea, a Medellín-born naturalist and diplomat who presided over the Angostura Congress, treated size as a form of security. A large republic might also negotiate on better terms with Britain and the United States, and the name Colombia, which Francisco de Miranda had popularized as a label for all of Spanish America, gave the project a continental resonance.

Yet aspiration and sovereignty were different things, and the distance between them is the central problem of this division. When the Fundamental Law united the two republics on 17 December 1819, Spanish forces still held Caracas and most of the Venezuelan coast, Quito, Pasto, and Cartagena. Bogotá was in patriot hands, but its allegiance was provisional, and New Granada's political class had spent a decade governing itself through provincial juntas and rival congresses. The centralist-federalist quarrel of the patria boba (1812–1816), when Cundinamarca and the United Provinces fought each other while Spanish reconquest approached, was still a living memory. Many New Granadans accepted the union because Bolívar's army had liberated them; their acceptance was an act of necessity as much as consent, and an assembly of notables in Bogotá ratified it only in 1820.

Historians read the origins of Colombia in two ways. The older nationalist tradition, associated with accounts by participants such as José Manuel Restrepo, whose Historia de la revolución de la República de Colombia appeared in 1827, treated union as the natural outcome of a common struggle. More recent work stresses that the republic was declared by an assembly of one region on behalf of three, that its legitimacy rested on military success, and that it left unresolved who held sovereignty. The evidence for these questions lies in the acts of the Angostura Congress, Bolívar's correspondence, and the later protests of provincial leaders, all of which were written by participants with something to prove. The chapter therefore treats the union as a political project whose viability depended on institutions yet to be built.

Section 60.2

Angostura, the Fundamental Law of 1819, and the Birth of Colombia

The Congress of Angostura, which met in the Orinoco river town now called Ciudad Bolívar, opened on 15 February 1819 with delegates from the Venezuelan provinces then under patriot control, not from the whole of Venezuela. In his opening address Bolívar urged a strong and stable executive, an independent judiciary, and a hereditary senate drawn from the military heroes of the revolution, arguing that a republic needed institutions that could resist demagogy. He proposed a fourth branch, the Moral Power, to supervise education and public morals on an Areopagite model. The Congress adopted a Venezuelan constitution on 15 August 1819, but it did not adopt the hereditary senate, and the Moral Power was never put into operation.

After the victory at Boyacá, Bolívar returned to Angostura and persuaded the Congress to proclaim the union. The Fundamental Law of the Republic of Colombia of 17 December 1819 declared in Article 1 that Venezuela and New Granada were joined in a single republic from that day, and Article 5 divided the republic into three great departments: Venezuela, Quito, and Cundinamarca, the last comprising the provinces of New Granada, whose name was suppressed. Their capitals were to be Caracas, Quito, and Bogotá, with the old addition "Santa Fe" dropped. Each department was to have a vice-president, and the republic a president and a vice-president named provisionally by the Congress. Bolívar became president, Zea vice-president of the republic, and Santander, who had organized the New Granadan provinces behind the army, vice-president of Cundinamarca. Article 8 summoned a General Congress to meet at Villa del Rosario de Cúcuta on 1 January 1821, and Article 9 charged it with writing a constitution.

The law was a provisional settlement, but it embodied choices. Making the republic a single sovereign unit with three departments rather than a federation of three states meant that no region would retain its own government. Suppressing the name New Granada and renaming the Spanish viceregal capital simply Bogotá signalled a break with the colonial past, and also a break with a New Granadan identity that many provincial elites valued. The omission of detail about finance, military command, and the relationship between departments and the national executive left these matters to be settled by practice. Bolívar's critics later noted that no New Granadan had voted for the law, while its defenders replied that provisional unity was the only way to continue the war.

The roles of Bolívar and Santander were already distinct. Bolívar remained the commander whose campaigns would complete the liberation of Quito and Peru, and he treated the law as a wartime union under his leadership. Santander, a lawyer-soldier from the Cúcuta region who had risen as Bolívar's lieutenant in the Boyacá campaign, administered the liberated provinces of New Granada from Bogotá and expected the union to become a constitutional republic governed by law. Both men needed the other, and the later breakdown of their partnership cannot be understood without recognizing that each had a coherent conception of what the Fundamental Law was for.

Primary Source Window

The Fundamental Law of the Republic of Colombia, 17 December 1819

Issuing body: Sovereign Congress of Venezuela, Angostura; promulgated by Simón Bolívar the same day. Editions consulted: the text as published in the Colombian Función Pública Gestor Normativo (Ley 1 de 1819) and in the Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes; an early printing appears in Restrepo, Historia de la revolución de la República de Colombia (documentary appendices).

Context: The Congress that issued the law was the second Venezuelan Congress, convened by Bolívar at Angostura in 1819, and its delegates represented only the Venezuelan provinces in patriot hands. The law was issued four months after the victory at Boyacá, while Caracas and Quito were still royalist.

What the text provides (paraphrase; article numbers given only where checked against the published text): Article 1 reunites Venezuela and New Granada into a single republic under the title Republic of Colombia. Article 5 divides it into three departments, Venezuela, Quito, and Cundinamarca, with Caracas, Quito, and Bogotá as capitals and the name New Granada suppressed. Each department is to have its own vice-president. Article 8 summons a general congress to Villa del Rosario de Cúcuta on 1 January 1821, and Article 9 charges it with forming a constitution. The opening of Article 1 reads, in Spanish, that the republics of Venezuela and New Granada "quedan desde este día reunidas en una sola."

Later interpretation (distinct from the text): Nationalist historians treated the law as the birth certificate of a republic; critics, among them New Granadan contemporaries cited in modern scholarship such as Víctor Uribe-Urán's study of the Congress of Cúcuta, noted that New Granadans had not been represented at Angostura.

Limitations: The law is a framework, not a constitution, and says almost nothing about revenue, command, or provincial rights. It records what Venezuelan delegates and Bolívar decided, not what Quiteños or New Granadans thought. It does not show how the union worked in practice.

Verification status: The provisions above were checked against the published text of the law consulted in the Función Pública and Cervantes Virtual editions; the interpretive remarks come from secondary scholarship.

Section 60.3

From Boyacá and Carabobo to the Territorial Union

The campaigns that made union possible belong to Chapter 53, and this section concerns only their political consequences. After Boyacá, the patriots held Bogotá and the highlands of New Granada, and Santander used the autumn of 1819 to organize them: appointing governors, collecting contributions, and raising recruits. The armistice negotiated at Trujillo in November 1820 and the accompanying agreement to regularize the conduct of the war, which Bolívar and the Spanish commander Pablo Morillo concluded in person in the Venezuelan Andes, mattered politically because they treated the republic as a belligerent with whom a Spanish general would sign treaties. Spanish reluctance to continue the war after the liberal revolution of 1820 in Spain strengthened the patriots' hand.

Carabobo on 24 June 1821 destroyed the principal royalist army in Venezuela and opened Caracas to the patriots. In the weeks that followed, Cartagena, which had endured a long siege, capitulated in October 1821 to forces led by Admiral José Prudencio Padilla, an Afro-descended officer from the Guajira coast whose naval victories on Lake Maracaibo in 1823 would help finish the Venezuelan war. Maracaibo, Puerto Cabello, and the coast fell in succession, the last royalist garrison at Puerto Cabello surrendering to Páez in November 1823. The territory of Colombia was therefore assembled over four years, and for much of that time the republic's institutions were directed by officers in the field.

Military victory shaped union in two directions. It made the union practically possible by giving it an army, a common enemy, and a body of veterans whose careers depended on it. It also created a political logic that favored centralization: civilian leaders who might have objected to a strong executive found it difficult to argue against unity of command while royalist garrisons held the coast and Pasto. The Congress of Cúcuta, which finally assembled in May 1821, ratified the union in a Fundamental Law of the Union of the Peoples of Colombia on 12 July 1821 before it wrote the constitution, and its delegates came only from provinces freed by that date.

War also determined who held power. Venezuelan officers such as Páez, Mariño, Bermúdez, and Soublette had won rank in the field and expected to exercise authority in the provinces they had liberated. New Granadan leaders, above all Santander, had built the civil administration that fed and funded the army. The union therefore joined two kinds of legitimacy, one based on command and one on law, and the relations between them, rather than the union's legal form, would decide its fate. The soldiers' expectation of political reward, the civilians' insistence on legal procedure, and the republic's chronic shortage of money would reappear in every crisis of the 1820s.

Section 60.4

Quito, Guayaquil, and the Incorporation of the Southern District

The southern district was the last part of the republic to be assembled and the one with the weakest claim to have chosen union. After the war in the north, Bolívar turned to Quito, whose highlands remained under royalist control and whose approaches to Peru Colombia needed. Sucre's victory at Pichincha on 24 May 1822 opened Quito, and Bolívar entered the city on 16 June. The capitulation was followed by rapid petitions from the Quito elite to join Colombia, motivated by exhaustion after more than a decade of war and requisition, by hope of protection against Peruvian claims, and by the practical fact that Colombian troops now held the city. Pasto, the royalist stronghold to the north, had resisted Bolívar at Bomboná in April and capitulated in June, only to rise again in late 1822 and to be repressed with severity that left a lasting memory in the southwest.

Guayaquil presented a different problem. The port had declared independence on 9 October 1820 under a junta that included José Joaquín de Olmedo, a poet and lawyer, and had governed itself for nearly two years as an autonomous province. Its merchants, whose wealth rested on cacao exports, shipbuilding, and trade through the Pacific, wanted free commerce and local control over customs. Factions favored independence, union with Peru, whose liberator San Martín also had claims to the province, or union with Colombia. Bolívar arrived on 11 July 1822, and on 13 July the junta, under pressure from his troops and from Colombian-aligned factions, declared the province under Colombia's authority. His meeting with San Martín at Guayaquil on 26 and 27 July, whose content is not fully documented, was followed by San Martín's withdrawal from Peru, though historians disagree about what was said and what caused the withdrawal.

The incorporation of the south shows that Gran Colombia was assembled from regions with competing options. The highlands around Quito were densely populated, with an Indigenous majority who paid tribute and worked in the obrajes (textile workshops) and on haciendas, and elites who depended on Church institutions and on markets for cloth in Colombia and Peru. Guayaquil's merchants looked to the Pacific. Cuenca's elites felt marginal to both. The southern district was governed through a military intendant, first Sucre and later other officers, and Bogotá's officials lived at a distance of weeks from the people they governed. Later attempts to describe the south's secession in 1830 as a revolt against Bolívar neglect that these regions had never fully chosen the union.

Peruvian claims added a further complication. Peru regarded Guayaquil, Jaén, and Maynas as properly connected to Lima by colonial administration, and Colombia regarded them as part of the Audiencia of Quito, which had been attached to New Granada in 1739. The question was not resolved by Bolívar's presence at Guayaquil, and it would return in the war of 1828 and 1829. It also illustrates the problem of uti possidetis: colonial jurisdictions had overlapped, and the new republics inherited the ambiguities.

Section 60.5

Panama, the Caribbean, and the Geography of the Republic

Panama joined Colombia without a battle. The town of Los Santos declared independence from Spain on 10 November 1821, and Panama City followed on 28 November, deciding at the same time to unite with Colombia. The isthmus mattered because its overland routes between the Chagres River and Panama City connected the Atlantic and Pacific, and merchants and muleteers depended on the traffic that crossed it. Panama's decision to join Colombia reflected a calculation that no other option was viable, since Peru and Spain were distant and unreliable and an independent isthmus would have been weak. The decision did not eliminate local ambitions: Panamanian leaders would attempt separation in 1830 and again in 1840 and 1841, episodes that belong to New Granada's history in Division VI. The isthmus's later separation in 1903 is the subject of Chapter 70.

The geography of the republic imposed constraints that no constitution could erase. The Caribbean ports of Cartagena, Santa Marta, Maracaibo, Puerto Cabello, and La Guaira linked it to Atlantic trade and absorbed most of its commerce. The Magdalena River, the principal artery of New Granada, carried goods from the coast to Honda, where mule trains climbed the Andes to Bogotá; steam navigation, introduced under a privilege granted to Juan Bernardo Elbers in 1823, was an early attempt to improve this route. In Venezuela, the Orinoco and the llanos of Apure and Barinas formed an interior of cattle ranches and rivers, while the Maracaibo basin connected Mérida, Trujillo, and the Cúcuta region to the sea. Beyond the Andes, the Amazonian forests lay largely outside effective control.

Population is difficult to measure. Colonial censuses, adjusted for war losses, suggest that Gran Colombia's population in the 1820s was roughly two and a half to three million, with Venezuela somewhat under a million after the war, New Granada the largest share, and the southern district perhaps half a million or more. These figures are approximate and rest on estimates by contemporary officials. What matters is the pattern: most people lived in mountain valleys, river corridors, and a handful of cities, with large stretches between them thinly peopled, and with significant Indigenous and African-descended populations whose conditions differed by region.

Bogotá was chosen as capital because of its central location, its inherited institutions, and its wealth, but it was far from Caracas and farther from Quito, and communications by mule and river took weeks. Official correspondence between Bogotá and the southern district or Venezuela could take a month or more, and the republic's army, dispersed across the Andes and Peru, depended on supply lines that crossed several ecological zones. The practical meaning of this geography was that decisions made in Bogotá often reached their destinations after circumstances had changed, and that regional commanders had to act on their own judgment. This is one of the most important structural facts about the union, and it explains why constitutional centralization sat so uneasily with administrative reality.

Section 60.6

The Constitution of Cúcuta and Centralized Republican Government

The Congress that assembled at Villa del Rosario de Cúcuta in the spring of 1821 consisted of delegates from the provinces freed by that date, chosen through indirect elections that varied by region, and it included lawyers, clergy, landowners, and military men. It opened its sessions in May 1821, confirmed the union in July, and adopted the Constitution of the Republic of Colombia on 30 August 1821. The text, consisting of 191 articles in ten titles, was printed at Rosario de Cúcuta in 1821 by Bruno Espinosa and is reproduced in modern editions. It created a unitary republic, popular and representative, divided into legislative, executive, and judicial branches. A president and vice-president, elected through indirect elections, held executive power; a bicameral Congress of Senate and Chamber of Representatives legislated; and a High Court headed the judiciary. The republic was divided into departments, provinces, cantons, and parishes, and officials at each level above the parish were appointed from the center.

The outline of this volume refers to Bolívar's federation, but the constitution of 1821 was not federal. The president appointed intendants to govern departments and governors to govern provinces, and the legislature made laws binding throughout the republic. Federalism, the arrangement favored by many provincial leaders and by Santander's allies at Ocaña in 1828, was a competing demand, not the system in place. This distinction matters because many critics of the republic blamed Bogotá for decisions that the constitution had concentrated there, and because the later federalist argument depended on the claim that centralism had failed.

Indirect elections built local notables into the system. Citizens voted in parish assemblies, which chose electors, who chose representatives and senators and, at the apex, the president. Article 15 set the qualifications for voting in parish assemblies: voters had to be Colombians who were married or over twenty-one, with a requirement of literacy that was postponed until 1840, and the article also demanded a modest property or an occupation or income qualification. Higher offices required greater wealth. The effect was to confine formal political participation to a minority of free adult men who held property or a trade, and to give power to the landowners, merchants, lawyers, and clergy who dominated provincial assemblies. Women, the enslaved, domestic servants, and most Indigenous peasants were excluded in practice.

Two provisions had large consequences. Article 128 allowed the executive, in zones of military operations, to exercise extraordinary powers that suspended the ordinary operation of the constitution, a clause that was intended for wartime but whose limits were never sharply defined. Article 191 provided that the constitution could be reformed only through a general convention to be held after ten years' experience, a provision that gave the document a decade of protection and made any earlier revision legally contentious. Both provisions would be tested in 1826 to 1828: the first when Bolívar invoked emergency powers, the second when the Congress called the Convention of Ocaña before the decade had elapsed. The constitution therefore combined a strong executive, local electoral mediation, and rigid amendment, a combination that made compromise difficult.

Historical Vocabulary
Centralism and Federalism

Centralism is a form of government in which authority is concentrated in a national capital, with provincial officials appointed by and answerable to the central executive. Federalism divides sovereignty between a national government and constituent states or provinces, each with its own elected officials and powers.

In Spanish America after independence these were not abstract theories. Centralists argued that a weak, war-damaged state needed unity of command, uniform law, and a single treasury, while federalists argued that distant provinces knew their own needs and that the colonial administration had been too remote. Cúcuta's constitution was centralist, but federalism, often invoked by provincial elites resentful of Bogotá, became a rallying cry. Students should avoid equating federalism with liberalism or centralism with conservatism: both labels were used by competing factions in different countries, and their meanings shifted over time.

Section 60.7

Bolívar, Santander, and the Division of Executive Authority

From the first, executive authority in Colombia was divided in practice. Bolívar, elected president by the Congress of Cúcuta, spent almost all of the years from late 1821 on campaign: in Pasto and Quito in 1822, in Guayaquil and then in Peru from 1823, where he remained, with brief interruptions, until the autumn of 1826. Santander, elected vice-president, exercised the presidency from Bogotá, administering the treasury, appointing officials, negotiating with Congress, and answering to a president who corresponded from thousands of kilometers away. The Congress, in authorizing Bolívar's departure for Peru in 1823, granted him extraordinary powers that did not extend to domestic government, a distinction that would matter in later quarrels.

The arrangement worked for a time because both men needed the other. Bolívar needed Santander's administrative skill, his mastery of the budget and of provincial correspondence, and his ability to raise men and supplies from New Granada's provinces. Santander needed Bolívar's prestige, since the union held together in large part because Venezuelan officers and southern elites accepted it for his sake. Santander's own reputation as "the man of laws" reflected genuine priorities: public education, including the 1826 plan of studies, fiscal regularity, a civilian-led administration, and procedures that respected the constitution and the legislature's role.

The tension between them was partly personal and partly structural. Bolívar and his Venezuelan lieutenants regarded the Peruvian campaign as the completion of independence and complained that Bogotá supplied too little money and too few recruits. Santander and the New Granadan civilian elite worried that the republic was being drained to finance wars of liberation abroad, that New Granada bore the heaviest fiscal burden, and that the army's prestige would eventually override civil authority. Each man also depended on regional constituencies that distrusted the other: Venezuelan generals who resented a lawyer who controlled their salaries, and New Granadan legislators who feared the generals. The army officers' expectation of reward and the legislators' insistence on procedure were rival conceptions of what the republic owed to those who had created it.

By 1826 the personal rivalry had become a political fault line. Bolivarians, who stressed order, a strong executive, and the army's role, and Santanderistas, who stressed law, civilian government, and limits on executive power, competed for control of Congress, the press, and the officer corps. These were factions, not parties: they had no formal organization, shared members with overlapping interests, and changed as individuals shifted positions. But they hardened into rival camps over the course of the crisis, and each side came to see the other as a threat to the republic itself. When Bolívar returned from Peru in November 1826, the two men were no longer partners, and the structure that had allowed a divided executive to function was gone.

Section 60.8

Departments, Provinces, Municipalities, and Administrative Integration

The Law of Territorial Division of 25 June 1824 reorganized the republic into twelve departments: Orinoco (capital Cumaná), Venezuela (Caracas), Apure (Barinas), and Zulia (Maracaibo) in the Venezuelan region; Boyacá (Tunja), Cundinamarca (Bogotá), Cauca (Popayán), Magdalena (Cartagena), and Istmo (Panama) in New Granada; and Ecuador (Quito), Guayaquil (Guayaquil), and Azuay (Cuenca) in the south. Each department was headed by an intendant appointed by the executive, with provinces under governors, cantons under political chiefs, and parishes at the base. The purpose was to make the republic governable by replacing a patchwork of colonial jurisdictions with a uniform hierarchy answering to Bogotá. Reorganization also created new units that corresponded to emerging regional economies, notably the separate departments of Guayaquil and Azuay, which acknowledged the south's internal divisions.

The system tried to substitute appointed officials for the colonial cabildos' role in local government. City councils survived as municipalities, with narrower responsibilities, their members chosen through a restricted electoral process. Appointments rested on the executive and, in practice, on Santander's patronage, which Venezuelan and southern leaders sometimes viewed as favoring New Granadans. The administrative class was small, and many officials were lawyers or military officers shifted from other regions; there was no career civil service. The law's uniformity assumed a capacity for supervision that did not exist, and the tasks that fell to local officials, collecting taxes, recruiting soldiers, administering justice, and keeping the peace, varied from province to province according to local circumstances.

In Venezuela and the south, the formal chain of civil authority was frequently overridden by military command. Because those regions had been theaters of war, and because Article 128 permitted extraordinary powers in zones of military operations, military commanders often exercised civil authority as well. Páez, as commander in Venezuela, effectively controlled the department of Venezuela and the surrounding region; in the south, officers appointed by Bogotá or by Bolívar governed through a mixture of civil and military authority. The constitution gave Bogotá formal control, but effective control depended on negotiation with regional notables and officers, a pattern that shaped how La Cosiata unfolded in 1826.

The institutional result was a republic whose reach exceeded its practical capacity. Communications between Bogotá and distant intendants were slow, and the intendants' own staff were thin; instructions took weeks to arrive and might be modified by local commanders. The state collected revenue through customs houses and monopolies at ports and in cities more reliably than in the countryside. The law of 1824 also demonstrates what political union meant in practice: a uniform legal framework without uniform capacity. A provincial notable in the llanos or in highland Ecuador might encounter the republic principally in the person of a recruiting officer or a tax collector, and might judge the union by that experience.

Section 60.9

Public Finance, Foreign Loans, Diplomacy, and the Panama Congress

The republic's revenue came from customs duties, the tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, the alcabala sales tax, the tithe, and Indigenous tribute and its successors, all inherited from the colonial order. War had disrupted trade and many sources of income, and most of the revenue that remained went to the army and navy. To finance the republic, Colombian agents in London, working through banking houses, arranged two loans: one in 1822 with a nominal value of two million pounds, and one in 1824 with a nominal value of 4.75 million. Both were sold at substantial discounts and burdened by commissions, so that Colombia received far less in cash than the nominal sums, while owing interest on the full amount. When the London capital market collapsed in 1825 and 1826, Colombia, like other new Latin American borrowers, could not service the debt, and defaulted in 1826. The unpaid obligation, with accumulating arrears, would remain a source of dispute among the successor states into the 1830s and beyond.

Diplomatic recognition proceeded alongside borrowing. The United States recognized Colombia in 1822, and Britain, after the Canning ministry concluded that commerce with the new republics could not be ignored, did so in 1825 with a treaty of amity and commerce. Colombian envoys such as Zea, Manuel José Hurtado, and José Rafael Revenga sought recognition and credit in European capitals, often with little knowledge of local finance. Recognition brought commerce and the prospect of immigration and investment, but also obligations and dependency, particularly on British merchants and bankers whose goods and credit shaped Colombian trade.

Bolívar's call for a congress of the American republics at Panama came out of this diplomatic confidence. Invitations issued in 1824 produced a meeting that opened on 22 June 1826 with delegates from Colombia (Pedro Gual and Pedro Briceño Méndez), Central America, Mexico, and Peru, and with a British observer and a Dutch one. The United States named delegates, but one died en route and the other arrived too late. On 15 July the congress signed a treaty of union, league, and perpetual confederation providing for mutual defense and for arbitration of disputes, and a set of related conventions. Only Colombia ratified it. The congress adjourned to Tacubaya, in Mexico, and never meaningfully reconvened.

The Panama Congress shows both the union's ambition and its limits. It placed Colombia at the center of a proposed system of American republics, anticipating later arrangements for collective security and arbitration. It also met when La Cosiata was already unsettling Venezuela, when the republic could not meet its debt payments, and when its own political cohesion was fraying. The delegates' inability to bring their governments to ratify the treaty reflected the same factors that weakened the union itself: distance, mistrust, and the preoccupation of each government with domestic politics. The congress also shows that Colombia's diplomacy was not an object of foreign policy but an expression of Latin American agency, one that foreshadowed later efforts at regional cooperation.

Section 60.10

Citizenship, Slavery, Indigenous Communities, and Social Hierarchy

The Congress of Cúcuta passed a free-womb law on 21 July 1821. Children born to enslaved mothers after its promulgation would be free, but they owed labor to their mothers' enslavers until early adulthood, a period set at eighteen years, in return for their maintenance. Manumission juntas, funded partly by inheritance taxes, were to free adult enslaved people gradually, giving priority to those of long service and good conduct. The law reflected Bolívar's long-standing commitments, since he had promised abolition to the Haitian president Alexandre Pétion in exchange for aid in 1816, and the influence of elite fears of both continued slavery and sudden abolition, which they associated with Haiti. The republic's enslaved population, perhaps on the order of a hundred thousand, though estimates vary, was concentrated in the gold mines of the Cauca valley and Chocó and on the cacao and sugar estates of the Venezuelan coast and valleys.

In practice, the manumission juntas freed relatively few adults, and the labor obligation of freed-womb children kept many in dependence until the 1840s. Enslaved people did not wait for the state: they ran away, petitioned courts, purchased their freedom, formed maroon communities, and in the war years enlisted in the armies, where service promised freedom. Free people of color, known as pardos, had also served in large numbers, and the navy's most prominent commander, Padilla, rose from the Caribbean coast. Leaders like Bolívar rewarded service while fearing what contemporaries called pardocracia, the political rise of an Afro-descended majority, and the tension between reward and containment would appear again at the trial of Padilla in 1828.

Indigenous communities faced a different set of burdens. The colonial tribute, a per-capita payment, and the resguardos, communal lands protected by the Crown, were treated by many liberal legislators as relics of inequality. The Congress of Cúcuta abolished tribute in October 1821, ordered the division of communal lands among their holders, and expected the state to recover the revenue through other taxes, but the republic's fiscal needs and the continued burden of labor service soon reintroduced payments under new names, such as the personal contribution of Indigenous people, and the dictatorship of 1828 formally restored tribute. In the southern district, where Indigenous people were the majority, the continued demand for labor in the obrajes and on haciendas, and the heavy recruitment for Peru, made the republic's promises of equality hard to credit.

Citizenship was legally limited to free adult men who met property, occupation, and later literacy requirements. Women could not vote or hold office, but inherited Castilian law gave them rights to own and manage property, to inherit, and to appear in court, and many used these rights as widows, merchants, and petitioners. Formal equality before the law coexisted with a social hierarchy based on ancestry, wealth, occupation, and reputation, in which the distance between a Bogotá lawyer and a Cauca mine worker was far greater than any constitutional text acknowledged. This gap between constitutional citizenship and actual social equality is one of the threads that connects the five republics studied in this chapter.

Division III · 1826–1829Constitutional Crisis and Bolívar's Final Government
Section 60.11

The Structural Sources of Regional Opposition

Opposition to Bogotá grew from material grievances before it acquired a constitutional vocabulary. Venezuela had been fought over more bitterly and for longer than any other part of the republic, and it emerged from the war with damaged estates, depleted herds, and a smaller population. Its merchants and planters depended on Atlantic trade and resented customs rules, monopolies, and tax collection controlled from a capital that seemed remote and preoccupied with New Granadan concerns. Venezuelan officers, who had won the war on the plains and in the coastal cordillera, complained that lawyers in Bogotá controlled the treasury and the courts, and that the salaries and pensions the officers considered their due arrived late or not at all. Conscription for the campaigns in Peru fell heavily on rural men, and the landowners who had to supply recruits and provisions saw little compensation for the loss.

The southern departments had parallel complaints. Guayaquil's merchants favored free trade and disliked decisions about tariffs and customs revenue made in Bogotá. Quito's textile producers, their workshops threatened by imported cloth from Britain, wanted protection that Guayaquil's merchants opposed. Cuenca's elites felt marginal to both. The southern district contributed men and money to the Peruvian campaigns and endured the garrisoning of a large army, yet its elites saw few of the benefits of being part of a republic that was governed from a capital weeks away. The tithe, the alcabala, the tobacco and aguardiente monopolies, and the Indigenous contribution fell on rural producers across the republic, and provincial elites resented that the executive appointed intendants and governors who were often outsiders to the places they governed.

Fiscal burdens were also unevenly felt. The share of customs revenue spent on the army, and the obligation to service the London loans from 1826, fell more heavily on the ports and their hinterlands, and the collapse of the London market in 1825 and 1826 contributed to a commercial downturn in which merchants and landowners looked for someone to blame. Church matters added another source of conflict: legislation reducing convents and regulating clerical privileges alarmed some clergy, while Santander's educational reforms, including the teaching of Bentham, aroused clerical suspicion. None of these grievances implied a desire to dissolve the union. Many regional leaders hoped to renegotiate its terms.

These tensions were structural but did not make dissolution inevitable. Regional grievances can be accommodated through fiscal adjustment, bargaining, or federal arrangements, and many of the same grievances persisted in the successor states, where they were managed with varying success. What made them politically dangerous in Colombia was their combination with military authority and constitutional rigidity. Venezuelan generals like Páez possessed armed followers and local prestige, and the absence of Bolívar, the one figure acceptable to most factions, removed the arbiter who might have negotiated between regional interests. The constitution's ten-year rule against reform prevented adjustments, and the intensification of the Bolivarian-Santanderista rivalry converted ordinary disagreements into questions of loyalty.

Section 60.12

La Cosiata and the Venezuelan Challenge of 1826

La Cosiata, a Venezuelan colloquialism that can be rendered "the business" or "the thing," began with a dispute over military recruitment. Páez, the military commander in Venezuela, ordered the enrollment of men in the militia in the Caracas region, including members of prominent families who had expected exemption. The Caracas municipality, a body dominated by landowners and merchants, complained, and a petition reached the Chamber of Deputies in Bogotá, which accepted an accusation against Páez for arbitrary conduct. In April 1826 the Senate summoned him to appear in the capital, and the executive suspended him from his command. In constitutional form, the case was an example of the legislature holding a military commander accountable, and Santander's decision to pursue it reflected his commitment to civilian authority.

Páez refused to submit. In the last days of April and the first days of May 1826, the municipalities of Valencia and Caracas asked him to assume civil and military command, and he did. The movement quickly assumed a political character. Some supporters called for Venezuela to reorganize itself, by federal arrangements or a new constitution; others called for the return of Bolívar as dictator to settle the dispute; still others simply wanted to be rid of Santander. The movement's aims were ambiguous, and Páez himself spoke both of obedience to Bolívar and of Venezuelan rights. Municipalities elsewhere in Venezuela, and in parts of the south, issued their own declarations, some supporting Páez and some warning against disorder.

Because the sources are partisan, historians continue to debate La Cosiata's character. Páez's later Autobiografía, written in exile, presented the episode as a defense of Venezuelan liberty against an overbearing capital. Bolívar's critics saw it as evidence that he or his friends had encouraged a movement to justify dictatorship. Santander's critics saw an effort to assert civilian authority that provoked an unnecessary crisis. The surviving municipal acts show a vocabulary of public order and local rights, but they cannot show whether councils acted freely or under the pressure of Páez's forces and clients.

The political significance of the episode is clear. It showed that a regional military commander with popular support and the backing of municipal governments could defy the national government and be defended in terms of constitutional principle, and that the republic's institutions had no means of disciplining him without war. It also destroyed the compromise that allowed Bolívar's absence: with Venezuela in effective rebellion, the president had to return. Bolívar's decision to settle the matter through negotiation, leaving Páez in command, undermined Santander's authority and encouraged the view that force and personal prestige, not constitutional procedure, settled disputes. This is the sense in which La Cosiata and the later Convention of Ocaña are connected: both were attempts to settle a conflict over centralism and civilian authority, and the outcome of the first limited the options available in the second.

Historical Vocabulary
Caudillismo

A caudillo was a leader whose authority rested on personal loyalty, regional prestige, and the ability to command armed followers rather than primarily on formal office. Caudillismo names the political pattern in which such leaders dominated early republican politics.

Páez, with his llanero cavalry and networks of patronage, is a classic example, and Flores, Santa Cruz, and Gamarra shared aspects of the type. The term can mislead if it implies mere lawlessness: many caudillos used constitutions, elections, and legal forms, and they provided order and protection that weak states could not. The relationship was reciprocal, with followers expecting land, offices, protection, and a share of spoils in return for loyalty. Caudillismo was a product of war, weak institutions, and regional fragmentation, not of a particular national character, and the same men could appear as defenders of constitutions and as their violators.

Section 60.13

Bolívar's Return and the Failure of Political Reconciliation, 1826–1827

Bolívar left Lima in early September 1826, leaving the Peruvian government in the hands of a council and a Colombian division, and reached Bogotá in mid-November. In the capital he faced a divided government, a Congress in recess, and a Venezuela that had effectively suspended Bogotá's authority. His priority was to restore unity without civil war, and in December he set out for Venezuela by way of the Caribbean coast. He reached Puerto Cabello on the last day of 1826 and on 1 January 1827 issued an amnesty for those involved in the movement, confirmed Páez as Superior Chief of Venezuela, and promised that the questions raised by Venezuelan leaders would be considered through constitutional processes. The arrangement avoided bloodshed, but it left Páez's command intact and established that Venezuelan officers could defy Bogotá without penalty.

Bolívar's relations with Santander deteriorated sharply. Santander had been at the center of the effort to prosecute Páez, and he now saw the settlement as a repudiation of his authority and of the constitution. The two men disagreed about the constitutional future. Bolívar had written a constitution for Bolivia in 1826, with a lifetime president and a tricameral legislature, and some of his supporters in Guayaquil, Quito, and Venezuela proposed that Colombia adopt it. In Guayaquil, municipal assemblies in the second half of 1826 called for Bolívar to assume extraordinary powers. Santander and his allies, who feared a drift toward monarchy and regarded the Bolivian text as incompatible with republican equality, objected. Meanwhile, events in Peru showed the limits of Bolivarian authority: in January 1827 the Colombian troops in Lima mutinied, the lifetime constitution was repudiated, and the Colombian division left.

Bolívar remained in Venezuela through the middle of 1827, and in the course of that year he offered to resign the presidency; Congress refused. By September 1827 he was back in Bogotá, taking up the presidency again, and the republic was split into rival camps. The Congress, in sessions in 1827, called for a general convention to meet at Ocaña in March 1828 to reconsider the constitution, a decision that both Bolivarians and Santanderistas supported for different reasons. Bolivarians hoped to strengthen the executive; Santanderistas hoped to limit it and to protect civilian government.

The decision to hold a convention before the ten years specified in Article 191 had elapsed reflected the sense that the existing arrangement could not hold. It was constitutionally irregular, and both sides recognized that the legal basis was weak. It also began a process that neither could control. Each side prepared by mobilizing supporters in the provincial electoral colleges to choose delegates, and the elections for Ocaña became contests for control of the future constitution. By early 1828 Bolívar's early settlement with Páez and the earlier Santander prosecution had made the stakes personal, and the convention was approached less as a deliberative body than as a battlefield.

Section 60.14

The Convention of Ocaña and the Constitutional Breakdown of 1828

The Convention of Ocaña, a town in the northeast of New Granada, opened on 9 April 1828 with delegates from across the republic. The Santanderistas held a majority. They favored a constitution that limited the president's powers, strengthened Congress, and gave provinces greater autonomy; some, including the lawyer Vicente Azuero, advocated provisions that approached a federal arrangement, though the republic would remain formally unitary. The Bolivarian delegates, among them the Cartagena jurist José María del Castillo y Rada, argued for a stronger executive, longer terms, and the preservation of a single state under a leader capable of commanding the army. Bolívar himself, who stayed in Bucaramanga during the convention's sessions, made it clear that he would not accept a constitution that left the executive weak.

Debate focused on executive power, the structure of provincial government, and, implicitly, what to do about Bolívar. Santanderistas proposed term limits and legislative checks; Bolivarians warned that such measures would invite anarchy and Spanish reconquest. Neither side was willing to accept the other's central demands. By early June it was clear that the majority would adopt a text unacceptable to Bolívar. Around 10 and 11 June the Bolivarian delegates withdrew, leaving the convention without a quorum, and it dissolved without completing a constitution.

The breakdown was a constitutional crisis rather than merely a political one. The convention had been called under irregular authority, and its collapse removed the one mechanism that might have produced a negotiated settlement. In the following weeks assemblies in Bogotá and other cities, among them a meeting of notables in the capital on 13 June, petitioned Bolívar to assume supreme power. Whether these petitions were spontaneous, orchestrated, or both remains debated; evidence of organization by Bolívar's allies exists, but so does evidence of genuine alarm among property-holders and officials who feared that a Santanderista constitution would invite disorder.

Ocaña and La Cosiata must be read together. Both were attempts to resolve through legal procedure the same dispute over centralism, civilian supremacy, and military power. La Cosiata showed that the national government could not enforce its authority against a regional commander; Ocaña showed that the contending factions could not agree on a new constitutional settlement. The failure of both strengthened the case of those who argued that only a personal and extraordinary authority could hold the republic together, and it narrowed the space for moderate positions. The outcome illustrates how constitutional crises can generate authoritarian solutions not because anyone planned them but because the alternatives had been exhausted.

Section 60.15

Bolívar's Dictatorship, the September Conspiracy, and Political Repression

On 27 August 1828 Bolívar issued an Organic Decree assuming supreme power. It abolished the vice-presidency, concentrating authority in his hands and a council of ministers, and promised that a congress would be called to establish a constitution when circumstances permitted. Bolívar and his supporters justified the measure as necessary to prevent anarchy and civil war, and the decree itself stated, in Article 25, that the government would uphold and protect the Catholic religion as the religion of Colombians. The decree removed Santander from office and put the legislature under suspicion. During the following months Bolívar restored some colonial institutions, including the Indigenous tribute and certain convents, and restricted the teaching of Bentham in universities, in an effort to win the support of the Church and conservative opinion.

On the night of 25 September 1828 a group of conspirators, some of them young civilians and officers, attacked the presidential palace in Bogotá. Bolívar escaped, helped by Manuela Sáenz. Born in Quito in 1797, she had married an English physician, James Thorne, and had left him to become Bolívar's companion from 1822; she kept his papers and managed his household. According to many accounts she confronted the attackers and urged him to flee through a window, an act for which Bolívar is said to have called her the "Liberator of the Liberator," though the details vary between sources. The government blamed Santander's circle. Santander was arrested and sentenced to death, and the sentence was commuted to exile in 1829. Admiral Padilla, the Afro-Colombian hero of Cartagena and Lake Maracaibo, was tried and shot on 2 October 1828. Many historians doubt that the evidence justified his execution and see in it the convergence of political suspicion and racial hostility.

Contemporary interpretations differed sharply. Bolívar's defenders held that the dictatorship was a temporary emergency measure to save the republic and that the conspiracy proved the danger. His critics, among them Santanderistas and republicans in Venezuela and Quito, saw tyranny and the betrayal of the republican principles for which they had fought. The repression deepened polarization. Padilla's execution, in particular, raised questions about race and the treatment of Afro-descended officers, and Santander's exile removed the republic's leading civilian from public life. The dictatorship also deprived the regime of a procedure for peaceful change: as an extraordinary government answerable to no assembly, it had no means of reconciling the provinces except through the personal authority of Bolívar, whose health was failing.

Women's presence in this episode is a reminder that political life extended beyond formal institutions. Sáenz was excluded from office and the vote but exercised influence as a confidante, a political correspondent, and a figure of both admiration and hostility. After Bolívar's death she was expelled from Bogotá in 1834 and lived in exile until her death at Paita in Peru in 1856. Her case also shows how reputation, gender, and politics were intertwined: her enemies used her relationship with Bolívar to attack him, and she had no institutional recourse.

Section 60.16

War with Peru, Tarqui, and the Militarization of Andean Politics, 1828–1829

The war between Colombia and Peru grew from unfinished business of independence. Colombia and Peru disputed the status of Guayaquil, Jaén, and Maynas, which controlled access to the upper Amazon; Peru's government also resented the long Colombian military presence in Lima, which ended with the mutiny of January 1827, and the influence Bolívar had exercised in Bolivian affairs. La Mar, the president of Peru, had been born in Cuenca and regarded Guayaquil as properly Peruvian. In 1828 Peruvian forces under Gamarra entered Bolivia and forced Sucre's resignation (Section 60.41), and Colombia, regarding this as a hostile act that threatened Colombian influence and Sucre's security, prepared for war. Colombia's declaration came in the middle of 1828.

The fighting began at sea and at the port. Peruvian naval forces blockaded Guayaquil in November 1828, and after a siege the city surrendered to Peruvian forces on 19 January 1829. La Mar then advanced with an army into the southern highlands, occupying Loja and approaching Cuenca. Sucre, commanding the Colombian forces in the south with Flores as a principal subordinate, met him at Portete de Tarqui, near Cuenca, on 27 February 1829 and defeated him. The Convention of Girón, signed the next day, ended the immediate fighting and provided for the resolution of the dispute by negotiation. In June 1829 Gamarra, with the army, overthrew La Mar, who went into exile, and the Treaty of Guayaquil of September 1829 restored peace. Guayaquil returned to Colombian control, but the treaty left the frontier undefined, and the question of Jaén and Maynas remained open for more than a century.

The war illustrates the militarization of Andean politics. Both states were governed by officers, both used the army to settle domestic politics, and both used external conflict to legitimize authority. The Colombian army in the south, a mixture of Venezuelan and New Granadan units and local recruits, was poorly paid and often resentful, and its officers expected political roles. Venezuelan officers commanded garrisons in Quito and Guayaquil, and the presence of an army whose loyalty was to commanders rather than to the republic shaped the south's politics for the following years. Peruvian politics was no different: La Mar's defeat led directly to his removal.

Tarqui strengthened Colombia's military reputation and Sucre's prestige, but not the republic's political cohesion. The war cost money that Colombia did not have and increased the autonomy of the commanders in the south. Within a year Quito's elite and Flores would move toward separation, and Sucre, the victor, would be assassinated on his return from Bogotá. The war thus connected the unfinished business of independence with the dissolution of the union: the south's military establishment, led by Flores, would become the core of the new state of Ecuador, and Peru's political crisis would feed into the rise of Gamarra and the instability of the following decade.

Section 60.17

Venezuela's Separation from the Union

Venezuela's separation came from Páez and the leading figures of Caracas and Valencia, who concluded that Bolívar's dictatorship and Bogotá's policies threatened local interests. Alarm was heightened in 1829 by reports that Bolívar's ministers had discussed the possibility of a constitutional monarchy under a European prince, a project that Bolívar did not endorse but that, once known, alarmed republicans in Venezuela and elsewhere. Municipalities passed resolutions calling for a change. On 25 November 1829 an assembly of notables in Caracas declared that Venezuela would no longer obey the government at Bogotá, and Páez accepted the leadership of the movement. Venezuelan authorities also made it clear that Bolívar would not be welcome in the country.

Bolívar tried to respond through negotiation, sending commissioners and hoping that the Congress he had called for January 1830 would settle the question. Venezuelan leaders insisted that separation precede any discussion of union. A Constituent Congress opened at Valencia on 6 May 1830, with thirty-three of the forty-eight elected deputies present at the start. Its members were mostly property-owning lawyers, landowners, and military officers, and its sessions ran until October. They debated the form of the new state, the status of Páez, who served as provisional leader, and a question that was central to the whole episode: whether a people had a right to withdraw from a union they had joined. The Congress approved a constitution on 22 September 1830.

Venezuelan separation shows the role of regional elites and caudillos. Páez drew on a coalition of llaneros, landowners, merchants, and municipal officials who regarded Bogotá as an obstacle to their interests, and the movement presented separation as a defense of republican liberty and local self-government. Many who supported separation had fought under Bolívar, and its leaders acknowledged his role in independence while rejecting his government. Venezuelan separation therefore divided the revolutionary generation instead of pitting loyalists against rebels, and its leaders worked to present it as the restoration of rights that the union had violated. Bolívar's partisans in Venezuela, including some officers, were purged or exiled.

The new state also had to define its relationship to the republic it was leaving. The separation did not immediately end the debate over whether Venezuela had left or whether the union had dissolved, and negotiations over debt, borders, and the status of military units continued for years. Venezuelan separation also set a precedent for the south. When Quito's notables declared a separate southern state in May 1830, they could cite the example of Caracas and Valencia, and the disintegration of Colombia in 1830 had the character of a cascade in which each region's decision made the next easier.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and Secession

Sovereignty is supreme political authority within a territory. Secession is the withdrawal of a territory from an existing state. In the early republics, who held sovereignty was an unsettled question: the nation as a whole, the provinces, the municipalities, or the people acting through juntas.

Venezuelans and Ecuadorians did not describe their actions as rebellion but as the reassertion of sovereignty that had never been fully surrendered. Bogotá's defenders argued that the constitution bound the departments and that a part could not withdraw. The question was neither simple nor settled by law, and it was decided by force, bargaining, and the choices of regional elites. The term "secession" should be used carefully, since contemporaries often spoke of "separation" or the "reassumption of sovereignty," and the legal vocabulary of the time distinguished between a union of peoples and a single nation.

Section 60.18

Ecuador's Separation and the Southern Political Crisis

In the south, the movement toward separation began with the Quito act of 13 May 1830, in which an assembly of notables declared the departments of the south a separate state, at least until the union could be restored on acceptable terms. The leading figure was Juan José Flores, a Venezuelan-born officer who had risen through the independence wars, had served the royalists before changing sides, and had married into a prominent Quito family. As military commander of the south, he had both the force and the local connections to lead the movement. The act of May was followed by adhesion from Guayaquil and Cuenca, reached through negotiation and with the support of the army, and then by a constituent congress that opened at Riobamba in August and adopted a constitution on 11 September 1830, which was promulgated on 23 September. These four steps, a declaration, regional adhesions, a congress, and a constitution, need to be distinguished, because each took place at a different time and under different pressures.

The three principal regions of the south had different interests. Guayaquil's merchants wanted free trade and a share in decision-making, and they remembered that Guayaquil had been an autonomous province before Bolívar's arrival. Quito's highland landowners wanted protection for their textiles and stability for the estates and obrajes that depended on Indigenous labor. Cuenca's elites wanted autonomy from both. Flores balanced these interests by relying on Quito's support, military patronage, and alliances with prominent Guayaquil figures, notably Olmedo, who served as vice-president of the new state. The result was a compromise that did not resolve the underlying regional conflicts but allowed a new state to emerge.

The new state also sought to extend its territory northward. In late 1830, after the upheavals in Bogotá, Flores annexed Pasto, Popayán, and Buenaventura, territory belonging to the department of Cauca, with the argument that these regions had joined Ecuador voluntarily. This provoked conflict with New Granada, and the disputed region was returned after fighting and negotiation in 1832. Ecuador's independence thus involved both a break with Bogotá and an attempt to claim territory that Bogotá considered its own, an early example of the border disputes discussed in Section 60.21.

Bolívar's own position toward the southern separation was ambivalent. He had lamented the divisions among his lieutenants and urged unity, but he had also written of the south as a region whose attachment to the union was uncertain, and his health and political weakness limited what he could do. The south's separation also reflected the military position: the officers who had fought at Tarqui and held the garrisons of Quito, Cuenca, and Guayaquil formed the backbone of the new state, and their loyalty was to Flores. The independence of Ecuador was therefore the work of a small group of officers and elites, who spoke in the name of three regions and who would spend the following decades attempting to reconcile them.

Section 60.19

The Admirable Congress, Sucre's Assassination, and Bolívar's Death

The Admirable Congress, as it came to be called by its admirers, met in Bogotá from 20 January 1830 under the presidency of Sucre, to write a constitution and settle the union. On the opening day Bolívar presented a message offering to resign, and the Congress deferred its decision, urging him to remain. In the following months it worked on a constitution, which it adopted on 29 April 1830 and which entered into force on 5 May. The text declared the Catholic religion to be the religion of the Republic in Article 6, and Article 7 made it a duty of the government, in exercise of the patronage of the Colombian Church, to protect it and not to tolerate the public worship of any other. The constitution, however, never took effect in practice, because Venezuela and the south were already separating. On 4 May 1830 the Congress elected Joaquín Mosquera president and Domingo Caycedo vice-president, and Bolívar, no longer in office, left Bogotá on 8 May.

On 4 June 1830, Sucre was murdered in the woods of Berruecos, in the mountains between Pasto and Popayán, while traveling from Bogotá to Quito. His killers were identified in several ways, and their motives are debated. Suspicion fell on General José María Obando, a Cauca officer, and on others whose interests were threatened by Sucre's prestige or by the movement toward southern separation, but the investigation was unfinished and politicized. The accusation against Obando followed him through his later political career, in which he became an important figure in New Granadan politics, and historians have not reached agreement on who ordered the murder. Sucre's death removed the most respected military figure aside from Bolívar, a symbol of Andean unity and of a statesmanlike form of leadership, and left the south without a figure capable of mediating between Quito and Guayaquil.

Bolívar reached the Caribbean coast, ill with what was probably tuberculosis, and died on 17 December 1830 at the estate of San Pedro Alejandrino, near Santa Marta. In a proclamation issued a week earlier he asked his countrymen to maintain union and to avoid the conflicts that threatened to destroy the republic, and he forgave his enemies; his admirers treated it as a political testament and his critics saw a leader who had failed to practice what he preached. With his death, Gran Colombia lost the one figure around whom a union had been organized. The legacy of his political thought, which combined a commitment to republican liberty with a belief in strong executive authority, remained in dispute in every successor state.

The sequence of 1830 shows the importance of distinguishing among separations, constitutions, and effective control. The Congress adopted a constitution for a republic that had already ceased to exist in Venezuela and the south. Bolívar's resignation, Mosquera's election, Sucre's murder, and Bolívar's death all took place within a single year, and the republic's institutions could not absorb the shocks. The Congress's achievement lay in its attempt to preserve legal continuity; its failure lay in the fact that the regions whose consent was needed had already withdrawn it.

Section 60.20

The End of Colombia and the Creation of New Granada

After Mosquera took office, a military faction in Bogotá led by General Rafael Urdaneta, a Venezuelan and a committed Bolivarian, overthrew the government on 31 August 1830, with the declared aim of restoring Bolívar's authority and defending the unity of the republic. Mosquera resigned and Urdaneta governed for several months, but without Bolívar, who was dying, and with Venezuela and Ecuador already separated, he had no secure base. Provincial opposition grew in New Granada itself, and by April 1831 his regime collapsed. A settlement signed at Apulo on 28 April 1831 restored civilian government under Caycedo and opened the way for a new constitutional order in the territory that remained, the provinces of New Granada and Panama.

A convention met in Bogotá in late 1831. On 17 November it issued a fundamental law creating the State of New Granada from the provinces that remained, and it then drafted a constitution, dated 1 March 1832. Its structure and religious provisions are examined in the source window in Section 60.27. The new constitution established a unitary republic but gave provincial chambers some authority over local matters, a compromise reflecting the federalist demands of Ocaña. The convention chose Santander as president; he was in exile in Europe and returned in 1832, taking office in October and serving until April 1837. Obando and other generals had meanwhile commanded in the south, and the transition was neither smooth nor uncontested.

The dissolution was a process, not a single act. Venezuela separated in 1829 and 1830, the southern departments in 1830, and New Granada's institutions were reorganized in 1831 and 1832. Panama and the Cauca provinces were brought into the new state through negotiation and, in some cases, military pressure: Panama's leaders separated briefly in 1830 under the leadership of local commanders and rejoined in 1831, and the Cauca was recovered from Ecuador in 1832. Contemporaries did not use the term Gran Colombia to describe the republic they had lived in; the label came later, when the modern Colombian state needed a way to distinguish itself from its predecessor, and the name Colombia was not used for the nation again until 1863.

The transition to New Granada also involved a change in political culture. The new state's leaders, among them Santander, Caycedo, Márquez, and Mosquera, were largely civilians and lawyers, and they emphasized legality, fiscal order, and the subordination of military to civil authority, though they relied on generals such as Obando and Mosquera himself when rebellion threatened. The army, reduced in size, remained a political force. The experience of the dictatorship and of Urdaneta's coup had convinced many New Granadans that executive power needed limits, and the constitution of 1832 reflected that view. The result was a republic whose political class, deeply shaped by the failure of the union, would work to build institutions that could survive without a charismatic leader.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did Gran Colombia Dissolve?

The question: Was the dissolution principally a consequence of structural regional differences, political choices, constitutional design, or fiscal and military crises? Or do these factors overlap so closely that the question itself needs reframing?

Structural explanations: Some historians emphasize distance, regional economies, and separate colonial histories. Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito were joined by revolution but not by roads, markets, or institutions. On this view, no constitution could have held them together, and Bolívar's personal authority was a temporary substitute for the structures that did not exist. They point to the fact that the same regional problems persisted in the successor states.
Political and constitutional explanations: Others emphasize contingent choices: Santander's prosecution of Páez, Bolívar's absence in Peru, the early convention at Ocaña, the dictatorship, and the failure to accept a federal compromise. On this view, a union with more flexible institutions might have survived, and the dissolution resulted from political failures at specific moments. They point to the rapidity of the collapse after 1826 as evidence that it was not predetermined.

Evidence: The evidence includes constitutional texts, congressional debates, fiscal records, and the correspondence of the principal actors, all of which are partisan. Comparisons with the successor states, which also struggled with regionalism, support structural views, while the timing of the separations supports contingent ones. Fiscal evidence, including the London loans and customs records, points to a third set of factors that cut across both positions.

Why it matters: The question affects how Bolívar's project is judged: as visionary but premature, as flawed by his own choices, or as undone by forces beyond any individual. It also shapes national memory in each successor state and informs later debates about regional integration.

Section 60.21

Borders, National Debts, Diplomatic Recognition, and the Inheritance of Union

The end of the union left unresolved questions about debts, borders, and institutional assets. The foreign debt contracted in London in 1822 and 1824, on which Colombia had defaulted in 1826, was divided by a convention signed at Bogotá in December 1834: New Granada accepted 50 percent, Venezuela 28.5 percent, and Ecuador 21.5 percent. The division reflected population, revenue, and bargaining power, and each state subsequently struggled to service its share, with interest arrears and renegotiations recurring through the middle of the century. The debt also meant that each successor state entered European credit markets burdened by a record of default.

Borders were harder to settle. The successor states agreed in principle to use the colonial boundaries of 1810, the principle called uti possidetis, but colonial jurisdictions were vague, overlapping, and sometimes contradictory. Venezuela and New Granada disputed the Guajira peninsula and the eastern frontier; Ecuador and New Granada disputed the Cauca provinces of Popayán and Pasto until the settlement of 1832; and Ecuador and Peru disputed Jaén and Maynas, a conflict that was not resolved until the twentieth century. Brazil's frontier with the Amazonian territories added a further source of dispute. Diplomatic recognition proceeded unevenly: Spain recognized Ecuador in 1840 and Venezuela in 1845, but New Granada only later in the century.

Other inheritances were shared and contested. The flag's tricolor, the language of Bolívar's republic, the army's institutional memory, and legal traditions survived in different forms. The navy, the archives, and the stock of public buildings had to be distributed or claimed, and pensions for veterans of independence, owed by the former republic, became a political issue in each state. Each state also claimed the memory of Bolívar differently: Venezuela eventually returned his remains to Caracas in 1842, while New Granada was more divided between admirers and critics. The union's failure produced competing national histories, but it also left shared legal traditions, trade connections, and diplomatic habits that continued to connect the Andean republics for decades.

The settlement of debts, borders, and recognition illustrates a general point about the end of unions: partition is as demanding as unification. The successor states had to reconstruct institutions, define their relationships, and manage a legacy of obligations and disputes at the same time as they faced internal challenges. The ways in which they handled this task, through treaties, negotiations, and occasional military pressure, shaped their relations with one another for the rest of the nineteenth century. The 1834 agreement also shows a degree of diplomatic cooperation that contrasts with the bitterness of 1830, suggesting that the elites of the three states recognized their continuing interdependence.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Constitutional Union Failed and How Its Collapse Shaped the Successor States

Long-term causes

Distance and poor communications; separate colonial administrations and regional economies; the legacy of war, which created military leaders with local followings; fiscal weakness and the debt of 1822 and 1824; unresolved debates over centralism and federalism; a constitution that could not be amended for ten years.

Short-term causes

The prosecution of Páez and La Cosiata; Bolívar's absence in Peru; the early convention at Ocaña; the dictatorship and the conspiracy; the war with Peru; Bolívar's declining health; rumors of monarchy in 1829.

Event or transformation

Venezuela, Ecuador, and New Granada separated between 1829 and 1832 and reorganized as distinct republics.

Immediate effects

Debts were divided, borders disputed, and constitutions rewritten; Bolívar and Sucre died; military leaders consolidated regional authority; the south's army became the core of Ecuador.

Long-term consequences

Successor states adopted constitutions that blended centralism with provincial elements, and some, like Venezuela and Ecuador, were dominated by the caudillos who led separation; unresolved borders and debts created recurring disputes; the memory of union became a resource for later integration movements.

Historical complexity

No single cause is sufficient. Structural conditions made union difficult, but the specific timing and form of dissolution depended on choices by Páez, Santander, Bolívar, and regional elites. The successor states faced the same problems and solved them differently, which suggests that union's failure was neither inevitable nor unique.
Division V · 1830–1850Venezuela after Gran Colombia
Section 60.22

Páez and the Constitution of 1830

Venezuela's constitution, approved by the Congress of Valencia on 22 September 1830 and promulgated by Páez two days later, was a long and detailed document of 228 articles in 28 titles. Its first article declared the Venezuelan nation to be the union of all Venezuelans, a formula that located sovereignty in the people as a whole and not in provinces or municipalities. The structure it created has often been called centro-federal, a hybrid: a unitary republic with a president serving a four-year term and barred from immediate re-election, a bicameral Congress, and an independent judiciary, but with elected provincial deputations that shared in administration and in the choice of provincial officials. The deputations gave provincial elites a voice in the allocation of local revenue and public works, a response to the grievances that had fed La Cosiata, though governors were still appointed from the center. The text also maintained the state's claim, inherited from the Spanish Crown, to exercise patronage over the Church, and it limited the vote to men who met property, income, or occupational qualifications, so that the political nation remained a small minority of free adult men.

The constitution rested on a political coalition built by Páez. He had the loyalty of the llaneros, the plains cavalrymen and cattle workers who had formed the backbone of his army, and he commanded the respect of Caracas merchants and landowners who saw in him a guarantor of order. Civilian jurists and administrators, such as Carlos Soublette, a veteran general with administrative experience, Santos Michelena, a financier and diplomat, and the lawyer Miguel Peña, provided legal and financial competence. Historians have called this arrangement the Conservative oligarchy, though the term should be used carefully: its members called themselves defenders of order and constitutional government, many had been liberals in the 1810s, and the label comes from later opponents. The coalition combined a caudillo's military prestige with an elite's administrative skill and aimed to keep the army subordinate to civil government while preserving the social order.

Páez served as the first constitutional president from 1831 to 1835, and his influence continued afterwards because he retained the loyalty of much of the army and could intervene when he judged the constitution in danger. This gave Venezuela a measure of stability that New Granada and Ecuador lacked in the 1830s, but it rested on one man's arbitration. A president who needed the commander's support to survive, and a commander who needed the president's legal authority to act, were bound by mutual dependence. The system worked as long as the principal actors cooperated, and in 1835 it faced its first serious test.

Venezuela's new elites also had to define their relationship to the Bolivarian inheritance. The Congress of Valencia had refused to receive Bolívar, and Páez's government treated his memory with caution, although Páez himself, in 1842, would organize the return of the Liberator's remains from Santa Marta to Caracas as a gesture of national reconciliation. The constitution's silence about the slavery that persisted in the coastal valleys, and its restrictions on suffrage, reflect the priorities of a landowning elite that valued stability above reform. What it provided was a framework within which regional elites could bargain over taxes and offices without recourse to arms, and in this respect it was more successful than the document it replaced.

Section 60.23

José María Vargas and the Revolution of the Reforms, 1835–1836

The election of 1834 tested whether a civilian could govern Venezuela. José María Vargas, a Caracas physician and rector of the Universidad Central, with a reputation for scientific learning and personal integrity, was elected president and took office in February 1835. He defeated military candidates, among them General Santiago Mariño, a hero of eastern Venezuela, and his election, backed by Páez and by Caracas elites who valued legal order, seemed to symbolize a new era. Vargas was committed to reform: he wanted to reduce the privileges of the army and the clergy, especially the fueros, the special courts and exemptions that shielded soldiers and clerics from ordinary justice, and he sought to professionalize the administration and to modernize the university.

On 8 July 1835 a group of officers, among them Pedro Carujo and Mariño, seized the president and declared a revolution. The so-called Revolution of the Reforms demanded the preservation of military and clerical privileges, a government led by the heroes of independence, and, according to some of its manifestos, a more federal organization of the state. A much-repeated anecdote has Carujo telling Vargas that the world belongs to the brave and Vargas answering that it belongs to the just and honorable; the exchange rests on later tradition rather than on verified documentation, and students should treat it as part of the myth of Vargas as the civilian hero. The rebels were not simply reactionaries: many were veterans who felt that the republic had forgotten their sacrifices and that civilian lawyers were taking the rewards of independence.

Páez, who was on his estates in Apure, marched to defend the constitution. He defeated the rebels over the following months, in a campaign in which his llanero cavalry again proved decisive, and returned Vargas to office. Vargas, discouraged by the experience and by his inability to enact reforms, resigned in 1836, and interim authorities governed until Soublette's election as president. The rebels were exiled, and the episode ended with the Conservative order confirmed and Vargas's reputation enhanced.

The episode illustrates the central tension in early Venezuelan politics between constitutional authority and the military prestige inherited from independence. A civilian president needed a general's help to survive a military revolt, and the general who saved the constitution thereby increased his own indispensability. It also reveals what the rebels feared: reductions in the privileges that had accompanied independence and a diminished place for the Church and army in the republic. Vargas's later reputation, as a civilian who stood for law against arms, made him a symbol in Venezuelan memory, and his body was later moved to the National Pantheon, but his presidency also shows how limited the space was for civilian politics in the 1830s.

Section 60.24

The Conservative Political Order, Trade, and Regional Power

After Vargas, power circulated among Páez and his ally Soublette: Soublette served as president from 1837 to 1839 and again from 1843 to 1847, and Páez from 1839 to 1843. The economic base of this order was agricultural exports, with coffee overtaking cacao as the leading export in the course of the 1830s and 1840s. Coffee grew in the central valleys and the Andes of Trujillo, Mérida, and Táchira, and it favored both smallholders and larger estates. Merchant houses in Caracas, Maracaibo, and La Guaira financed the trade, often through British, German, and North American partners whose credit connected Venezuelan planters to Atlantic markets, and the government depended on customs duties as its principal source of revenue.

Policy favored creditors and landowners. A law of 10 April 1834, the freedom of contracts law, removed colonial limits on interest and made it easier for creditors to foreclose on debtors, which benefited merchants and large landowners but exposed small producers, who borrowed against the next harvest, to debt and loss of land. The state also had to pay its share of the debt inherited from Gran Colombia, and it did so with greater regularity than some of its neighbors, a policy that won it credit among foreign merchants but constrained public spending. Provincial deputations managed local roads, schools, and public works with limited funds. Caracas dominated finance and politics, though Maracaibo, Valencia, and Barquisimeto retained commercial importance and local power.

The regime's strengths were institutional. It maintained civil order, collected taxes, honored its debts, and avoided the cycle of coups that afflicted neighbors, and it sustained a degree of constitutional government in which Congress, the courts, and the press operated with limited interference. Its weakness was narrow participation. Property qualifications limited the electorate to a small proportion of free men, and urban artisans, rural workers, and the enslaved had little voice. The Conservative leaders also had a tendency to treat opposition as disorder: the law of 1834 and the repression of dissent in the 1840s reflect a ruling group more committed to order than to broadening representation.

A generation of Venezuelans who had fought in the independence wars, or who were their children, felt shut out of the rewards. Economic downturns in the 1840s, when coffee prices fell and credit tightened, sharpened that discontent. Debtors, small farmers, and artisans in Caracas and the provinces resented the creditors' law, and ambitious provincial figures resented the dominance of a Caracas coterie. These grievances, combined with the political ambitions of excluded leaders, produced the Liberal opposition that emerged around 1840 and the peasant unrest of the mid-1840s. The Conservative order was therefore stable but not secure, and its stability depended on conditions that the economic downturn of the 1840s eroded.

Section 60.25

Slavery, Free People of Color, Llaneros, and Rural Venezuela

Slavery continued in Venezuela through mid-century. The enslaved, numbering in the tens of thousands and perhaps about forty thousand at abolition in 1854, worked on cacao and sugar estates, in coffee, and in domestic service, concentrated on the central coast and in the valleys of Aragua and the Tuy. The free-womb law of 1821 continued to operate, and Venezuela's legislators passed supplementary manumission measures, including legislation in 1830 and later, but these were slow and provided compensation to enslavers. Full abolition came only in 1854, under José Gregorio Monagas. Enslaved people continued to run away, to petition authorities, to purchase their freedom, and, in the war years, to join armies that promised liberty. Free people of color, including pardos who had served in the armies, were a large part of the population of coastal towns and of the militia.

The plains and the cattle economy shaped Venezuelan politics. The llaneros, cowboys and cattle workers who had formed the backbone of Páez's cavalry, remained a source of armed power, and Páez's authority rested in part on his ability to command them. Rural society in the llanos was organized around large cattle estates (hatos) and dependent workers; laws on vagrancy and rural police, passed in the 1830s and 1840s, controlled mobility and labor and required rural workers to carry papers proving employment. Peasants working small plots on others' land, the conuqueros, were vulnerable to eviction and debt, particularly after the law of 1834 made foreclosure easier.

Rural discontent could take political form. In 1846 Ezequiel Zamora, a trader and landowner, and others led peasant uprisings in the plains under the banner of land and free men, forcing the government to deploy troops, and the movement linked the economic distress of the countryside to the Liberal opposition. The relationship between popular mobilization and caudillo power was ambivalent: caudillos mobilized rural followers by offering protection, land, and status, but they also contained them, and the elites feared that mobilization might escape control. Pardos had been central to the independence armies, and their claims to equality, sometimes voiced in the language of liberal egalitarianism, continued to disturb elites who remembered Haiti.

The social history of rural Venezuela in these years illustrates the limits of the republic's reach. For most rural people, the state appeared as a tax collector, a recruiter, a judge, or a landowner's ally. Laws on vagrancy, rural police, and contracts structured their lives, while the formal politics of Caracas and Valencia were remote. Enslaved people were legally property, though many could negotiate their conditions, and free workers were subject to debt and surveillance. These realities help to explain why the Liberal critique of the Conservative order found an audience beyond the educated elite, and why the crisis of 1848 had a rural dimension that earlier historians neglected.

Section 60.26

Liberal Opposition, the Monagas Presidency, and the Crisis of 1848

In 1840 Antonio Leocadio Guzmán, a Caracas journalist and politician who had once been close to Páez, founded the Liberal Party and its newspaper El Venezolano, criticizing the oligarchy's monopoly of office and its economic policies, particularly the law of 1834. Liberals attracted artisans, small producers, debtors, and ambitious provincial figures, and they spoke of equality, free press, and reform of the credit laws. The term should not be read in a modern party sense: the Liberals were an opposition movement with a newspaper and a network, not an organized party with a platform. In the 1846 election Guzmán campaigned against Conservative candidates, was arrested for his criticism, and was sentenced to death, a sentence later commuted; the repression strengthened his cause and divided the Conservative elite.

José Tadeo Monagas, a veteran of the independence wars from eastern Venezuela and a regional caudillo in his own right, was elected president in 1847 with Conservative support. He soon broke with his patrons, moved toward the Liberals, and freed Guzmán. The conflict with Congress, dominated by Conservatives, grew until, on 24 January 1848, an armed crowd attacked the legislature as it debated measures against him, killing several legislators in an episode that Conservatives denounced as an assault on the constitution and Monagas's supporters described as a spontaneous reaction. The crisis revealed a political order unable to resolve disputes within its own institutions.

Páez, who had retired from the presidency but retained prestige, rebelled in defense of the constitution in 1848 and 1849, in an effort to defeat Monagas by force. The rebellion was defeated in 1849 at Los Araguatos, and Páez was captured, imprisoned, and in 1850 sent into exile, from which he did not return for years. The Monagas brothers, José Tadeo and José Gregorio, dominated the presidency until 1858, and in 1854 José Gregorio Monagas decreed the abolition of slavery. The Conservative oligarchy was displaced, and the forces that had challenged it, Liberal intellectuals, provincial caudillos, and rural movements, had mixed fortunes.

The change did not produce a more representative state so much as a new set of rulers backed by an army and by opponents of the old elite. The episode of 1848 showed that the constitutional order of 1830 had depended on a balance among caudillo, army, and oligarchy that could be upset when one party defected. It also showed how personal authority persisted despite the republic's formal institutions: Páez's defeat, like Vargas's survival in 1835, was decided by armed force and the loyalty of commanders. Venezuela's experience between 1830 and 1850 thus offers a case of a state that achieved real institutional continuity, collected revenue, and honored its debts, but did so within a political order whose limits became clear when its principal supports shifted.

Division VI · 1832–1850New Granada and the Colombian Republic
Section 60.27

Santander, the Constitution of 1832, and Republican Reconstruction

Santander returned from exile in 1832 and took office as president of New Granada on 7 October that year, serving until April 1837. He governed under the constitution of 1832, which combined a unitary state with provincial chambers that gave provinces a limited voice in local affairs. His priorities were fiscal discipline, administrative regularity, and public education. He reorganized the treasury, supported primary schools and colleges, and restored the teaching of utilitarian authors, including Bentham, in the universities, which provoked clerical opposition. He also promoted the improvement of the Magdalena route and encouraged immigration and commerce. The Santander of the 1830s was a more cautious figure than the one who had argued with Bolívar: having experienced exile, he sought to build institutions that could survive without a charismatic leader.

Fiscal reconstruction was central. New Granada had inherited the largest share of Gran Colombia's debt, 50 percent under the convention of 1834, and its revenue came from customs, the tobacco and salt monopolies, and the tithe. Santander worked to restore credit, negotiate with creditors, and establish more regular tax collection. The provinces, whose chambers shaped local budgets, gained a role in administration that reflected federalist pressures, yet the executive retained control over appointments. The republic's small army was reduced, and civilian lawyers played leading roles in his cabinets.

Santander also managed political succession, and the manner in which he did so exposed fault lines in the republic. His chosen successor, José Ignacio de Márquez, a lawyer from Boyacá, defeated General José María Obando in the congressional vote that followed the election of 1836, because no candidate won a majority of the electoral votes. The decision angered Obando's supporters in the southwest and the Cauca, who regarded the lawyers' victory as a repudiation of the soldiers who had made independence. The contest foreshadowed the civil war of 1839, as it suggested that the republic's lawyers and its soldiers could not easily share power, and that the outcome of elections could be contested.

New Granada's political culture in these years was marked by a pronounced attachment to legal form. The press flourished, Congress met regularly, and political disputes were argued in constitutional terms. The Church remained a powerful institution, and the constitution's religious clauses, examined in the source window below, committed the government to protect Catholic worship. At the same time, regional loyalties remained strong, and the provincial chambers became arenas for local elites. Santander died in May 1840, in the midst of the civil war that he had foreseen, and he left a political legacy that both Liberals and Conservatives claimed.

Section 60.28

The War of the Supremes, 1839–1842

The War of the Supremes began over a religious dispute. A law of 1839 ordered the closure of small convents, those with fewer than eight religious, and the use of their property for education, a measure in line with the government's reformist and fiscal aims. In Pasto, a region with a long royalist and Catholic tradition, a large Indigenous population, and bitter memories of the repression of 1822, the order provoked a local uprising in mid-1839, led by priests and notables. The rebellion drew on resentment of Bogotá's authority, on the economic marginality of the southwest, and on the defense of convents that served as schools, hospitals, and landlords for local communities. Indigenous communities around Pasto, whose relations with the clergy and with the state were complex, played a role that historians have emphasized in recent decades.

General José María Obando, a Cauca officer who had been denied the presidency in 1837, joined the rebellion in the following months, and it spread across the republic. Provincial leaders, known as "supremes," declared themselves heads of their own territories: in Cartagena, Santa Marta, Socorro, and elsewhere, often under the banner of federalism and local rights. The Isthmus of Panama declared itself a separate state from 1840 to 1841 under Tomás Herrera, before rejoining New Granada. The war was an alliance of grievances, among them federalism, resentment of Bogotá's fiscal policies, regional rivalry, the politics of the 1837 election, and religious sentiment, and no single ideology united the rebels.

The government of Márquez, with Pedro Alcántara Herrán and Tomás Cipriano de Mosquera as principal generals, defeated the rebels in a series of campaigns over three years, with key government victories including Huilquipamba on 29 September 1840 and Aratoca in February 1841. The war was costly and brutal, involved large irregular forces, and devastated parts of the country. By 1842 the rebellion was largely over, and Obando went into exile in Peru, returning later in the decade. The government's victory depended on the reliability of its generals, on the loyalty of Antioquia and much of Bogotá's hinterland, and on the rebels' lack of coordination.

The war strengthened the government's case for a stronger executive and for Conservative control, but it also revealed the weakness of national authority and the persistence of regional loyalties. It left a legacy of distrust between the center and the periphery, a veteran class with political claims, and a political vocabulary in which federalism became associated with rebellion and centralism with order. The religious dimension of the conflict also had long consequences: the Church emerged as a defender of order, and the government, having been challenged in the name of religion, moved toward a closer relationship with it. The episode thus shaped the political alignments from which Liberals and Conservatives later emerged.

Section 60.29

The Constitution of 1843 and the Strengthening of Executive Authority

After the war, Herrán, who became president in 1841 and served until 1845, and his ministers, among them the Conservative lawyer Mariano Ospina Rodríguez, argued that New Granada needed stronger national institutions. The 1843 constitutional reform was sanctioned on 20 April and promulgated on 8 May; its commencement was regulated separately for 1 October. It curtailed the powers of provincial chambers, enlarged the president's authority to appoint governors, and lengthened terms of office. Article 15 preserved the 1832 government duty to protect Granadans in the exercise of Catholicism, but Article 16 added a distinct provision declaring Catholicism the only faith whose worship the republic would support and maintain. That exclusivity clause belongs to the 1843 text, not Article 16 of the 1832 constitution. Supporters presented the changes as a return to order after a period of rebellion; critics saw a retreat from the provincial voice acknowledged in 1832.

The Herrán government also took other steps that reflected the Conservative turn. In 1844 it permitted the return of the Jesuits, who had been expelled from the Spanish dominions in the eighteenth century, a decision that was applauded by the Church and criticized by anticlericals. It reorganized education and the army, promoted public works, and resisted calls for provincial autonomy. These measures were presented as part of a program of "moral reform" that would restore the authority of religion and law in a society shaken by civil war, and they gave Conservatives a distinctive vocabulary.

Tomás Cipriano de Mosquera, born into a prominent family in Popayán and a major landowner in the Cauca, succeeded Herrán in 1845 and served until 1849. He was an energetic modernizer who promoted steam navigation on the Magdalena, supported roads and public works, reduced some tariffs to encourage trade, and sought to attract foreign capital. His government took a pragmatic approach to economic development and maintained the strong executive of the constitution of 1843, but its tariff policy created opposition among artisans who were threatened by imported goods. Mosquera later became a Liberal leader, illustrating the fluidity of political alignments before the formation of parties and the importance of personal and regional ties.

The constitution of 1843 and the Mosquera administration established a pattern of strong executive authority combined with a lawyer-led elite. The pattern provided stability, but it also created the conditions for a Liberal reaction. Artisans, small producers, and provincial intellectuals, who had little voice in the system, began to organize in the later 1840s, and the debate over the nature of the republic reopened. The experience of the years after 1843 demonstrates that strengthening executive authority could produce order, but did not resolve the questions of representation, religion, and economic policy that had divided New Granadans since the 1820s.

Section 60.30

Economy, Slavery, Indigenous Lands, and Provincial Society

New Granada's economy was regional. Gold mining in Antioquia, the Cauca, and the Chocó provided exports and employed enslaved and free workers; the Magdalena valley carried tobacco and other goods to the coast; haciendas and ranches supplied internal markets; and the highlands of Boyacá and Santander had a large population of small farmers and textile artisans. Steamboat service on the Magdalena began in the 1820s and expanded in the 1840s, but roads remained poor, mule transport was expensive, and many regions traded more with neighbors than with Bogotá. Antioquia, relatively isolated and entrepreneurial, developed a distinct pattern of mining, commerce, and colonization of new lands, while the Caribbean coast, centered on Cartagena and Santa Marta, was oriented toward Atlantic trade and suffered from the decline of its colonial role.

Slavery persisted, concentrated in the Cauca valley and the gold mines of the Pacific lowlands, where enslavers held thousands in the aggregate. Manumission under the 1821 law and individual manumissions reduced the enslaved population over time, to a small fraction of its earlier size by the late 1840s, but abolition came only in 1851, after the Liberal victory. Enslaved people in the Cauca and Chocó had long bought their freedom, worked independently in mining, and formed communities that preserved African-derived religious and cultural practices. Indigenous communities held lands in resguardos, which liberal reformers wanted to divide and which provincial authorities sometimes dissolved; resistance was strong, notably in the Pasto region and the southwest, and litigation was frequent. Indigenous people also participated in the wars of the 1830s and 1840s on different sides.

These economic structures produced unequal consequences of independence. Textile artisans in Socorro and other highland towns suffered from imported cloth after tariffs fell, which turned some into protectionists and later supporters of the Liberals. Cauca landowners, dependent on enslaved labor, were a powerful constituency in the politics of the 1840s and the principal opponents of abolition. Provincial society was dominated by local notables who controlled land, credit, and office, while poorer rural families depended on them for work, protection, and access to the courts. National politics reflected these local hierarchies: the lawyers and merchants of Bogotá, Medellín, Cartagena, and Popayán negotiated with each other, while artisans and peasants entered politics primarily through riots, petitions, and military recruitment.

The impact of civil warfare was heavy. The war of 1839 to 1842 involved the requisition of animals and food, the recruitment of men, and the burning of villages in some regions. Debt and taxation affected households differently according to region and wealth, and the financing of the war through forced loans and increased taxes fell hardest on small producers. Women ran households and farms in the absence of men, sold food and goods in markets, and defended family property in court. The social history of the 1830s and 1840s in New Granada is a reminder that the republic's constitutional debates took place in a society in which most people lived by agriculture, mining, and trade, and encountered the state as a source of burdens and, occasionally, protection.

Section 60.31

The Emergence of Liberal and Conservative Politics, 1848–1850

Political parties in the modern sense emerged only at mid-century. Earlier divisions, Bolivarians and Santanderistas, ministerials and opposition, were factions with changing membership, tied to individuals and regional interests. In the 1840s the economic dislocations of free trade, the memory of the war of 1839 to 1842, and the example of European revolutions combined to create a political opening. In 1847 artisans in Bogotá formed the Democratic Society of Artisans, demanding tariff protection and political voice. The revolutions of 1848 in Europe inspired young radicals, and newspapers multiplied, reaching beyond the small elite that had dominated the political debates of the 1830s.

In July 1848 Ezequiel Rojas published an article in La Aurora that is often treated as the first Liberal program, calling for individual liberties, the end of slavery, a reduced role for the Church in public life, and federal arrangements. In October 1849 Mariano Ospina Rodríguez and José Eusebio Caro published a Conservative program in La Civilización, defending order, religion, and legal continuity. Neither program existed as a formal party organization in the 1820s, and the chapter should not project them backward. Their antecedents were older and more complex: the Santanderista emphasis on legality, the Bolivarian stress on order, the quarrels over the Church, and the experience of the war of 1839 to 1842. Members of the political elite shifted between camps: Mosquera, once a Conservative general, became a Liberal leader.

The 1849 presidential election turned on the pressure of Liberal crowds in Bogotá, who surrounded the Congress as it chose among candidates because no one had won a majority, and José Hilario López, a general from Popayán who had fought in the independence wars, became president. His administration enacted the abolition of slavery in 1851 and a series of Liberal reforms, including the expulsion of the Jesuits and the extension of freedoms. These measures provoked a Conservative reaction and a civil war in 1851. The party system that took shape in these years would structure Colombian politics for more than a century.

The political world of Gran Colombia had thus given way to a new party system rooted in the experiences of the 1830s and 1840s. The shift from personal factions to organized parties reflected changes in the press, in the participation of artisans, and in the sharpening of conflict over religion and economic policy. It also meant that the great questions of the 1820s, centralism versus federalism, civilian versus military authority, and the role of the Church, were reformulated in the language of Liberals and Conservatives. The new parties did not resolve those questions, but they gave them a lasting institutional form, and the history of New Granada after 1850 is in large part the history of their conflict.

Division VII · 1830–1850Ecuador and the Politics of Regional Integration
Section 60.32

The Constitution of 1830 and the First Government of Juan José Flores

The constituent congress that met at Riobamba from August 1830 approved Ecuador's first constitution on 11 September, and the text was issued on 23 September. It is a short document of seventy-five articles in nine titles. It declared the government of the State of Ecuador popular, representative, alternative, and responsible (Article 7) and the Catholic, Apostolic, Roman religion the religion of the State, with a duty on the government, in exercise of the patronage, to protect it to the exclusion of any other (Article 8). It created a president serving a four-year term, a Congress, and a judiciary, divided the territory into three departments, Quito, Guayas, and Azuay, and gave deputies four-year terms. Citizenship and the vote depended on conditions that excluded most of the population: citizens could not be in a relation of dependence as domestic servants or day laborers, and they had to be able to read and write. Deputies had to be thirty years old and own real property worth four thousand pesos or enjoy an income of five hundred. Quito became the capital, a decision that satisfied the highland elites but irritated Guayaquil.

Flores became the first president and governed from 1830 to 1834. A Venezuelan-born officer who had served the royalists before joining the patriots, he had no native regional base, but he had married into a Quito family and cultivated landowners, clergy, and officers. His government relied on military patronage and an alliance with highland elites, with Guayaquil's leaders included at the margins. The challenge was integrating three regions that had few common institutions: a highland economy linked to Colombia and Peru through textiles and agriculture, a coastal economy oriented to the Pacific and to world trade, and a southern city, Cuenca, with its own agricultural and commercial interests. Roads between the regions were poor, and in the rainy season the descent from the sierra to the Guayas basin was difficult.

The government also tackled external problems. It negotiated its share of Gran Colombia's debt, which the convention of 1834 fixed at 21.5 percent, settled the conflicts over the Cauca provinces, and in 1832 claimed the Galápagos Islands, taking possession of Floreana and sending a small colony there. Domestically it faced a persistent fiscal shortage and a dependence on a small tax base that included Indigenous tribute, customs duties, and monopolies. The army, much of it inherited from the Colombian presence, was a heavy charge on the treasury and a source of political influence for Flores's officers.

The constitution of 1830 thus created a state whose institutions depended on a military leader's ability to balance regions. Its provisions on citizenship and its religious clauses reflect the preferences of an elite that wished to preserve social hierarchy and the Church's role, and its silence on slavery, which persisted in small numbers on the coast, shows the priorities of the framers. The later constitutions of 1835 and 1843 reflect the repeated attempts to revise the balance of regional and executive power set up in 1830.

Section 60.33

Coast, Highlands, Indigenous Communities, and the Regional Economy

Ecuador's economy was divided by geography. The coast, centered on Guayaquil, exported cacao, with merchants financing planters and importing foreign goods, notably textiles. Shipyards and the port's trade made it the country's commercial gateway, and its merchants favored free trade. The cacao economy was organized around estates worked by free laborers, tenant farmers, and a smaller number of enslaved people, and the cycle of harvests and prices tied Guayaquil to markets in Europe and Mexico. The highlands produced wheat, potatoes, livestock, and textiles, with the Quito obrajes (textile workshops) and household weavers supplying markets in Colombia and Peru, though these markets had shrunk after independence and the rise of imported cloth. Cuenca's economy rested on agriculture, quinine bark, and later the straw hats that would be exported through Panama, and Loja on quinine and local trade. The total population was small, probably well under a million, and mostly Indigenous.

Imports threatened highland textiles, and the tariff became a regional issue. Highland landowners wanted protection; coastal merchants wanted low duties. Indigenous communities paid a personal tribute and labor obligations, and many were bound to haciendas through debt, a system known as concertaje, in which a worker received an advance, often in cash or goods, and owed labor until the debt was repaid. Tribute was a significant revenue source and remained into the 1850s. Landownership was concentrated in highland estates, and Indigenous communities lost land through sale, debt, and encroachment, though many retained communal holdings, and some used the courts to defend them.

These regional differences shaped politics. Guayaquil's elites, wealthy and mercantile, resisted Quito's dominance and highland landowners' economic priorities. The Sierra elite, tied to haciendas, the Church, and Indigenous labor, formed a different political culture. Flores built his support in Quito and among his officers, and his opponents found theirs in Guayaquil, so that the contest between presidents was also a contest between regions. Cuenca, with its own economy and its sense of marginality, shifted between the two and gave neither a secure majority.

Indigenous people were not passive in these arrangements. They negotiated with hacendados over advances and obligations, petitioned local officials about tribute and labor demands, defended communal lands through litigation, and occasionally rose in arms. The religious and legal tradition that treated Indigenous people as wards of the Church and the state, and the fiscal importance of tribute, gave them some leverage and some protection, but also bound them to the system. The coastal economy, with its seasonal labor needs, drew migrants from the highlands, and the movement of people between sierra and coast was one of the forces that gradually linked the regions.

Section 60.34

Opposition, Rocafuerte, and the Constitutional Experiment of 1835

Opposition to Flores began in Guayaquil. Vicente Rocafuerte, a Guayaquil statesman who had served as a diplomat in Mexico and Europe, a convinced liberal and an admirer of the United States, returned in 1833 and led a coalition of coastal liberals and disaffected officers. In October 1833 a revolt in Guayaquil, known as the revolution of the Chihuahuas (a nickname), challenged Flores, and the war continued into 1834 and 1835. The movement combined coastal grievances over taxes and the dominance of Quito with liberal objections to the army's power and to Flores's foreign origins and personal ambition. Rocafuerte proclaimed himself Supreme Chief in Guayaquil in 1834.

Flores defeated his opponents in the field at Miñarica in January 1835, but he chose not to destroy them. He and Rocafuerte reached a settlement under which a constitutional convention met at Ambato in 1835, Rocafuerte became president, and Flores remained head of the army. The agreement joined a civilian reformer and a military leader, a pattern that would shape Ecuadorian politics for a decade. The convention adopted a new constitution that revised that of 1830, reflecting the compromise between the sides and a more restrictive approach to suffrage.

Rocafuerte's government, from 1835 to 1839, stressed education, roads, prisons, the postal service, and public administration, and he was strongly anticlerical for his time, which brought him into conflict with the Church while winning him allies among liberals. He promoted schools, including provision for women's education, and tried to reform the treasury and to reduce corruption. His success was limited by the poverty of the state, the persistence of regional rivalry, and the fact that the army remained under Flores. Nonetheless, the Rocafuerte years are remembered as the first sustained attempt at civilian reform in Ecuador.

The settlement illustrated how Ecuadorian politics worked. Neither a military caudillo nor a coastal civilian could govern alone, and their uneasy alliance gave the new state a degree of stability. It also reflected a bargain between regions: the highland elite and the army on one side, the coastal merchants and liberals on the other. But the arrangement depended on cooperation between two men with different regional bases and different political ideas, and the question of who would succeed Rocafuerte, and under what rules, remained open. When Flores returned to the presidency in 1839, he did so with the army behind him and the memory of civilian reform fading.

Section 60.35

Flores's Return, the Constitution of 1843, and Political Centralization

Flores was elected president again in 1839, serving until 1845, and drew on his control of the army and on patronage networks in Quito. His government faced a fiscal squeeze, reflecting the costs of the army and the burden of Gran Colombian debt, which strained a revenue base dependent on customs, tribute, and monopolies. It also confronted continuing coastal opposition, particularly in Guayaquil, where merchants resented the tax burden and the capital's dominance, and where liberal exiles kept up a campaign in print.

In 1843 a convention in Quito adopted a constitution that lengthened the presidential term to eight years and permitted reelection, strengthening executive power. Its opponents called it the "Charter of Slavery," and it provoked opposition from Guayaquil and from liberals. Flores was reelected under its terms. His government used patronage and military power to maintain control, and critics accused him of using the constitution to perpetuate his rule. The convention also took measures to centralize administration, giving the president greater control over provincial officials and over the army.

The 1843 constitution aimed at stability but intensified opposition. Coastal merchants objected to the tax burden and to Quito's dominance; liberals objected to executive power; and some highland elites worried about Flores's ambition and about the cost of his government. Flores's critics also pointed to his foreign origins and to his contacts with European courts, which fed accusations of monarchist designs. The government's fiscal difficulties, aggravated by a decline in cacao prices in some years, limited its ability to buy loyalty. Indigenous tribute and labor demands, which fell heavily on highland communities, continued, and some communities supported the movement against Flores.

By early 1845 the conditions for revolt were in place. The combination of a long-lived president, an army that served him, a constitution that seemed designed for his benefit, and a coastal elite that felt excluded created a political situation in which a single event could trigger a widespread rising. The question for historians has been why the system, which had survived in 1835 through compromise, could not do so in 1845, and the usual answer points to the narrowing of the political bargain that Flores had been willing to make a decade earlier.

Section 60.36

The March Revolution of 1845 and the Reconstruction of the Ecuadorian Republic

On 6 March 1845 a movement in Guayaquil proclaimed a revolution against Flores. Coastal leaders, including Olmedo, Vicente Ramón Roca, and Diego Noboa, joined with military officers and liberals, and the movement spread to other regions. Flores, facing widespread opposition and a divided army, negotiated the Treaty of Virginia in June 1845, under which he left Ecuador with his rank and a pension. A provisional government of three leaders, Olmedo, Roca, and Noboa, took office, and a new assembly at Cuenca wrote a constitution in 1845 and chose Roca as president, serving until 1849.

The Roca government sought to heal regional divisions, to reduce executive power, and to reform finances. It faced the persistent problem of a state with limited revenue and divided regions, and it had to contend with the army's loyalties and the legacy of Flores's patronage. In 1846 and 1847 Flores organized an expedition in Europe to recover power, with some support from Spanish circles, which collapsed before it reached Ecuador, demonstrating both his ambitions and the limits of foreign support. The episode strengthened the government by uniting Ecuadorians against a foreign-backed return.

The March Revolution did not resolve Ecuador's regional problem, but it altered the balance toward Guayaquil. Through about 1850, politics continued to be shaped by competition between coast and highlands, between military and civilian leaders, and between liberals and conservatives. In 1850 and 1851 the generals Diego Noboa and José María Urbina contested power, and Urbina, a coastal officer, presided over the abolition of slavery in 1851 and 1852. Indigenous tribute persisted into the late 1850s. The republic had survived, but its integration remained incomplete.

Ecuador's experience from 1830 to 1850 shows how a regional compromise could create a state and how its breakdown could remove a leader without destroying the state. It also shows the importance of regional economies for politics: the contest between Flores and his opponents was in important respects a contest between the sierra's hacienda economy and the coast's export economy. The early republic's constitutions, those of 1830, 1835, 1843, and 1845, each tried to find a balance, and each was undermined by the tension between military authority and civilian politics, between Quito and Guayaquil, and between a small enfranchised elite and a population that had little voice.

Division VIII · 1823–1850Peru, Bolivia, and Competing Andean Unions
Section 60.37

Peru's Early Constitutions and the Rejection of the Bolivarian Model

Peru's first republican constitution, adopted in November 1823, was liberal and legislature-centered. It stated that the executive could never be lifelong, and still less hereditary, and set the president's term at four years, with no re-election of the same person until another four had passed. It was suspended almost immediately because Congress, facing the royalist threat and military disorder, gave Bolívar dictatorial powers in early 1824. After the victory at Ayacucho in December 1824, Bolívar governed Peru through delegates, and through the Colombian army, until 1826. His presence was both a protection and a burden: the army he commanded was costly, and many Peruvians resented foreign officers and the prominence of Colombian officials.

In 1826 Bolívar presented the Bolivian constitution to Peru. Its lifetime president and tricameral legislature were adopted by Peruvian electoral colleges in December 1826, which named Bolívar president for life, though he had left the country. The decision was unpopular. Colombian troops in Lima mutinied in January 1827, and the lifetime constitution was suspended and repealed soon afterward. The new Congress that met in 1827 under Francisco Javier de Luna Pizarro elected José de La Mar president on 9 June 1827 and drew up a liberal constitution, promulgated on 18 March 1828.

The constitution of 1828, in 182 articles ordered in ten titles, restored the tradition of legislative supremacy and provincial participation. It created a unitary republic with a president serving a four-year term, a bicameral Congress, and departmental juntas elected to promote local interests, a gesture toward federalist sentiment that was meant to prepare later federal arrangements, and it provided for a review of the text after five years. Its adoption represented a rejection of the Bolivarian model and a reassertion of Peruvian sovereignty, and it became a reference point for Peruvian liberals for decades. It also left the republic with weak executive authority and a politicized army, and it arrived as war with Colombia loomed.

The contrast between Peru's experience and Colombia's is instructive. In Colombia, the question was whether to strengthen the executive to hold a union together; in Peru, the question was how to prevent a foreign liberator from becoming a permanent ruler. Peruvian leaders saw the Bolivarian model as a threat to sovereignty, and they associated it with Colombian domination. The result was a constitutional tradition that was liberal in text and military in practice, since generals continued to decide who held power.

Section 60.38

José de La Mar and the Republic after Bolívar

José de La Mar, born in Cuenca and a veteran of the Spanish army before he joined the patriots, was elected president by Congress in 1827 and served until 1829. His government reconstructed Peruvian authority after the departure of Colombian troops. It faced fiscal problems, since customs revenues and the Indigenous contribution had to support an army and a new bureaucracy at a time when trade was depressed and foreign loans, contracted in 1822 and 1825, were in default, and political problems, since factions of Lima liberals, among them Luna Pizarro and Manuel Lorenzo de Vidaurre, and provincial military leaders competed for influence.

Peruvians' perspective on Colombia differed from that in Bogotá. They viewed Bolívar's presence as an occupation, resented the lifetime constitution, and blamed Colombia for retaining Guayaquil, Jaén, and Maynas, territories that Lima had administered or claimed in the late colonial period. La Mar's own origins in Cuenca and his belief that Guayaquil belonged to Peru made the dispute personal. When war came, it was in part an effort to complete what Peruvians understood as the unfinished business of independence. It was also an effort to unify a divided political class around a patriotic cause, a strategy that, in the event, failed.

The Peruvian intervention in Bolivia in 1828, led by Gamarra, further strained relations. Peruvian officials argued that Sucre's presence in Bolivia and the Colombian troops there threatened Peru, while Colombians argued that the intervention was an attack on a sister republic. The defeat at Tarqui in February 1829 and the failure of the campaign damaged La Mar's standing with the army. In June 1829 General Agustín Gamarra overthrew him, and La Mar went into exile, dying in Costa Rica in 1830.

His fall demonstrated that military defeat could dissolve civilian authority, and that the Peruvian presidency depended on the loyalty of generals. The episode also showed the difficulty of the liberal constitutional order established in 1828: its executive had been given limited powers, its Congress was not strong enough to command the army, and its first president was removed by a military coup within fifteen months of his promulgating the text. La Mar remained a symbol for later generations of a civilian-minded patriot, and his case entered Peruvian memory as an example of the cost of defeat.

Section 60.39

Agustín Gamarra and the Search for a Durable Peruvian Government, 1829–1833

Gamarra, born in Cusco and formerly a royalist officer, ruled Peru from 1829 to 1833. He strengthened the army and sought to centralize authority. Peru's governments in these years experimented with protective duties, and Gamarra's economic policy leaned toward protection of domestic industries, particularly southern textiles and agriculture, which won him support in Cusco and the southern highlands, but antagonized Lima merchants who favored free trade. The state continued to depend on customs duties and the Indigenous contribution, restored after independence, and on arrears on foreign loans contracted in 1822 and 1825, which continued to burden its credit.

His wife, Francisca Zubiaga, known as the Mariscala, was a prominent political figure who accompanied him on campaign, intervened in political maneuvers, and became a subject of both admiration and hostility. Her case shows that women could exercise political influence in elite circles even without formal rights, though contemporaries often used her to attack her husband. She was a vocal supporter of Gamarra's authority and had a reputation for toughness and independence; after his fall in 1833 and 1834 she went into exile, and she died in Valparaíso in 1835. Her biography is one of the best documented of any woman in the politics of early republican Peru, and it illustrates the gendered language through which public women were criticized.

Gamarra's government was challenged by liberals in Congress, including Luna Pizarro and Vidaurre, and by regional rivalries between Lima and the southern highlands. Provincial officials, the prefects and subprefects, were appointed by the executive, and the departmental juntas established in 1828 had limited influence. In 1833 Congress, which had no wish to extend his term, elected Luis José de Orbegoso to succeed him, and the transition marked a rare constitutional succession, though it was soon undermined by Gamarra's allies.

The Gamarra years illustrate the problems of consolidating authority in a country whose elites were divided by region and whose army was the arbiter of politics. The government's aims, order, an army loyal to the executive, and a fiscal system that could pay salaries, were reasonable, but its methods, including favoritism and repression of opponents, and its dependence on a narrow base, left it vulnerable. The central question of the next phase, from 1834 to 1836, was whether a civilian president could survive without the support of the generals.

Section 60.40

Orbegoso, Salaverry, and Peru's Constitutional Crisis, 1834–1836

Orbegoso took office in December 1833 but faced a military revolt almost at once, as Gamarra's allies, backed by part of the army, tried to retain power. The crisis was resolved by the so-called Embrace of Maquinhuayo in April 1834, in which rival forces reconciled, and a National Convention then adopted a new liberal constitution, promulgated by Orbegoso as provisional president on 10 June 1834. The 1834 text was in reality a revision of the 1828 constitution, designed to limit the excesses of military caudillismo, and it gave Peru its fourth charter in eleven years. The peace was short. In February 1835 Felipe Santiago Salaverry, a young general of twenty-eight, seized Lima and declared himself Supreme Chief.

Orbegoso, who had withdrawn to the south and was supported there, appealed for help to Andrés de Santa Cruz, the president of Bolivia, who had long been involved in Peruvian politics and who had a base among the southern elites. Santa Cruz entered Peru in 1835 and defeated Salaverry at Socabaya, near Arequipa, on 7 February 1836, and Salaverry was shot soon afterward. Gamarra, in exile, also took part in the maneuvering, and the south's resentment of Lima shaped events. The sequence of 1834 to 1836 shows a civil war in which regional interests, personal ambitions, and the fear of a rival's victory interacted.

The crisis demonstrates how constitutional politics in Peru was entangled with regional rivalries and military factionalism. Lima, Arequipa, and Cusco had distinct interests: Lima's merchants and officials favored free trade and a strong capital; Arequipa's wool exporters wanted open trade through the south; Cusco's landowners and textile producers wanted protection. Generals served as regional power brokers, and the constitutions of 1828 and 1834 did little to restrain them. The invitation to a foreign president reflected the weakness of civilian institutions and the appeal of an alternative political project that promised order and southern interests.

For Orbegoso, the choice was between defeat and dependence. He survived, but his government became a client of Santa Cruz, and the legitimacy of Peruvian constitutional authority was compromised. The assemblies that proclaimed South and North Peru as separate states in 1836 were products of this situation, and they led directly to the Confederation. Peruvian historians have long debated whether Orbegoso's appeal was an act of necessary defense or of national betrayal, and the question remains linked to differing assessments of Santa Cruz.

Section 60.41

Bolivia after Independence: Sucre, the Constitution of 1826, and Santa Cruz

Bolivia was created in August 1825 from Upper Peru, and its first constitution was drafted by Bolívar, who sent his project and an accompanying address to the Constituent Congress in May 1826. The Congress sanctioned the constitution on 6 November 1826, and Sucre promulgated it on 19 November. It set up four powers, electoral, legislative, executive, and judicial, rather than the usual three (Article 9). The electoral power was exercised by citizens, who chose one elector for every hundred (Article 20). The legislature had three chambers of twenty members each, Tribunes, Senators, and Censors (Articles 27 and 28), the Censors serving for life (Article 61) and watching over the government's observance of the constitution. The executive consisted of a lifetime president, a vice-president, and three ministers (Article 77).

The provisions on succession were more complicated than a simple right to name an heir. The first president was to be named by the Constituent Congress (Article 78). The president was the head of the administration, without responsibility for its acts (Article 80). The president proposed a vice-president, and the legislature had to approve the nomination (Articles 30, paragraph 2, 83, paragraph 2, and 85); the vice-president was the head of the ministry (Article 88) and, on the president's resignation, death, illness, or absence, succeeded him immediately (Article 81). The legislature's general powers included naming the president and confirming successors by absolute majority (Article 30, paragraph 1). The result was a lifetime presidency whose holder chose the successor, but within a system in which the legislature approved the choice and the vice-president ran the government day to day. Bolívar's critics saw monarchy in disguise, while its defenders pointed to the legislature's role and the censors' power to accuse the executive.

Sucre, the first president under the constitution, accepted office for two years. He reorganized finances, established schools, restored Indigenous tribute under a new name, and tried to reform the mining sector, but he faced opposition. Elites in Chuquisaca resented Colombian officers and soldiers, and Peru's government under La Mar intrigued against him. On 18 April 1828 troops mutinied at Chuquisaca and Sucre was wounded. Peruvian forces under Gamarra entered Bolivia, and by the Treaty of Piquiza in July 1828 the Colombian troops left. Sucre resigned and departed in August 1828, a fall that made him a symbol of the failure of Bolivarian rule beyond Colombia.

Andrés de Santa Cruz, born in La Paz in 1792 to a Spanish father and an Indigenous mother of a cacique family, had served the royalists before joining the patriots. He became president in 1829 and held office until 1839. A new constitution, approved in August 1831, abolished the lifetime presidency, and he introduced legal codes, reorganized the army and the treasury, and promoted the Pacific port of Cobija, hoping to expand Bolivia's access to the sea. His government gave Bolivia a more stable order, and it nurtured his ambition to reunify the Andean highlands under a single government, an ambition that would lead him into Peruvian politics.

Section 60.42

Indigenous Tribute, Silver Mining, Regional Elites, and the Bolivian State

Indigenous tribute was the foundation of the early Bolivian state. The contribución indigenal, as it was called, was paid by Indigenous communities as a per-capita tax. It had been abolished in principle in the early republican decrees, but, as scholars emphasize, it was restored quickly, because the state could not afford to lose one of its principal revenues and because Indigenous communities themselves often defended it as the basis of their recognition by the state and their rights to land. Santa Cruz approved a regulation for the periodic census of tribute-payers, the revisitas, in 1829. One study of the period, citing earlier work, reports that the contribution made up about 30 percent of national state income in 1830, and the historians Tristan Platt, Brooke Larson, and others have shown that the share, and the precise sums, varied by region, by decade, and by the performance of other revenues such as customs and mining. Figures for the 1840s and 1850s should be taken from specific fiscal studies and not generalized; this revision has not verified decade-by-decade percentages.

The silver mines of Potosí and Oruro, which had declined during the independence wars, recovered slowly. Flooded mines, scarce capital, and the end of the colonial mita draft made production difficult, and the state tried to support miners through credit, the mint, and the purchase of silver. Highland valleys such as Cochabamba produced grain, Chuquisaca remained a legal and clerical center, and the eastern lowlands of Santa Cruz and the Beni were distant and sparsely settled. Cobija, on the Pacific, was a small but strategic outlet. Elites in La Paz, Cochabamba, Potosí, and Chuquisaca competed for influence over a state whose capital was a small highland city, and their differing economic interests, in trade, textiles, and mining, shaped debates over tariffs and currency.

Indigenous communities, organized in ayllus, retained communal lands and defended them through litigation and negotiation. The state's reliance on tribute also made it a guarantor of communal landholding in practice, since payment of tribute was tied to recognition as a community member. Under José Ballivián (1841–1847), a circular of 14 December 1842 asserted state ownership over Indigenous community lands and treated their possessors as emphyteutic users rather than owners. This challenged communal title and increased officials' discretion over tenure, although its implementation and the ability of communities to resist or negotiate the claim differed locally. The policy cannot be described simply as protecting or preserving communal holdings. This relationship, sometimes called a colonial pact carried into the republic, shaped Bolivian politics until liberal reforms of the later nineteenth century attacked communal property.

Scholars such as Platt and Erick Langer have argued that Andean communities in this period were not simply victims of the tributary state: they were integrated into regional markets, often favored free trade, and bargained with the state over taxes and rights, and in some regions community members in the 1850s were prosperous traders. These interpretations complicate older accounts that treated the republican period as a continuation of colonial oppression. They also suggest that the Bolivian state's stability, based on a tributary pact, differed from the paths taken in Ecuador, where tribute coexisted with concertaje, and in Peru, where guano would later replace it.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous Tribute and Corporate Rights

Tribute was a regular payment owed by Indigenous communities to the state, introduced under Spanish rule. After independence it was often renamed, for example contribución personal de indígenas or contribución indigenal, and reintroduced or retained because the new states needed revenue. Corporate rights refers to the legal recognition of Indigenous communities as collective bodies holding lands, with their own authorities and legal standing.

Liberal reformers sought to abolish tribute and divide communal lands in the name of equality and individual property. But Indigenous communities often defended tribute as part of an arrangement that protected their lands, while states resisted losing revenue. The tension between liberal ideals and fiscal necessity shaped Andean republics' relations with Indigenous peoples. Students should avoid assuming that abolishing tribute was uniformly beneficial or that its retention was merely oppressive; the historical record shows communities using the tributary relationship strategically, and also suffering from its burdens.

Section 60.43

The Peru-Bolivian Confederation, 1836–1839

After defeating Salaverry, Santa Cruz organized Peru into two states. The south declared its independence at Sicuani on 17 March 1836, and a northern assembly at Huaura followed in August 1836; Bolivia's own assembly met at Tapacarí. On 28 October 1836 Santa Cruz issued a decree establishing the Peru-Bolivian Confederation, composed of the North Peruvian State, the South Peruvian State, and the Republic of Bolivia, and convening a congress of nine plenipotentiaries, three from each state, to meet at Tacna to fix its bases. The congress was inaugurated in April 1837 and approved the Fundamental Law of the Confederation on 1 May 1837, known as the Pact of Tacna. Santa Cruz took the title of Protector, and he governed first from Lima and later from Tacna.

The Confederation had an economic logic. Southern Peru and Bolivia had long been connected by trade, with the Pacific ports of Arica and Tacna serving the highlands, and Arequipa's wool trade and the altiplano's commerce depended on these routes. Santa Cruz's tariff and free-port policies favored the southern ports and challenged Lima and Valparaíso, Chile's commercial rival. His own Andean origins, with an Indigenous mother from a cacique family, and his base in the highlands made him a plausible leader of highland interests. The structure that the Pact created, three states equal in rights with a shared citizenship and a general government, was an attempt to join the sentiment for autonomy with the need for a central authority.

But the Confederation had powerful enemies. Lima elites resented losing their capital's primacy and being governed by a ruler from Bolivia; Peruvian exiles, notably Gamarra, plotted against it; Chile's minister Diego Portales saw it as a threat to Chile's commercial and strategic position; and Argentina's ruler, Juan Manuel de Rosas, had his own quarrels with Bolivia over Tarija. These opponents did not need to defeat the Confederation's army alone; they needed only to exploit its internal divisions. The ratification process was also incomplete, which gave opponents an argument that the pact had not entered into force.

The Confederation also raises historical questions that scholars continue to debate. Was it a visionary Andean union that would have been a counterweight to Chile and Argentina, or an extension of Santa Cruz's personal power that would have collapsed under its own contradictions? Bolivian and some Peruvian historians have tended to favor the first view, Chilean and many Peruvian historians the second. The evidence, which includes the Pact, Santa Cruz's decrees, and the accounts of participants on all sides, supports both, and the question cannot be answered without assessing how the state would have developed over decades that it did not have.

Section 60.44

War against the Confederation and the Battle of Yungay

Chile declared war on the Confederation in December 1836, after a dispute over the seizure of Chilean ships at Callao and an expedition to Peru led by the exile Ramón Freire. Argentina, under Rosas, followed in May 1837, though its military effort in the north was limited. A first Chilean army, under Manuel Blanco Encalada, landed in southern Peru and was surrounded near Arequipa, and on 17 November 1837 the Treaty of Paucarpata ended the campaign and restored peace on terms favorable to the Confederation. Chile's government repudiated the treaty, particularly after Portales was murdered by mutineers in June 1837, an event that heightened Chilean determination and turned the war into a national cause.

A second army, led by Manuel Bulnes with Peruvian exiles under Gamarra, advanced into Peru. After the Battle of Portada de Guías near Lima in August 1838, in which the Restoration Army defeated the Confederate forces in the capital's outskirts, and months of campaigning in the highlands, the two armies met at Yungay, in the northern Andes, on 20 January 1839. Santa Cruz was defeated, and on 20 February 1839, at Arequipa, he resigned as Protector. He went into exile in Ecuador and later in Europe. The Confederation was dissolved, and Gamarra's government, supported by the Chilean army, took over in Lima.

The war's consequences were lasting. Chile emerged as a naval and commercial power in the Pacific, and its victory was a foundation of national pride and of its self-image as the guardian of the continental balance. Peru and Bolivia were left divided and often hostile. The memory of the Confederation became part of national narratives: for Chile it was a victory over a threat to its security, for Peru it was an episode of foreign interference or of Andean union, depending on the historian, and for Bolivia a moment of lost opportunity. The later rivalries, discussed in Chapter 65, built on the geography and grievances this war created, but they should not be read back into the 1830s.

The war also shows how internal and international factors intersected. The Confederation's collapse resulted partly from the military defeat at Yungay, but also from the opposition of Peruvian elites, the exhaustion of the southern economy, and the fragility of the Protector's alliances. The Chilean victory depended on Peruvian allies, and the army that won at Yungay included thousands of Peruvians. For many Peruvians the war was a civil conflict as much as a foreign war.

Section 60.45

Peru and Bolivia after the Confederation, 1839–1850

After Yungay, Gamarra returned as president of Peru, and a congress meeting at Huancayo adopted a new constitution on 10 November 1839. It replaced the liberal text of 1834 with a more authoritarian charter of 192 articles that suppressed popularly elected municipalities, did not restore the departmental juntas of 1828, and reduced individual rights. Gamarra then sought to annex Bolivia, a goal that ended on 18 November 1841 at Ingavi, where Bolivian forces under José Ballivián defeated and killed him. Peru then entered a period of instability, sometimes called the "military anarchy," from 1841 to 1845, with competing generals such as Manuel Menéndez, Francisco de Vidal, and Manuel Ignacio de Vivanco, who in 1843 established a Directorate, until Ramón Castilla defeated Vivanco's forces at Carmen Alto in July 1844 and became president in April 1845.

Castilla's presidency, from 1845 to 1851, began a new phase. The guano deposits on Peru's coastal islands, exploited under contracts from the early 1840s, notably a consignment contract with the British merchant house of Antony Gibbs in 1842, produced revenue that allowed the state to stabilize its finances, pay salaries, and settle internal and external debts. Guano revenue made Peru less dependent on Indigenous tribute and customs duties, although the contract system enriched merchants and created new dependencies. Castilla also built a more professional army and pursued public works. This transformation, which grew in the 1850s, is a key difference between Peru and its neighbors, and it laid the foundation for the abolition of tribute and slavery in 1854 and for the fiscal order of the later nineteenth century. The social effects of guano, including the labor of Chinese contract workers, belong to later chapters.

Bolivia, under Ballivián from 1841 to 1847 and then under Manuel Isidoro Belzu from 1848, remained fiscally dependent on tribute and silver. After Ingavi, Ballivián, the victor, consolidated power, reformed administration, promoted education, and sought to attract investment to the Beni and the Pacific coast. The Bolivian constitution, sanctioned on 11 June and promulgated on 17 June 1843, strengthened presidential authority and lengthened the presidential term. He was overthrown in 1847, and after a period of disorder Belzu, a military man from Chuquisaca, came to power in 1848. Belzu built a populist following among artisans, small traders, and Indigenous communities, appealing to resentment of the Chuquisaca and La Paz elites and of foreign imports, and his government marked a more popular style of caudillismo. His rule, which lasted until 1855, involved repeated conspiracies and a brutal repression of opponents.

Both states continued the search for stability, and both faced the consequences of a decade of conflict. Peru's guano revenue promised a break with the tributary past, while Bolivia remained tied to tribute and silver. The experiences of the two countries after 1839 diverged in ways that would shape their histories, and the memories of Ingavi, in Bolivia, and of Yungay and the Confederation, in Peru and Chile, would feed later nationalisms. The relationship between the two republics, which had been so closely connected under Santa Cruz, now became one of suspicion and rivalry, though trade and kinship continued to cross the border.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxes, Recruits, Petitions, Debts, and Ports in the Early Andean Republics

In the Andean highlands of Bolivia, Peru, and Ecuador, an Indigenous household's relation to the state was most visible at tribute time. Community authorities, the caciques and kurakas whose position the republics inherited from the colonial order, gathered payments, and lists of tribute-payers were maintained and periodically revised in the censuses known as revisitas. Disputes over who owed what, whether a man was a community member with land or a landless migrant who paid a different rate, or whether a widow or an old man was exempt, were argued before local officials, and the surviving petitions show families using the language of law and custom to bargain. In Bolivia, the payment of tribute was tied to recognition of rights to communal land, and in the 1830s and 1840s many communities regarded it as a guarantee. The labor owed alongside tribute, for road repair, for official travelers, or for local churches, could be as burdensome as cash.

The republics' demands for soldiers fell on villages and haciendas alike. Recruiters, often officers accompanied by local officials, took men for the armies of Colombia, Peru, Bolivia, and the Confederation, and for the civil wars of New Granada and Venezuela. Conscription removed workers from fields at planting and harvest, and in households where men were taken women assumed work on the land, in markets, and in the care of animals. Women also sold food, chicha, and cloth in the plazas of Cusco, Potosí, Quito, and Guayaquil, ran small businesses, and defended family property in court; their appearance in notarial records and lawsuits shows that, though excluded from the vote, they were active economic and legal agents. Soldiers' widows petitioned for pensions, which were often promised and rarely paid.

Debt shaped life in the countryside. In Ecuador, concertaje bound workers to estates through advances that were passed from parents to children; in Venezuela, the law of 1834 made it easier for creditors to seize land from small producers; in the Peruvian and Bolivian highlands, merchants and landowners extended credit against harvests and wool, and officials advanced tribute payments on behalf of communities. Enslaved people in the Cauca, the Venezuelan valleys, and the coastal plantations of Peru and Ecuador sought to purchase their freedom, hired out their labor, or fled, and the courts heard their petitions; free people of color negotiated with employers and officials from positions of precarious independence.

The ports of Guayaquil, Cartagena, La Guaira, Callao, Arica, and Valparaíso were centers of commerce, rumor, and disease. Customs officials, merchants, sailors, porters, and washerwomen formed a world in which news of political changes arrived by ship, and in which foreign merchants, British, German, French, and North American, built networks that financed trade and sometimes politics. Port towns were also places of epidemic disease and of unrest when prices rose or soldiers went unpaid.

Civil war affected daily life in ways that official sources only partly record. Armies requisitioned mules, cattle, and food, sometimes paying with receipts that were never redeemed; forced loans fell on merchants and landowners; harvests were disrupted; and villages were burned in the wars of New Granada and the campaigns against the Confederation. The silences in official records about these experiences, and the dominance of elite voices, mean that historians must read tax lists, petitions, notarial documents, court cases, and parish registers with care to recover the lives of those who bore the burdens of republican state-building. They also remind students that the constitutional texts studied in this chapter described the intentions of a small group of men, while the republic was experienced by millions of people through the tax collector, the recruiter, the priest, the court, and the market.

Section 60.46

Why Some Republican Institutions Survived and Others Collapsed

Comparison begins with the caution that stability is not a measure of merit. Venezuela's constitution of 1830 lasted until 1857 in form, and its Conservative order lasted until the crisis of 1848, because Páez could arbitrate between army, oligarchy, and provinces; because the centro-federal structure gave provincial elites deputations through which to bargain; and because coffee exports and customs revenue supported a modest but regular fiscal base. The order excluded most Venezuelans from politics, and it survived the Revolution of the Reforms in 1835 only because Páez's cavalry defeated the rebels. New Granada's order rested on a lawyer-led elite, a tradition of constitutional argument, and the compromise of 1832, but it endured the War of the Supremes and answered it in 1843 by recentralizing executive power. Its religious articles, nearly identical in 1832 and 1843, show a political class that treated the Church as a pillar of order.

Ecuador and Peru illustrate different institutional dilemmas. Ecuador, with a constitution of seventy-five articles written by twenty-one signatories in 1830, built a state whose cohesion depended on Flores's ability to balance Quito, Guayaquil, and Cuenca; the settlement of 1835 with Rocafuerte worked for a decade, and the constitution of 1843 narrowed the bargain until the coastal revolution of 1845 broke it. Peru adopted five different constitutions between 1823 and 1839, in 1823, 1826, 1828, 1834, and 1839, as well as the Pact of Tacna in 1837, a rate of constitutional change that reflected the army's role as arbiter. The liberal charters of 1828 and 1834 were followed by the authoritarian charter of Huancayo in 1839, and only the revenue from guano after the early 1840s gave the state a base independent of the military's goodwill. Bolivia, with a constitution in 1826 and another in 1831, had a more stable executive under Santa Cruz, who ruled for a decade, and a fiscal system anchored in the tribute that made up about 30 percent of national income in 1830.

Fiscal bases explain much. Bolivia's dependence on Indigenous tribute created a state that was stable but narrow, with little incentive to change land or labor relations. Peru's guano revenue after the 1840s offered an alternative that allowed the state to reduce reliance on tribute but encouraged contract-based enrichment and new disputes over the distribution of revenue. New Granada's customs and monopolies tied it to Atlantic trade and the Magdalena, and Venezuela's coffee and cattle exports created a different commercial elite. Ecuador's mix of cacao customs, highland tribute, and monopolies left the state poor. Public finance shaped who could afford to maintain an army and whom the state needed to bargain with.

Other differences include the relationship between military and civilian authority, the strength of provincial autonomy, the role of the Church, and the degree of elite cohesion. In Venezuela, Páez's authority and the Conservatives' unity reduced conflict until 1848. In New Granada, religious and regional issues produced rebellion in 1839, but the lawyers' political class survived. In Ecuador, the sierra-coast division was decisive. In Peru, regional rivalries among Lima, Arequipa, and Cusco prevented consolidation, and in Bolivia, the single highland base made a stronger executive possible. No simple ranking by stability captures these differences, since each state's institutions reflected particular bargains, and states that appeared stable could be fragile, as Venezuela's 1848 shows, while apparently chaotic politics, as in Peru, could coexist with the slow construction of administrative capacity.

Section 60.47

Citizenship, Social Hierarchy, Slavery, and Indigenous Sovereignty after Independence

Republican citizenship in all five states was unequal, and the constitutions studied in this chapter show how. The Cúcuta constitution of 1821 limited parish voting to men over twenty-one or married who met property or income qualifications, with a literacy rule postponed to 1840. Ecuador's constitution of 1830 excluded domestic servants and day laborers and required literacy. Bolivia's constitution of 1826 required citizens to be married or twenty-one, to have an occupation, and not to be domestic servants, with literacy required from 1836, and it gave electoral power to citizens only through one elector for every hundred. These rules together excluded most rural workers, most Indigenous villagers, the enslaved, and all women, though they differed in detail. Women had no political rights, but retained property rights under inherited law, and some, like Manuela Sáenz and Francisca Zubiaga, exercised political influence outside formal channels. Juana Azurduy, the guerrilla leader of Upper Peru, received a pension from Bolívar in the 1820s but died in poverty in 1862, an example of how military service did not secure a lasting place in the republic. The Afro-descended soldiers who had won independence found their claims to equality unmet, as the execution of Padilla in 1828 suggests.

Slavery's end was gradual and uneven. Colombia's free-womb law of 1821 began a slow process; Bolivia's constitution of 1826 declared the formerly enslaved free but bound them to their masters' houses until a law decided otherwise; and Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador, and Peru abolished slavery only in the early and middle 1850s, New Granada in 1851, Ecuador in 1851 to 1852, and Venezuela and Peru in 1854, after decades of continued bondage and compensation to enslavers. Enslaved people shaped this process through flight, litigation, military service, and revolt, and their actions pressured elites. The gap between constitutional equality and practice was greatest in the Cauca and Chocó, on the Venezuelan coast, and on the coastal plantations of Peru.

Indigenous peoples were governed under changing mixes of tribute, communal land protection, and liberal attempts at individual property. In Bolivia, tribute was restored soon after independence and supplied about 30 percent of national revenue in 1830, and under Ballivián the state asserted protection over communal lands. In Ecuador, tribute and concertaje coexisted, and in New Granada resguardos came under pressure while Pasto's communities resisted division. In Peru, tribute was restored after independence and abolished only in 1854. Indigenous communities used litigation, petitions, negotiation, and sometimes rebellion to defend lands and customs, and in the Bolivian case some defended tribute itself because it secured recognition.

The republic's extension of state power was uneven, reaching markets, ports, and elite households more reliably than remote villages, and its promises of equality coexisted with enduring hierarchies based on property, ancestry, occupation, and honor. Religious articles that committed governments to protect or sustain Catholic worship, in Bolivia in 1826 to the exclusion of other public worship, in Ecuador in 1830 with a duty of protection, and in New Granada in 1832 and 1843 with a duty to protect Granadans in the exercise of Catholicism and to sustain its worship, show that republican citizenship was also defined in religious terms. The conclusion for students is that independence changed who ruled, and in whose name, more quickly than it changed the conditions in which most people lived.

New Granadan legislators: They tried to preserve legal order and then rebuilt it in 1831 and 1832, balancing provincial demands with a strong executive and committing the state to protect Catholic worship.
Ecuadorian coastal and highland elites: Guayaquil's merchants sought free trade and local power; Quito's landowners sought protection and stability; both sought to avoid domination by the other, and the compromise held until 1845.
Indigenous communities: They experienced the republic mostly through tribute, labor demands, and threats to communal land, and they used petitions, litigation, and sometimes arms to defend themselves; in Bolivia some defended tribute as the basis of their recognition.
Afro-descended people and the enslaved: They fought in the armies, pressed claims to freedom and equality, and encountered continued slavery and suspicion, as the execution of Padilla showed; enslaved people pursued freedom through flight, purchase, and petition.
Women: Excluded from formal politics, they managed households, property, and in some cases political networks; figures like Sáenz and Zubiaga show the possibilities and limits, and Azurduy the uncertain rewards of service.
Peruvian and Bolivian factions: Peruvian liberals rejected Bolivarian authority; southern Peruvians and Bolivians sometimes favored Andean union; Lima elites and Chilean leaders opposed Santa Cruz.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Colonial Institutions and Republican Citizenship

What Changed

  • Constitutions replaced royal authority with republican government and declared sovereignty to lie with the nation or the people
  • Slavery began a slow legal decline through free-womb and manumission laws and ended by about 1854
  • Indigenous tribute was renamed and sometimes abolished, then restored, and in Peru ended in 1854
  • Municipalities, provincial deputations, and departmental juntas replaced or reshaped cabildos
  • Military leaders acquired political authority through war and kept it through patronage

What Remained Continuous

  • Castilian law and Church institutions persisted, and the patronage claimed by the Spanish Crown was claimed by republican governments
  • Slavery, tribute, concertaje, and haciendas continued to structure labor
  • The alcabala, the monopolies, and the tithe supported state revenues
  • Regional networks of trade and kinship remained strong
  • Social hierarchies based on property, ancestry, and status endured

Who experienced change most directly: Soldiers, lawyers, and regional notables experienced the most direct change through new offices and political arenas. Who experienced greater continuity: Indigenous villagers, enslaved workers, and rural women experienced greater continuity in taxation, labor, and legal subordination. Why the pattern matters: Independence changed who governed and in whose name, but it did not immediately alter the social and economic structures on which governments depended; recognizing this helps explain why republican institutions often appeared fragile and why social conflict continued.

Section 60.48

The Failure of Political Union and the Persistence of Andean Interdependence

Gran Colombia failed as a constitutional union, but the connections among its regions did not disappear. Trade continued along the Magdalena, the Pacific coast, and the Andean highlands. Legal traditions, military memories, family ties, and a shared political vocabulary crossed the new borders. Veterans of Bolívar's armies lived in all three successor states and in Peru and Bolivia, and the networks of friendship and rivalry formed in the independence wars shaped politics for decades. Bolívar's remains were transferred from Santa Marta to Caracas in December 1842, a gesture of reconciliation led by Páez, who had once opposed him. The memory of Bolívar became a shared symbol, even as each republic claimed it differently, and the republic of his dreams became a subject for historians, polemicists, and, later, regional integration projects.

The unresolved borders and debts kept the Andean republics in a relationship of dispute and cooperation. Ecuador and Peru contested Jaén and Maynas; Venezuela and New Granada negotiated their frontier and the Guajira; and the debts, divided in 1834, were serviced irregularly. The idea of Andean union also persisted: the First Lima Congress of 1847 and 1848, attended by Bolivia, Chile, Ecuador, New Granada, and Peru, tried to create common defensive and diplomatic arrangements in response to rumors of a Spanish-backed expedition led by Flores, though its treaties were not widely ratified. These efforts show that the idea of union survived the failure of the particular constitutional union attempted in 1819, and that the successor states saw their security as interdependent.

The chapter's central lesson is that winning independence, forming a republic, maintaining political union, and building an effective state were separate achievements. Gran Colombia achieved the first two and failed at the third and fourth. Its successors built states of different kinds, from Venezuela's caudillo-oligarchy to New Granada's lawyerly republic, Ecuador's regional balance, Peru's guano state, and Bolivia's tribute state, and the variety of outcomes underlines that there was no single path from independence to nationhood. It also warns against reading the nineteenth-century Andes as a story of simple failure: the states that emerged collected taxes, fought and settled wars, wrote and rewrote constitutions, and, in the case of Venezuela and New Granada, sustained civilian institutions that outlived their founders.

The failure of union and the persistence of interdependence leave a question that later chapters take up. How did Andean states, born of the same wars and divided by the same disputes, develop distinct national identities while remaining entangled in one another's affairs? Chapter 65 returns to the Pacific rivalries that the Confederation's war helped to create, and Chapter 70 considers how the United States entered a hemispheric order in which the Andean republics were no longer the center of attention. The history of Gran Colombia thus ends not as an endpoint but as a beginning: the experience of failure shaped the way successor states thought about sovereignty, federalism, and the relationship between military and civilian authority.

Chapter Summary

This chapter began with a contradiction: a union formed by revolutionary victory and a charismatic leader that did not survive the leader's own lifetime. Gran Colombia was created on 17 December 1819 by the Congress of Angostura, after Bogotá had fallen to the patriots but while Caracas, Quito, and much of the coast were still royalist. The Fundamental Law united Venezuela and New Granada and divided the republic into three departments. It was organized in 1821 at Cúcuta as a centralist republic under a constitution that deferred amendment for ten years. The strategic logic of union was strong: a single government could mobilize resources, command respect abroad, and complete the liberation of the Andes.

The union's governing structure divided executive authority between Bolívar, often absent on campaign, and Santander, who administered from Bogotá. Administrative integration, culminating in the twelve departments of 1824, imposed uniform institutions on regions separated by mountains and rivers. Public finance depended on customs, monopolies, and tribute, and the London loans of 1822 and 1824 produced a debt that could not be serviced after 1826. The Panama Congress of 1826 showed the republic's diplomatic ambition and its limits, and the free-womb law and the treatment of Indigenous tribute showed how far constitutional citizenship was from social equality.

Regional opposition grew from fiscal burdens, conscription, commercial interests, and resentment of appointed officials. La Cosiata in 1826 showed that a regional commander could defy Bogotá with municipal backing; Bolívar's compromise of January 1827 left Páez in command. The Convention of Ocaña in 1828 failed to produce a constitution when Bolivarian delegates withdrew, and Bolívar assumed dictatorial powers on 27 August 1828, which polarized opinion further. The conspiracy of 25 September, Santander's exile, and Padilla's execution deepened divisions, and Manuela Sáenz's role showed how political influence flowed outside formal institutions.

The war with Peru reflected the unfinished business of independence and the militarization of Andean politics. Peru blockaded Guayaquil in November 1828 and took it on 19 January 1829; Sucre defeated La Mar at Tarqui on 27 February 1829. Venezuela separated in November 1829 and constitutionally in September 1830 under Páez; the south declared separation at Quito on 13 May 1830, held a constituent congress at Riobamba in August, approved a constitution on 11 September, and issued it on 23 September. The Admirable Congress deferred Bolívar's resignation and elected Mosquera on 4 May 1830, and it could not save the union. Sucre was murdered on 4 June and Bolívar died on 17 December 1830. New Granada's reorganization in 1831 and 1832, with a constitution dated 1 March 1832, completed the transition, and a convention in 1834 divided the debt.

Venezuela's Conservative oligarchy under Páez maintained order through a balance of caudillo, oligarchy, and army, surviving the Revolution of the Reforms in 1835 but falling to the Monagas brothers after the attack on Congress in 1848. New Granada rebuilt under Santander, endured the War of the Supremes, centralized in 1843, and saw Liberal and Conservative programs emerge in 1848 and 1849. Its constitutions of 1832 and 1843 shared a commitment to protect Catholic worship. Ecuador navigated the division between Sierra and Coast, with Flores, Rocafuerte, and the March Revolution shaping its early politics, and with a state whose revenue base remained small.

Peru and Bolivia, though never part of Gran Colombia, were part of the same Andean political world. Peru rejected the Bolivarian constitution, adopted a liberal charter in 1828, and entered a period of military politics in which it wrote five constitutions between 1823 and 1839. Bolivia, under Sucre and then Santa Cruz, depended on tribute and silver, and its 1826 constitution gave a lifetime president a role in choosing a vice-president who required legislative approval. Santa Cruz's Confederation of 1836 to 1839 offered an alternative Andean union under the Pact of Tacna and was destroyed by Chile, Peruvian exiles, and internal opposition at Yungay.

Across all five republics, citizenship was unequal. Slavery persisted into the 1850s, Indigenous communities paid tribute and defended land, women were excluded from formal politics but participated in other ways, and Afro-descended soldiers were rewarded unevenly. Colonial institutions continued under republican names, and rural people experienced the state mostly through taxes, recruitment, debts, and the courts. The Everyday Life feature shows how much of the republic's reality lay beyond the constitutions.

After 1839, Peru and Bolivia diverged. Gamarra's death at Ingavi in 1841 ended Peruvian ambitions toward Bolivia, and after a period of military anarchy Castilla used guano revenue from 1845 to stabilize the state, while Bolivia remained dependent on tribute and silver under Ballivián and Belzu. The divergence shows how fiscal bases shaped state capacity.

Comparing the successor states shows that stability depended on particular bargains among military leaders, elites, and regions, and that fiscal bases, from tribute to coffee to guano, shaped state capacity. The failure of union did not end Andean interdependence, and projects like the Lima Congress of 1847 and 1848 show that the idea survived. Interpretations of the dissolution remain divided between structural and political explanations, and students should hold both in view.

Key Terms

Gran Colombia
The modern historiographical name for the Republic of Colombia of 1819–1831, covering Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito, to distinguish it from today's Colombia.
Centralism
A system in which provincial officials are appointed by and answer to the national executive, as under the Cúcuta constitution.
Federalism
A system dividing sovereignty between a national government and provinces or states; a competing demand from the mid-1820s.
Caudillismo
A pattern of rule by leaders whose power rested on personal loyalty and armed followers rather than formal office alone.
Sovereignty
Supreme political authority; whether it resided in the nation, provinces, or municipalities was contested in the early republics.
Secession
The withdrawal of a territory from an existing state; contemporaries often called it separation or reassumption of sovereignty.
Fundamental Law (1819)
The act of the Congress of Angostura that joined Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito as the Republic of Colombia.
Constitution of Cúcuta
The centralist constitution of 30 August 1821, which could not be reformed for ten years.
Intendant
An appointed official governing a department under the 1824 territorial law.
Department
The largest administrative division of Gran Colombia; twelve existed after 1824.
La Cosiata
The 1826 Venezuelan movement, centered on Páez, that defied Bogotá's authority.
Convention of Ocaña
The 1828 constitutional convention that failed when Bolivarian delegates withdrew.
Organic Decree (1828)
Bolívar's decree of 27 August 1828 assuming supreme power and abolishing the vice-presidency.
Free-womb law
The 1821 law freeing children born to enslaved mothers but requiring labor service until adulthood.
Manumission
The freeing of an enslaved person, by purchase, will, or law.
Pardo
A person of mixed African descent, often free; also a term for Afro-descended soldiers and militia.
Revisita
The periodic census of Indigenous tribute-payers in the Andean republics, regulated in Bolivia in 1829.
Pact of Tacna
The Fundamental Law of the Peru-Bolivian Confederation, approved on 1 May 1837 by nine plenipotentiaries.
Pardocracia
Elite fear of Afro-descended majority power in politics and the army.
Tribute
A regular payment owed by Indigenous communities, often renamed after independence.
Resguardo
Communal Indigenous landholding, which liberal reforms tried to divide.
Alcabala
A colonial sales tax retained by the republics.
Estanco
A state monopoly, such as those on tobacco and aguardiente.
Fuero
A special legal privilege of clergy or military, central to the Revolution of the Reforms.
Uti possidetis
The principle of using colonial administrative boundaries of 1810 to define national borders.
War of the Supremes
The 1839–1842 civil war in New Granada, sparked by the Pasto convent dispute.
Peru-Bolivian Confederation
The 1836–1839 union of North Peru, South Peru, and Bolivia under Santa Cruz.
Protector
The title of Santa Cruz as executive head of the Confederation.
Guano
Seabird droppings exported as fertilizer from the 1840s, transforming Peruvian finances.
Concertaje
An Ecuadorian system binding Indigenous workers to estates through debt.
Contribución indigenal
Bolivia's Indigenous tribute, the largest source of state revenue.

Chapter Reflection

Gran Colombia matters because it shows that the end of empire did not settle what would replace it. Bolívar's union was among the boldest political experiments of the independence era, and its collapse within a dozen years is not simply a story of personal failure. It reveals the limits of constitutional design when distance, debt, regional economies, and military patronage pull in different directions.

The chapter also shows that the successor republics were not failed versions of a single model. Venezuela's caudillo-oligarchy, New Granada's lawyerly republic, Ecuador's regional balance, Peru's military politics and guano state, and Bolivia's tribute state each reflected local bargains. Indigenous tribute, slavery, women's exclusion, and racial hierarchy persisted across them, and ordinary people experienced republican citizenship through taxes, recruitment, petitions, and local disputes more than through constitutions.

Many of the questions raised here return in later chapters: the quarrel between centralism and federalism, the relationship between military and civilian authority, the fiscal dependence on export commodities, and the unresolved borders. Chapter 61 turns to a different outcome of independence, the Brazilian Empire, where monarchy and slavery shaped a very different political order, and Chapter 65 returns to the Pacific rivalries that the Confederation's war helped create.

Students should carry forward the lesson that independence, republican government, political union, and effective state-building were separate achievements. The failure of one union did not end the connections among Andean peoples, and the memory of Bolívar's project would be invoked again by generations seeking regional integration.
