Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan
7

The Kofun Age and the Rise of Powerful Chiefs

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On a low rise above the Nara Basin, a community once devoted enormous labor to a single, unmistakable purpose: raising an earthen mound so large that it would remain visible on the landscape for more than seventeen centuries. Shaped like a keyhole when viewed from above — a rounded rear mound joined to a trapezoidal front — surrounded in places by a moat, and eventually crowned with rows of unglazed clay cylinders, such a monument required the coordinated labor of thousands of people, the command of considerable resources, and a shared understanding, among at least some communities across a wide region, of what such a structure meant. No inscription survives to tell us the name of the person buried within it. What survives is the mound itself, and what archaeologists can learn from studying its shape, its scale, its contents, and its place among hundreds of other such monuments scattered across the Japanese archipelago.

This is the world of the Kofun age.

Chapter 6 examined how a Chinese dynastic history, the Wei zhi, described a fragmented, competitive world of small polities in the Japanese archipelago during the third century CE, dominated in the account of that text by the polity of Yamatai and its ruler Himiko. That chapter relied on a foreign document, written by outside observers for their own political and administrative purposes, and it necessarily left many questions unanswered: precisely where Yamatai was located, how power operated within it, and how it related to other communities across the archipelago. This chapter turns from that documentary evidence to a different, and in some ways more direct, body of evidence: the archaeological record of large elite burial mounds, known in Japanese as kofun, that begin to appear across parts of the archipelago at almost exactly the same historical moment addressed in Chapter 6, and that continue to be built, in ever greater number and in some cases ever greater scale, for the following three and a half centuries.

The appearance of these tombs marks something historically significant, but it is important to be precise about what that significance is and is not. It does not mean that a unified Japanese state suddenly came into being in the third century. It does not mean that the person who commissioned the earliest and largest of these tombs commanded the same kind of authority that later centralized rulers would claim. And it does not mean that every community that adopted a similar tomb form recognized the political authority of whichever community built the first or largest examples. What the Kofun archaeological record does show, when read carefully, is a real and important transformation: local and regional chiefs across a widening portion of the archipelago began investing enormous resources in monumental burial, standardized certain elements of tomb construction and mortuary ritual over a wide geographic area, exchanged and displayed prestige goods that marked status and connection, and organized increasingly capable military forces equipped with iron weapons, armor, and eventually horses. Together, these patterns reveal the emergence of a new scale of elite power and a widening network of interregional hierarchy — the raw material, so to speak, from which the more institutionally developed Yamato polity examined in Chapter 8 would eventually be built.

This chapter's central historical problem, then, is one of interpretation as much as description. Archaeologists can measure a tomb's length with considerable precision; they cannot measure, in the same direct way, how much political authority the person buried within it actually exercised over neighboring regions. When keyhole-shaped tombs of a broadly similar design appear not only in the Nara Basin but in Kibi, in northern Kyushu, in Izumo, and eventually across much of the archipelago, historians must ask what that similarity actually demonstrates. Does it indicate direct political control radiating outward from a single center? Does it indicate a looser alliance or ritual-exchange network among nominally independent chiefs who found it advantageous to adopt a shared elite culture? Does it indicate emulation — regional elites imitating a prestigious and successful form because it enhanced their own local standing, whether or not they recognized any outside authority at all? The honest answer, as this chapter will show, is that different regions, and different phases of the Kofun period, likely involved different combinations of these possibilities, and that historians must resist the temptation to collapse this complexity into a single, tidy narrative of steadily expanding Yamato conquest.

This chapter also continues a methodological discipline introduced in earlier chapters: the need to distinguish securely dated archaeological evidence from later literary and legendary tradition. The eighth-century chronicles known as the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki, compiled centuries after the events this chapter describes, contain genealogies and narratives that later readers — and sometimes modern popular writers — have used to identify specific kofun with specific named rulers. This textbook treats such identifications with considerable caution. The chronicles are important historical sources in their own right, and Chapter 8 will examine what they can and cannot tell historians about the earliest phases of Yamato rule. But they were compiled long after the tombs discussed in this chapter were built, by a court with strong political interests in presenting a particular version of its own deep past, and they cannot simply be read backward as reliable contemporary testimony about who built a given third- or fourth-century tomb or why.

By the end of this chapter, students should understand the Kofun age neither as a simple story of Yamato unification completed ahead of schedule, nor as a scattered collection of disconnected regional curiosities, but as a genuinely transitional period: a moment in which chiefly societies across a widening portion of the archipelago became measurably larger in scale, more hierarchical, more militarized, and more interconnected with each other and with the Korean Peninsula — setting the stage for, but not yet completing, the political formation examined in the chapter that follows.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Kofun period as an archaeological periodization and explain its approximate chronological boundaries.
  2. Trace the transition from regional late-Yayoi mounded burial traditions to the appearance of monumental keyhole-shaped tombs.
  3. Describe the characteristic form, construction methods, and scale of the keyhole-shaped kofun (zenpō-kōen-fun).
  4. Analyze what changing burial-good assemblages — mirrors, iron weapons, armor, agricultural implements, jewelry — reveal, and do not reveal, about elite status and political office.
  5. Explain the function and historical significance of haniwa and other mortuary ritual practices, while distinguishing early cylindrical forms from later figurative haniwa.
  6. Evaluate competing interpretations of what the regional spread of keyhole tombs reveals about political control, alliance, and cultural emulation.
  7. Assess the evidence for an emerging hierarchy among regional chiefs, including the possibility of a paramount center in the Kinai region.
  8. Trace the development of military equipment and mounted-warrior culture across the Kofun period, with attention to chronological change.
  9. Explain the multidirectional movement of people, technology, and political influence between the archipelago and the Korean Peninsula during this period.
  10. Distinguish archaeological evidence for chiefly hierarchy from later, more institutionally developed claims about the Yamato polity discussed in Chapter 8.
  11. Avoid anachronistically identifying specific kofun with specific rulers named in the eighth-century chronicles without appropriate source criticism.
  12. Contextualize the Japanese Kofun period within broader East Asian patterns of elite burial, state formation, and interregional exchange.

Historical Context

Chapter 5 traced the Yayoi transformation: the spread of wet-rice agriculture, metalworking, and increasingly large, defended settlements across the archipelago beginning roughly in the first millennium BCE, alongside growing social inequality and the first clear archaeological evidence of organized warfare. Chapter 6 then examined how, by the third century CE, this competitive landscape of small polities had become visible to outside observers: the Chinese dynastic history known as the Wei zhi describes a land of "Wa," containing dozens of named political communities, considerable internal conflict, and a leading polity called Yamatai ruled by a figure named Himiko, who conducted formal diplomatic relations with the Chinese Wei court. That chapter emphasized the limits of relying on a single outside textual source: the Wei zhi was written for Chinese administrative and diplomatic purposes, its geographic descriptions are difficult to reconcile with certainty, and it offers only a partial, externally filtered view of a complex and largely non-literate society.

This chapter picks up the story at almost the same historical moment — the third century CE — but shifts its evidentiary foundation from a foreign text to the archipelago's own archaeological record. Beginning in this period, communities in various parts of the archipelago, and especially in the Kinai region surrounding the Nara Basin, began constructing burial mounds of a new order of magnitude: tombs incorporating tens of thousands of cubic meters of earth, built in a distinctive keyhole-shaped plan, and furnished with imported and locally produced prestige goods far exceeding anything seen in the late Yayoi period. Over the following three and a half centuries, thousands of kofun of varying size and elaborateness would be constructed across most of the archipelago, from northern Kyushu to the Kantō region, with regional traditions in Hokkaido, the far north, and the Ryukyu Islands following distinct trajectories of their own that lie substantially outside this chapter's scope.

Understanding this transformation requires holding two facts in tension. On one hand, the sheer scale of the largest early kofun — several of which exceed two hundred meters in total length — indicates a command of labor and resources unmatched by anything in the Yayoi archaeological record, and this scale is concentrated overwhelmingly in a relatively small number of locations, above all the Nara Basin and the Osaka Plain. On the other hand, the geographic breadth over which broadly similar tomb forms and burial goods appear — well beyond the Kinai region, and in areas with their own long-standing regional identities and resources — means that historians cannot simply read the Kofun archaeological record as a straightforward map of direct political control from a single center. This chapter's task is to examine the evidence for both possibilities without prematurely resolving the tension between them, leaving the fuller institutional and political account of Yamato authority to Chapter 8.

Section 7.1

What Was the Kofun Age?

The term kofun (古墳) means, quite literally, "old tomb," and Japanese and international archaeologists use it in two related but distinct senses. In its narrower sense, a kofun is a specific type of monumental earthen burial mound, typically containing a stone-lined or, in later periods, corridor-style burial chamber, and often furnished with valuable grave goods. In its broader sense, "the Kofun period" (or "Kofun age") designates the archaeological era, conventionally dated from roughly the mid-third century to the late sixth or early seventh century CE, during which such tombs were built across much of the archipelago in ever-changing forms, scales, and regional distributions.

As with every periodization examined so far in this textbook, students should treat the boundaries of the Kofun period as analytical conveniences rather than sharp historical breaks experienced uniformly across the archipelago. The transition from late Yayoi mortuary practice to the earliest monumental kofun was gradual and regionally uneven: some communities in the Kinai region were already constructing unusually large mounded burials before the conventional start date of the Kofun period, while other regions continued distinctive local burial traditions well into the period covered by this chapter, adopting keyhole-tomb elements only partially or not at all. Similarly, the "end" of the Kofun period, conventionally associated with the decline of very large tomb construction and with the increasing textual and institutional documentation of Yamato court authority in the sixth and early seventh centuries, did not occur simultaneously everywhere; smaller kofun continued to be built in some regions even as the largest, most elaborate tomb construction was already declining elsewhere.

It is also essential to be precise about what the archaeological label "Kofun period" does and does not imply politically. The label describes a shared repertoire of mortuary architecture, ritual practice, and material culture that spread, with real but uneven consistency, across a wide portion of the archipelago. It does not by itself establish that a single political authority controlled this entire territory, any more than the spread of a particular pottery style or burial custom across a modern international boundary would necessarily indicate a single unified government. Understanding how much political unity the Kofun archaeological record can support — and how much it cannot — is one of the central interpretive tasks of this chapter, developed most fully in Sections 7.6, 7.7, and the Historical Debate features below.

Section 7.2

From Yayoi Mounded Burials to Monumental Tombs

Chapter 5 described the growth of social inequality across the Yayoi period, visible archaeologically in increasingly differentiated burials: some individuals interred with bronze mirrors, weapons, or other valuable goods, while the majority of community members received far simpler treatment. By the second and third centuries CE, several regions of the archipelago had developed distinctive local traditions of moderately large mounded burial for particularly prominent individuals, including square-shaped mounds with projecting corners in parts of the Sanin region near Izumo, and various other regional mound forms whose details differed considerably from place to place. These late-Yayoi mounded burials should not be understood as a single evolutionary sequence converging inevitably on the keyhole-shaped kofun; rather, they represent a set of regionally diverse experiments in monumentalizing elite death, out of which one particular combination of elements — most prominently associated with the Nara Basin — came to be widely, though never universally, adopted.

The transition to what archaeologists classify as the earliest true kofun is most clearly marked by a combination of factors appearing together for the first time: a substantial increase in overall tomb scale, the standardization of the round-rear/trapezoidal-front keyhole plan (examined in detail in Section 7.3), and burial assemblages featuring imported bronze mirrors and other prestige goods in quantities and combinations not seen in the preceding period. The Hashihaka tomb in the Nara Basin, discussed further in this chapter's Case Study feature, is widely regarded by most current archaeological analysis as either the earliest or among the earliest monuments displaying this full combination of features, and its construction — dated by most recent stratigraphic and ceramic-sequence analysis to the middle or later third century CE — is often used to mark the conventional beginning of the Kofun period.

Students should resist two tempting oversimplifications here. The first is to imagine that a genuinely new form of burial appeared abruptly and without local precedent; in fact, elements of the keyhole form — a rounded burial mound combined with a rectangular ritual platform or approach — can be traced in embryonic form in some late-Yayoi mounded burials in the Kinai region itself, suggesting continuity as much as sudden innovation. The second is to assume that the appearance of an unusually large tomb in the Nara Basin automatically proves that a unified political authority already existed there capable of commanding labor from a wide surrounding territory; the tomb's scale demonstrates considerable local or regional authority, but the geographic reach of that authority requires separate and more cautious evaluation, taken up later in this chapter.

Section 7.3

The Keyhole-Shaped Kofun

The zenpō-kōen-fun, or "front-square, rear-round tomb," is the most distinctive and, over the course of the Kofun period, the most prestigious tomb form in the archaeological record. Viewed from above, the tomb consists of two connected elements: a rounded mound, usually at the rear, within which the primary burial chamber was typically located, and a trapezoidal or triangular projection extending from one side, used in many cases as a ceremonial platform for ritual activity associated with burial and, in some interpretations, ongoing ancestor veneration. Not every kofun took this form: circular mounds (enpun), square mounds (hōfun), and various other shapes also appear throughout the period and across different regions, generally associated with lower-ranking elites or with regions that did not fully adopt the keyhole convention. The keyhole form itself, however, appears to have carried particular prestige, and its presence, scale, and orientation form one of the most important bodies of evidence available to Kofun archaeologists.

Constructing even a modest keyhole kofun required substantial planning and labor: clearing and leveling the site, excavating and often lining a surrounding moat (present at many, though not all, kofun and sometimes arranged in multiple concentric rings at the largest examples), transporting and compacting enormous quantities of earth in successive layers, constructing the internal burial facility — typically a pit-style or vertical-access stone-lined chamber in earlier phases of the period, and, at many sites in later Kofun phases from around the fifth century onward, an unusually elaborate horizontal corridor-style stone chamber — and finally covering the completed mound with a layer of fist-sized river stones (fukiishi) in many cases, along with rows of haniwa arranged around the mound's terraces, discussed further in Section 7.5. For the largest tombs of the fifth century, such as those in the Mozu-Furuichi tomb group on the Osaka Plain, modern engineering estimates suggest construction projects requiring the labor of many thousands of workers over a period that may have extended across multiple years, though such estimates necessarily involve considerable uncertainty about ancient labor organization, tools, and work schedules.

The sheer scale of labor mobilization implied by the largest kofun is one of the clearest and most direct forms of archaeological evidence available for this period's elite power. Unlike burial goods, which can in principle be acquired through trade, gift, or inheritance without necessarily reflecting the deceased's own direct authority, the successful organization of a multi-year, thousands-strong labor project implies a functioning system of command, provisioning, and social obligation extending well beyond the immediate household of the person being buried. This is one reason many archaeologists treat tomb scale, more than any single category of grave goods, as the most reliable single indicator of relative elite standing across the Kofun period — while cautioning that scale alone cannot specify the exact nature of the political relationships that made such labor mobilization possible.

Section 7.4

Burial Goods and the Language of Elite Power

Kofun burial chambers, where they have survived intact or been carefully excavated, have yielded an extraordinarily rich body of material evidence for elite status, exchange networks, and changing sources of authority across the period. In the earliest phase of the Kofun period, burial assemblages are dominated by bronze mirrors — some produced domestically, others imported from continental workshops or made locally in imitation of continental models — along with jade and glass beads, bracelets, and ritual or ceremonial bronze objects whose precise function is not always certain. The large quantities of mirrors found in some early kofun, including sets of mirrors sharing the same mold, have led some archaeologists to argue that mirror distribution functioned as a form of political currency: a paramount chief distributing mirrors to allied or subordinate chiefs as a mark of recognized status within a wider network, an interpretation connected to, but distinct from, the debates about Himiko and Chinese-bestowed mirrors discussed in Chapter 6.

Over the course of the fourth and fifth centuries, burial assemblages shift markedly toward military and equestrian equipment: iron swords and daggers, iron arrowheads, iron armor of increasingly sophisticated design (including both plate and lamellar construction), horse trappings such as bits, stirrups, and elaborately decorated saddle and harness fittings, and iron ingots or unworked iron material that itself appears to have functioned as a valuable and status-bearing commodity, given iron's continued importance for tools and weapons and the archipelago's continued reliance on imported iron and iron-working expertise from the Korean Peninsula during significant portions of this period. Agricultural implements, including iron-tipped hoes and other farming tools, also appear in some elite burials, suggesting that control over agricultural production and technology remained an important component of elite identity even as military display grew more prominent.

Historians and archaeologists must exercise real caution when interpreting these assemblages. A burial containing extensive military equipment does not necessarily mean the deceased personally fought in battle, any more than an elaborately equipped tomb necessarily means the deceased held a specific, clearly defined political office comparable to later, more institutionally documented positions. Grave goods reflect how a community wished to represent the deceased's status, connections, and role after death — a statement made by the survivors as much as a record of the deceased's life. Changes in the kinds of objects placed in elite burials over time — from mirrors and beads toward weapons, armor, and horse equipment — most plausibly reflect a broader shift in the sources and symbols of elite authority, from ritual and exchange-based prestige toward military and equestrian power, though the precise causes and pace of this shift, and its relationship to peninsular contacts examined in Section 7.9, remain active subjects of scholarly research.

Section 7.5

Haniwa and Mortuary Ritual

Among the most visually distinctive and historically informative objects associated with kofun are haniwa (埴輪), unglazed fired-clay objects arranged on the mound's terraces, around its base, and at other significant points in its construction. The earliest haniwa, appearing alongside the first monumental kofun in the third and fourth centuries, took the form of simple, undecorated cylinders, developed from Late Yayoi special vessel stands and jars used in ritual contexts, a tradition especially well documented in the Kibi region. Over the course of the fourth and especially the fifth and sixth centuries, haniwa production diversified considerably, first coming to include representations of houses and ceremonial equipment such as shields, quivers, and parasols, and only later the human figures — warriors, musicians, dancers, female attendants, and others — and animals, including horses, birds, and dogs, most often associated with haniwa in popular imagination.

Interpreting the precise ritual or symbolic function of haniwa remains, in important respects, an open scholarly question rather than a settled matter. Some archaeologists have proposed that rows of cylindrical haniwa functioned primarily as a form of sacred boundary marker, delineating the mound as ritually distinct from the surrounding landscape. Others have argued that figurative haniwa, particularly those depicting human attendants, warriors, and household structures, may have represented an idealized retinue or household accompanying the deceased into the afterlife, functioning in a manner loosely — though not identically — comparable to practices found in some other early state societies elsewhere in East Asia. An older, now largely secondary hypothesis has also proposed that haniwa arrangements served practical construction purposes, such as helping to retain the mound's earthen terraces against erosion, though current scholarship gives considerably greater weight to their ritual, spatial-delimitation, and mortuary-display functions than to this earlier structural explanation. Given this range of interpretations and the absence of any surviving contemporary written explanation, this textbook presents haniwa as important and richly informative evidence for elite self-representation and ritual practice, while explicitly declining to assert a single confirmed meaning for the practice as a whole.

What can be said with greater confidence is that the chronological development of haniwa forms — from simple cylinders toward an increasingly elaborate figurative repertoire — closely parallels the chronological development of tomb scale and burial-good assemblages described elsewhere in this chapter, suggesting that all these elements developed together as part of a broader, evolving elite culture of monumental death and remembrance, rather than as separate or unrelated practices. Students encountering later popular or museum depictions of haniwa as generic, unchanging "ancient Japanese art" should recognize that these objects, like the tombs on which they were placed, belong to a specific and evolving historical sequence rather than to a timeless cultural category.

Section 7.6

Regional Networks and the Distribution of Kofun

Perhaps no question in the archaeology of this period is more consequential, or more difficult to resolve with confidence, than the question of what the wide geographic distribution of similarly shaped tombs actually indicates. Keyhole-shaped kofun, or close variants of the form, appear not only in the Nara Basin and the Osaka Plain, where the strongest sustained concentration of the largest examples is found, but also in the Kibi region to the west, in northern Kyushu, in parts of the Kantō region to the east, and, in smaller or later examples, across much of the remaining archipelago outside Hokkaido, the far northeast, and the Ryukyu Islands. This distribution raises directly the interpretive problem this chapter has flagged from its opening pages: does shared tomb architecture indicate direct political control extending outward from a single center, a looser network of alliance and exchange among formally independent chiefs, cultural emulation of a prestigious and successful elite form, or some historically variable combination of all three depending on region, period, and specific political relationship?

Several patterns in the archaeological record are relevant to this question without fully resolving it. First, many of the largest tombs of the Kofun period, with the greatest sustained concentration, are found in the Kinai region, above all the Nara Basin in the earlier centuries and the Osaka Plain by the fifth century, suggesting that whatever combination of political, ritual, and economic factors allowed for the mobilization of the greatest labor and resources was most fully realized in this specific geographic zone. Second, regions such as Kibi developed substantial kofun of their own — including, at certain points, tombs rivaling all but the very largest Kinai examples — indicating that regional elites elsewhere in the archipelago commanded real and independent resources of their own, rather than functioning merely as subordinate outposts of a single Kinai authority. Third, the specific combination of tomb form, orientation, and grave-good assemblage varies meaningfully by region and period in ways that resist a single simple explanation: some regional elites appear to have closely imitated Kinai conventions in considerable detail, suggestive of close alliance or direct political connection, while others adopted the general keyhole form while maintaining distinctive local burial practices, suggestive of looser emulation or a different kind of relationship altogether.

This textbook does not claim to resolve this interpretive problem definitively, because the archaeological evidence, honestly evaluated, does not currently support a single confident answer applicable uniformly across the whole period and the whole archipelago. Instead, students should understand the Kofun-period archaeological record as documenting a widening and increasingly hierarchical network of chiefly power, with the Kinai region occupying an increasingly central and probably increasingly dominant position within that network over time, without treating every instance of a keyhole tomb outside the Kinai region as proof of direct administrative control from Yamato. The Historical Debate feature later in this chapter develops this question in more structured detail.

Section 7.7

Powerful Chiefs and Emerging Political Hierarchy

Even without resolving the precise political meaning of tomb distribution discussed in Section 7.6, the archaeological record provides considerable evidence for a growing hierarchy among chiefs across the Kofun period. Differences in tomb size, construction elaborateness, and burial-good quality and quantity, both within individual regions and between regions, allow archaeologists to construct relative rankings of elite status at a given place and time, even where the exact political relationships connecting different ranks remain uncertain. Within the Nara Basin itself, for example, the presence of numerous kofun of clearly differing scale, clustered in particular areas and periods, suggests a layered hierarchy of chiefly households rather than a single undifferentiated elite class.

The strongest sustained concentration of the largest tombs of the period in the Kinai region, and the progression of that concentration from the Nara Basin in the earlier centuries to the Osaka Plain by the fifth century, is most plausibly explained by the sustained ability of chiefly lineages based in this specific area to command greater resources, labor, and prestige-good access than chiefly lineages elsewhere, over an extended period of multiple generations. Many archaeologists therefore describe the Kinai lineages of this period as occupying a "paramount" position within the wider chiefly network — a position of clearly superior scale and resource command relative to other regional centers, without this term necessarily implying the kind of direct, institutionally codified administrative authority over distant regions that later, more centralized Japanese governments would eventually claim.

This distinction matters historically because it allows the chapter to describe real and significant political development without overstating its institutional character. The Kofun-period Kinai elite plausibly commanded alliance relationships, marriage connections, prestige-good distribution networks, and perhaps some forms of military cooperation or tribute extending well beyond their immediate territory — a genuinely significant achievement relative to the more fragmented late-Yayoi political landscape described in Chapters 5 and 6. But the archaeological evidence, on its own, does not yet demonstrate the kind of standing bureaucratic administration, formalized succession rules, or documented territorial governance that Chapter 8 will show developing more clearly, with the aid of both continued archaeological evidence and, increasingly, textual sources, in the sixth and seventh centuries. The precise moment and manner in which this expanding chiefly hierarchy became something historians can more confidently call the Yamato polity is the central question left open at the end of this chapter and addressed directly in Chapter 8.

Section 7.8

Warriors, Horses, Armor, and Military Change

Military technology and organization changed considerably across the Kofun period, and careful chronological attention is essential to avoid a common oversimplification: the image of the fully armored, mounted Kofun-period warrior, though a durable and vivid one in popular presentations of ancient Japan, characterizes primarily the later phases of the period rather than its beginning. The earliest Kofun-period elite burials, from the third and earlier fourth centuries, do contain iron weapons — swords, daggers, and arrowheads — reflecting the continuation and intensification of iron-working traditions already well established by the late Yayoi period discussed in Chapter 5. However, evidence for large quantities of sophisticated iron armor, and especially for horse-riding equipment, becomes substantially more prominent only from the later fourth century onward, intensifying further through the fifth century.

The appearance of horse-related equipment — bits, cheekpieces, saddle fittings, and eventually elaborately decorated ceremonial harness ornaments — in the archaeological record marks a significant development, since there is no strong evidence for horse use in warfare or elite transport in the archipelago during the Yayoi period or the earliest Kofun phase. Most archaeologists connect the appearance of mounted-warrior culture in the archipelago closely to intensifying contact with Korean Peninsula polities, examined further in Section 7.9, where horse-based warfare and associated equipment were already well established by this period. Whether horses themselves, horse-riding techniques, associated craft specialists, or some combination of all three were the primary vehicle for this technological transfer remains a subject of ongoing archaeological investigation, though the peninsular connection itself is well supported by the close similarity between archipelago and peninsula horse equipment of this period.

Alongside horse equipment, iron armor construction became notably more sophisticated across the fourth and fifth centuries, progressing from simpler riveted plate constructions toward more flexible lamellar armor assembled from numerous small iron plates laced together, allowing greater mobility for the wearer. The concentration of the most elaborate weapons, armor, and horse equipment in the largest tombs of the Kinai region, alongside their appearance in somewhat less elaborate form in regional centers such as Kibi and northern Kyushu, provides further evidence for the widening but hierarchically organized elite network discussed in Section 7.7: military technology, like ritual prestige goods, appears to have circulated through connections between chiefly centers of differing rank, rather than developing in complete isolation at each individual site.

Section 7.9

The Korean Peninsula, Migration, Technology, and Exchange

No account of the Kofun period is historically adequate without sustained attention to the archipelago's relationships with communities on the Korean Peninsula, which intensified and diversified considerably across the third through sixth centuries. During this period the peninsula itself was home to several competing and evolving polities, including Baekje and Silla in the south and southwest, the smaller polities collectively known as Gaya (or Kaya) in the region nearest the archipelago, and Goguryeo, the powerful northern polity whose military pressure on the southern peninsula shaped much of the period's diplomacy and conflict. Archipelago elites, especially those based in northern Kyushu and, increasingly, the Kinai region, maintained diplomatic, military, and economic relationships with these polities that shifted considerably over time in response to the peninsula's own shifting balance of power.

These relationships involved movement in multiple directions and should not be reduced to a simple narrative of the archipelago "borrowing" from a more advanced Korean civilization, nor to the reverse and equally misleading claim, found in some older and now heavily revised scholarship and popular nationalist writing, of extensive direct archipelago political control over southern Korean territory during this period. Iron, iron-working technology, horse-related equipment and riding culture, certain forms of ceramic production including the hard, high-fired gray stoneware known as Sue ware, and administrative and craft techniques including early forms of writing and record-keeping moved into the archipelago substantially through contact with peninsular polities, particularly Baekje and the Gaya polities. This transfer of technology and culture was carried in significant part by migrant specialists — often referred to in later Japanese historical tradition using terms that can be translated as "people who crossed over," or toraijin — including metalworkers, potters, scribes, and other craft and administrative specialists who settled in various parts of the archipelago, particularly in the Kinai region, bringing valuable technical knowledge with them and, in many cases, becoming integrated into local elite society over succeeding generations.

At the same time, archipelago polities were not merely passive recipients of continental influence. Textual sources compiled later, including both Korean and Japanese chronicles that require careful source criticism of their own, along with more securely dated archaeological and, in a small number of cases, epigraphic evidence such as inscribed swords and mirrors, indicate that archipelago rulers and military forces were, at various points across this period, actively engaged in peninsular politics and conflict, including military cooperation with Baekje and the Gaya polities against pressure from Goguryeo and, at times, Silla. The exact scale, duration, and political character of this archipelago involvement on the peninsula — whether it amounted to sustained territorial control, more limited military alliance and intervention, or largely symbolic and diplomatic engagement magnified by later tradition — remains a genuinely contested question among historians and archaeologists in Japan, Korea, and elsewhere, shaped in part by the ongoing political and national sensitivity of this history in the modern era. This textbook presents the underlying evidence for substantial, multidirectional archipelago-peninsula interaction as well established, while treating specific claims about the scale and character of any archipelago political or military presence on the peninsula with appropriate caution and awareness of ongoing scholarly and national debate.

Whatever the precise political character of these relationships, their consequences for the archipelago's internal development, examined throughout this chapter, were substantial: peninsular contact provided much of the technological foundation for the military transformation described in Section 7.8, contributed craft specialists and administrative techniques that would prove important for the more developed institutions of the Yamato polity examined in Chapter 8, and connected the archipelago's chiefly elites to a wider East Asian world of diplomacy, exchange, and conflict that would remain central to Japanese history for centuries to come, as later chapters, including Chapter 9's direct treatment of Japan, Korea, and the continental world, will further explore.

Section 7.10

From Powerful Chiefs toward Yamato

By the close of the period examined most fully in this chapter — roughly the end of the fifth century and the earlier part of the sixth — the archaeological record documents a chiefly world transformed almost beyond recognition from the fragmented, competitive Yayoi polities described in Chapters 5 and 6. Tombs of extraordinary scale, requiring the mobilization of thousands of laborers over multiple years, had been constructed at several points across the archipelago, with the strongest sustained concentration of the largest examples found in the Kinai region. A widening network of regional elites, from northern Kyushu to the Kantō region, had adopted, in varying degrees of fidelity, a shared repertoire of tomb architecture, burial ritual, and prestige-good display, connected to one another and to the wider East Asian world through exchange relationships that brought iron, horses, craft technology, and migrant specialists into the archipelago in substantial numbers. Military technology and organization had been transformed by the adoption of horse-riding culture and increasingly sophisticated armor, closely tied to the archipelago's deepening involvement in peninsular politics and conflict.

And yet, as this chapter has emphasized throughout, the archaeological evidence examined here does not, by itself, establish the existence of a fully formed Yamato state in the sense of a centralized government with codified law, standing administrative institutions, and clearly defined succession to a paramount office recognized uniformly across the archipelago. What it does establish is a substantially widened and intensified hierarchy of chiefly power, increasingly centered on, though probably not yet fully controlled by, lineages based in the Kinai region — precisely the raw historical material from which a more institutionally developed polity could, and eventually did, emerge.

This leaves the chapter, and the textbook, with an open and historically important question, one that this chapter has deliberately declined to resolve: at what point, and by what process, did this widening chiefly hierarchy become something historians can legitimately describe as the Yamato polity — a political formation with a more clearly documented court, a developing set of institutions, and an expanding claim to authority over the wider archipelago? Answering that question requires combining the archaeological evidence examined in this chapter with additional evidence, including the earliest reasonably reliable textual sources bearing on early Yamato governance, the development of clan (uji) organization, and the gradual construction of ritual and political claims to paramount authority. That combined analysis is the specific task of Chapter 8, "The Emergence of the Yamato Polity," to which this chapter now hands off its unfinished business.

Case Study

The Hashihaka Tomb: Scale, Chronology, and Construction

The Hashihaka kofun, located in present-day Sakurai, Nara Prefecture, in the Miwa area of the southeastern Nara Basin, is one of the earliest tombs displaying the full combination of features that define the beginning of the Kofun period: a large keyhole-shaped plan, a total length exceeding 270 meters, a surrounding moat, and construction techniques and associated pottery sequences that most current archaeological analysis dates to the middle or later third century CE. Its scale considerably exceeds that of any securely dated late-Yayoi mounded burial, marking a genuine step-change in the resources and labor commanded by whoever ordered its construction.

Readers familiar with popular treatments of early Japanese history may know Hashihaka primarily through its frequent identification, in some popular and a smaller number of scholarly accounts, with the burial place of Himiko, the ruler of Yamatai described in the Chinese Wei zhi and examined in Chapter 6. This identification rests on a combination of chronological proximity — the tomb's dating overlaps with the period in which Himiko is recorded as having died — and a passage in the Wei zhi describing a large mounded burial for the Yamatai ruler. It remains, however, an unproven hypothesis rather than an established fact: no inscription, no securely associated grave good, and no independent Japanese textual source confirms the identification, and reputable archaeologists continue to disagree about it. This chapter therefore treats Hashihaka primarily as invaluable archaeological evidence in its own right — for tomb scale, construction sequence, regional chronology, and the earliest phase of keyhole-tomb development — rather than as a solved puzzle about Himiko's final resting place.

Excavation and non-invasive survey at Hashihaka (full excavation of the presumed burial chamber has not been permitted, as the tomb is registered as an imperial-associated site under the administration of the Imperial Household Agency, a restriction affecting many of the largest kofun and discussed further in the Primary Source Window below) have documented its layered earthen construction, surrounding ritual precinct, and associated ceramic assemblages, which have played a central role in establishing the relative chronology used to date the beginning of the Kofun period across the wider archipelago. In this sense, Hashihaka's greatest historical value lies not in resolving a single biographical question about Himiko, but in anchoring the archaeological sequence that allows historians to understand how quickly, and on what scale, monumental tomb-building began in the Kinai region.

Primary Source Window

Reading a Kofun as Evidence

Type of evidence: Archaeological site plan and excavation record (non-textual primary evidence) · Example: A representative large keyhole-shaped kofun of the fourth or fifth century, based on patterns documented across numerous excavated and surveyed sites · Access note: Many of the largest kofun, including several imperial-associated tombs administered by Japan's Imperial Household Agency, remain closed to full archaeological excavation, limiting direct access to burial-chamber contents

Context: Because the early Kofun period left very little contemporary writing produced within the archipelago, and even the later Kofun period preserves only a small body of epigraphic evidence such as inscribed swords and mirrors, the tomb as a physical structure functions as this period's most important primary source, in much the same way that stratigraphy and stone tools functioned as primary evidence for the Paleolithic period examined in Chapter 2. A tomb's overall plan (keyhole, circular, or square), its total length and mound height, the presence and configuration of a surrounding moat, the construction and orientation of its burial chamber, the type and quantity of grave goods it contained, and the arrangement of haniwa and other surface features together constitute a body of evidence that can be measured, compared, and dated using the stratigraphic and typological methods introduced earlier in this textbook.

Illustrative description, not a specific site report: A typical large keyhole tomb of this period presents a rounded rear mound roughly twice the diameter of its trapezoidal front projection, surrounded by a single or double moat, with a stone-lined burial chamber near the summit of the rear mound oriented along a consistent regional axis, and rows of cylindrical haniwa arranged along the mound's terraced surfaces.

Analysis: Such a structure can establish, with reasonable confidence, the scale of labor mobilized for its construction, its approximate date relative to other tombs through comparison of construction technique and associated ceramics, and the relative status of its occupant compared to other burials of the same period and region. It cannot, on its own, establish the deceased's name, the exact nature of their political authority, the precise boundaries of any territory they may have controlled, or their relationship — whether kinship, alliance, subordination, or rivalry — to chiefs buried in similarly shaped tombs elsewhere in the archipelago. Historians must therefore combine this archaeological evidence carefully with later textual sources, themselves treated with appropriate source criticism, in order to construct a fuller political narrative — the task taken up in Chapter 8.

Historical Vocabulary
Kofun

Kofun (古墳), literally "old tomb," refers both to the monumental earthen burial mounds constructed across much of the Japanese archipelago from roughly the mid-third through the sixth centuries CE and to the archaeological period named after them. The most prestigious kofun form, the keyhole-shaped zenpō-kōen-fun, combines a rounded rear mound with a trapezoidal front projection, though circular, square, and other mound shapes also occur, generally associated with lower-ranking elites or regions with distinct local traditions.

Students should understand "kofun" as a term describing a widely, but not universally, shared elite mortuary practice rather than as evidence, by itself, of a single unified political authority. The term's usefulness lies precisely in its ability to describe a real and important archaeological pattern without prejudging the political questions that pattern raises.

Historical Vocabulary
Haniwa

Haniwa (埴輪) are unglazed fired-clay objects placed on and around kofun mounds. The earliest cylindrical forms developed out of Late Yayoi special vessel stands and jars used in ritual contexts, a lineage especially well documented in the Kibi region, and cylindrical haniwa remained the most common form throughout the period. Beginning in the fourth century, haniwa production diversified to include representations of houses and ceremonial equipment such as shields, quivers, and parasols; only later, chiefly in the fifth and sixth centuries, did the more famous figurative forms — human figures such as warriors, musicians, and female attendants, and animals such as horses, birds, and dogs — become common.

Archaeologists continue to debate haniwa's precise ritual meaning, with interpretations ranging from sacred boundary markers to representations of an idealized retinue accompanying the deceased, alongside an older and now largely secondary hypothesis emphasizing their practical role in retaining the mound's earthen terraces. Students should treat haniwa as rich evidence for elite ritual and self-representation whose exact symbolic function remains an open scholarly question rather than a settled fact.

Historical Debate

What Does the Spread of Keyhole-Shaped Kofun Actually Mean?

The question: Keyhole-shaped tombs of broadly similar design appear across a wide geographic area of the archipelago during the Kofun period, from northern Kyushu to the Kantō region, with the largest examples concentrated in the Kinai region. What does this shared architectural pattern actually demonstrate about the political relationships connecting these regions?

Interpretation A — Expanding political control from a Kinai center: Some archaeologists and historians argue that the adoption of keyhole tomb forms, particularly where regional examples closely imitate Kinai conventions in scale, orientation, and grave-good assemblage, indicates that regional chiefs recognized, in some meaningful sense, the political authority or precedence of a Kinai-based paramount lineage, and that tomb construction itself may have required some form of sanction, recognition, or even material support from that center.
Interpretation B — A prestige-good and ritual alliance network: Other researchers argue that the evidence more plausibly supports a network of formally independent chiefs who found it advantageous to participate in a shared elite culture of tomb-building, mirror and prestige-good exchange, and marriage alliance, without this participation implying direct political subordination to any single center. On this view, a regional chief building a keyhole tomb was asserting status and connection within a prestigious network, not necessarily submitting to outside rule.
Interpretation C — Regional emulation of a successful elite form: A third position emphasizes that some regional adoption of keyhole tomb architecture may reflect independent emulation of a successful and prestigious elite cultural form, comparable to the way architectural or artistic styles can spread through admiration and imitation without any political relationship at all connecting the communities involved.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same underlying body of evidence — tomb distribution, scale, construction technique, and grave-good assemblage — but differ in how much political significance they attribute to architectural and material similarity, and in how much weight they give to regional variation and independent local resources (such as those visible in Kibi) as evidence against direct centralized control.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how confidently later chapters, including Chapter 8, can describe the geographic reach of early Yamato authority, and shapes how cautiously students should treat popular narratives describing early Japanese unification as a swift or straightforward process.

Historical Debate

When Did Something Recognizable as the Yamato Polity Actually Emerge?

The question: At what point in the process traced by this chapter can historians reasonably begin speaking of a "Yamato polity" rather than simply a dominant chiefly lineage among several competing and cooperating regional centers?

An early-emergence view: Some historians place the effective beginning of a recognizable Yamato political center as early as the construction of the earliest large Kinai tombs in the third century, treating the concentration of resources visible at sites such as Hashihaka as evidence of an already meaningfully centralized authority.
A later-emergence view: Other historians reserve the term "Yamato polity" for a later stage, typically placed in the fifth or sixth century, when additional evidence — including clearer evidence of clan (uji) organization, more securely dated diplomatic and military engagement with Korean Peninsula states as a unified actor, and eventually textual evidence discussed in Chapter 8 — suggests a more institutionally developed and more widely recognized center of authority.

Evidence: This debate depends heavily on how much political weight is placed on tomb-based archaeological evidence alone versus additional textual, institutional, and epigraphic evidence that becomes available only in later centuries.

Why it matters: This question defines the boundary between this chapter and Chapter 8. This chapter deliberately does not resolve it, leaving Chapter 8 to examine the fuller institutional evidence needed to answer it responsibly.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Kofun World

Note: because no named individuals below the level of a small number of possible elite figures are securely documented for this period, the perspectives below describe categories of experience reconstructed from archaeological evidence, not quotations or biographies of specific people.

A Kinai chiefly household commissioning a large kofun: Building a tomb of impressive scale required the ability to mobilize labor from surrounding communities, to acquire or produce prestige goods for burial, and to coordinate ritual specialists overseeing haniwa production and mortuary ceremony — an assertion of status directed as much at rival and allied chiefly households elsewhere in the archipelago as at the immediate local community.
A regional chief in Kibi or northern Kyushu: Adopting elements of Kinai tomb architecture and burial custom could serve to assert independent regional prestige, to signal alliance or kinship connection with Kinai lineages, or both simultaneously — a choice shaped by local political calculation as much as by any obligation to a distant center.
A migrant craft specialist (toraijin) from the Korean Peninsula: Metalworkers, potters, and scribes who settled in the archipelago, particularly in the Kinai region, brought valuable technical knowledge in ironworking, horse equipment, ceramic production, and record-keeping, and in many cases became integrated into local elite society over succeeding generations, contributing directly to the technological transformations traced throughout this chapter.
A laborer mobilized to build a monumental tomb: For most participants in kofun construction, the experience likely involved sustained, physically demanding communal labor — moving earth, quarrying and transporting stone, and maintaining the mound's structure — organized through social obligations to chiefly households whose specific terms are not directly documented in the surviving evidence.
A modern archaeologist studying a kofun: Professional practice requires combining stratigraphic, typological, and increasingly scientific dating evidence with careful, comparative analysis of tomb distribution across regions, while explicitly resisting the temptation to read shared architecture as automatic proof of centralized political control.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Yayoi to Kofun

What Changed

  • Elite burial scale increased dramatically, from moderately differentiated Yayoi burials to monumental tombs requiring thousands of laborers
  • Burial-good assemblages shifted over time from an emphasis on mirrors and ritual bronze objects toward military and equestrian equipment
  • A standardized tomb architecture — the keyhole form — became a widely shared prestigious form across many regions, while substantial regional variation and non-keyhole burial traditions persisted alongside it
  • Contact with the Korean Peninsula intensified and diversified, bringing new technologies including horse-riding culture and more sophisticated ironworking

What Stayed the Same

  • Political power remained fragmented among multiple regional centers rather than becoming immediately and fully centralized
  • Regional identity and independent resource bases, visible in Kibi and elsewhere, persisted alongside growing Kinai prominence
  • Continued reliance on continental contact, particularly with the Korean Peninsula, for key technologies, echoing Yayoi-period patterns of exchange
  • The absence of a unified, centrally administered territorial state comparable to later historical periods

Why the pattern matters: Treating the Kofun period as a period of significant but incomplete political consolidation—rather than either "still Yayoi-style fragmentation" or "already a unified Japanese state"—prepares students to understand Chapter 8's more institutionally focused account of Yamato's emergence as a continuation of this chapter's story rather than a sudden new beginning.

Cause and Effect

From Chiefly Competition to Elite Hierarchy

Long-term causes

Yayoi-period agricultural surplus, growing social inequality, and increasingly organized warfare (Chapter 5) created the social and economic foundation for more concentrated elite power.

Short-term causes

Intensifying diplomatic and commercial contact with Korean Peninsula polities in the third and fourth centuries provided new prestige goods, iron, and eventually horse-riding technology that regional elites could use to enhance their status and military capability.

Effect

Chiefly centers, especially in the Kinai region, mobilized labor and resources at an unprecedented scale to construct monumental tombs, while a widening network of regional elites adopted shared elements of tomb architecture and burial ritual.

Long-term consequence

An increasingly hierarchical and interconnected chiefly network, centered on but not yet fully controlled by Kinai lineages, provided the political and material foundation from which the more institutionally developed Yamato polity examined in Chapter 8 would emerge.

Historical complexity: The growth of Kinai elite power did not mechanically or automatically produce a unified state; it expanded the range of political possibilities and pressures faced by chiefs across the archipelago, who continued to make their own strategic choices about alliance, resistance, and emulation within those changing conditions.

Everyday Life and Environment

Beyond the Tomb: Ordinary Life in the Kofun Period

Although the archaeological record of this period is dominated, for understandable reasons, by monumental tombs and elite grave goods, most people living in the Kofun-period archipelago experienced a way of life that, in its material basics, changed more slowly and less dramatically than the elite culture this chapter has emphasized. Excavated settlement sites from this period indicate continued reliance on wet-rice agriculture established during the Yayoi period, supplemented by other crops, fishing, and gathering, organized around villages of pit-dwellings similar in general form, though not identical in detail, to Yayoi-period settlements. Iron tools, including agricultural implements, became more widely available over the course of the period, gradually improving agricultural productivity even outside elite contexts, though access to the finest iron goods and imported prestige items remained sharply stratified by status.

The labor demands associated with monumental tomb construction, discussed in Section 7.3, would have represented a significant, if likely periodic rather than constant, claim on the time and effort of surrounding communities, alongside continuing agricultural, craft, and household labor. Sue ware ceramic technology, introduced from the Korean Peninsula during this period and produced using a potter's wheel and high-firing kiln techniques quite different from earlier Yayoi and contemporary Haji ware traditions, gradually spread beyond elite contexts, illustrating how technologies first associated with elite prestige could, over time, become integrated into wider material life. Understanding these everyday dimensions of Kofun-period life helps prevent this chapter from reducing the period entirely to a story of tombs and chiefs, and reminds students that the widening elite hierarchy examined throughout this chapter rested upon, and drew its resources from, the ordinary agricultural and craft labor of a much larger population whose own experiences are far less directly documented in the surviving evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the archaeological transformation of the archipelago's chiefly societies from the fragmented, competitive world of late Yayoi Wa, described through Chinese eyes in Chapter 6, into the substantially larger-scale, more hierarchical, and more interconnected world documented by the Kofun period's monumental tombs. Beginning in the mid-third century CE, chiefly centers in the Nara Basin and, over the following centuries, in a widening set of regional centers across the archipelago began constructing keyhole-shaped tombs of unprecedented scale, furnished with grave goods that reveal both continuity with earlier Yayoi elite culture and significant new sources of status and power.

The chapter has emphasized that this transformation was real and historically significant without being a simple story of Yamato conquest or sudden national unification. The scale of the largest tombs, concentrated overwhelmingly in the Kinai region and progressing from the Nara Basin to the Osaka Plain over the course of the period, provides strong evidence for the growing resources and labor-mobilization capacity of Kinai chiefly lineages. At the same time, the existence of substantial and partly independent regional kofun traditions, particularly in Kibi, and the genuine ambiguity surrounding what shared tomb architecture actually demonstrates about political relationships between regions, mean that historians must resist collapsing this evidence into an oversimplified narrative of centralized political control.

The chapter has traced significant changes in burial-good assemblages over time, from an early emphasis on bronze mirrors and ritual prestige objects toward a later emphasis on military equipment, iron armor, and horse-riding equipment, reflecting a broader shift in the sources and symbols of elite authority across the fourth and fifth centuries. It has examined haniwa as a rich but interpretively complex body of evidence for elite ritual and self-representation, tracing their development from simple cylindrical forms toward an elaborate figurative repertoire that closely parallels the chronological development of tomb scale and grave-good complexity described elsewhere in the chapter.

Sustained attention has been given to the archipelago's relationships with the Korean Peninsula, emphasizing multidirectional movement of people, technology, and political influence involving Baekje, Silla, the Gaya polities, and Goguryeo, and the important role of migrant craft and administrative specialists in transmitting ironworking, ceramic, equestrian, and record-keeping technologies into the archipelago, particularly the Kinai region. The chapter has treated contested questions about the scale and character of archipelago political and military involvement on the peninsula with appropriate historical caution, acknowledging genuine scholarly disagreement and the continued national sensitivity of this history in the present day.

At the same time, the chapter has consistently declined to resolve, prematurely, the question of exactly when and how the widening Kofun-period chiefly hierarchy became the more institutionally developed Yamato polity examined in Chapter 8. It has presented multiple interpretive possibilities for the meaning of shared tomb architecture — direct political control, alliance and exchange networks, and cultural emulation — without asserting a single confident resolution applicable uniformly across the whole period and the whole archipelago, and it has treated the popular identification of specific tombs, including Hashihaka, with specific named rulers from later chronicles as an interesting but unproven hypothesis rather than an established fact.

Finally, this chapter has kept ordinary life visible alongside its necessary focus on elite tombs and prestige goods, tracing continuities in agricultural production, settlement form, and craft technology that connected most of the Kofun-period population's daily experience to patterns established in the preceding Yayoi period, even as elite culture and military technology were being dramatically transformed above them. This combination of substantial elite political and technological change alongside significant continuity in ordinary material life is itself an important historical lesson, applicable well beyond this particular chapter.

These findings prepare students directly for Chapter 8, which will examine how the widening chiefly hierarchy documented archaeologically in this chapter developed into a more clearly institutionalized Yamato polity, drawing on both continued archaeological evidence and the earliest reasonably reliable textual sources bearing on early Yamato governance, clan organization, and the gradual construction of claims to paramount authority over the wider archipelago.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Kofun
Monumental earthen burial mound constructed across much of the archipelago from roughly the mid-third through the sixth centuries CE; also the name of the archaeological period itself.
Zenpō-kōen-fun
The characteristic keyhole-shaped kofun form, combining a rounded rear mound with a trapezoidal front projection; the most prestigious tomb form of the period.
Tumulus
A general archaeological term for an artificial burial mound, applicable to kofun and to comparable structures in other world regions.
Haniwa
Unglazed fired-clay objects placed on and around kofun, beginning as simple cylinders and later including figurative forms such as houses, animals, and human figures.
Moat
A water-filled or dry ditch surrounding many kofun, sometimes arranged in multiple concentric rings at the largest sites.
Burial chamber
The stone-lined or, in later periods, corridor-style interior space within a kofun mound containing the deceased and grave goods.
Grave goods
Objects placed with the deceased in a burial, used by archaeologists to infer status, connections, and changing sources of elite authority.
Prestige good
A valuable or symbolically significant object, such as a bronze mirror or imported bead, whose possession and display marked elite status and network connections.
Bronze mirror
A prestige object prominent in early Kofun-period burials, sometimes interpreted as a marker of political recognition distributed through chiefly networks.
Iron ingot
Unworked iron material that functioned as a valuable, status-bearing commodity given the archipelago's reliance on imported iron and iron-working expertise.
Sue ware
Hard, high-fired gray stoneware introduced from the Korean Peninsula during the Kofun period, produced using wheel-throwing and advanced kiln technology.
Haji ware
A continuation of earlier reddish, lower-fired earthenware pottery traditions that persisted alongside the newer Sue ware technology during the Kofun period.
Mounted warrior
A horse-riding fighter equipped with saddle, bit, and related equipment; evidence for this military role becomes prominent in the archipelago from the later fourth century onward.
Stirrup
A horse-riding equipment component appearing in the archaeological record from the later fourth and fifth centuries, associated with the spread of mounted-warrior culture from the Korean Peninsula.
Corridor-style chamber
A later and more elaborate form of stone burial chamber, accessed via a corridor, appearing in some larger and later kofun.
Regional chief
A leader of a local or regional chiefly center whose status is reflected archaeologically in tomb scale and grave-good assemblage relative to other chiefs of the period.
Paramount chief
A term some archaeologists use to describe Kinai-region chiefly lineages whose tombs and resources consistently exceeded those of other regional centers, without necessarily implying formal centralized administrative authority.
Chiefly hierarchy
A layered structure of relative elite status and resource command, visible archaeologically through differences in tomb scale, construction, and grave goods.
Prestige-good network
A system of exchange in valuable objects, such as mirrors or iron goods, used to establish and display status relationships among chiefs.
Emulation
The adoption of a prestigious cultural or architectural form by a community or elite without this adoption necessarily implying political subordination to the form's originators.
Alliance network
A set of relationships among formally independent chiefs based on marriage, exchange, or mutual recognition rather than direct political control.
Kinai region
The area surrounding the Nara Basin and Osaka Plain, associated with the strongest sustained concentration of the largest kofun across the period.
Kibi
A regional center (present-day Okayama area) with its own substantial and partly independent kofun tradition, important evidence against simple models of total Kinai control.
Peninsular migration
The movement of people, including craft and administrative specialists, from the Korean Peninsula into the archipelago during the Kofun period.
Toraijin
A term used in Japanese historical tradition for migrants, especially craft and administrative specialists, who "crossed over" from the Korean Peninsula and contributed technical and administrative knowledge to archipelago society.

Major Sites, Peoples, and Features

Identification & Significance

Hashihaka Kofun
An early, large keyhole-shaped tomb in the Nara Basin, dated to the mid-to-late third century CE, widely used to mark the conventional beginning of the Kofun period; popularly but unreliably associated with Himiko.
Miwa/Yamato area (Nara Basin)
The geographic cluster of the earliest and among the largest kofun, regarded as the earliest center of concentrated elite power in the Kofun archaeological record.
Mozu-Furuichi tomb group
A concentration of extremely large fifth-century kofun on the Osaka Plain, representing the peak of monumental tomb construction; inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2019.
Kibi region
A regional center with substantial and partly independent kofun of its own, complicating simple models of centralized Kinai political control.
Northern Kyushu
A region with long-standing continental contact and continued strong connections to the Korean Peninsula throughout the Kofun period.
Izumo region
A region with distinctive local burial traditions persisting alongside partial adoption of keyhole tomb forms.
Baekje, Silla, Gaya, Goguryeo
Korean Peninsula polities whose diplomatic, military, and economic relationships with archipelago elites shaped much of the Kofun period's technological and political development.
Toraijin (migrant specialists)
Craft and administrative specialists who migrated from the Korean Peninsula, contributing ironworking, ceramic, equestrian, and record-keeping technology to archipelago society.
Kojiki and Nihon Shoki
Eighth-century court chronicles containing genealogies and narratives later, and unreliably, used to identify specific rulers with specific kofun; treated with source-critical caution.
Imperial Household Agency
The modern Japanese government body that administers many large kofun designated as imperial-associated sites, restricting full archaeological excavation of several of the period's most significant tombs.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the story of the archipelago's political development from the externally documented, textually fragmentary world of third-century Wa examined in Chapter 6 into the richly documented, but politically ambiguous, archaeological world of the Kofun age. It has shown that even without contemporary written sources produced within the archipelago itself, historians and archaeologists can reconstruct a genuine and evidence-based account of growing elite power, widening interregional networks, and transformative technological change — while remaining honest about how much, and how little, this evidence can establish about specific political institutions and relationships.

The chapter's sustained refusal to declare a premature answer to the question of centralized political control matters beyond its immediate archaeological subject. It models a historical discipline this textbook will return to repeatedly: the responsibility to let evidence determine the confidence of a claim, rather than allowing a satisfying or dramatic narrative to outrun what the evidence can actually support. Students should carry this lesson of interpretive restraint into Chapter 8, where additional evidence — some archaeological, some textual — will allow for more confident, though still carefully qualified, claims about the emergence of the Yamato polity.

The chapter has also reinforced an important theme carried forward from earlier chapters: the archipelago's historical development cannot be understood in isolation from its connections to the wider East Asian world. The technologies, migrants, and political relationships connecting the archipelago to the Korean Peninsula during this period were not peripheral details but central forces shaping the military, technological, and political transformations this chapter has traced, a pattern that will recur, in new forms, throughout the textbook's treatment of later periods.

Students who understand the Kofun period as a genuine field of ongoing archaeological inquiry — marked by real evidence, real interpretive disagreement, and a disciplined refusal to overstate what monumental tombs alone can prove — are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 8, where the more institutionally focused evidence for the Yamato polity's emergence builds directly upon the widening chiefly hierarchy this chapter has described.
