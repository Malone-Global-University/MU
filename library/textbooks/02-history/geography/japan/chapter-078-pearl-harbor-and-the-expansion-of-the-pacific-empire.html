Pearl Harbor and the Expansion of the Pacific Empire

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Shortly after midnight on December 8, 1941, local time, Japanese transports off the coast of northeastern Malaya began landing troops near Kota Bharu. The landings met resistance, and the fighting that followed opened a war that would be fought across more than a third of the globe's surface, from the Aleutian Islands to the Indian frontier and from Manchuria to the Solomon Islands.

The Malayan landings are a useful way into this chapter because they correct a common picture. In popular memory the Pacific War begins with the carrier attack on Pearl Harbor. By the clock, the first landings at Kota Bharu came earlier, and the date difference arises from the International Date Line: it was December 7 in Hawaii when it was December 8 in Malaya, Hong Kong, the Philippines, and Japan. The attack on the American fleet was one element of a single plan that struck British, Dutch, and American possessions within a few hours. This chapter uses local dates throughout and avoids implying that every offensive followed Pearl Harbor in sequence.

The assigned title of this chapter, from the Authoritative Outline, is Pearl Harbor and the Expansion of the Pacific Empire. Its purpose is to explain how Japan's war, already stalemated in China (Chapter 76) and supported by a mobilized home society (Chapter 77), became a war against the United States, the British Empire, and the Netherlands; how Japanese forces conquered much of Southeast Asia and the western Pacific within about five months; and how that empire, vast on the map, proved impossible to supply, defend, or legitimize. The chapter follows three connected processes: the decision to seize a resource-rich southern empire while attempting to neutralize Western military opposition; the conquest itself and its effects on colonial societies and nationalist movements; and the changing military balance from 1942 to 1945.

Two simplifications deserve resistance. The first holds that Japan was driven to war by an oil embargo and had no choice. Economic pressure, the China war, and strategic calculation narrowed Japan's options, but they did not make war inevitable; Japan's own leaders, civilian and military, authorized the offensive after a long debate in which alternatives were available and sometimes argued. The second holds that Japan was doomed from the moment of Pearl Harbor. Japan's early victories rested on real operational skill, coordination, and Allied weakness. They did not show that Japan could win a long war against the United States, but the later outcome was shaped by campaigns, logistics, intelligence, and decisions that remained open for some time.

The societies of Southeast Asia are not merely the setting for these campaigns. Malaya's Chinese, Malay, and Indian communities, Filipinos, Indonesians, Burmese, Thais, Vietnamese, Pacific Islanders, and Papuans each experienced conquest and occupation differently. Japan proclaimed that it was liberating Asia from Western imperialism. Some nationalists saw opportunity in the collapse of European rule, some collaborated, some resisted, and many tried to survive; and the chapter takes care not to collapse these responses into a single category. Independence was not granted by Japan; the movements concerned had longer histories and their own aims.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and unusually partisan. It includes Japanese diplomatic and military records, notably the diplomatic documents held by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records (JACAR) and the materials of the National Diet Library; American and British official histories and action reports; Australian, Dutch, and Indian records; the National Archives of Singapore's occupation records and oral histories; memoirs by participants such as Ba Maw; contemporary newspapers and propaganda; and statistics on ships, aircraft, and petroleum compiled by different agencies using different definitions. Wartime claims about enemy losses were often inflated on every side, and the chapter keeps contemporary claims distinct from conclusions supported by later research. Figures are approximations unless stated otherwise.

The chapter's boundaries matter. Atrocities and the treatment of civilians and prisoners, including the Sook Ching massacres, the Bataan Death March, forced labor, and the destruction of Manila, are acknowledged here where necessary to explain the campaigns and their consequences, while their systematic treatment belongs to Chapter 79. The material experience of the Japanese home front, the Battle of Okinawa, strategic bombing, and the atomic bombs belong to Chapter 80; the surrender and its aftermath belong to Chapter 81. Chapter 78 ends with Japan's overseas position in collapse in 1945.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the China war, Indochina occupations, American, British, and Dutch economic measures, and Japan's oil dependence shaped, without determining, the decision for war.
  2. Trace the sequence of Imperial Conferences and cabinet changes from July to December 1941 and distinguish the roles of the army, navy, foreign ministry, and emperor.
  3. Reconstruct the opening offensive using local dates, and explain why it spanned so wide a geographic area.
  4. Analyze the reasons for Japanese success in Malaya, the Philippines, the Netherlands East Indies, and Burma, including air power, mobility, planning, and Allied weakness.
  5. Distinguish capturing resources from delivering them, using petroleum and shipping as the principal case.
  6. Compare the political experiences of Indonesia, Burma, the Philippines, Malaya, Thailand, and Indochina under Japanese power.
  7. Evaluate the claims of the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere against occupation practice.
  8. Assess Coral Sea, Midway, Guadalcanal, and New Guinea as stages in a changing balance, avoiding both inevitability and single-battle explanations.
  9. Explain the Allied strategies of island-hopping, bypassing, and maritime blockade and why isolated garrisons could remain intact yet ineffective.
  10. Interpret the failure of the Imphal–Kohima offensive in relation to supply, Indian nationalism, and the Burma front.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for Japan's defeat and the legacies of occupation for postwar Southeast Asia.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits, and identify whose perspectives are missing.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Japan's leaders choose war against the United States, Britain, and the Netherlands in 1941 when many of them doubted Japan could win a long war?
  2. How did resource strategy, the China war, imperial ideology, and inter-service politics combine in the decision, and which carried the most weight?
  3. Why did Japanese forces succeed so rapidly in 1941–1942, and what does that success reveal about the Allied defenses it overcame?
  4. What is the difference between occupying territory and securing an empire, and how did the difference shape Japan's position after 1942?
  5. How did Southeast Asian nationalists, Chinese and Indian communities, Pacific Islanders, and colonial subjects respond to Japanese conquest, and why do single labels such as "collaboration" distort their choices?
  6. Were Coral Sea and Midway turning points in a strict sense, and what other processes, such as attrition at Guadalcanal and shipping losses, mattered as much?
  7. Why could Japan retain large armies and territories in 1945 while losing the ability to connect and supply them?
  8. What kinds of evidence allow historians to separate wartime claims from verified outcomes, and where does the evidence remain contested?

Historical Context

Chapters 74 through 77 established the setting. After the Manchurian Incident of 1931 and the establishment of Manchukuo, Japan's army and navy gained wide independence from civilian government, and the full-scale war in China that began in 1937 consumed hundreds of thousands of troops without producing a political settlement (Chapter 76). Japan's government responded with a mobilized economy and a state-directed society (Chapter 77). By 1940 Japan was committed to a war it could neither end nor win through the methods it had used, and its leaders increasingly looked for ways to cut off external support for China and to secure resources that would lessen dependence on potentially hostile suppliers.

The international situation shifted sharply in 1939–1940. Germany's victories in Europe, culminating in the fall of France and the Netherlands in 1940, left French Indochina and the Netherlands East Indies under metropolitan governments that could not reinforce them, and left Britain fighting for survival. The Tripartite Pact of September 1940 linked Japan to Germany and Italy, and the Neutrality Pact with the Soviet Union in April 1941 secured Japan's northern flank. Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 changed the calculations again, as Japanese leaders debated whether to strike north, south, or both. The United States, still formally neutral, increased its assistance to China and Britain and used export controls as instruments of pressure.

Japan's economy depended on imports for most of its petroleum, as well as for iron ore, scrap, rubber, tin, bauxite, and other materials. The Netherlands East Indies, Malaya, and Burma were the nearest large sources of petroleum, rubber, tin, and rice. The southern region was also commercially and strategically connected: its sea routes joined the Indian Ocean to the Pacific, and its British, Dutch, French, and American possessions lay across Japan's routes to those resources. Japan's navy, built over decades with the United States as the presumed opponent, planned for a decisive fleet engagement in the western Pacific, a concept examined below for its limits.

Finally, the societies of Southeast Asia were under colonial rule, but not in uniform ways. British Malaya and Burma, the Netherlands East Indies, French Indochina, and the American-administered Philippines each had different political structures, nationalist movements, and economic orders. Thailand alone was formally independent. Nationalist organizations existed in all of them, from the Indonesian movement associated with Sukarno and Hatta to the Burmese Thakin movement and Philippine politics under the Commonwealth, which had been promised independence by 1946. The Japanese arrival would intersect with these histories, not begin them.

Section 78.1 · Division I — The Decision for War and the Opening Offensive, 1940–1941

The Southern Advance: China, Resources, and Japan's Strategic Dilemma

The idea of a southward advance (nanshin) had long circulated in Japanese naval and commercial circles, but it became policy only when the war in China and the war in Europe interacted. By 1940 the China war had settled into occupation of the major cities and rail lines, guerrilla and regular resistance, and a Nationalist government in Chongqing that continued to receive supplies through the Burma Road and through French Indochina. Japanese leaders believed that cutting those routes might finally force a settlement. In September 1940, after pressure on the Vichy French authorities, Japanese troops entered northern Indochina. In the same month Japan signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy, a step intended to deter the United States but one that persuaded Washington that Japan had committed itself to the Axis. Foreign Minister Matsuoka Yōsuke had in August 1940 given public form to a larger aim, a "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere" under Japanese leadership, a phrase that in practice meant a political and economic order in which Japan, not the European powers, would be dominant.

The summer of 1941 was the decisive period. In April Japan signed a neutrality pact with the Soviet Union, and when Germany attacked the Soviet Union in June, an Imperial Conference on July 2 adopted a policy of continued advance to the south while preparing for contingencies in the north, and accepting that this might lead to war with Britain and the United States. In late July Japanese forces moved into southern French Indochina, placing airfields and naval bases within range of Malaya, Singapore, and the Philippines. The American response was to freeze Japanese assets on July 25–26, with Britain and the Netherlands following, and by early August oil exports from the United States to Japan had effectively stopped. The United States had supplied roughly four-fifths of Japan's petroleum imports, so the measure was a serious blow, even though Japan held substantial stocks.

How serious it was is the heart of the strategic dilemma. Japan's navy estimated that its oil reserves, drawn down by fleet operations and the China war, would last perhaps a year and a half to two years in wartime, and that each month of delay would worsen its position relative to a United States that was beginning large-scale rearmament. The Netherlands East Indies could supply oil, but its government had been negotiating with Japan reluctantly and could be expected to align with Washington. Japanese planners thus perceived a closing window: act soon, or accept permanent dependence and the possible loss of the capacity for war. This reasoning was powerful, but it rested on assumptions. It assumed that the China war could not be ended on terms short of the Japanese position, that the United States would not accept a Japanese sphere in Asia, and that a limited conflict was unlikely to be possible if Japan took the Indies and Malaya, since the United States would be threatened on its flank in the Philippines.

Economic calculation was joined to political ambition. The "new order" in East Asia, announced in 1938 and elaborated afterward, defined a goal larger than resources: an imperial order in which Japan's position in China, Manchukuo, Korea, and Taiwan would be secured and extended. Withdrawing from China, which Washington increasingly demanded, would have meant abandoning what the army regarded as the fruit of years of sacrifice and, in the view of many officers, the only justification of those sacrifices. The dilemma was therefore not simply between oil and no oil. It was between a compromise requiring retreat from China and a gamble to secure a southern empire that would let Japan continue the China war and resist American pressure. Chapter 76 explained why the first path was politically unacceptable to the army; the second was chosen.

Nothing in this situation made the choice for war inevitable. A faction of the navy doubted that Japan could prevail over the United States in a long war, civilian officials such as Foreign Minister Tōgō Shigenori later pressed for compromise, and the emperor's court expressed repeated unease. But the structure of Japanese decision-making, in which army and navy leaders each held effective vetoes over cabinet decisions and neither would accept a policy that surrendered what it regarded as vital, meant that diplomacy had to meet military demands that the United States would not accept. That structural fact, and not an irresistible economic necessity, explains why the decision unfolded as it did.

Historical Vocabulary
Strategy and Empire: Terms Used in This Chapter

Nanshin ("southern advance") named the long-debated strategy of expanding toward Southeast Asia; hokushin ("northern advance") named the alternative of striking the Soviet Union. Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere (Dai Tōa Kyōeiken) was the official name for the political and economic order Japan proposed for East Asia and Southeast Asia; it combined genuine anti-Western appeals with the assumption of Japanese leadership. Strategic perimeter refers to the defensive line of islands and bases Japanese planners intended to hold after conquest, and the later Absolute National Defense Sphere (zettai kokubōken), adopted in 1943, was a contracted line that Japan was supposed to hold at all costs. Carrier warfare is combat in which aircraft launched from aircraft carriers attack opposing fleets at distances beyond gun range, making reconnaissance, aircrew training, and deck operations decisive. Blockade is the interdiction of an enemy's seaborne supply, and bypassing is the decision to isolate a fortified position rather than assault it.

These terms matter because modern English words can mislead. "Co-Prosperity" implied mutuality that the occupation did not deliver. "Perimeter" suggests a fixed wall, whereas the actual line depended on supply by sea and air that could be cut. Students should treat each term as describing an official concept, with its historical performance a separate question.

Section 78.2 · Division I

Diplomacy, Imperial Conferences, and the Decision for War in 1941

Japanese decision-making in 1941 ran through two bodies. The Liaison Conference brought together the cabinet's principal ministers and the army and navy chiefs of staff and worked out policy in practice. Its decisions on matters of war and peace were then presented at an Imperial Conference, a formal meeting in the emperor's presence, which ratified rather than debated the policy. The emperor's role at such meetings was to hear and sanction; by convention he did not initiate decisions. How much this convention constrained Hirohito, and how much he exercised influence behind the scenes, is a continuing subject of scholarly debate, but the record shows that he asked probing questions of military leaders before each decision and that his formal approval was given.

Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro pursued negotiations with Washington through Ambassador Nomura Kichisaburō in the spring and summer, and in August proposed a direct meeting with President Roosevelt. The United States insisted that Japan commit in advance to principles, including respect for China's territorial integrity, and the proposed summit did not occur. On September 6, 1941, an Imperial Conference adopted a policy that made war preparations to be completed in late October and stated that, if diplomacy offered no prospect of success by early October, Japan would decide on war. Hirohito, in an episode documented in several records, indicated his unease by reciting a poem by his grandfather, the Meiji emperor, that expressed a hope for peace among nations. The words themselves are better paraphrased than quoted, since versions differ, but the incident is well attested and shows that the throne's concern was visible without becoming a veto.

The deadline passed without agreement, and the army insisted that troops remain in China. Konoe resigned on October 16. Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal Kidō Kōichi proposed Tōjō Hideki, the army minister, to succeed him on the reasoning that the army's leader could, as prime minister, be asked to reconsider the September decision, which Hirohito reportedly indicated he wished to be reviewed. Tōjō became prime minister on October 18 and, in addition to the army portfolio, held the home affairs ministry for a time. A series of Liaison Conferences in late October and early November reviewed the policy. The navy, now represented by Navy Minister Shimada Shigetarō and Chief of Staff Nagano Osami, stated that the oil position made delay disadvantageous, and the army maintained that the China position could not be abandoned.

The Imperial Conference of November 5 adopted a two-track policy. Plan A was a broader proposal to Washington, and Plan B, a limited provisional arrangement, offered a withdrawal from southern Indochina in return for the resumption of American oil supplies and a pledge not to obstruct the settlement of the China war. Military preparations were to be completed by early December, and if diplomacy failed by a deadline, set at the start of December, war would begin. Ambassador Nomura was joined in Washington by Kurusu Saburō, and Plan B was presented on November 20. American leaders, who had been reading Japan's diplomatic ciphers through the Magic intercepts, knew that a deadline existed. Washington considered a modus vivendi along the lines of Plan B but dropped it, in part because Chinese and British objections suggested it would sacrifice China and in part because of reports of Japanese convoys moving south.

On November 26 the American side delivered what is known as the Hull Note, a statement of principles proposed as a basis for settlement. It called for the withdrawal of Japanese forces from China and Indochina and for Japan to cease recognizing any Chinese regime except Chiang Kai-shek's, along with other proposals on nondiscrimination in trade. Japanese leaders, including Tōgō, read the note as an ultimatum that demanded the abandonment of Japan's continental gains. Historians point out, however, that the note was not a declaration of war or a formal ultimatum; it set out the American position in a negotiation; and that Japan's fleet, the Kidō Butai, had already left its northern anchorage on the same day, with instructions that it could return if diplomacy succeeded. The Hull Note did not cause the decision for war. It confirmed that the United States would not accept the terms Japan's leaders considered necessary, which left them to choose between those terms and a war.

On December 1 an Imperial Conference formally approved war against the United States, Britain, and the Netherlands. The discussions that preceded it show strategic assumptions that deserve examination. Planners did not expect to defeat the United States in a conventional sense. A policy statement of mid-November outlined a strategy of securing the southern resource area, destroying British and Dutch positions, building a defensive perimeter, and pressing the war until the opponents lost the will to continue, with the hope that Germany's progress in Europe would help bring a settlement. Admiral Yamamoto reportedly told Konoe that he could run wild for six months to a year and had no confidence beyond that. The sources record these views in memoirs written after the event, so the precise wording remains uncertain, but they agree on the general judgment. The plan was thus an attempt to create conditions for a negotiated end to a war that the planners themselves doubted they could win outright.

It is also necessary to state what the decision was not. The Hull Note did not compel it; the American oil embargo and asset freeze constrained Japan's options but did not determine them, since the army's refusal to leave China was a Japanese choice; and the fleet's departure on November 26 shows that preparations were advanced before the diplomatic exchange concluded. At the same time, the American side was not passive. Its demands about China were firm, its economic pressure was deliberate, and its leaders understood that the measures they took risked war. Responsibility for the decision to attack lies with Japan's leaders, while the diplomatic context was shaped by all the parties.

Section 78.3 · Division I

Pearl Harbor, December 7, 1941: Strategy, Surprise, and Consequences

The Pearl Harbor operation was the product of Admiral Yamamoto Isoroku's judgment that Japan's only chance in a war with the United States was to deliver a blow that would cripple the American Pacific Fleet and give Japan time to complete its conquests and organize its defenses. Yamamoto had studied the United States, had served as an attaché in Washington, and doubted that Japan could win a prolonged war. He insisted on the plan against the objections of some naval staff officers who thought it too risky and preferred to wait for the American fleet to come westward. The operation was developed with the help of officers such as Genda Minoru, who stressed concentration of carrier aircraft, and it drew lessons from the British carrier attack at Taranto in 1940, including the adaptation of aerial torpedoes for shallow water.

The strike force of six carriers (Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū, Hiryū, Shōkaku, and Zuikaku) under Vice Admiral Nagumo Chūichi left the Kuril Islands on November 26, Tokyo time, sailed a northern route to avoid shipping, and on the morning of December 7 launched about 350 aircraft in two waves. The attack began shortly before eight o'clock Hawaii time. American battleships moored along Ford Island were the principal targets. Four, including Arizona and Oklahoma, were sunk or capsized, others were damaged, and about 190 American aircraft were destroyed. Roughly 2,400 Americans were killed, most of them military personnel and a small number civilians. Japanese losses were comparatively light, about two dozen aircraft in the main attack and five midget submarines that had been sent in with little effect.

The raid was a tactical success and a strategic problem. The three American fleet carriers in the Pacific were not in port, since two were delivering aircraft to outlying islands, so the force that would prove decisive in the following years was untouched. The attackers did not strike the fuel storage tanks, repair shops, dry docks, or submarine base, installations that allowed the fleet to return to operations quickly. Nagumo chose not to launch a third wave, a decision that remains debated; his supporters point to the risks to his carriers and the uncertain location of the American carriers, and his critics point to the missed opportunity against the shore infrastructure. Of the battleships, all but Arizona and Oklahoma (and the old target ship Utah) eventually returned to service. The loss of the battleships was also less decisive than prewar assumptions implied, because carriers and submarines, not battleships, would carry the American offensive.

The diplomatic circumstances sharpened American outrage. Japan had instructed its embassy in Washington to deliver a long message ending negotiations shortly before the attack, but delays in decoding and typing meant that it reached the secretary of state after the attack had begun. Historians agree that the attack was launched without a formal declaration of war preceding it, and that Japan's leaders had intended notification to precede the strike by a short interval; the delay was the result of embassy procedure and not of a decision to conceal. Whatever the intention, the effect in the United States was to make the attack appear treacherous, and the Congress declared war on Japan on December 8. Germany and Italy declared war on the United States on December 11, a step that the Tripartite Pact did not require, since it applied only to defense against an attack by a power not already at war, and that joined the Pacific conflict to the war in Europe.

Yamamoto's calculation had been that a decisive blow would produce time and, perhaps, a political shift in the United States. The opposite result followed. The attack unified American opinion, committed the United States to a war of unconditional aims, and put it on a course to mobilize an industrial capacity far larger than Japan's. It would be an oversimplification to say that the attack made Japan's defeat certain, since the outcome depended on events of 1942–1944. But it is fair to say that Japan's leaders had staked the national future on a sequence of assumptions, that the Americans would not fight on after early defeats, that Germany would hold Britain and the Soviet Union, and that a defensive perimeter could be held long enough, none of which was secure.

Section 78.4 · Division I

A Coordinated Offensive: Hong Kong, Guam, Wake, Thailand, and the Western Pacific

The opening offensive was designed as a single operation executed over thousands of kilometers. By local dates, the landings at Kota Bharu in northern Malaya began shortly after midnight on December 8, and the Pearl Harbor strike followed roughly an hour and a half later by the clock, on the morning of December 7 in Hawaii. Within hours on December 8 Japanese aircraft struck American airfields on Luzon, Japanese troops crossed into Thailand and landed at Singora and Patani for the advance into Malaya and Burma, and the army attacked Hong Kong from the mainland. Guam, Wake, and the Gilbert Islands were attacked, and the navy sent forces toward Malaya and the Netherlands East Indies. The geographical breadth was a product of the strategy: the resource regions of the south, the sea lanes connecting them, and the American, British, and Dutch bases that could threaten them lay across an immense arc, and Japan intended to seize them nearly simultaneously before the Allies could reinforce.

Coordination between the army and the navy was real but required negotiation and mutual dependence. The army provided the troops for Malaya, the Philippines, and the other land campaigns, and the navy provided the carrier and land-based naval air power and the escort of the transports. The two services nonetheless maintained separate planning cells, separate aircraft programs, and separate priorities. The plan succeeded in the opening phase because the objectives were clear, the forces were trained, and the Allied defenses were thin. The same separation would later prove a weakness when the war demanded joint decisions about shipping, aircraft, and reinforcement.

At Hong Kong, the British, Canadian, and Indian garrison, supported by the Hong Kong Volunteer Defence Corps, held out for about two and a half weeks against the Japanese 23rd Army, operating from Guangdong, before surrender on December 25. Hospitals and civilians were among the sites of atrocities in the days that followed, including the killing of patients and staff at a hospital at Stanley, a subject treated in Chapter 79. Guam, lightly garrisoned and isolated, fell on December 10. At Wake Island, American Marines and civilian contractors repulsed an initial landing on December 11, sinking two Japanese destroyers, before a larger attack succeeded on December 23. The episode became a symbol in the United States, but strategically the island, like Guam, had little means of resistance. In the Gilbert Islands, Japanese forces occupied Makin and Tarawa, an act with later consequences when Americans returned in 1943.

In Thailand, the situation differed. Japanese troops landed on the morning of December 8, and after a few hours of fighting at several points, the Thai government under Prime Minister Plaek Phibunsongkhram agreed to permit passage. On December 21 Thailand and Japan signed an alliance, and in January 1942 Thailand declared war on Britain and the United States. The decision reflected Phibun's calculation about relative power and his nationalist aim of recovering territory lost to British and French colonial arrangements, though the Thai ambassador in Washington, Seni Pramoj, declined to deliver the declaration, and a Free Thai movement later developed. Thailand's case is a reminder that the Japanese advance involved negotiation as well as force, and that Thai leaders preserved a form of independence at the price of alliance.

By the end of December Japan had struck six or seven major objectives at once, with an economy of force that few contemporaries had anticipated. The success owed much to prewar preparation, including air bases in Indochina and Taiwan that placed Japanese fighters and bombers within range of Malaya and the Philippines, and to Allied weakness: Britain's forces in Asia were thin because of commitments in the Middle East and Europe, the Dutch had little more than colonial forces, and the American garrison in the Philippines was incompletely mobilized. Japan's offensive had defeated the opening line of its opponents. What it had not yet done was to demonstrate that it could hold the vast area it was beginning to occupy. The next division examines the campaigns of conquest and the political world they created.

Section 78.5 · Division II — The Conquest of Southeast Asia and the Construction of a Southern Empire

The Malayan Campaign and the Fall of Singapore, 1941–1942

The conquest of Malaya was the most consequential of Japan's early campaigns, because it destroyed Britain's principal military position in Asia and shattered the prestige of European colonial rule. The Japanese 25th Army under Lieutenant General Yamashita Tomoyuki, numbering perhaps 30,000 combat troops with air and naval support, faced a British Commonwealth force that was larger on paper, drawn from British, Indian, Australian, and local units, but that was inexperienced in many units, poorly equipped with modern aircraft, and uncertain in its doctrine. The campaign was waged along a peninsula of roughly 1,100 kilometers, with a spine of jungle-covered hills and a road network along the west coast that favored an attacker able to outflank defensive positions.

The landings at Singora and Patani in Thailand and at Kota Bharu on December 8 placed Japanese forces on both coasts. Air superiority came quickly: Japanese army and navy aircraft, flying from Indochina and from captured airfields, destroyed or drove off the obsolete types, such as the Brewster Buffalo, that the Royal Air Force had in Malaya, and by mid-December much of the northern air strength was lost. On December 10, Japanese naval aircraft from Indochina sank the battleship HMS Prince of Wales and the battle cruiser Repulse, which had sailed from Singapore without air cover to challenge the landings. The sinking of two capital ships by land-based aircraft in open water demonstrated what Pearl Harbor had already suggested, that the battleship's era was passing, and it deprived Britain of any remaining naval deterrent in the region.

On land, Japanese tactics emphasized speed and infiltration. Infantry advanced on bicycles and on the roads, using captured vehicles, with light tanks that British commanders had not expected to find effective in jungle terrain. Rather than assault prepared positions, Japanese troops flanked them through plantations and mangrove, and made short amphibious landings along the west coast to cut British lines of retreat. British and Indian units fought hard in places, including Jitra, Kampar, and Slim River, but a pattern of withdrawal followed, with demoralization compounded by the lack of air cover and by confusion in command. By the end of January the Allied forces had fallen back across the Johore Strait to Singapore Island, and the causeway was breached on January 31.

The assault on Singapore began on the night of February 8, with Japanese troops crossing the strait in the northwest. Within a week the defenders had been pushed back toward the city, the water supply was threatened, and the British commander, Lieutenant General Arthur Percival, surrendered on February 15, 1942. Some 80,000 or more troops became prisoners, part of a larger total of well over 100,000 Allied prisoners across Malaya and Singapore, a catastrophe that Prime Minister Winston Churchill described as among Britain's worst. Popular accounts claim that the fortress guns faced the wrong way, but the guns could fire inland, and the more accurate explanation is that the defense was weakened by command disunity, poor intelligence, inadequate air and tank forces, and the fact that Singapore had never been prepared for a siege from the landward side. Some accounts also stress that Yamashita's supplies were thin at the end and that he pressed for rapid surrender; historians continue to examine how far this reflected a gamble or bluff.

Singapore's strategic importance had been as the keystone of Britain's imperial defense in Asia, a naval base and commercial entrepôt of about three-quarters of a million people, mostly of Chinese origin, with Malay, Indian, and European communities. Its fall had a political consequence beyond the military one. Asian subjects of European empires had seen an Asian power defeat a European garrison, and for many in the colonial world, including those who feared the Japanese, the aura of European invincibility was broken. The Japanese renamed the city Syonan-tō, "Light of the South," adopted the Japanese calendar and time zone, and began an occupation that would last three and a half years.

The occupation began with violence directed at the Chinese community. In the weeks after the surrender, the Japanese military carried out the Sook Ching ("purge through cleansing") operations, screening Chinese men and killing many who were judged hostile, in part because of Chinese support for the war against Japan in China and for relief funds to the Nationalist government. The National Museum of Singapore’s education guide reports Japanese estimates of approximately 5,000–6,000 victims, alongside unofficial estimates of 20,000–30,000 for the Singapore operation. Other figures refer to broader geographic scopes or different records and must not be treated as interchangeable. Complete victim registers do not survive; Chapter 79 examines the evidentiary problems in depth. The events shaped Chinese attitudes toward Japanese rule for the rest of the war, and they contributed to the support that the Malayan Communist Party-led Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army would later receive from Chinese communities.

Section 78.6 · Division II

The Philippines Campaign: Bataan, Corregidor, and American Defeat

The Philippines was an American territory in transition. Under the Tydings–McDuffie Act of 1934, it was a self-governing Commonwealth, led by President Manuel Quezon, with independence promised for 1946. In 1941 Douglas MacArthur, who had been military adviser to the Commonwealth, was recalled to American service as commander of United States Army Forces in the Far East (USAFFE), which incorporated the Philippine Army, then being built, along with American units and the Philippine Scouts. The force was substantial in numbers but incompletely trained and equipped. American planners had long assumed that the Philippines could not be held against a major attack and had prepared a plan to withdraw to the Bataan Peninsula and Corregidor Island and await relief, but MacArthur sought a forward defense of the beaches, which complicated the preparations.

On December 8, Japanese aircraft from Taiwan, delayed by fog, attacked Clark Field and other airfields. A significant part of the American air force, including B-17 bombers, was destroyed on the ground. After limited landings in northern Luzon and elsewhere, the main landing of the 14th Army under Lieutenant General Homma Masaharu came at Lingayen Gulf on December 22. MacArthur then ordered the withdrawal into Bataan under the earlier plan, and Manila was declared an open city on December 26. Japanese forces entered it on January 2, 1942. Quezon and his government retreated to Corregidor and later were evacuated, and the Philippine political leadership would be divided between those in exile, those who remained, and those who cooperated with Japanese authorities.

On Bataan, the American and Filipino forces, a majority of them Filipino, held for three months. Supplies of food were short from the start because of the late decision to withdraw, and malaria, dysentery, and malnutrition spread. Filipino soldiers and civilians were not auxiliaries of an American force; they were its majority and bore a large share of the losses. Japanese forces, themselves weakened by disease and casualties, paused to rebuild and renewed their offensive in April. MacArthur was ordered by Roosevelt to leave, and departed on March 11–12 for Australia, where he stated that he would return, leaving Lieutenant General Jonathan Wainwright in command. On April 9, Major General Edward King surrendered the forces on Bataan, about 75,000 men, and on May 6 Wainwright surrendered Corregidor and ordered the other forces in the Philippines to follow.

The surrender was followed by the Bataan Death March, the forced movement of prisoners, in stages and by various means, from the southern end of the peninsula toward a railhead and then to camps. Prisoners already weakened by hunger and disease were beaten, denied water, and killed, and deaths numbered in the thousands. Estimates range widely, from several hundred to several thousand Americans and a larger number of Filipinos, with uncertainty because of incomplete records and the difficulty of separating deaths on the march from deaths in the camps. Japanese commanders had underestimated the number of prisoners and had not arranged transport or supply, but the evidence also shows deliberate brutality. Chapter 79 returns to the treatment of prisoners; here it is enough to note that the event was among the first to expose the nature of Japanese military conduct to Allied publics.

Conquest did not end resistance. Some soldiers escaped into the hills, others formed guerrilla units, and communist-led peasant organizations in central Luzon created the Hukbalahap (the People's Anti-Japanese Army) in March 1942. Other guerrilla organizations were loyal to the United States, and some were more concerned with local power. The Japanese military administration controlled the towns and main roads but never the countryside, and the guerrilla war, together with Allied submarine contacts and radio communication, kept the Philippines in the conflict. The Japanese also found that their promise of liberation from American rule had less purchase than they had hoped, because many Filipinos associated the United States with the promise of independence, not with domination.

Section 78.7 · Division II

The Netherlands East Indies: Oil, Naval Warfare, and the Capture of Java

The Netherlands East Indies, the colony that became Indonesia, was the principal object of the southern advance. Its oilfields at Palembang in Sumatra and at Balikpapan and Tarakan in Borneo produced the petroleum that Japan lacked, and its other resources included rubber, tin, bauxite, and rice. The colony's defense rested on the Royal Netherlands East Indies Army (KNIL), whose soldiers were largely drawn from the local population under European officers, and a small navy, together with whatever aid Britain, Australia, and the United States could provide. After Germany's conquest of the Netherlands in 1940, the Dutch government in exile in London had little means to reinforce the colony.

Japanese operations proceeded from the north and east through a series of amphibious landings designed to capture airfields from which air cover could be extended. Tarakan fell on January 11–12, 1942, and Balikpapan on January 24, where a night attack by American destroyers sank several Japanese transports. Dutch forces destroyed or damaged the oil installations before withdrawing, but Japanese engineers later repaired many of them. On February 14, Japanese parachute troops landed near Palembang, and the city and its refineries were taken. By then the Allied command, set up in January as ABDA (American, British, Dutch, Australian) under General Sir Archibald Wavell, was already failing, as resources were insufficient and national interests diverged, and it was dissolved on February 25.

The decisive naval action was the Battle of the Java Sea on February 27, in which a Dutch-led Allied squadron of cruisers and destroyers under Rear Admiral Karel Doorman attempted to intercept the Japanese invasion convoys. The Allied force, which had not operated together before and lacked air cover, suffered heavy losses, including the Dutch cruisers De Ruyter and Java and Admiral Doorman himself, and the following night, in the Sunda Strait, the American cruiser Houston and the Australian cruiser Perth were sunk. Japanese forces landed on Java on February 28 and March 1. The Dutch commander capitulated on March 9, and the Allied surrender in Java and, soon afterward, in the other islands completed the conquest of the Indies in about three months.

Capturing the oilfields was not the same as delivering oil to Japan, and this distinction is central to the war's later course. Production recovered gradually, and in 1942 and 1943 Japan's imports of petroleum from the Indies rose, but never to the levels planners had projected. The scarcity of tankers was a persistent constraint, since Japan's tanker fleet was small and, as American submarines grew in effectiveness, its losses mounted. Refined products also required refineries and suitable crudes in the home islands, and the distances of the supply line, some 4,000 to 5,000 kilometers, made the whole system dependent on the safe passage of shipping. In 1944 and 1945, as sinkings accelerated, much of the southern oil that Japan had fought to control could no longer reach it, and fleet units had to be stationed near the oilfields instead of in the home waters where the fuel was needed. The gap between capture and use, rather than the capture itself, defined the limits of the southern strategy.

The conquest also divided the Indies between army and navy administration. The army governed Java and Sumatra, and the navy governed Borneo, Celebes, and the eastern islands, which gave different political climates in different regions. Dutch officials and many civilians were interned, in camps whose conditions are examined in Chapter 79, and the colonial state that had ruled the archipelago for centuries was gone almost overnight. For Indonesian nationalists who had been jailed or exiled by the Dutch, including Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta, this changed the political landscape fundamentally, and Section 78.9 returns to the choices they made.

Section 78.8 · Division II

Burma, Thailand, and the Strategic Threat to India

Burma had been separated from British India administratively in 1937, and its political life included a strong nationalist movement, among them the Thakin organization, whose young members included Aung San. Some Burmese nationalists, who regarded Britain as the main obstacle to independence, sought outside help, and in 1940–1941 a group of them, later known as the Thirty Comrades, were taken secretly to Hainan Island and trained by Japanese officers associated with a special organization led by Colonel Suzuki Keiji. They formed the nucleus of the Burma Independence Army (BIA), which entered Burma with the Japanese in 1942. Burma's strategic importance to Japan was twofold: the Burma Road, running from Lashio to Kunming, carried supplies to China, and Burma's rice, oil, and position on India's frontier gave it larger value.

The Japanese 15th Army under Lieutenant General Iida Shōjirō advanced from Thailand in January 1942. British, Indian, and Burmese forces were poorly equipped and had little armor or air support, and after reverses on the Sittang River and the loss of Moulmein, Rangoon fell on March 8. British forces then withdrew northward, and Chinese expeditionary forces sent to assist the defense fought battles of their own under the American General Joseph Stilwell. By late April the Japanese took Lashio, cutting the Burma Road, and by May the Allied forces had retreated into India, after a long and costly withdrawal that also included hundreds of thousands of Indian civilians who fled on foot, many dying of disease and exhaustion. The monsoon halted operations, and Japan did not pursue an invasion of India in 1942, a decision whose significance was clear only later.

The conquest of Burma had direct consequences for the China war covered in Chapter 76: it ended the overland supply route and led the Allies to rely on the air route over the Himalaya, known as the Hump, and eventually to build the Ledo Road from Assam. It also made India the Allies' base for further operations, so that the Burma frontier would become the line along which Japanese expansion was reversed. For India, the war brought the presence of a large army, the Bengal famine of 1943, and political tension over British demands for Indian support, which formed the setting in which Subhas Chandra Bose and others sought Japanese aid, a subject developed in Sections 78.9 and 78.17.

Thailand's position, unlike Burma's, rested on alliance. Its government under Phibun had accepted Japanese demands, and in 1943 Japan transferred to Thailand some territory in the Malay states and Shan states as a reward. Thailand's economy was nonetheless drawn into the war effort, as the Burma–Thailand railway built with Allied prisoners and Asian laborers, discussed in Chapter 79, crossed Thai territory. Opposition developed in the Free Thai movement, which cooperated with the Allies and which, along with the government's own maneuvering, helped Thailand avoid treatment as a defeated enemy at the war's end. Thailand's experience, a formally independent state that allied with Japan under duress and calculation, shows the range of relationships that the Japanese advance created.

Section 78.9 · Division II

The Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere: Occupation, Collaboration, and Asian Nationalism

Japan justified its advance in the language of Asian liberation. Official statements declared that the war was fought to free Asia from Western domination and to create a community of Asian peoples, and some Japanese officers and intellectuals sincerely believed this. But the policy documents adopted before the offensive were more specific and less idealistic. Plans for administering the occupied areas stressed three purposes: restoring order, acquiring resources critical to national defense, and ensuring that occupying forces could be self-supporting. Independence was not promised broadly; it was to be considered case by case, and its terms were subordinate to Japanese needs. In November 1942 the government created a Greater East Asia Ministry to manage relations with the region, a step that Foreign Minister Tōgō opposed as a reduction of his ministry's role and that led to his resignation.

The occupation was governed by military administration (gunsei), which differed by region. The army administered Malaya and Sumatra (together under the 25th Army for a time), Java, the Philippines, and Burma, and the navy administered Borneo, Celebes, and the eastern archipelago. Policy toward the populations combined control with cultural campaigns. Japanese-language instruction, ceremonies honoring the emperor, the adoption of Tokyo time and a new calendar, mass organizations, and propaganda by radio, film, and newspapers sought to bind the populations to the new order. Economic policy extracted resources, requisitioned rice and other goods, and issued military currency without backing, producing severe inflation. Forced labor, including the recruitment of rōmusha (laborers) in Java and the labor taken for railway and construction projects, is treated in Chapter 79, but its scale shaped the experience of occupation in every region.

Within this structure, nationalist movements responded in widely different ways, and the differences were rooted in prewar histories. In Indonesia, Sukarno, Hatta, and other leaders had been the targets of Dutch repression, and they reasoned that cooperation with the Japanese offered a platform to organize the population and, they hoped, to prepare independence. The army administration in Java established a mass organization, Putera, in 1943 under Indonesian leadership, and trained auxiliary forces, including the volunteer army PETA (Defenders of the Homeland) in October 1943, whose officers later formed a core of the republic's military. The navy-controlled regions were more repressive and slower to allow political activity. In September 1944, as the military situation worsened, Prime Minister Koiso Kuniaki announced that independence would be granted to the Indies in the future, and in 1945 an investigating committee began work on a constitution. The republic was proclaimed by Sukarno and Hatta on August 17, 1945, two days after Japan's announced surrender, an act that was theirs and that depended on the political organization built over decades as well as the wartime opportunity.

In Burma, Aung San and the BIA fought alongside the Japanese in 1942, but the army's treatment of the force, its undisciplined growth, and Japan's refusal to grant real independence created tension. In August 1943 Japan declared Burma independent under Ba Maw, who headed the state, with Aung San as defense minister and the Burma National Army as its military force. Independence was nominal, as Japanese advisers and military authority remained decisive, and a treaty of alliance bound Burma to Japan. Aung San and others began secret contact with the British and organized the Anti-Fascist Organisation, and on March 27, 1945, the Burma National Army turned against Japan, a date still commemorated in Myanmar. This trajectory shows how a nationalist leader could cooperate with an occupier to build an army and then use it against the occupier when the balance shifted.

In the Philippines, the Japanese established an Executive Commission under Jorge Vargas in 1942, and in October 1943 declared the Philippines independent under President José P. Laurel. The Republic was a Japanese creation, subordinate to military authority, and many Filipinos, including those who served in it, regarded it as an unavoidable accommodation, while others condemned it as collaboration. The guerrilla movements and the broad popular loyalty to the United States limited its legitimacy. After the war, questions of collaboration divided Philippine politics, an example of how the occupation's consequences extended beyond the war years. In Malaya, the Japanese gave preference to Malays in administration and encouraged some Malay nationalists, such as members of the Kesatuan Melayu Muda, while repressing the Chinese community; Indian communities were courted for the Indian Independence League and the Indian National Army, and the Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army, led by the Malayan Communist Party and supplied with British help in the later years, fought a guerrilla war. In French Indochina, Japan left the Vichy colonial administration in place until March 1945, when it overthrew it, declared nominal independence for Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos, and encountered the Việt Minh; a severe famine in northern Vietnam in 1944–1945, with estimates of deaths ranging from several hundred thousand to two million, was linked to wartime requisitions, transport disruption, and the policies of both Japanese and French authorities.

Indian nationalism entered the picture through Rash Behari Bose and the Indian Independence League, and through the Indian National Army (INA), first formed in 1942 from Indian prisoners taken at Singapore under Captain Mohan Singh and dissolved after disputes with the Japanese. Subhas Chandra Bose, who had left India and gone to Germany, traveled by submarine to Southeast Asia in 1943, revived the INA, and in October 1943 proclaimed a Provisional Government of Free India at Singapore. The INA recruited from prisoners and from the Indian population of Malaya and Burma, including civilians, and included a women's unit, the Rani of Jhansi Regiment. Historians debate how many INA members joined from conviction and how many from fear of the conditions of captivity, and the postwar trials of INA officers in India became a nationalist cause.

The contradiction at the center of the Co-Prosperity Sphere was between its proclaimed equality and its practice. Japanese officers and administrators held authority, Japanese goods and currency dominated, and the occupation often brought humiliation, including public slapping and the insistence on bowing to Japanese soldiers, that contradicted the language of Asian brotherhood. Japan's decision in May 1943 to retain Malaya, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and Celebes as parts of its own territory while granting the appearance of independence elsewhere reveals the priority of resource security over liberation. The conference of Asian leaders held in Tokyo in November 1943 displayed the sphere's ideals, but its limits were apparent in who was excluded, among them the Indonesian leaders, whose territory Japan intended to keep.

It is nonetheless important evidence, for three reasons. It shows how Japan sought to claim anti-imperial legitimacy, it shows the language that nationalist participants chose to adopt or to test, and it was later used by participants such as Bose to argue for the independence it promised. Section 78.10 · Division III — The Turning of the Pacific War, 1942–1943

The Coral Sea and Midway: The Limits of Japanese Carrier Superiority

By April 1942 Japan had achieved most of its initial objectives with remarkably few losses, and its leaders confronted the question of what to do next. The army favored consolidation, the navy's Combined Fleet staff favored continued offensives, and Admiral Yamamoto argued that the American carriers must be destroyed before American industrial power could be brought to bear. The Doolittle Raid of April 18, in which sixteen American bombers launched from the carrier Hornet struck Tokyo and other cities, did little physical damage but embarrassed the military and strengthened the case for extending the defensive perimeter eastward. Two operations followed: one directed toward Port Moresby in southeastern New Guinea, and a larger one against Midway Atoll.

The Battle of the Coral Sea, fought May 4–8, 1942, was the first naval battle in which the opposing fleets did not sight one another, since all attacks were delivered by aircraft. A Japanese force covering the invasion convoy for Port Moresby included the fleet carriers Shōkaku and Zuikaku and the light carrier Shōhō, and it was opposed by American task forces built around the carriers Lexington and Yorktown. American aircraft sank Shōhō on May 7; on May 8 both sides struck, and the Japanese aircraft sank Lexington and damaged Yorktown, while Shōkaku was heavily damaged and Zuikaku lost many aircraft and aircrew. The tactical result was arguably a Japanese advantage in tonnage sunk, but the strategic result favored the Allies, since the Port Moresby invasion by sea was called off, and the two Japanese fleet carriers were unavailable for Midway.

The Midway plan was complex. Yamamoto intended to seize the atoll, draw out the American carriers, and destroy them with the combined force, while a diversionary attack on the Aleutian Islands in the north would, in theory, split American attention. The plan divided Japanese forces over a huge area, with the First Air Fleet carriers under Nagumo (Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū, and Hiryū) separated from Yamamoto's battleships by hundreds of kilometers. It assumed that the American carriers would respond only after the atoll was attacked and that Japanese reconnaissance would find them in time. The American side, under Admiral Chester Nimitz, had the carriers Enterprise and Hornet under Rear Admiral Raymond Spruance and Yorktown under Rear Admiral Frank Fletcher, the last hurriedly repaired at Pearl Harbor in a matter of days.

Intelligence was an important factor but not the only one. A Navy codebreaking unit at Pearl Harbor under Commander Joseph Rochefort, working on the Japanese navy's main operational code, which was only partly readable, concluded that a target designated in intercepted messages as "AF" was Midway, and a test message about a water shortage at the atoll, sent in a code the Japanese were known to read, appeared to confirm it in Japanese traffic. This gave Nimitz an estimate of the time, direction, and approximate composition of the attack. But the codebreakers did not know every Japanese move, did not read the code completely, and could not tell American commanders where the Japanese carriers would be at a given hour. Victory still depended on what happened at sea.

On the morning of June 4, Nagumo's carriers launched a strike on Midway and kept a reserve armed for ships. A reconnaissance aircraft from the cruiser Tone, delayed in launching, reported American ships, and Nagumo, after a period of changing orders as the Midway strike returned and he debated what to do, rearmed his reserve aircraft with bombs for the ground target and then rearmed with torpedoes for ships. American torpedo bombers from the three carriers attacked in separate, uncoordinated groups and suffered devastating losses, almost none scoring hits, but they pulled Japanese fighters down to low altitude and kept the carriers maneuvering. At about 10:20 a.m., dive bombers from Enterprise and Yorktown, which had found the fleet after a search, arrived over the Japanese carriers while the decks were crowded with aircraft, fuel, and munitions. Within minutes Akagi, Kaga, and Sōryū were fatally damaged. Hiryū, which had been separated, launched strikes that crippled Yorktown, but American bombers found and destroyed it in the afternoon. Yorktown was later torpedoed by a Japanese submarine and sank on June 7. Yamamoto cancelled the invasion on June 5, and the cruiser Mikuma was sunk during the withdrawal.

Japan lost four fleet carriers, a heavy cruiser, about 250 aircraft, and approximately 3,000 men, while the United States lost one carrier, a destroyer, roughly 150 aircraft, and about 300 men. The battle ended the period of Japanese offensive initiative in the Pacific. But its significance requires care. The popular claim that Midway destroyed Japan's carrier pilots overstates the case: the more serious losses of experienced aircrews came later, in the Solomons and in the campaigns of 1943–1944. Japan still had other carriers and was building more, and the United States had only a handful of carriers in the Pacific in mid-1942. What Midway did was to remove a large part of Japan's most capable striking force at a moment when replacement was difficult, given the lengthy training of carrier aircrews and the demands of constructing carriers, and to demonstrate that carrier warfare was a contest of reconnaissance, concentration, and the management of flight decks, not a simple comparison of ships. It was a turning point in the sense that it removed Japan's initiative, not in the sense that Japan's defeat had become inevitable in June 1942.

Section 78.11 · Division III

Guadalcanal and the Solomon Islands: From Expansion to Attrition

In July 1942 Japanese engineers began building an airfield on Guadalcanal, an island in the southern Solomons, which would have allowed bombers to threaten the sea lanes between the United States and Australia. The Allies decided to seize it. On August 7, the 1st Marine Division under Major General Alexander Vandegrift landed on Guadalcanal and on Tulagi across the sound, capturing the airfield, which the Americans named Henderson Field. The Japanese response, which came from the army's 17th Army under General Hyakutake Harukichi and from the navy's forces at Rabaul and Truk, turned the island into the center of a six-month contest that was fought on land, at sea, and in the air.

The naval war around Guadalcanal was a series of night actions at which the Japanese navy, with its night-fighting training and powerful torpedoes, initially excelled. At Savo Island on the night of August 8–9, a Japanese cruiser force sank four Allied heavy cruisers in one of the worst American naval defeats, then withdrew without attacking the transports, a decision that preserved the Marines' supplies. In the following weeks and months there were carrier battles at the Eastern Solomons and Santa Cruz and a series of surface battles, culminating in the naval battle of November 12–15, in which two Japanese battleships, Hiei and Kirishima, and several transports were lost. Both sides lost many ships, and the waters off Guadalcanal became known as Ironbottom Sound.

On land, Japanese attacks were launched with forces insufficient for the task. The Ichiki Detachment, a few hundred men sent in the expectation of an easy recovery, was annihilated in August at the Tenaru River; the Kawaguchi Brigade attacked a ridge south of the airfield in September and failed; and in October a larger assault, again repulsed, ended hopes of a quick victory. Japanese reinforcements arrived mostly by destroyers on night runs, called by the Americans the "Tokyo Express," that could deliver men but not heavy equipment and adequate supplies. By day Allied aircraft operating from Henderson Field, the so-called Cactus Air Force, dominated the waters around the island. Japanese soldiers on the island, short of food and medicine, suffered from malaria and starvation, and the Japanese nickname for Guadalcanal, rendered as "Starvation Island," reflected the reality.

On December 31, 1942, Imperial General Headquarters approved withdrawal, and between February 1 and 7, 1943, destroyers evacuated about 10,000 men. Japanese losses on the island were on the order of 20,000 dead, most from disease and starvation as well as combat, while American losses were about 7,000 dead across the services. Japan also lost large numbers of aircraft and many experienced air and naval personnel. The costs fell on the navy's air arm, whose trained crews could not easily be replaced. The campaign also drew in the people of the Solomon Islands, whose coastwatchers, scouts, and laborers worked with the Allies while villages were caught between armies, an experience seldom recorded in military histories.

Guadalcanal demonstrated the logic of the war in the Pacific. Control of a small island depended on control of the air and sea routes to it, and the side that could sustain reinforcement and supply gained the advantage. The United States, with a growing production base and a developing logistics system, could replace losses; Japan, drawing on a smaller industrial base and tied to overextended shipping, could not. The campaign shifted the war from the swift, coordinated offensives of 1941–1942 to a prolonged struggle of attrition, which favored the side with greater material capacity and which would define the following two years.

Section 78.12 · Division III

New Guinea and the Southwest Pacific: Jungle Warfare, Airfields, and Supply

After the failure of the seaborne attack on Port Moresby at the Coral Sea, the Japanese army tried to take it by an overland route. In July 1942 forces landed at Buna and Gona on the north coast of Papua and advanced over the Kokoda Track, a narrow trail through the Owen Stanley Range, with its steep ridges, rain forest, and mud. They were opposed first by Australian militia battalions and then by veteran units of the Australian Imperial Force brought back from the Middle East. The fighting at Isurava in late August and the retreat that followed gave the Australians their principal test of the Pacific War. By mid-September the Japanese South Seas Detachment under Major General Horii Tomitarō had reached Ioribaiwa, within view of the lights of Port Moresby, but it was out of supply, and the demands of Guadalcanal and the success of Australian resistance led to a Japanese withdrawal.

At the same time, in August and September 1942, Australian troops at Milne Bay at the eastern tip of New Guinea defeated a Japanese landing, which is widely regarded as the first major defeat of Japanese land forces in the war. The Allied pursuit across the Owen Stanleys, and the fight for the Japanese beachheads at Buna and Gona between November 1942 and January 1943, were costly. American forces, committed with little experience and poor equipment, struggled before improved leadership and supply allowed Allied troops to overcome the defenders. Throughout the campaign, Papuan carriers, recruited under an Australian wartime labor regime and often called by Australian soldiers "Fuzzy Wuzzy Angels," carried supplies and evacuated the wounded. Their service, together with the disruption of villages and the fate of those who worked for or were forced to work for the Japanese, belongs to the history of Papua and New Guinea as well as to the military narrative.

Disease was a major cause of casualties for both sides. Malaria, dysentery, and scrub typhus filled hospitals, and in many units the number of men lost to sickness exceeded the number lost to combat. The Japanese, who lacked an effective system for evacuating the sick and wounded, suffered particularly. The terrain, which prevented mechanized transport and placed a premium on porters, small boats, and aircraft, favored whoever could control the air, and by 1943 the Allies, under the commander of the Southwest Pacific Area, General Douglas MacArthur, and the air commander General George Kenney, were gaining that control.

The Battle of the Bismarck Sea, March 2–4, 1943, demonstrated this change. A Japanese convoy of eight transports and eight escorting destroyers carrying about 6,900 troops to reinforce Lae was attacked over three days by Allied bombers, American and Australian, using low-level techniques including skip-bombing against ships. All the transports and four destroyers were sunk, and about 3,000 troops were lost. After this, the Japanese largely abandoned large convoys on the approach to New Guinea, relying on barges, submarines, and small vessels, which could not carry heavy equipment. In late 1943 the Allies took Lae and Salamaua and began to advance along the northern coast, and in April 1944 landings at Hollandia, bypassing the Japanese strongholds at Wewak and Hansa Bay, isolated the Japanese 18th Army. The campaign showed how airfields and aircraft range, more than ground combat alone, determined the pace of advance.

Section 78.13 · Division III

Strategic Attrition: Aircraft, Warships, Manpower, and the Changing Balance of Power

By the middle of 1943 Japan was fighting a war of attrition that its prewar planning had not anticipated. Its naval aviation, which had been among the best-trained in the world in 1941, had lost a large share of its experienced aircrews in the carrier battles of 1942 and in the Solomons campaign, and training systems designed for peacetime or limited war could not replace them. Pilots were trained in small numbers and at length; as the demand grew, training time was shortened, and new pilots entered combat with far less experience than their predecessors. The American system, by contrast, rotated experienced pilots back to the United States as instructors and trained large numbers of new pilots with standardized methods, and American aircraft and tactics improved. The Grumman F6F Hellcat, which entered service in 1943, was a fighter designed to counter the Japanese Zero.

Allied advantages extended beyond aircraft. American carrier production, centered on the Essex-class fleet carriers, began to deliver ships from 1943, and more than a dozen entered service before the war's end. Radar, including shipboard and fighter-direction systems, gave American fleets better warning of air attack and improved night fighting. Allied codebreaking, which read portions of Japanese naval and army traffic, offered operational advantages, as in the interception of the itinerary of Admiral Yamamoto, whose aircraft was shot down over Bougainville on April 18, 1943. The proximity fuze, which began to appear in 1943 and spread widely in 1944, improved antiaircraft fire. On the Japanese side, the army and the navy competed for aircraft production and for materials, maintained separate aircraft types with similar roles, and cooperated imperfectly in shipping and operations.

The war on shipping was equally important and developed more slowly. American submarines were hampered in 1942–1943 by defective torpedoes and by tactics that were not suited to the task, but after the problems were corrected, by late 1943, their effectiveness grew. Japan's merchant fleet had started the war at about six million gross tons, and Japan had given little attention to convoy escort and antisubmarine warfare. Escort forces were small and poorly equipped, and a unified command for the protection of shipping was created only in late 1943. Losses mounted in 1944 and 1945. This was a constraint on every other element of Japanese strength, because aircraft factories, steel, and fleets all depended on imported raw materials and fuel, which had to travel by sea.

Japanese leaders recognized the changing situation. In September 1943 an Imperial Conference adopted the concept of an Absolute National Defense Sphere, a contracted perimeter running through the Marianas, Carolines, western New Guinea, and Burma, which was to be held at all costs, while operations beyond it were to delay the Allies. The decision implicitly acknowledged that the earlier perimeter could not be held, but it assumed that the new one could be, and it was undermined by the speed with which the Allies broke into it in 1944. In the same period the government also confronted shortages of workers and aircraft, mobilizing students and others for factories (see Chapter 77) and pushing aircraft production sharply upward.

The relationship between production statistics and combat capability is complex, and the Data Study below illustrates it. Japan's aircraft output rose greatly in 1943 and 1944, but it faced shortages of aluminum and fuel, quality problems, dispersion of factories, and, above all, a shortage of trained pilots, so that more aircraft did not translate into proportionate combat power. American production, however, was several times larger and was matched by trained crews, fuel, and supply. Japan's strategic problem was not that its factories had stopped working. It was that it faced an opponent whose capacity to produce, train, and transport grew faster than its own, while its own system of supply was being attacked.

Section 78.14 · Division IV — Allied Counteroffensives and the Collapse of Japan's Overseas Position, 1943–1945

The Central Pacific Offensive: Gilbert and Marshall Islands, 1943–1944

By late 1943 the American Pacific Fleet had grown strong enough to open a second axis of advance beside the Southwest Pacific campaign under MacArthur. The Central Pacific drive, directed by Admiral Chester Nimitz, aimed to move across the island groups of Micronesia, each of which could provide airfields and anchorages for the next advance, toward the Marianas and the Philippines or Taiwan. The two axes, one along the northern coast of New Guinea and one across the central ocean, were different in method and mutually supportive, because they forced Japan to divide its limited forces and shipping between two threats. The Central Pacific drive depended on the new fast carrier task forces, which provided air cover and neutralized Japanese air power, and on a fleet train that supplied and repaired ships at sea.

The first target was the Gilbert Islands, which had been occupied by Japan in December 1941. The assault on Tarawa Atoll, at the small island of Betio, began on November 20, 1943. The island was only about three kilometers long but heavily fortified with log and concrete bunkers, and its garrison of some 4,500, including Japanese naval troops and Korean laborers, was commanded by Rear Admiral Shibasaki Keiji. Planners had misjudged the tide over the surrounding reef; landing craft grounded far from shore, and Marines waded hundreds of meters under fire. After three days of fighting, the island was taken at a cost of about 1,000 American dead and about 2,000 wounded, while almost all the defenders died, with only a handful of Japanese and a larger number of Korean laborers surviving as prisoners. Makin Atoll fell more easily. The battle shocked American opinion, but its lessons, including the need for longer preliminary bombardment, armored amphibious tractors, and better reconnaissance of reefs and tides, were applied in later operations.

The Marshall Islands came next. Rather than attack the most heavily defended outer atolls, American planners chose to strike directly at Kwajalein, in the center of the group, bypassing several Japanese-held atolls. The landings on Kwajalein and Roi-Namur at the end of January 1944 were conducted with greater preparation and overwhelming support, and after about a week the atoll was secured with far lower losses than at Tarawa. Eniwetok, at the western edge of the group, was taken in February. In the same month a carrier raid on Truk, the Japanese fleet's main forward base in the Carolines, destroyed aircraft and shipping, though most of the larger warships had already moved elsewhere. The raid demonstrated that Japan's central bastion could no longer protect its fleet.

Small islands could be strategically decisive because of the geography of air and sea power. A coral atoll with a runway allowed aircraft to operate over hundreds of kilometers of ocean, and a lagoon offered a sheltered anchorage for ships. Whoever held such a place could extend the reach of air cover, threaten convoys, and project forces. For Japan, the islands of Micronesia, which it had held under a League of Nations mandate since World War I and fortified in secret and then openly, were meant to form a barrier. Once the American fleet could reach them with air cover, however, each garrison, isolated from supply and reinforcement, became a position that could be taken or neutralized in turn. Their inhabitants, among them Gilbertese, Marshallese, Chamorros, and Carolinians, as well as Korean and Okinawan laborers and Japanese civilian settlers, endured bombardment, forced labor, and displacement, and in many cases heavy casualties.

Section 78.15 · Division IV

Rabaul, the Bismarck Archipelago, and the Strategy of Bypassing Strongholds

Rabaul, on the northeastern tip of New Britain, had been seized by Japan in January 1942 and became its principal forward base in the South Pacific, with airfields, a fine harbor, and a garrison that grew to about 100,000 army and navy personnel. From it the Japanese had supported operations against Guadalcanal and New Guinea, and it posed a barrier to the Allied advance up the Solomons and through New Guinea. The Allied strategy, known by the code name Cartwheel, involved a series of coordinated advances by MacArthur's Southwest Pacific forces and Admiral William Halsey's South Pacific forces to approach and isolate it.

The campaign proceeded by stages. In the Solomons, the Allies captured New Georgia in mid-1943 and landed at Empress Augusta Bay on Bougainville in November, establishing an airfield from which fighters could reach Rabaul. In December they landed at Cape Gloucester on the western end of New Britain, and in early 1944 on the Green Islands and the Admiralty Islands. Japanese air attacks on the Allied forces, including the large air operations conducted in 1943, were costly and failed. By February 1944 Allied air forces had overwhelmed Rabaul's air defenses, and the Japanese withdrew their remaining aircraft. Allied commanders decided not to assault the base itself. Instead they left it encircled, to be neutralized by continuing air attacks and by the loss of shipping.

The decision to bypass reflected a calculation about costs. An assault on a position held by tens of thousands of defenders in prepared tunnels and fortifications would have produced heavy casualties, and the objective, to eliminate Rabaul as a threat to Allied lines of communication, could be reached without it. The same logic was applied to other places: Truk, the Marshall atolls such as Wotje and Jaluit, and the Japanese 18th Army around Wewak in New Guinea, which was isolated by the Hollandia landing in April 1944. These garrisons remained in existence, but they were cut off from supply and from the main routes of the war. Many turned to subsistence farming and to tunnel construction. Some, such as those at Rabaul, surrendered only after the general surrender in 1945.

The result illustrates a central argument of this chapter: a garrison could remain militarily intact yet strategically ineffective. Tens of thousands of Japanese troops in these bases consumed supplies, suffered from disease and hunger, and held territory, but they could not influence the course of the war, and they could not be redeployed because Japan lacked the shipping to move them and the Allied fleet and air forces controlled the routes. Their local effects were nonetheless significant for the civilian populations who lived near them, since the Japanese presence meant requisitions of food and labor, and the collapse of trade and administration.

Section 78.16 · Division IV

The Mariana Islands and the Battle of the Philippine Sea, 1944

The Mariana Islands, comprising Saipan, Tinian, and Guam, were a central part of the Absolute National Defense Sphere. Japan had held the northern Marianas since World War I, and the islands contained airfields, harbors, and a significant civilian population: about 25,000 on Saipan, including Japanese settlers, many of them from Okinawa, as well as Koreans and Chamorro residents. Guam, an American possession seized by Japan in 1941, was occupied by a Japanese garrison and a Chamorro population of about 20,000 that had experienced harsh occupation. For the Americans, the Marianas were attractive because they were within range for the new B-29 long-range bomber of the home islands, about 2,400 kilometers away, and because their capture would cut Japan's lines to the south.

American forces landed on Saipan on June 15, 1944, in an operation commanded by Admiral Raymond Spruance with Task Force 58, the fast carrier force under Vice Admiral Marc Mitscher, in support. The Japanese Combined Fleet, now commanded by Admiral Toyoda Soemu, had prepared a plan, A-Gō, to bring the American fleet to battle in the western Pacific, using land-based aircraft from island airfields and carrier aircraft whose longer range might permit attacks from beyond the reach of American planes. Vice Admiral Ozawa Jisaburō led nine carriers, but his pilots were inexperienced, and the land-based air forces on which the plan depended had been largely destroyed in earlier raids.

The Battle of the Philippine Sea, on June 19–20, was decided in the air. On June 19, Japanese carrier aircraft launched in several waves were intercepted by American fighters directed by radar, and the great majority were shot down, in a rout the American pilots nicknamed the "Great Marianas Turkey Shoot." American submarines sank the new carrier Taihō and the veteran Shōkaku. On the evening of June 20, American aircraft attacked at the limit of their range and sank the carrier Hiyō, with many aircraft lost when they ran out of fuel returning in darkness. Japan lost three carriers and, over the whole battle and the associated operations, several hundred aircraft, and Ozawa's force was left with few aircrews. The Japanese carrier force was never again a significant striking force; for the rest of the war, its carriers were used mostly as decoys.

On Saipan, the fighting lasted until early July under General Saitō Yoshitsugu, with the organized resistance ending after a mass attack on July 7 and the deaths of Saitō and Nagumo, the commander of the Pearl Harbor strike. Thousands of Japanese civilians died, many by jumping from cliffs at the island's northern end or by suicide encouraged by soldiers, after years of propaganda that told them capture meant torture. These events, treated more fully in Chapter 80 and in discussions of civilians in wartime, show how the defense of the empire's territories drew in noncombatants. Tinian fell at the beginning of August and Guam on August 10.

The loss of Saipan had immediate political consequences in Tokyo. Prime Minister Tōjō, who had also held the post of army chief of staff since February 1944 in an attempt to unify command, faced growing opposition from senior statesmen (jūshin), including former prime ministers, who regarded the war as being lost and sought a change of leadership. When Tōjō tried to reorganize his cabinet and retain power, Kishi Nobusuke, a minister, refused to resign, and the cabinet fell on July 18. General Koiso Kuniaki, with Admiral Yonai Mitsumasa, formed a new government. The change did not alter the commitment to continue the war, but it showed that the failure of the strategic perimeter had brought down the government that had begun the war. The first B-29 raid on Tokyo from the Marianas came on November 24, 1944, putting the home islands within regular reach of the Allied bombers; the campaign that followed is the subject of Chapter 80.

Section 78.17 · Division IV

Burma and the Indian Frontier: Imphal, Kohima, and the Reversal of Japanese Expansion

The Burma front had been quiet after 1942 except for British attempts to advance in the Arakan, which were repulsed, and the long-range penetration raids of the Chindits under Brigadier Orde Wingate in 1943. The Japanese 15th Army, now under Lieutenant General Mutaguchi Renya, concluded that the best defense of Burma was an offensive. The plan, designated Operation Ū-Gō, aimed to capture the British base at Imphal in Manipur and the nearby supply center at Kohima, forestalling an Allied advance and, in the hopes of some officers, opening a path into India. Imperial General Headquarters approved it in January 1944 despite doubts about supply, since the offensive depended on capturing British stocks and on animals and porters to cross rugged jungle country.

The offensive began on March 8, 1944, and Japanese forces surrounded Imphal and cut the road to Kohima. Participating in the offensive were units of the Indian National Army, with Subhas Chandra Bose seeing the campaign as the start of the liberation of India; the INA contributed several thousand men, but its role was limited, and its units, short of supplies and in many cases poorly equipped, suffered from hunger and desertion. On the Allied side, the 14th Army under Lieutenant General William Slim, which included British, Indian, Gurkha, African, and other units, withdrew into prepared positions at Imphal, where the IV Corps was supplied by air, and defended Kohima, where a small garrison, relieved in April, fought a notorious battle around the district commissioner's tennis court. The Allies had learned from earlier campaigns and had improved medicine, air transport, and training.

The Japanese offensive failed above all for logistical reasons. Rations for the troops had been planned for about three weeks, and the capture of British supply dumps did not happen. Monsoon rains turned the tracks into mud, disease spread, and Allied air supply sustained the defenders while the Japanese starved. The road from Kohima to Imphal was reopened on June 22, and in early July Mutaguchi's offensive was called off. The retreat across the Chindwin during the monsoon was a disaster, with tens of thousands dead from combat, starvation, and disease; Japanese casualties for the campaign are often estimated at well over 50,000, with roughly 30,000 dead, though figures vary. The battles were among the largest defeats of Japanese ground forces in the war, and they broke the army's capacity to defend Burma.

The Allies renewed their advance in late 1944 and 1945. British and Indian forces crossed the Chindwin, won battles at Meiktila and Mandalay in February and March 1945, and recaptured Rangoon in early May. In the north, the Ledo Road, built by American engineers and local laborers, connected India to the old Burma Road in January 1945, easing the supply of China, a subject bound up with Chapter 76. In Burma, the Burma National Army under Aung San rose against Japan on March 27, 1945, and the British worked with it and its political organization, so that postwar Burmese politics was shaped by the same leaders who had worked with the Japanese. For India, the INA's trials became a major political issue after the war, even though its military role had been marginal. The Burma campaign shows that the same event can have different significance for military history, imperial logistics, and nationalist politics.

Section 78.18 · Division IV

Leyte Gulf and the Return to the Philippines, 1944–1945

In the summer of 1944 American planners debated whether the next major objective should be the Philippines or Taiwan. MacArthur, who had pledged to return, argued that the Philippines were a political and moral obligation and a strategic necessity, and Nimitz and others favored alternative routes; the decision was made to land in the Philippines, first at Leyte. Japan's navy had prepared a plan, Shō-Gō, for a decisive battle in the Philippines, in which its weakened carrier force would serve as bait to draw the American carriers away from the landing area, enabling battleships and cruisers to strike the transports and supporting ships.

The landing on Leyte took place on October 20, 1944, and the Battle of Leyte Gulf, October 23–26, was in effect four separate actions across a large area. On October 24 the Japanese Center Force under Vice Admiral Kurita Takeo, passing through the Sibuyan Sea, was attacked by American carrier aircraft, which sank the giant battleship Musashi. A Southern Force under Vice Admiral Nishimura Shōji was destroyed in the night action in Surigao Strait by American battleships, cruisers, and destroyers. Meanwhile Ozawa's Northern Force, whose carriers had few aircraft, succeeded in attracting the attention of Admiral Halsey's Third Fleet, which went north to attack, and four Japanese carriers were sunk off Cape Engaño. This left the landing area exposed to Kurita's force, which emerged from San Bernardino Strait and on October 25 engaged a force of American escort carriers and destroyers off Samar. In an action in which the American ships fought with extraordinary determination, Kurita, uncertain of the situation and having lost several cruisers, turned back before reaching the transports.

Japan lost four carriers, three battleships, ten cruisers, and numerous destroyers, a total of some twenty-five to thirty major warships, in the largest naval battle of the war by the number of ships involved. The Japanese navy retained ships but had lost the ability to use them: with the Marianas and the Philippines lost, fuel in the home islands was scarce, and the fleet had no air cover. The battle also saw the first organized use of aircraft deliberately flown into ships, the special attack units (tokkōtai), later called kamikaze, organized by Vice Admiral Ōnishi Takijirō, which sank an escort carrier on October 25. The tactic reflected the shortage of experienced pilots and the army's and navy's decision to compensate with sacrifice; it caused heavy damage and casualties in the following months but did not alter the strategic situation. The institutional and ideological dimensions are discussed in Chapter 77 and elsewhere.

The campaign on Leyte continued into December, and in January 1945 American forces landed at Lingayen Gulf on Luzon, where Japan's forces were now commanded by General Yamashita, who had been brought from Manchuria. The Japanese army decided to fight in the mountains, but the naval forces in Manila, under Rear Admiral Iwabuchi Sanji, chose to defend the city. The battle for Manila, from early February to early March 1945, destroyed much of the city. Japanese forces committed massacres of civilians, and American artillery, used in dense urban areas, also caused many deaths. At least 100,000 Filipino civilians are estimated to have died, a figure that remains an estimate, and the historic walled city of Intramuros and much of the capital were ruined. The liberation of prisoners from camps in Manila, among them the civilian internees at Santo Tomás, accompanied the fighting. Filipino guerrillas and soldiers played a significant part in the campaign, and Japanese forces in northern Luzon held out until the surrender. Overall deaths of Filipinos during the war are estimated in the hundreds of thousands, and Chapter 79 treats the evidence for atrocities and the postwar trials of Yamashita and Homma, with Chapter 83 examining war crimes prosecution more broadly. The Japanese-sponsored Philippine government, under Laurel, fled to Japan.

Section 78.19 · Division IV

Maritime Isolation, Iwo Jima, and the Collapse of Japan's Strategic Position in 1945

By 1945 the maritime lifeline connecting the southern resource area to the home islands had been broken. American submarines, with aircraft, carriers, and mines, sank merchant vessels in growing numbers from 1943, and the American advance into the Philippines and the Marianas placed Japanese shipping routes within reach of land-based air power. Japan lost most of its tanker fleet. By early 1945 little oil was reaching Japan from the south; the Combined Fleet's ships, including its remaining battleships, were stationed near the oil sources at Lingga, near Singapore, where fuel was available, rather than in Japan where there was none. In the spring of 1945, American long-range bombers began mining Japanese home waters in an operation later known as Starvation, which further disrupted coastal shipping. The material consequences of the blockade for the Japanese population, including shortages of food and fuel, are discussed in Chapter 80.

The garrisons of the empire were thus stranded. Hundreds of thousands of Japanese soldiers remained in the Philippines, the East Indies, Malaya, Burma, Indochina, New Guinea, the Pacific islands, and China, together with large civilian populations of settlers and laborers. They held territory, and some units remained formidable, but they could not be supplied, reinforced, or withdrawn, and their loss of connection to one another and to the home islands meant that Japanese strategic direction was increasingly divorced from the realities on the ground. This is the paradox noted at the beginning of the division: Japan in 1945 occupied extensive territories and commanded large armies, yet it had lost the practical means to connect, supply, and defend them.

The Battle of Iwo Jima, from February 19 to March 26, 1945, illustrates the American approach to the approaches of the home islands. The small volcanic island, about 1,200 kilometers from Tokyo, was desired for its airfields, which could host fighters escorting B-29s from the Marianas and provide emergency landing fields for damaged bombers. Lieutenant General Kuribayashi Tadamichi, commanding some 21,000 troops, abandoned the earlier doctrine of defending the beaches and built a defense in depth, with an extensive network of tunnels and fortified positions, intending to inflict maximum casualties. The Marines landed on February 19 and took five weeks to secure the island. American casualties were around 26,000, including some 6,800 killed, and almost all the Japanese defenders died, with about a thousand captured. The battle's strategic worth is debated: the airfields proved useful for emergency landings, and many bombers made use of them, while critics question whether the cost was warranted. The Battle of Okinawa and the attacks on the home islands are treated comprehensively in Chapter 80.

The strategic situation by the spring of 1945 can be summarized as follows. The Japanese navy had lost its carrier striking force and most of its surface fleet, and had little fuel; its air forces were reduced to inexperienced pilots and to suicide attacks; its merchant fleet was largely destroyed; its overseas armies were isolated; and its home islands were under air attack and blockade. Japan's leaders hoped to inflict enough casualties on an invasion of the home islands to obtain better terms, a strategy examined in Chapters 80 and 81. The question for this chapter is not why Japan lost but how a war that began with sweeping victories became a progressive loss of the capacity to sustain them.

Cause and Effect

From the Southern Resource Strategy to Maritime Vulnerability and Collapse

Long-term causes

Dependence on imported fuel and raw materials; the unresolved China war; a navy built to meet the United States in a decisive fleet battle; army-navy rivalry; imperial ideology of a Japan-led order in Asia; the weakness of the European colonial powers after 1940.

Short-term causes

The 1941 asset freeze and oil embargo; the occupation of southern Indochina; the failure of negotiations; the decision at the Imperial Conferences of November and December 1941; belief that a perimeter could be held until the Allies sought terms.

Event or transformation

The rapid conquest of the southern resource area and the western Pacific in 1941–1942, creating an empire dependent on sea lanes thousands of kilometers long.

Immediate effects

Control of oil and raw materials on paper; an overextended perimeter; shortages of escort vessels and tankers; occupation burdens on Southeast Asian societies; nationalist politics reshaped.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

Allied submarine, air, and naval campaigns severed the connection between resources and industry; garrisons were isolated; the navy lost its carrier force and fuel; and the home islands were exposed to bombing and blockade. No single cause explains the outcome. The loss of shipping was a product of Japanese neglect of escort, American technological and tactical improvement, and the vast distances of the empire. Allied industrial capacity mattered, but so did decisions made in Tokyo about perimeter, replacement of pilots, and the allocation of shipping between army and navy.
Section 78.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Chapter Review

The Pacific War and Japan's Southern Empire: Historical Synthesis and Review

The preceding sections can be gathered into one argument. Japan's leaders chose in 1941 to widen a continental war into a war against the United States, Britain, and the Netherlands, under pressures that included the stalemate in China, the loss of American oil, and the opportunity created by Europe's war, but also through a decision-making structure in which neither army nor navy would accept a settlement that surrendered its gains. Their plan was to seize the southern resource area, build a perimeter, and wait for their opponents to tire. The opening offensive achieved its objectives with speed and skill, and the conquest of Malaya, the Philippines, the Netherlands East Indies, and Burma remains a striking operational achievement. But the empire it created was a chain of captured positions joined by sea lanes that Japan could not protect, held against opponents whose industrial and logistical capacity was growing, and administered over populations whose political aspirations the occupation frustrated.

Two oversimplifications should be rejected. The first is that Japan was bound to lose from the moment of Pearl Harbor. The outcome depended on events that were open in 1942: the carrier battles, the contest for Guadalcanal, the effectiveness of the American submarine force, and decisions about escort and shipping that Japan might have made differently. The second is that early victories demonstrated an ability to sustain indefinite war against the United States. They showed the effectiveness of a prepared offensive against thinly defended targets, but Japan's own planners doubted they could win a long war, and the structure of the empire made them correct to doubt it.

The political legacy of the conquest was equally significant. Japan's occupation destroyed the colonial order in Southeast Asia in a manner that Europeans could not restore. Nationalist movements gained organization, military training, and a sense of possibility; at the same time, they experienced Japanese exploitation, violence, and broken promises. Indonesia proclaimed independence within days of the surrender, Burma's political life was led by men who had worked with and then turned against Japan, the Philippines faced the aftermath of collaboration and guerrilla resistance, and Malaya, Singapore, Indochina, and India each carried distinctive memories. Independence was not Japan's gift, and neither was it merely the postwar return of the status quo. It emerged from the interaction of prewar nationalism, wartime upheaval, and postwar contests with the returning European powers.

Historical Debate

Why Did Japan Lose: Strategic Decisions, Industrial Disparity, or an Unsecurable Empire?

The question: Historians offer different explanations for Japan's defeat in the Pacific, and the emphasis they place on each affects judgments about responsibility and inevitability.

Interpretation A — The 1941 decision as the fundamental error: Some historians argue that the decision to attack the United States, taken by leaders who doubted a long war could be won and who depended on assumptions about German success and American irresolution, locked Japan into a conflict it could not win. On this view later developments, including the failure of the perimeter, followed from the strategic mistake.
Interpretation B — Allied industrial and logistical superiority: Others stress the disparity in production, shipbuilding, training, and technology, which meant that once the United States was fully mobilized, it could replace losses and sustain operations at a scale Japan could not match. On this view the outcome was largely determined by material capacity, whatever Japanese decisions were taken.
Interpretation C — The impossibility of securing the conquered empire: A third group emphasizes the structure of the empire itself: its dependence on long sea lanes, Japan's neglect of convoy protection and antisubmarine warfare, the divided army-navy command, and the hostility or ambivalence of the populations it governed. This view treats operational choices of 1942–1944 as significant and not entirely determined either by the 1941 decision or by industrial disparity.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the records of Imperial Conferences and Liaison Conferences, production and shipping statistics, naval and army operational records, postwar surveys, and the testimony of participants. Many scholars treat the three explanations as complementary rather than exclusive. The disagreement lies chiefly in the weight assigned to each and in whether alternative decisions could have changed the outcome.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes judgments about responsibility for the war, about the role of contingency, and about what Japan's wartime experience demonstrates for the study of empire and total war.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Colonial Politics Before, During, and After Japanese Occupation

What Changed

  • European colonial administrations collapsed in 1942 and could not be restored on prewar terms.
  • Nationalist leaders gained mass organizations, auxiliary armies, and administrative experience.
  • Local economies were disrupted by requisitions, inflation, forced labor, and the loss of trade.
  • Ethnic communities, notably the Chinese of Malaya, experienced targeted violence, and communal relations were reshaped.
  • Several nominally independent governments were created under Japanese supervision.

What Remained Continuous

  • Nationalist movements with prewar roots and distinct aims persisted throughout.
  • Rural economies remained dependent on rice and export crops, with farmers bearing the costs of requisition.
  • Political power remained concentrated among elites, some of whom adapted to each new regime.
  • Colonial hierarchies of race and status were replaced by a Japanese hierarchy that resembled them in some respects.

Who experienced change most directly? Europeans who were interned or expelled, urban Chinese in Malaya and Singapore, laborers drawn into forced work, and nationalists who gained political space. Who experienced greater continuity? Many villagers whose lives centered on subsistence and local authority, though their burdens grew. Why the pattern matters: It explains why the postwar contests between returning colonial powers and nationalists took the forms they did, and why the occupation is better seen as a catalyst than as a creator of independence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan's war in China became a war against the United States, Britain, and the Netherlands, how Japan conquered much of Southeast Asia and the western Pacific, and how that empire became impossible to sustain. Its central argument is that Japan's expansion created a vast but strategically vulnerable empire: early victories depended on operational coordination, surprise, and Allied weakness, but the empire could not be securely supplied or defended against opponents whose industrial, naval, and logistical capacities continued to grow, and it conflicted with the political aspirations of the populations Japan claimed to liberate.

The decision for war emerged from the China stalemate, the occupation of Indochina, the American, British, and Dutch asset freezes and the effective oil embargo, and Japan's dependence on imported petroleum. These pressures constrained choices without making war inevitable. Japanese leaders, working through Liaison and Imperial Conferences in which army and navy held effective vetoes, chose war in the Imperial Conferences of November 5 and December 1, 1941. Their strategy was to seize the southern resource area, build a defensive perimeter, and wait for their opponents to accept a settlement, a plan that rested on doubtful assumptions.

The opening offensive, launched across the International Date Line on December 7–8, 1941, struck Pearl Harbor, Malaya, Thailand, Hong Kong, Guam, Wake, and the Philippines nearly simultaneously. The carrier attack on Pearl Harbor sank or disabled much of the American battleship force and killed about 2,400 people, but it missed the American carriers and shore installations and united American opinion for war. Germany's declaration of war on the United States joined the Pacific and European conflicts.

The conquest of Southeast Asia was rapid. Malaya and Singapore fell to the 25th Army by February 15, 1942, in a defeat that broke European colonial prestige, and the occupation began with massacres of Chinese civilians. The Philippines resisted until the surrender of Bataan and Corregidor, followed by the Bataan Death March and a guerrilla war. The Netherlands East Indies fell by March 1942, giving Japan its oilfields, though not the means to transport their output reliably. Burma was taken by May, cutting the Burma Road and bringing Japan to the frontier of India.

Japan's occupation was governed by military administration oriented toward resource extraction and military self-sufficiency, accompanied by cultural campaigns and by claims of Asian liberation. Southeast Asian nationalists responded differently according to their prewar histories: Indonesians used the Japanese-sponsored organizations to build mass networks and armed auxiliaries; Burmese leaders worked with and then against Japan; Filipinos divided among collaboration, guerrilla resistance, and loyalty to the United States; Chinese communities in Malaya faced targeted violence; and Indian nationalists, including Bose, allied with Japan. The Greater East Asia Joint Declaration of November 1943 proclaimed sovereignty and equality that occupation practice contradicted.

The war turned between May 1942 and early 1943. At the Coral Sea, Japan's Port Moresby operation was checked, and at Midway Japan lost four fleet carriers and the initiative, through a combination of American intelligence, scouting, command decisions, and chance. At Guadalcanal and in New Guinea, the contest became one of supply, air power, and disease, in which Japan lost large numbers of trained personnel and could not match Allied reinforcement. By 1943 Japan faced growing disparities in aircraft, carrier production, radar, and trained aircrew, and a submarine campaign that would devastate its shipping.

From late 1943 the Allies advanced along two axes. The Central Pacific drive took Tarawa, Kwajalein, and Eniwetok, and the Southwest Pacific forces moved along New Guinea, isolating Rabaul and other garrisons. The capture of the Marianas in mid-1944, following the Battle of the Philippine Sea in which Japan's carrier air arm was effectively destroyed, brought the home islands within bomber range and caused the fall of the Tōjō cabinet. In Burma, the Imphal–Kohima offensive failed for logistical reasons and led to the recovery of Burma, while the INA's role had little military effect.

The battle of Leyte Gulf in October 1944 ended the Japanese navy's ability to contest sea control, and the return to the Philippines brought the destruction of Manila and heavy civilian suffering. In 1945 Allied submarines, aircraft, and mines had severed the connection between southern resources and Japanese industry, while Iwo Jima showed the cost of the approach to Japan. Japan still held large armies and territories, but it had lost the capacity to connect and supply them. The chapter concludes that the defeat resulted from a combination of the 1941 strategic decision, Allied material superiority, and the inability to secure the empire, and that Japan's occupation catalyzed, without creating, the end of colonial rule in Southeast Asia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how an empire can reach its greatest extent at the moment its foundations are weakest. In 1942 Japan controlled more territory than at any other time, and within three years it had lost the ability to use it. That pattern, a gap between occupying territory and sustaining control, appears in the histories of many empires, and Japan's case illuminates it with unusual clarity because the instruments of loss, shipping, fuel, trained crews, and political legitimacy, can be traced in the sources.

The chapter also connects the archipelago to the societies surrounding it in ways earlier chapters could not. The conquest placed Japanese soldiers and administrators among Malays, Chinese, Indians, Indonesians, Burmese, Filipinos, Thais, Vietnamese, Pacific Islanders, and Papuans, and the occupation changed the political futures of all of them. Their responses were varied and active, and they shaped postwar Asia as much as Japan's defeat did.

The developments examined here shaped what followed. The strategic collapse of 1944–1945 placed the home islands under air attack and blockade, the subject of Chapter 80; the violence of the occupation and its treatment of civilians and prisoners, introduced here, requires the systematic examination of Chapter 79; and the surrender, the loss of the empire, and the repatriation of millions follow in Chapter 81. Memory of the occupation has remained part of Japan's relations with Southeast Asia, China, Korea, and the Western Allies, and arguments about responsibility continue.

Finally, the chapter invites students to hold together outcomes and contingencies. Japan's defeat was not fated in December 1941, yet the structure of its strategy made the later collapse understandable. Students who read the war as a series of decisions made under pressure by people with limited information, and as experiences lived by millions with no voice in those decisions, are prepared to approach the chapters on atrocity, devastation, and defeat that follow.
