Part IV — The Rise of the Samurai and Kamakura Japan

Religion, Culture, and Martial Life in Kamakura Japan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the early thirteenth century, a warrior returning from campaign might stop first not at his own hall but at a temple gate, to have a sutra copied on behalf of a fallen comrade, to commission a memorial image, or simply to sit for a while within the sound of a bell that had rung, in one form or another, since long before his family had ever held land. He might also, on another day, sit on a riverbank and listen to a wandering preacher chant the name of Amida Buddha to anyone who passed, farmer and beggar and minor retainer alike, and be told that this simple invocation, repeated with sincerity, could open a path to salvation regardless of rank, learning, or the accumulated weight of a violent life. Neither experience would have struck him as unusual, and neither would have struck him as new in any absolute sense. Both were part of a religious world in vigorous motion, a world inherited from Nara and Heian Japan and being reshaped, unevenly and without any single architect, by generations that had lived through civil war, dislocation, and the rise of an unfamiliar kind of government answering to warriors rather than to courtiers alone.

Chapter 25 explained how the Hōjō regency turned Yoritomo's improvised wartime authority into a durable, institutionalized form of government: a system of shugo and jitō, a body of vassalage law, a machinery for resolving land disputes, and a working, if uneasy, division of authority between Kamakura and the imperial court in Kyoto. That chapter answered a question about political durability. This chapter asks a different kind of question. Within the mature warrior-governed society that the Hōjō had built by the middle and later thirteenth century, how did people actually live their religious, cultural, and martial lives? What did warriors believe, and what did they patronize? What did monks teach, and to whom? What did courtiers in Kyoto continue to create, and how did their world interact with the newer world of Kamakura? What did it mean, in concrete and unglamorous terms, to be trained as a warrior in this period, well before any fully articulated code of samurai ethics had been written down?

The honest answer this chapter will develop is that Kamakura Japan's religious and cultural life was neither a single "medieval Buddhism" imposed uniformly across society nor a simple replacement of an exhausted aristocratic tradition by a vigorous new warrior faith. It was plural, overlapping, and cumulative. The great Nara- and Heian-period institutions — Tendai's headquarters on Mount Hiei, Shingon's center at Mount Kōya, the ancient temples of Nara, the countless shrine-temple complexes scattered across the provinces — remained enormously wealthy, enormously influential, and central to elite religious life throughout the Kamakura period. At the very same time, a series of remarkable religious teachers, most of them trained within those very institutions before breaking from or reforming them, proposed new and often more accessible paths to salvation: Hōnen's exclusive reliance on the nenbutsu, Shinran's radical rethinking of faith and clerical identity, Ippen's itinerant preaching among ordinary people on the roads, Nichiren's insistence on the singular authority of the Lotus Sutra, and the introduction and adaptation of Zen meditation traditions by Eisai and Dōgen. None of these figures set out to found the separate, institutionally distinct sects that would eventually carry their names; those institutional boundaries hardened only gradually, often long after the founders themselves had died.

This chapter will also take seriously a stubborn and popular misconception: the idea that Kamakura-period warriors possessed, from the outset, a unified ethical code — usually called bushidō, "the way of the warrior" — that governed their conduct with the moral clarity later associated with Edo-period treatises or modern popular retellings. No such fully articulated code existed in the thirteenth century. What did exist was a demanding, practical occupational culture built around horsemanship, mounted archery, hunting, weapon maintenance, household management, and the concrete obligations of military service to a lord — a culture with its own values of courage, reputation, and loyalty, but one considerably more improvised, regionally variable, and materially grounded than later idealizations would suggest. Reading Kamakura warrior culture correctly requires resisting the temptation to project four or five centuries of later moral philosophy backward onto men whose most pressing daily concerns were often the condition of their horses, the terms of their land tenure, and the standing of their family within a network of lords and rivals.

Alongside religion and martial training, this chapter turns to culture in the broader sense: the literature that transformed the memory of the Genpei War into something between history and legend, most famously The Tale of the Heike; the poetry anthologies that carried Heian courtly aesthetics forward even as warrior patrons entered the picture; the strikingly realistic sculpture produced by the Kei school workshops as they rebuilt temples destroyed in the recent fighting; and the essays of writers such as Kamo no Chōmei, whose reflections on impermanence in works like the Hōjōki were shaped directly by the disasters, famines, and political instability of the period. None of this cultural production existed apart from religion or from warrior society; all three were woven together in the daily experience of thirteenth-century Japan.

Finally, this chapter deliberately does not narrate the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281, nor the political and financial strains that would eventually help bring down the Kamakura shogunate. Those events belong to Chapter 27, which examines how the mature warrior society and religious world described here would soon confront an unprecedented external threat. This chapter's task is more limited and, in its own way, more intimate: to understand how people inside Kamakura Japan worshipped, created, remembered, and trained, before that external crisis reshaped the world they had built.

Historical Context

Chapter 25 established the institutional architecture within which this chapter's religious, cultural, and martial history unfolds. By the middle of the thirteenth century, the Hōjō regents governed a warrior polity centered at Kamakura, exercising authority through shugo military governors and jitō estate stewards distributed across provinces still nominally under the authority of an imperial court resident in Kyoto. That court, though sharply constrained in its independent military and political autonomy, retained substantial landed and fiscal resources, remained the source of ultimate ceremonial and cultural legitimacy, continued to hold substantial landed wealth through the shōen estate system, and remained the center of an aristocratic culture with roots extending back through the Heian and Nara periods. Warriors, for their part, had not displaced the court so much as constructed a parallel and increasingly dominant structure of military and administrative power alongside it, one whose relationship to land, family, and lordship this chapter now examines from the standpoint of religion, culture, and everyday martial practice rather than law and administration.

Religiously, this dual political structure mapped onto an equally dual, and considerably older, religious landscape. Since at least the Nara period, Buddhism in Japan had been institutionally concentrated in a small number of enormously wealthy temple complexes—Tōdaiji and other great Nara temples, the Tendai headquarters at Enryakuji on Mount Hiei above Kyoto, and the Shingon center founded by Kūkai at Mount Kōya—each possessing extensive landholdings, armed temple affiliates and monastic forces, and deep connections to the imperial court and aristocratic families. Shrine traditions and kami worship, meanwhile, had never developed as an institutionally separate religion in the modern sense; kami worship and Buddhist practice had been intertwined for centuries through the framework historians call shinbutsu shūgō, discussed later in this chapter. By the start of the Kamakura period, this landscape had already produced centuries of doctrinal scholarship, ritual practice, and institutional wealth that did not simply evaporate because a new warrior government had arisen at Kamakura.

At the same time, the twelfth century had been a period of severe social strain: the Hōgen and Heiji disturbances, the Genpei War itself, repeated famine, and what many contemporaries experienced as a spiritual crisis tied to the Buddhist concept of mappō, the "latter age of the Dharma," in which the historical Buddha's teachings were believed to have entered a period of decline in which traditional monastic discipline and difficult meditative practices were thought no longer sufficient to guarantee salvation for most people. This sense of crisis forms essential background for understanding why devotional movements promising simpler, more universally accessible paths to salvation found such wide audiences in the decades surrounding and following the Genpei War, a conflict examined in Chapter 23, and why literature memorializing that same war's violence and its defeated participants became one of the defining cultural achievements of the age.

Section 26.1

Religion in a Changing Kamakura World

The rise of warrior government did not create a religious vacuum that new warrior-oriented teachings simply rushed in to fill. Kyoto remained the seat of the imperial court and the center of an aristocratic culture with its own religious patronage networks stretching back centuries, and the city's surrounding mountains held two of the most powerful religious institutions in the country: Enryakuji on Mount Hiei, headquarters of the Tendai school, and, somewhat further away, Kongōbuji on Mount Kōya, headquarters of Shingon. Both institutions possessed vast landholdings organized as shōen estates of the kind examined in Chapter 20, maintained armed temple affiliates and monastic forces—often labeled sōhei, "warrior monks," in later historiography, though modern scholarship shows this armed personnel actually included a diverse mix of low-ranking clerics, lay temple affiliates, and estate warriors rather than a single distinct military class—capable of pressing political demands with real coercive weight, and trained generations of scholar-monks in doctrine, ritual, and administration. Nara's ancient temples—Tōdaiji, Kōfukuji, and others—likewise retained substantial wealth, political connections, and religious prestige, particularly after their partial destruction and subsequent reconstruction following fighting late in the Genpei War, discussed further in Section 26.15.

What changed with the rise of the Kamakura shogunate was not the wealth or prestige of these institutions in any simple, immediate sense, but rather the balance of political patronage available to religious movements, the geographic center of new wealth and authority, and the social composition of the audiences that new religious teachings could reach. Warriors newly enriched through jitō appointments and vassal service became patrons in their own right, commissioning temples, sponsoring memorial rites for fallen kin and comrades, and, in some cases, becoming devoted followers of the newer devotional movements examined later in this chapter. At the same time, the social dislocation associated with civil war, famine, and rapid political change created large audiences—among warriors, but also among farmers, merchants, and the urban poor—receptive to religious teachings promising accessible paths to salvation independent of wealth, literacy, or institutional standing.

It is essential to hold both halves of this picture together. The Kamakura period did produce genuinely influential new religious teachers and movements, several of which endure as major branches of Japanese Buddhism today. But it did so within, not instead of, a still-thriving older religious world whose institutions, personnel, doctrines, and wealth continued to shape Japanese religious life throughout the period and for centuries afterward. The chapter's remaining sections examine both halves of this picture in turn, beginning with the historiographical problem of how historians should even frame the relationship between old and new.

Section 26.2

What Historians Mean by "Kamakura Buddhism"

Twentieth-century textbooks, both in Japan and abroad, frequently organized the religious history of this period around a tidy narrative: aristocratic, ritual-heavy Heian Buddhism, associated above all with Tendai and Shingon, had grown remote from ordinary believers and unresponsive to the anxieties of an age many contemporaries understood through the Buddhist concept of mappō, the "latter age of the Dharma." Into this perceived vacuum stepped a cluster of reforming founders—Hōnen, Shinran, Ippen, Nichiren, Eisai, and Dōgen—who together produced a more accessible, more distinctly "Japanese," and more spiritually vital "new Buddhism" (shin bukkyō) suited to a new warrior age. This framework organized these six figures into a single narrative of religious progress and popularization, often implicitly treating their movements as already-formed, doctrinally distinct sects comparable to the institutionally mature schools that carry their names today.

More recent scholarship has complicated this picture substantially, without discarding it outright. Historians now emphasize several correctives. First, every one of the so-called "new Buddhism" founders trained for years, often decades, within older institutions, above all Tendai on Mount Hiei; their teachings emerged from internal reform, criticism, and eventual departure rather than an entirely external assault on establishment religion. Second, the institutionally distinct sects later associated with these founders—Jōdo-shū, Jōdo Shinshū, Nichiren-shū, Rinzai, and Sōtō—did not exist in fully formed, doctrinally settled shape during the founders' own lifetimes; sectarian boundaries sharpened considerably in the centuries after their deaths, shaped by later disciples, institutional competition, and, in some cases, political patronage under later governments. Third, older schools such as Tendai, Shingon, Kegon, Hossō, and Ritsu did not stagnate during this period; they produced their own vigorous reformers, such as Myōe and Eison, and remained the dominant recipients of elite patronage and landed wealth throughout the Kamakura period and well beyond it, as Section 26.10 will show in detail.

None of this means the "new Buddhism" framework is simply wrong or should be discarded. The devotional movements associated with Hōnen, Shinran, Ippen, and Nichiren, and the meditative traditions associated with Eisai and Dōgen, did represent genuinely significant developments: they proposed distinctive doctrinal positions, they attracted committed followings that would eventually organize into lasting religious communities, and they addressed real and widely shared anxieties about salvation in ways that differed meaningfully from the ritual and doctrinal emphases of the older schools. The historically responsible approach, and the one this chapter follows, is to treat these movements as significant developments emerging from within, and remaining in tension with, an older religious world that continued to flourish rather than decline—a plural and cumulative religious landscape rather than a story of straightforward succession from old to new.

Historical Vocabulary
Nenbutsu and Daimoku

The nenbutsu (literally "mindfulness of the Buddha") refers, in the Pure Land tradition central to Sections 26.3 through 26.5, to the invocation "Namu Amida Butsu" ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha"), understood by Hōnen and his successors as a practice capable of securing rebirth in Amida's Pure Land regardless of the practitioner's learning, wealth, or moral history. The daimoku ("title," referring here to the title of the Lotus Sutra) refers to Nichiren's chant "Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō" ("I take refuge in the Wonderful Dharma of the Lotus Sutra"), understood within the Nichiren tradition as the essential practice appropriate to the current declining age.

Both practices share a historically important feature: each reduced the core devotional act to a short, repeatable, easily learned phrase, in deliberate contrast to the more elaborate ritual, textual study, and meditative discipline emphasized by older schools such as Tendai and Shingon. This shared feature helps explain why both practices, despite their very different doctrinal justifications and their founders' considerable disagreements with one another, appealed to broad audiences across social ranks during a period of widespread anxiety about salvation.

Section 26.3

Hōnen and the Pure Land Path

Hōnen (1133–1212) trained for decades within the Tendai establishment on Mount Hiei, immersing himself in the school's broad curriculum of doctrine, ritual, and meditative practice. Around 1175, after years of study and, by his own later account, considerable spiritual dissatisfaction with the difficulty of achieving certainty about salvation through conventional practice, Hōnen began publicly teaching that reliance on the nenbutsu alone—repeated with sincere faith in Amida Buddha's vow to save all who called upon him—was sufficient for rebirth in the Pure Land, regardless of a practitioner's other religious accomplishments. This teaching, known as senju nenbutsu ("exclusive nenbutsu practice"), represented a genuinely significant doctrinal claim: it did not merely recommend the nenbutsu as one useful practice among many, as many Tendai and other clerics already did, but asserted that it alone was necessary and sufficient, rendering other traditional practices, while not necessarily harmful, ultimately unnecessary for salvation.

Hōnen's teaching attracted followers across a wide social spectrum, from courtiers and warriors to farmers and people of low social standing, precisely because it offered a clear and universally accessible path to salvation that did not depend on wealth, literacy, monastic discipline, or elite social connections. This same accessibility, however, provoked serious alarm among established clergy, who worried both about doctrinal error and about the social and institutional consequences of a teaching that seemed to render centuries of monastic practice, ritual sponsorship, and clerical authority unnecessary. Petitions from Kōfukuji and other institutions eventually persuaded the court to act: in 1207, following a scandal involving several of Hōnen's followers, the court exiled Hōnen to Shikoku and several of his leading disciples, including Shinran, to other remote locations, and executed several followers, including Jūren and Anraku. Hōnen was pardoned and allowed to return to the capital shortly before his death in 1212, but the episode illustrates vividly how new devotional teachings, however popular, could provoke serious institutional and political resistance rather than being welcomed as a harmless supplement to existing practice.

It would be a mistake to reduce Hōnen's movement to "Buddhism for commoners" alone. Hōnen retained sophisticated Tendai scholastic training throughout his life, engaged seriously with doctrinal controversy, and attracted aristocratic as well as humble followers; his teaching's significance lies less in its social class than in its radical claim about the sufficiency of a single, simple practice for salvation in an age when many believed traditional practices had lost their efficacy.

Section 26.4

Shinran, Faith, and the Development of Jōdo Shinshū

Shinran (1173–1263) studied under Hōnen for several years before being caught up in the 1207 exile that followed Hōnen's own condemnation. Sent to a remote province, Shinran developed over subsequent decades a distinctive theological position that, while building directly on Hōnen's teaching, pushed its logic further in ways that later followers would treat as the foundation of a separate tradition, Jōdo Shinshū ("True Pure Land school"). Where Hōnen emphasized the practice of repeated nenbutsu recitation as sufficient for salvation, Shinran increasingly emphasized shinjin, an inward moment of true faith or entrusting oneself to Amida's vow, arguing that a single sincere invocation arising from genuine shinjin was sufficient, and that additional repetitions functioned primarily as an expression of gratitude rather than as an accumulation of religious merit necessary for salvation.

Shinran also took the practical implications of his teaching further than Hōnen had in his own conduct. He married openly, fathered children, and described himself using a phrase often rendered "neither monk nor layman" (hi-sō hi-zoku), rejecting the special ritual purity and social status traditionally claimed by ordained clergy and insisting that ordinary lay life, including family and even the eating of meat, presented no fundamental obstacle to salvation for one who possessed genuine faith. This stance directly challenged centuries of assumptions about the necessity of monastic celibacy and ritual purity for religious authority, and it laid important groundwork for what would become, in later centuries, a married clergy characteristic of the Jōdo Shinshū tradition. Students should be careful, however, not to imagine Shinran founding an institutionally organized "Jōdo Shinshū" during his own lifetime; that institutional consolidation, including the construction of a formal temple hierarchy and the systematic compilation of Shinran's teachings, developed gradually over subsequent generations, reaching a particularly important stage of organization only in the fifteenth century under later leaders. Shinran himself remained, throughout his life, a wandering teacher and writer rather than the head of an established denomination.

Section 26.5

Ippen and Itinerant Pure Land Practice

If Hōnen and Shinran developed their teachings primarily through doctrinal argument and, in Shinran's case, extended written reflection, Ippen (1239–1289) represents a different and geographically more expansive mode of Pure Land practice: itinerant preaching carried directly to towns, villages, and roadside communities throughout the country. Ippen and the tradition he represents distributed printed nenbutsu charms (fusan) to travelers and villagers, encouraged communal chanting, and, according to later pictorial and textual accounts, incorporated ecstatic dancing (odori nenbutsu) into public devotional gatherings, creating a highly visible and participatory form of religious practice that required no temple attendance, no financial contribution, and no prior religious training.

Ippen's own decisive turn toward itinerant mission followed a formative pilgrimage experience at the Kumano shrine complex in 1274, after which his most active public teaching unfolded through the final fifteen years of his life. That mission built directly on decades of earlier itinerant Pure Land preaching by lesser-known holy men (hijiri) operating outside major institutional centers since at least the eleventh century, a tradition this chapter's chronology places well within the broader Kamakura-period spread of devotional practice. This itinerant dimension matters because it corrects a picture of Kamakura religious change centered too narrowly on court and warrior elites: devotional Buddhism during this period spread geographically as well as socially, reaching rural populations, travelers, and communities with little or no access to the great temple complexes of Kyoto, Nara, or Kamakura, and doing so through direct, personal, mobile preaching rather than through the construction of new fixed institutions.

Section 26.6

Nichiren and the Lotus Sutra

Nichiren (1222–1282) trained initially, like Hōnen before him, within Tendai teaching, which had long accorded special reverence to the Lotus Sutra among Buddhist scriptures. Where Tendai treated the Lotus Sutra as the highest teaching within an inclusive hierarchy that still accommodated other texts and practices, Nichiren, beginning around 1253, came to insist that the Lotus Sutra alone contained the teaching appropriate to the present declining age of mappō, and that devotion expressed through the daimoku chant "Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō" represented the sole reliable path to salvation available to people of his time. This claim carried an unusually sharp edge of exclusivity: Nichiren argued not merely that other practices were less effective but that reliance on rival teachings, including the Pure Land nenbutsu practice and various forms of Zen meditation, actively endangered both individual salvation and the wellbeing of the country as a whole.

Nichiren's religious claims were explicitly and unusually political. In 1260 he submitted to the Kamakura authorities a treatise, the Risshō Ankoku Ron ("Treatise on Establishing the Correct Teaching for the Peace of the Land"), arguing that Japan's recent natural disasters, famines, and civil unrest stemmed directly from the country's reliance on false teachings, and warning that continued error would invite further calamity, including foreign invasion—a warning that acquired considerable retrospective resonance once the Mongol invasions described in Chapter 27 actually occurred more than a decade later. This confrontational stance provoked severe institutional and political retaliation: Nichiren endured repeated exile, the destruction of his hermitage by hostile mobs, and, in 1271, a dramatic episode in which he was sentenced to execution at Tatsunokuchi before the sentence was unexpectedly commuted to exile to Sado Island. Students should understand Nichiren's career as unfolding through genuine controversy and genuine danger rather than as the calm founding of an already-organized denomination; the Nichiren-shū tradition that eventually formalized his teachings developed its institutional structure, as with the other movements discussed in this chapter, considerably after his own lifetime.

Section 26.7

Zen before the "Samurai Zen" Myth

Popular accounts of Japanese history often present Zen Buddhism as having arrived in the Kamakura period as the natural, almost inevitable religion of the samurai class, its emphasis on discipline, directness, and mental composure supposedly matching warrior temperament in some essential way. This narrative should be treated with considerable historical caution. Meditative traditions with strong affinities to what would become known as Zen (Chinese Chan) had already reached Japan in earlier centuries through Tendai and other channels, without producing an institutionally separate Zen school; genuinely organized, lineage-based Zen institutions arrived only in the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries through direct contact with Song-dynasty China, carried by monks such as Eisai and Dōgen who traveled there specifically to study, and their early Japanese institutions depended initially on court and aristocratic patronage in Kyoto every bit as much as on warrior patronage in Kamakura.

Zen practice centers on disciplined meditative training, typically seated meditation (zazen), often combined, particularly in the Rinzai tradition, with contemplation of paradoxical questions or statements (kōan) intended to break through ordinary conceptual thinking toward direct insight. Zen monastic institutions also imported, along with meditative technique, a body of Song-Chinese cultural and administrative practices—monastic architecture, tea culture, ink painting, and specific forms of institutional organization—that would later exercise enormous influence over Japanese elite culture generally, a development examined further in Chapters 30 and 38. None of this technical or institutional history supports the idea of an immediate, natural bond between Zen and a broad warrior class during the thirteenth century itself; that closer association between Zen institutions and warrior elite culture developed more fully in the subsequent Muromachi period, discussed in Chapter 30, when shogunal patronage of major Kyoto Zen monasteries became considerably more systematic and when Zen aesthetics became deeply entangled with elite cultural production.

During the Kamakura period itself, documented Zen patronage was concentrated especially among elite circles around the Hōjō regents and their immediate circle. The surviving evidence does not support treating Zen as "the religion of the samurai" generally; it simply does not allow historians to reconstruct anything like a comprehensive picture of ordinary warriors' religious affiliations, though what evidence does survive suggests many warriors remained connected to older Tendai, Shingon, and Pure Land traditions, or participated in more diffuse forms of devotional and memorial practice without strong sectarian identification of any kind.

Section 26.8

Eisai, Rinzai, and Elite Patronage

Eisai (Myōan Eisai, 1141–1215) traveled twice to Song China, in 1168 and again from 1187 to 1191, returning from his second journey with formal transmission in the Linji (Japanese: Rinzai) lineage of Chan Buddhism. Rather than abandoning his earlier Tendai training, Eisai continued throughout his life to hold Tendai affiliations and to present his newly imported Zen teaching as compatible with, and even protective of, established Buddhist institutions in Japan, a strategic and doctrinal positioning that helped him secure crucial patronage from both the Kyoto court and the Kamakura shogunate rather than provoking the kind of institutional hostility that met Hōnen's more exclusivist claims.

Eisai's most important institutional achievements—the founding of Kenninji in Kyoto in 1202, with the site donated and its construction patronized by the shogun Minamoto no Yoriie, and his later activity in Kamakura itself under continued shogunal patronage—illustrate how successfully he cultivated support from both the Kyoto aristocratic establishment and the Kamakura warrior government simultaneously. Notably, Kenninji at its founding was not a purely Rinzai institution: it combined Zen practice with continuing Tendai and esoteric (mikkyō) ritual under one roof, a hybridity that itself illustrates how gradually a distinct, institutionally separate Zen identity emerged in Japan. This dual patronage base helps explain why early Rinzai institutions grew rapidly during the thirteenth century, even though, as noted below, documented Zen patronage remained concentrated among comparatively narrow elite circles. Eisai's Rinzai lineage also carried with it Song-Chinese cultural practices, including the cultivation and consumption of tea, which Eisai promoted partly for its medicinal and meditative benefits in a work later known as the Kissa Yōjōki—an early link, well before the more formalized tea ceremony (chanoyu) that developed in later centuries and is examined further in Chapter 38, in a much longer historical chain connecting Zen monastic culture to Japanese tea practice.

Section 26.9

Dōgen and Sōtō Zen

Dōgen (1200–1253) followed a considerably different path than Eisai, both institutionally and geographically. After early Tendai training and a period of study within the emerging Japanese Rinzai community, Dōgen traveled to Song China in 1223, studying there under the Caodong (Japanese: Sōtō) lineage master Rujing before returning to Japan in 1227 convinced that authentic awakening was inseparable from the sustained, disciplined practice of zazen itself, rather than a distinct goal to be achieved through meditation as a means to a separate end. This teaching, often summarized by the phrase shushō ittō ("practice and enlightenment are one"), placed less emphasis than Rinzai teaching on kōan contemplation as a device for triggering sudden insight and more emphasis on prolonged, disciplined, and, in Dōgen's view, intrinsically enlightened seated meditation.

Rather than building his community primarily around the court and shogunal patronage networks Eisai had cultivated, Dōgen relocated to Echizen province in the early 1240s with the support of the regional warrior Hatano Yoshishige, whose patronage enabled the founding of the monastery later known as Eiheiji in 1244. There, away from the political and social centers of Kyoto and Kamakura, Dōgen emphasized strict monastic discipline and a community organized around sustained communal meditative practice rather than public teaching to lay elites. This does not mean Dōgen's tradition developed in complete isolation from warrior patronage—the Hatano family's support was indispensable to Eiheiji's founding—but rather that his patronage came from a regional warrior house rather than from the court or shogunal center itself, and that his own emphasis fell overwhelmingly on monastic practice rather than elite public teaching. His major written work, the Shōbōgenzō, compiled over many years in a philosophically demanding and often deliberately paradoxical style, addresses the nature of practice, time, being, and awakening with a subtlety that later generations of scholars, both Buddhist and secular, have continued to study closely. As with Eisai's Rinzai lineage, Dōgen's Sōtō tradition institutionalized more fully in the centuries after his death rather than during his own lifetime, and its later strong association with rural temple networks and lay funerary practice, in particular, developed considerably later than the thirteenth century itself.

Section 26.10

Older Buddhism Did Not Disappear

No account of Kamakura-period religion is historically responsible if it leaves the impression that Tendai, Shingon, and the older Nara schools faded into irrelevance once the devotional and meditative movements discussed above began to attract followers. The reverse is closer to the truth. Enryakuji on Mount Hiei and Kongōbuji on Mount Kōya remained among the wealthiest landholders in the country throughout the Kamakura period, trained large numbers of clergy and many of the period's major reformers, including, as this chapter has repeatedly noted, most of the founders of the supposedly "new" movements themselves, and continued to command court and aristocratic patronage on a scale that dwarfed the resources available to Hōnen's, Shinran's, or even Nichiren's followers during the thirteenth century itself.

These older schools also produced their own significant reformers during this period, figures whose careers are too easily overshadowed by the more famous founders discussed above. Myōe (1173–1232), a Kegon-school monk based at Kōzanji near Kyoto, mounted a serious doctrinal critique of Hōnen's exclusive nenbutsu teaching while pursuing his own rigorous program of meditative practice, textual scholarship, and devotion to the historical Buddha, illustrating that intellectually serious reform could and did occur from within established institutions rather than only against them. Eison (1201–1290) and his disciple Ninshō (1217–1303), monks associated with the Ritsu school's emphasis on strict monastic precepts, combined precept revival with extensive practical charity, including the establishment of hospitals, the care of travelers, outcasts, and the sick, and infrastructure projects such as bridge and road construction—activity that connected rigorous traditional monastic discipline directly to visible social welfare in ways that complicate any simple narrative pitting an inward-looking, ritual-obsessed old Buddhism against a socially engaged new Buddhism.

Understanding this institutional persistence matters for more than doctrinal accuracy. It shapes how students should read the rest of Japanese religious history: the eventual prominence of Jōdo Shinshū, Nichiren-shū, Rinzai, and Sōtō as major branches of Japanese Buddhism reflects centuries of later institutional development, not an instantaneous thirteenth-century religious revolution that swept the older schools aside.

Section 26.11

Kami, Buddhas, Shrines, and Temples

Alongside the Buddhist institutions and movements examined above, kami worship at shrines throughout the country continued as a pervasive feature of religious life in Kamakura Japan, but students must resist projecting the modern, institutionally separate categories of "Shinto" and "Buddhism" backward onto this period; this chapter therefore prefers the terms "kami worship" and "shrine traditions" to "Shinto" when describing the medieval religious world. Since at least the Nara period, Japanese religious practice had developed through the framework historians call shinbutsu shūgō, "the combinatory practice of kami and buddhas," in which local kami were widely understood as connected to, and in many interpretations as manifestations or protective guardians of, specific buddhas and bodhisattvas, a theological framework often described using the term honji suijaku, "original ground, manifest trace," in which a kami represented a locally accessible "trace" of an underlying Buddhist "original ground"—though the specific identifications proposed within this framework varied considerably across shrines, schools, and periods rather than following one fixed scheme. Major shrine complexes typically maintained affiliated Buddhist temples on the same grounds or in close association, staffed in part by Buddhist clergy performing rites intended to benefit the resident kami as well as human worshippers. The shrine deity Hachiman illustrates this combinatory world vividly: understood from an early period as a powerful protective kami, Hachiman was integrated into Buddhist frameworks and came to be honored with the title Hachiman Daibosatsu, "Great Bodhisattva Hachiman," and was closely associated with the Minamoto warrior lineage examined in Chapters 22 through 24.

Pilgrimage to shrines and temples alike remained a significant feature of religious practice across social classes throughout the Kamakura period, ranging from elite court pilgrimages to sites such as Kumano, already well established by the Heian period, to more modest local pilgrimage and festival participation among farming and urban communities. Warriors, like courtiers before them, patronized shrines as well as temples, sought divine favor and protection through shrine ritual before campaigns, and, in the case of the Minamoto and later Hōjō, cultivated particular association with Hachiman shrines as expressions of lineage identity and martial legitimacy. Students should understand that this shrine-temple religious world was not a secondary or peripheral feature of Kamakura religious life running alongside the "real" story of Buddhist sectarian development discussed in earlier sections, but rather the pervasive, everyday religious fabric within which court aristocrats, warriors, and ordinary people alike lived their devotional lives.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinbutsu Shūgō

Shinbutsu shūgō, "the combinatory practice of kami and buddhas," refers to the pervasive medieval Japanese religious pattern in which kami worship and Buddhist practice operated together rather than as separate, competing religious systems. Shrines routinely maintained affiliated Buddhist temples, Buddhist clergy performed rites at shrine precincts, and kami were widely interpreted, through the honji suijaku framework, as locally accessible expressions connected to specific buddhas and bodhisattvas, though the precise theological mapping varied across sites and traditions.

Students should treat the modern institutional separation between "Shinto" and "Buddhism"—formalized only during the Meiji-era policy of shinbutsu bunri, examined in Chapter 55—as a much later development. Applying that later separation to Kamakura-period religious practice would misrepresent a religious world in which most people experienced kami worship and Buddhist devotion as complementary, interwoven parts of the same everyday religious life rather than as distinct or competing traditions.

Section 26.12

Religious Institutions as Economic and Political Powers

Chapter 25 examined how the shōen estate system and the Kamakura shogunate's jitō and shugo appointments created overlapping and frequently contested claims to land, revenue, and jurisdiction. Major temples and shrines were central, not incidental, participants in this contested landscape. Enryakuji, Kongōbuji, Kōfukuji, and other great religious institutions held extensive shōen estates across many provinces, employed substantial administrative staffs to manage those holdings, and, particularly in the case of Enryakuji's armed monk forces, could bring direct coercive pressure to bear on rival institutions, provincial officials, and even the imperial court itself when disputes over landholding, appointments, or precedence arose. These institutions were also frequent litigants within the very land-dispute adjudication machinery Chapter 25 described, submitting claims to shogunal courts, negotiating with jitō stewards over revenue division, and defending established privileges against encroachment.

Religious institutions additionally served as centers of learning, manuscript production, medical knowledge, and, as Section 26.10 noted regarding Eison and Ninshō, organized charitable welfare activity, functions that gave them social importance extending well beyond narrowly religious or economic concerns. Understanding temples and shrines as landholders, employers, litigants, and political actors—rather than only as sites of worship and doctrine—connects this chapter's religious history directly back to the institutional and legal history developed in Chapter 25, and it helps explain why new devotional movements challenging established religious authority, such as those associated with Hōnen and Nichiren, provoked responses that were as much political and economic as they were doctrinal.

Section 26.13

Women and Religious Life

Women participated in Kamakura religious life in varied and sometimes contradictory ways. As patrons, aristocratic and warrior women alike commissioned temple construction, sutra copying, and memorial rites, particularly on behalf of deceased husbands, children, and other kin, continuing a pattern of female religious patronage well documented in the Heian period and discussed in Chapter 19. As direct participants in devotional movements, women appear among the recorded followers of Hōnen, Shinran, and later Pure Land teachers; Shinran's own wife, Eshinni, left behind letters that provide valuable, if limited, direct evidence of a woman's perspective on Pure Land practice and household religious life during this period, distinguishing her testimony from the retrospective hagiography that shapes much of the evidence for Shinran's own career.

At the same time, women's religious lives continued to be shaped by significant institutional and doctrinal constraints inherited from earlier periods. Ideas linking female physiology, particularly menstruation and childbirth, to ritual impurity continued to justify the exclusion of women from certain sacred spaces, most notably the summit precincts of Mount Kōya, from which women remained formally barred throughout the Kamakura period and for centuries afterward. Convents for women existed, and widowed aristocratic and warrior women not infrequently took religious vows following a husband's death—a pattern with clear political as well as spiritual dimensions, since religious retirement could allow a widow to manage household or estate affairs, as examined for Hōjō Masako in Chapter 25, from a position that combined religious authority with continued practical influence. Understanding women's religious lives in Kamakura Japan therefore requires holding together genuine opportunities for devotional participation and patronage alongside persistent structural exclusions rooted in older ideas about ritual purity and clerical authority.

Section 26.14

Literature and the Memory of War

The Tale of the Heike (Heike Monogatari) stands as one of the defining literary achievements of the Kamakura period and one of the most historically significant works discussed in this textbook, precisely because of the complicated relationship it holds to the historical events it describes. During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the work developed through interacting written and oral-performance traditions rather than through a single linear progression from spoken word to fixed text. Itinerant, often blind performers known as biwa hōshi recited episodes of the Genpei War, examined as a political and military event in Chapter 23, to musical accompaniment on the biwa lute, shaping and reshaping the material through repeated performance in important recitational lineages, while other versions circulated primarily as texts intended for reading, developed and copied by compilers working in parallel with, rather than simply after, the performance tradition. This process produced numerous distinct textual lineages rather than a single identifiable author working at a single moment; the oldest surviving dated manuscript, the read-lineage Engyōbon, carries a colophon dated 1309, well over a century into this ongoing process of textual development.

Historians must therefore use The Tale of the Heike with considerable care. It is an invaluable source for understanding how thirteenth-century audiences remembered, interpreted, and derived meaning from the Genpei War, and it preserves genuine historical information about major figures, battles, and political developments that can be cross-checked, where possible, against other sources. But it is not a contemporary eyewitness chronicle, and its narrative structure and thematic emphases—above all its famous opening invocation of impermanence, comparing the fate of the proud Taira clan to the fading sound of a temple bell—reflect Buddhist literary and religious sensibility as much as documentary intent. The work's enduring emphasis on mujō, impermanence, and its sympathetic, often tragic portrayal of defeated Taira warriors alongside triumphant Minamoto figures, reveals a great deal about how a society steeped in Buddhist teaching about the transience of worldly power made sense of a recent and traumatic civil war, functioning simultaneously as literature, popular entertainment, and a form of religious meditation on loss.

Kamo no Chōmei's short prose work Hōjōki (c. 1212) offers a complementary, more directly personal window onto the same broader sensibility. Writing from a small mountain hermitage after withdrawing from court society, Chōmei catalogued a series of disasters he had witnessed or heard about in Kyoto—a devastating fire, a whirlwind, the transfer of the capital, famine, and an earthquake—using these calamities to reflect on the impermanence of human dwellings, social position, and worldly security. Although Chōmei's immediate social world was more aristocratic than martial, his reflections on instability, loss, and withdrawal from society resonate closely with the same religious and cultural currents examined throughout this chapter, and his work is frequently read alongside The Tale of the Heike as a complementary meditation on the same historical moment.

Section 26.15

Poetry, Painting, Sculpture, and Warrior Patronage

Kamakura-period culture is too often described in popular accounts as a sharp break from Heian refinement toward a rougher, more martial aesthetic sensibility. The actual pattern is more interesting and more historically accurate: courtly aesthetic traditions continued to flourish, sometimes reaching new heights of technical accomplishment, even as warrior patrons entered the cultural picture and new artistic developments, particularly in sculpture, responded directly to the physical destruction and reconstruction needs created by recent warfare. The Shin Kokinshū, an imperially commissioned poetry anthology completed in 1205 under the guiding influence of the retired emperor Go-Toba, exemplifies the first pattern: a work of extraordinary formal sophistication and allusive complexity, continuing and refining Heian court poetic conventions well into the supposedly "warrior" Kamakura age, and demonstrating that Kyoto's aristocratic culture remained creatively vital rather than merely nostalgic during this period.

The Kei school of Buddhist sculptors, particularly the master carvers Unkei and Kaikei, illustrates the second pattern. Commissioned extensively to help rebuild Nara temples such as Tōdaiji and Kōfukuji following their partial destruction during fighting late in the Genpei War, Kei-school workshops produced monumental and strikingly realistic wooden sculpture—guardian figures, portrait statues, and devotional images—characterized by dynamic poses, careful attention to anatomical detail, and an expressive intensity that many art historians consider a hallmark achievement of Kamakura-period visual culture. This sculptural realism responded partly to religious and commemorative needs tied directly to recent warfare and its human and material costs, and it attracted patronage from court, temple, and warrior sponsors alike, illustrating cultural production as a genuine point of convergence between older aristocratic and newer warrior patronage networks rather than a battleground between opposing aesthetic worlds.

Portraiture more broadly, including realistic painted and sculpted images of individual monks, courtiers, and warriors, developed considerably during this period, reflecting both Buddhist memorial practice—portraits often served devotional functions connected to a subject's posthumous religious wellbeing—and a broader cultural interest in individual likeness and historical memory that connects directly to this chapter's earlier discussion of literary memorialization in The Tale of the Heike.

Section 26.16

Horses, Bows, and the Training of Warriors

Having examined religion and culture at length, this chapter now turns to the concrete, practical world of warrior training itself, a subject too often obscured by later idealized images of the samurai. The core skills demanded of a Kamakura-period warrior (bushi or musha) centered on mounted archery, horsemanship, and the practical management of weapons, horses, and household retainers—skills developed through years of practice beginning in childhood and reinforced through ritualized display, hunting, and actual campaign experience rather than through formal doctrinal instruction of any kind.

Mounted archery, combining skilled horsemanship with the ability to draw, aim, and release the distinctively long and asymmetrical Japanese composite bow (yumi) while riding at speed, represented the single most prestigious and demanding warrior skill of this period, reflected in the continued ritual importance of practices such as yabusame, ceremonial mounted archery performed at shrines before target boards, and inuoumono and kasagake, related mounted archery exercises performed at various targets, both practiced as much for ceremonial display and shrine ritual as for direct battlefield training, and both illustrating the close connection between martial skill and religious ritual noted in Section 26.11's discussion of Hachiman and warrior lineage identity. Hunting, particularly large organized hunts sponsored by shogunal or provincial authorities, likewise combined practical training in tracking, archery, and coordinated group movement with important functions of political display, retainer bonding, and territorial assertion.

Beyond mounted archery, warrior training included swordsmanship, though the sword held less battlefield centrality during this period than in later popular imagination, spear and polearm handling, wrestling and close combat techniques, and the practical maintenance of armor, weapons, and horses, a genuinely significant logistical undertaking given the expense of quality horses and equipment. Household organization also formed part of a warrior's practical competence: managing retainers, tenant cultivators, and family land holdings, negotiating obligations of service to a lord, and maintaining the family's standing and reputation among peers and rivals were as much a part of "warrior" life as combat skill itself, reflecting the fact that Kamakura-period bushi were simultaneously fighters, landholders, and household managers rather than specialized soldiers detached from economic and social responsibility.

Historical Skill Focus

Horse and Bow before Bushidō

Consider what a thirteenth-century warrior actually needed to know in order to serve effectively: how to ride a horse skillfully enough to control it while drawing and firing a bow at a moving target; how to select, feed, and maintain a war horse representing a substantial portion of household wealth; how to string, care for, and repair a composite bow that could lose effectiveness if improperly stored or maintained; how to organize and lead a small group of retainers on campaign, including provisioning and basic logistics; how to negotiate land tenure, service obligations, and inheritance within a vassalage system examined in Chapter 25; and how to conduct himself in ways that would maintain or improve his family's standing among lords and rival warrior houses.

This is a demanding, technical, and materially grounded occupational culture, closer in many respects to the concrete skill sets required of a professional mounted soldier and rural landholder than to the elaborate, formally codified ethical philosophy many people today associate with the word "samurai." The famous term bushidō, meaning "the way of the warrior" and often presented as an ancient and continuous moral code governing loyalty, honor, and self-sacrifice, was not compiled as a coherent body of ethical writing until the Edo period, and it took on much of its familiar modern form only through later reinterpretation, examined in Chapters 49 and 58. Kamakura warriors certainly held values connected to courage, family honor, reputation, and loyalty to a lord, but these values existed as practical, often regionally variable norms embedded in concrete social relationships, not as a systematized philosophical or ethical doctrine.

Section 26.17

Martial Life without Timeless Bushidō

This section makes explicit a historiographical correction implicit throughout Section 26.16. It is historically inaccurate to describe Kamakura-period warriors as adherents of a fully articulated ethical code comparable to the bushidō described in later Edo-period treatises such as the Hagakure, compiled in the early eighteenth century, or to the still later, more explicitly nationalist reformulations of warrior ethics developed during the Meiji period and examined in Chapter 58. Such retrospective projection creates several distinct historical distortions. It implies a false uniformity, treating warriors from different provinces, different lord-vassal relationships, and different levels of wealth and status as though they shared a single, coherent ethical outlook, when the actual evidence—court records, land disputes, vassalage agreements, and literary sources such as The Tale of the Heike—reveals considerable variation, self-interest, opportunism, and practical negotiation alongside genuine expressions of loyalty and courage.

It also implies a false continuity, obscuring the fact that warrior values changed considerably over subsequent centuries in response to changing political, religious, and social conditions; the disciplined, peacetime, bureaucratically embedded warrior ethic idealized in many Edo-period writings developed under circumstances—centuries of internal peace, a warrior class increasingly divorced from actual combat, and deliberate state-sponsored moral education—entirely different from the circumstances facing an actively campaigning, land-hungry, and politically insecure thirteenth-century bushi. Kamakura-period warrior conduct is better understood through the concrete institutional and legal mechanisms examined in Chapter 25—vassalage bonds, land grants in exchange for service, inheritance practices, and the shogunal courts that adjudicated disputes among warriors—than through appeal to an anachronistic, unified moral code.

None of this denies that Kamakura-period sources reflect genuine values: courage under arms, loyalty to a lord (though a loyalty that could be renegotiated or abandoned under sufficient strain, as various succession disputes and shifting allegiances throughout this period demonstrate), concern for family reputation, and pride in demonstrated skill all appear clearly in the sources. The historically responsible position is to recognize these as genuine, if variable and practically grounded, warrior values of their own time, distinct from the more systematized, literarily elaborated, and in some respects deliberately mythologized "way of the warrior" that later periods would construct, in part by looking back selectively at figures and episodes from exactly this Kamakura period examined in this textbook.

Section 26.18

Religion, Culture, and the Meaning of Warrior Society

Drawing the threads of this chapter together, the mature Kamakura world that emerges is neither the simple story of a rising warrior class replacing an exhausted court culture with a vigorous new martial religion, nor a story in which nothing changed beneath a continuous surface of unbroken Heian tradition. It is instead a genuinely plural and cumulative society: older Tendai, Shingon, and Nara-school institutions retained enormous wealth, political influence, and religious authority throughout the period, even as they produced their own internal reformers; new devotional movements associated with Hōnen, Shinran, Ippen, and Nichiren offered accessible, often controversial paths to salvation that provoked serious institutional resistance rather than being adopted as harmless supplements to existing practice; Zen traditions associated with Eisai and Dōgen entered Japan through direct connection to Song China and found early patronage among a comparatively narrow elite rather than the warrior class as a whole; shrine-temple religious practice continued to pervade daily and elite life alike without the sharp Shinto-Buddhist distinction familiar from later periods; and warrior culture developed through concrete, practical training in horsemanship, archery, and household management rather than through any fully formed ethical code.

Cultural production during this period likewise reflects convergence rather than replacement: the continued refinement of court poetry in works such as the Shin Kokinshū, the powerful new sculptural realism of the Kei school responding to wartime destruction, and the literary transformation of recent civil war into enduring meditations on impermanence in The Tale of the Heike and the Hōjōki all demonstrate a society processing rapid political change through inherited aesthetic and religious frameworks even as those frameworks themselves continued to develop. Understanding Kamakura Japan as plural, cumulative, and still in active religious and cultural motion—rather than as a settled, uniform "age of the samurai" governed by a timeless warrior code—prepares students to understand how this same society would respond, in Chapter 27, to a genuinely unprecedented external shock: the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281, which would test the religious institutions, warrior training, and political structures examined across this chapter and the one preceding it in ways no earlier crisis in the Kamakura period had done.

A Pure Land Teaching: Hōnen on the Nenbutsu

Creator: Hōnen and later compilers of his teachings · Date: teachings dating from the 1170s onward, compiled by disciples · Type: Religious instructional writing (paraphrase, not verbatim translation)

Context: Hōnen's teachings survive through a combination of his own shorter writings and the records and testimonies compiled by his disciples after his 1207 exile and subsequent death in 1212. Because exact wording varies across translations and manuscript traditions, the passage below is a careful paraphrase representing the substance of Hōnen's core teaching rather than a verbatim quotation from any specific translated edition.

Paraphrase of Hōnen's core teaching (not a verbatim quotation): Hōnen taught that in this present declining age, people of every rank—learned or unlearned, wealthy or poor, virtuous or burdened by past wrongdoing—could rely wholly on sincere invocation of Amida Buddha's name, trusting in Amida's vow to bring all who called upon him to rebirth in the Pure Land, without needing to complete the more demanding meditative and ritual practices required by earlier Buddhist teaching.

Analysis: This teaching's radical accessibility helps explain both its broad appeal across social classes and the alarm it provoked among established clergy, who worried that it rendered centuries of monastic training, ritual sponsorship, and institutional authority unnecessary. The passage should be read alongside Section 26.3's discussion of Hōnen's Tendai training and his eventual exile, since the teaching's simplicity did not mean it was doctrinally uncontroversial or institutionally unthreatening.

The Tale of the Heike: Literary Memory of the Genpei War

Creator: Composite oral and textual tradition developed by biwa hōshi performers and later compilers · Date: developed through the thirteenth century, with variant manuscript traditions extending later · Type: Epic literary narrative rooted in historical events

Context: The work opens with a well-known passage comparing the fate of the once-dominant Taira clan, examined in Chapter 22 and Chapter 23, to the fading sound of a temple bell, framing the entire narrative through the Buddhist concept of impermanence discussed in Section 26.14. The work developed through interacting oral-performance and written traditions, and survives in multiple textual lineages rather than a single authoritative original.

Paraphrase of the opening theme (not a verbatim quotation): The sound of a temple bell announces that all things are impermanent; the color of blossoms proclaims that what flourishes must eventually fall; the proud do not endure for long, resembling nothing more than a dream on a spring night, and even the mighty are, in the end, brought low like dust before the wind.

Analysis: As Section 26.14 explains, this framing reflects thirteenth-century Buddhist literary and religious sensibility as much as documentary intent. The work remains valuable evidence for how contemporaries and near-contemporaries made sense of the Genpei War, but its narrative and thematic choices should not be mistaken for the kind of direct documentary record historians would find in a legal record, land document, or diplomatic letter.

Historical Debate

Was There a "Kamakura New Buddhism"?

The question: Should historians describe the religious developments associated with Hōnen, Shinran, Ippen, Nichiren, Eisai, and Dōgen as a coherent, historically significant "new Buddhism" that transformed Japanese religious life, or should this framework be substantially set aside in favor of an emphasis on continuity with older institutions?

Interpretation A — The "new Buddhism" framework remains useful: Some historians argue that despite valid criticisms of oversimplification, these six figures did share genuinely significant characteristics: each developed a distinctive new lineage, practice, or critique of established teaching that attracted committed followings and produced enormous later institutional consequences. This holds even though the founders did not all respond to shared conditions in the same way—Hōnen's, Shinran's, and Nichiren's simplified, broadly accessible central practices differ considerably from Dōgen's demanding program of sustained monastic meditation—and even though not every founder invoked the "latter age of the Dharma" (mappō) as a central justification. Grouping them still highlights real, historically important parallels in institutional innovation during a turbulent religious age.
Interpretation B — The framework overstates a coherent, contemporaneous "movement": Other historians argue that treating these figures as a unified movement retroactively imposes a coherence they did not possess in their own lifetimes, obscures the fact that most trained within and initially remained connected to older institutions, and gives insufficient attention to the continued dominance, wealth, and vitality of Tendai, Shingon, and the Nara schools throughout the period, whose own reformers, such as Myōe and Eison, receive far less popular attention despite their genuine historical importance.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of biographical and institutional evidence concerning the six founders and the older schools, differing chiefly in how much emphasis to place on later sectarian institutionalization versus documented conditions during the thirteenth century itself.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes how later chapters, particularly those addressing Muromachi-period Zen culture (Chapter 30) and Tokugawa-period religious policy (Chapter 47), describe the long-term development of Japanese Buddhist institutions and their relationship to political authority.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation, and explain which framework you find more historically persuasive, and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Many Kamakura Religions: Six Points of View

A Kyoto aristocrat patronizing established Buddhism: Continued sponsorship of Tendai and Shingon ritual, temple construction, and sutra copying represented both genuine religious devotion and the maintenance of centuries-old family and institutional ties that connected aristocratic identity directly to established religious authority.
A Kamakura warrior sponsoring a temple: Temple sponsorship could serve simultaneously as an expression of religious devotion, a memorial obligation to fallen kin and comrades from recent warfare, and a public assertion of the sponsoring family's wealth, status, and legitimacy within the warrior social order examined in Chapter 25.
A Pure Land devotee of humble status: For a farmer, laborer, or urban poor believer with no realistic access to elaborate ritual sponsorship or years of monastic training, the promise that sincere nenbutsu recitation alone could secure rebirth in the Pure Land offered a genuinely transformative form of religious hope and dignity.
A Zen monk with Song Chinese connections: Participation in an internationally connected monastic lineage, transmitted directly from Song China, offered access to prestigious continental learning, meditative discipline, and, for figures such as Eisai, valuable patronage relationships with both court and shogunal elites.
A woman managing household memorial practices: Commissioning sutra copying, temple donations, and memorial rites for deceased family members offered a woman genuine religious agency and household authority, even as she remained subject to structural exclusions, such as prohibitions on entering certain sacred precincts, rooted in older ideas about ritual purity.
A village cultivator participating in shrine-temple ritual: Local festival and shrine practice, blending kami worship with Buddhist ritual elements in the combinatory pattern described in Section 26.11, provided a framework for communal identity, agricultural ritual, and protection from misfortune that operated largely independent of the sectarian religious developments unfolding among elites in Kyoto and Kamakura.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Heian Religion into Kamakura Religion

What Changed

  • Proliferation of new devotional movements emphasizing simple, repeatable central practices (nenbutsu, daimoku)
  • Introduction of institutionally organized Zen lineages directly connected to Song-dynasty China
  • Intensified itinerant preaching networks carrying devotional practice into rural and non-elite communities
  • Emergence of warrior patrons as significant, though not dominant, sponsors of religious institutions and culture
  • New literary forms, above all The Tale of the Heike, processing recent civil war through Buddhist frameworks of impermanence

What Stayed the Same

  • Continued dominance in wealth and landholding of Tendai, Shingon, and the great Nara temples
  • Continued practice of combinatory kami-buddha worship (shinbutsu shūgō) without a sharp institutional separation
  • Continued aristocratic patronage of court poetry and other Heian-derived cultural forms
  • Continued structural exclusions facing women in certain religious contexts
  • Continued reliance on land-based wealth and patronage networks to sustain major religious institutions

Why the pattern matters: Treating Kamakura religious history as additive and plural, rather than as a story of straightforward succession, prevents students from imagining Japanese religious history as a series of clean sectarian replacements rather than an ongoing, layered process of institutional persistence and innovation.

Cause and Effect

War, Instability, and Devotional Religion

Long-term causes

Centuries of institutional Buddhist development concentrated wealth and doctrinal authority in a small number of elite temple complexes, while, for many Buddhist thinkers and communities, ideas associated with the declining "latter age of the Dharma" (mappō) provided one important framework, among others, for interpreting religious difficulty and social disorder.

Short-term causes

The Hōgen and Heiji disturbances, the Genpei War, repeated famine, and the political upheaval accompanying the rise of warrior government created acute social dislocation and mortality that intensified demand for accessible religious consolation and memorial practice.

Effect

New devotional and meditative movements attracted broad followings across social classes, provoked serious institutional resistance from established schools, and generated new literary and artistic forms processing recent violence and loss.

Long-term consequence

The devotional and meditative movements of this period laid groundwork for religious traditions—Jōdo Shinshū, Nichiren-shū, Rinzai, and Sōtō—that would institutionalize fully in later centuries and remain major branches of Japanese Buddhism into the present.

Historical complexity: Social instability did not mechanically produce new religious movements in any simple, deterministic sense; it created conditions of heightened receptivity that particular teachers, drawing on particular doctrinal traditions and personal religious experience, responded to in distinct and not always compatible ways.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined religion, culture, and martial life within the mature warrior-governed society described institutionally in Chapter 25, arguing throughout that this world was plural and cumulative rather than a simple story of a rising warrior class replacing an exhausted aristocratic and religious order with a unified new samurai faith and ethic. Older Buddhist institutions—Tendai on Mount Hiei, Shingon on Mount Kōya, and the great Nara temples—retained enormous landed wealth, doctrinal authority, and political influence throughout the Kamakura period, continuing to train large numbers of clergy and many of the period's major reformers, including nearly all of the founders later associated with supposedly "new" religious movements.

At the same time, a series of significant devotional and meditative movements developed during this period. Hōnen taught exclusive reliance on the nenbutsu as sufficient for salvation, attracting broad followings and provoking institutional exile. Shinran extended this teaching toward an emphasis on inward faith (shinjin) and rejected traditional distinctions between monastic and lay identity. Ippen and the broader itinerant Pure Land tradition carried devotional practice directly to rural populations through mobile preaching. Nichiren insisted on the exclusive sufficiency of the Lotus Sutra and the daimoku chant, linking his religious claims explicitly to political warning and enduring severe persecution as a result. Eisai and Dōgen each introduced distinct Zen lineages from Song China, Eisai cultivating dual court and shogunal patronage while continuing Tendai affiliations, and Dōgen withdrawing to remote monastic practice centered on the inseparability of meditation and awakening.

The chapter has stressed that none of these movements existed as fully institutionalized, doctrinally settled sects during their founders' own lifetimes, and that older schools produced their own significant reformers, such as Myōe and the socially engaged Ritsu-school figures Eison and Ninshō, whose work complicates any simple narrative pitting a stagnant old Buddhism against a vital new Buddhism. The chapter has also examined the pervasive combinatory practice of kami and Buddhist worship (shinbutsu shūgō), the substantial economic and political power wielded by major temples and shrines within the same contested landholding system examined in Chapter 25, and the varied but structurally constrained religious roles available to women during this period.

Turning to culture, the chapter examined The Tale of the Heike as literary memory of the Genpei War rather than direct documentary evidence, alongside Kamo no Chōmei's Hōjōki as a complementary meditation on impermanence and disaster. It traced continued aristocratic cultural production in works such as the Shin Kokinshū alongside striking new developments in realistic Buddhist sculpture produced by the Kei school during the reconstruction of temples damaged in recent warfare, demonstrating cultural convergence between court and warrior patronage rather than displacement of one by the other.

Finally, the chapter examined the concrete, practical training of Kamakura-period warriors—mounted archery, horsemanship, hunting, weapon maintenance, and household management—and argued explicitly against attributing to these warriors a fully articulated ethical code of the kind later called bushidō, which developed its familiar form only through Edo-period and subsequently Meiji-period reinterpretation. Kamakura warrior values, while genuine, were practical, materially grounded, and considerably more variable than later idealizations suggest.

These findings matter for the textbook's ongoing argument about historical complexity and periodization. Just as Chapter 25 cautioned against imagining Kamakura government as either a complete break from or a simple continuation of earlier court rule, this chapter has cautioned against imagining Kamakura religion and culture as either a wholesale replacement of Heian tradition or an unchanged continuation of it. The mature warrior society examined here combined institutional persistence, genuine religious innovation, continued aristocratic cultural production, and a still-developing warrior occupational culture—a combination that sets the stage directly for Chapter 27's examination of how this same complex, plural society would respond to the unprecedented external threat of Mongol invasion.

Students who understand Kamakura religious and cultural life as layered and cumulative, populated by real historical actors negotiating genuine institutional tension rather than by a uniform cast of "medieval Buddhists" and "samurai," are well prepared to recognize similar complexity in the chapters that follow, particularly as the textbook returns repeatedly to questions of religious change, cultural production, and the historical construction of warrior identity across subsequent centuries.

Key Terms

Nenbutsu
The invocation "Namu Amida Butsu," central to Pure Land practice, understood by Hōnen and his successors as sufficient for rebirth in Amida's Pure Land.
Daimoku
Nichiren's chant "Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō," expressing devotion to the Lotus Sutra as the sole sufficient teaching for the current age.
Mappō
The Buddhist concept of the "latter age of the Dharma," a period of perceived spiritual decline; for many Kamakura-period Buddhists it provided one important framework for understanding the appeal of some more accessible devotional paths.
Shinjin
Shinran's concept of true, entrusting faith in Amida Buddha's vow, understood as more central to salvation than repeated ritual practice.
Zazen
Seated meditation, the central practice of Zen Buddhism, emphasized especially strongly by Dōgen as inseparable from enlightenment itself.
Kōan
A paradoxical question or statement used, especially within Rinzai Zen, to provoke insight beyond ordinary conceptual thought.
Shinbutsu shūgō
The combinatory practice linking kami worship and Buddhist devotion, in which local kami were widely understood as manifestations or protectors connected to specific buddhas and bodhisattvas.
Honji suijaku
The theological framework describing kami as a locally manifest "trace" of an underlying Buddhist "original ground."
Jōdo-shū
The Pure Land tradition later organized around Hōnen's teaching of exclusive nenbutsu practice.
Jōdo Shinshū
The "True Pure Land" tradition later organized around Shinran's teachings, emphasizing shinjin and rejecting strict monastic-lay distinctions; institutionalized fully well after Shinran's own lifetime.
Nichiren-shū
The Buddhist tradition later organized around Nichiren's teachings emphasizing the exclusive sufficiency of the Lotus Sutra.
Rinzai
A Zen lineage introduced to Japan by Eisai, emphasizing kōan practice alongside zazen and cultivating close ties with court and shogunal elites.
Sōtō
A Zen lineage introduced to Japan by Dōgen, emphasizing sustained zazen as inseparable from enlightenment, institutionalized around Eiheiji.
Tendai
A major Buddhist school headquartered at Enryakuji on Mount Hiei, within which Hōnen, Shinran, and Nichiren all trained before departing from mainstream practice.
Shingon
A major esoteric Buddhist school founded by Kūkai, headquartered at Mount Kōya, remaining wealthy and influential throughout the Kamakura period.
Kegon
An older Nara-period Buddhist school associated in this chapter with the reformer Myōe.
Ritsu
A Buddhist school emphasizing strict monastic precepts, revived in this period by Eison and Ninshō alongside extensive charitable activity.
Biwa hōshi
Itinerant, often blind performers who recited episodes of The Tale of the Heike to musical accompaniment, shaping the work's oral development.
Yabusame
Ceremonial mounted archery performed at shrines, linking martial skill with religious ritual and public display.
Kei school
A lineage of Buddhist sculptors, including Unkei and Kaikei, known for dynamic, realistic sculpture produced during postwar temple reconstruction.
Bushidō (as historiographical caution)
A later, largely Edo-period and subsequently Meiji-period, systematized ethical code of the warrior; historians caution against attributing this fully formed code to Kamakura-period warriors.
Mujō
The Buddhist concept of impermanence, central to the thematic framing of The Tale of the Heike and the Hōjōki.
Shōen
Private landed estates, the economic base of both aristocratic and religious institutional wealth, examined in detail in Chapter 20 and revisited here regarding temple and shrine landholding.
Hachiman
A major protective kami integrated into Buddhist frameworks and honored as Hachiman Daibosatsu ("Great Bodhisattva Hachiman"), closely associated with the Minamoto warrior lineage.
Sōhei
A later historiographical label for armed temple affiliates and monastic forces maintained by major temple complexes such as Enryakuji; modern scholarship shows this personnel was diverse (low-ranking clerics, lay affiliates, estate warriors) rather than a single distinct "warrior monk" class.
Odori nenbutsu
Ecstatic dancing practice associated with Ippen's itinerant Pure Land preaching tradition.
Risshō Ankoku Ron
Nichiren's 1260 treatise warning Kamakura authorities that reliance on false teachings endangered the nation, provoking severe persecution.
Shōbōgenzō
Dōgen's major philosophical and religious work, exploring the nature of practice, time, and awakening.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the institutional history of Kamakura government developed in Chapters 24 and 25 into the lived religious, cultural, and martial experience of the people who inhabited that world. It has shown that even a society undergoing genuine and consequential political transformation—the rise of warrior government alongside an enduring imperial court—did not experience its religious and cultural life as a simple, clean break between an old world and a new one. Older institutions persisted and adapted; new movements emerged from within, and in tension with, those same institutions; and warrior culture developed through concrete practical training rather than through the fully formed ethical philosophy later generations would attribute to it.

The chapter's insistence on distinguishing thirteenth-century reality from later idealization—whether regarding "Kamakura new Buddhism," the supposed natural bond between Zen and the samurai, or the anachronistic attribution of a unified bushidō code—matters beyond this chapter's immediate subject. It models a form of historical discipline this textbook has emphasized since its earliest chapters: resisting the temptation to read later categories, later ideologies, and later national narratives backward into periods that did not yet possess them. Students should carry this discipline forward into subsequent chapters, particularly Chapter 30's treatment of Muromachi-period Zen culture and Chapter 58's treatment of the modern reinvention of martial tradition, both of which directly address how later periods reshaped and reinterpreted precisely the religious and martial world examined here.

The chapter has also reinforced a theme central to this textbook's account of premodern Japan: that court and warrior worlds, far from existing in simple opposition, remained deeply entangled in patronage, aesthetic production, and religious practice throughout the Kamakura period. The Shin Kokinshū's courtly refinement, the Kei school's dynamic sculpture, and the shared devotional world of shrine and temple worship all demonstrate a society whose different social worlds continued to interact, borrow from, and shape one another rather than developing in isolation.

Students who understand Kamakura Japan as a religiously plural, culturally interconnected, and still-developing warrior society are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 27, where this same society confronts the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281—an external shock that would test the religious institutions, warrior training, and political structures examined across this chapter and the one preceding it, and that would set in motion the strains eventually contributing to the fall of the Kamakura shogunate itself.
