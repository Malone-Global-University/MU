Part V — Ashikaga Japan and the Maritime World

The Ashikaga Shogunate

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the late fourteenth century, a warrior family from the eastern provinces governed from a large residence in the heart of Kyoto, a few streets from the imperial palace. Its head held the title of shōgun, sat among courtiers whose families had served emperors for centuries, and received envoys from China. Yet the same ruler could not simply order the great provincial houses that supported him, and he depended on their armies, their tax collection, and their willingness to remain in Kyoto rather than return to their provinces and act independently.

This chapter is about that peculiar and durable form of rule: the Ashikaga shogunate, often called the Muromachi bakufu after the Kyoto street on which the third shōgun, Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, built his principal residence in the late 1370s. It governed, in one form or another, from the late 1330s until 1573, though its ability to direct affairs beyond the capital region weakened sharply in the second half of the fifteenth century. The chapter concentrates on the period from the founding of the regime to the mid-fifteenth century, when the system's characteristic structure is most visible and before the succession crisis and warfare that Chapter 34 treats in full.

The central question is how the Ashikaga shogunate governed a realm in which the shōgun was powerful but political authority remained dispersed among multiple institutions and regional powers. The question deserves care because it is easy to answer badly. One tempting answer measures the Ashikaga against the Nara state or the Tokugawa and concludes that Muromachi government was weak or failed. Another treats the sequence of shōguns as the story itself, explaining events through the personalities of Takauji, Yoshimitsu, or Yoshimasa. This chapter instead treats the shogunate as a political system with characteristic institutions, characteristic partners, and characteristic tensions, and asks how that system worked before asking why it eventually came apart.

Students should keep several distinctions in view. Imperial legitimacy, court authority, shogunal authority, the power of provincial military governors, and actual governing capacity were not the same thing, and the Ashikaga period is one in which they diverged conspicuously. The emperor remained the source of titles and ritual sovereignty, and the court continued to function as an institution. The shōgun commanded the largest network of warrior loyalties and, in Kyoto itself, took over many of the policing, judicial, and fiscal functions that had once belonged to the court. The military governors, the shugo, exercised expanding provincial authority, though their power varied greatly by region, and below them local warriors, temples, shrines, and village communities each exercised real authority in their own spheres.

The chapter also insists that the Ashikaga were a warrior government but not simply Kamakura rebuilt in Kyoto. The Kamakura bakufu had been located far from the imperial capital and had governed through vassals, military governors, and land stewards whose powers were relatively limited. The Ashikaga established their headquarters in the capital itself, absorbed responsibilities of the court, and depended on military governors whose powers over provincial land and taxation were considerably greater than their Kamakura predecessors'. The result was a regime that could be assertive, at times ruthless, and culturally prestigious. The resulting political order combined strong shogunal authority in its core, the capital and surrounding Kinai provinces, with negotiated and delegated authority elsewhere in the archipelago.

The regime was also not the whole of Japan. Ashikaga authority reached the Kantō through a separate Ashikaga branch based in Kamakura, reached Kyushu and the northeast through deputies of uneven effectiveness, and touched the northern frontier and the Ryukyu kingdom only indirectly. The Ainu communities of Ezo and the rulers of Ryukyu, discussed in Chapter 32, were neither subjects of the bakufu nor extensions of it. They will appear here only where their relations with Ashikaga-era actors help explain how far shogunal power reached.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich in documents produced by government itself: the shogunal code known as the Kenmu shikimoku, ordinances, judgments, oaths, and land records, together with diaries kept by monks and courtiers and with war tales such as the Taiheiki that shaped later memory. These sources allow historians to see administration at work, but they also carry the viewpoints of the institutions that produced them, a problem discussed in Section 29.9.

This chapter follows directly from Chapter 28, which treated the Kenmu Restoration and the split between the Northern and Southern Courts, and prepares the ground for the rest of Part V. Chapter 30 will turn to the culture of Zen temples, Noh, and gardens, much of which flourished under shogunal patronage; Chapter 31 will examine the commercial economy on which the shogunate partly depended; Chapters 32 and 33 will turn to Ryukyu; and Chapter 34 will follow the breakdown of central authority in the Ōnin War. Chapter 29 supplies the political framework that makes those chapters intelligible.

Historical Context

Chapter 28 traced the collapse of the Kamakura bakufu in 1333, Emperor Go-Daigo's attempt to restore direct imperial rule, and the split that followed when Ashikaga Takauji broke with him. By 1336 Takauji had entered Kyoto, installed a rival member of the imperial line as emperor, and driven Go-Daigo to flight; Go-Daigo established a separate court at Yoshino, in the mountains south of the Nara basin. The resulting division into Northern and Southern Courts is the immediate setting of this chapter. It gave the Ashikaga both a problem and an opportunity. The problem was that their opponents could claim the legitimacy of an emperor in possession of the sacred regalia, according to their own account. The opportunity was that a warrior leader who could protect and use a cooperative imperial court held a powerful instrument of legitimacy.

The institutions the Ashikaga inherited were of several kinds. The court in Kyoto retained its ranks, offices, ceremonies, and, in principle, its claims to landed income, though by the fourteenth century many of those claims had been eroded by warrior encroachment. The Kamakura bakufu had left behind a model of warrior government based on the relationship between the shōgun and his housemen (gokenin), on provincial military governors (shugo) with limited police and military authority, and on land stewards (jitō) attached to estates. It had also left behind a body of law, the Jōei code of 1232, and a set of legal and administrative practices centered on adjudicating land disputes among warriors. Chapter 25 examined these institutions.

The countryside had changed as well. The estate (shōen) system, treated in Chapter 20, had steadily lost coherence as local warriors, stewards, and provincial governors took over income that once flowed to distant noble and temple proprietors. Village communities were increasingly organized as self-governing bodies with their own rules and collective identity, a development that becomes clearer in the fifteenth century. Meanwhile, trade and money had grown, and Kyoto's markets, moneylenders, and brewers supplied a tax base that later governments would learn to exploit; Chapter 31 examines this commercial world in detail.

Finally, the civil wars of the 1330s and 1340s were fought less as a clean contest between two courts than as a struggle in which warriors across the archipelago chose sides according to local rivalries, family divisions, and calculations of advantage. That practice of shifting allegiance, and the habit of rewarding warriors with land and office in exchange for military support, shaped the Ashikaga regime from its beginning. A ruler who had won power by attracting and rewarding followers found it difficult afterward to command those same followers as subordinates.

Section 29.1

From Civil War to Ashikaga Government

Ashikaga Takauji came from a branch of the Minamoto (Seiwa Genji) family with long ties to the Kamakura bakufu, and he had served it as a leading warrior until, in 1333, he turned against the Hōjō and helped capture the Rokuhara Tandai, the Kamakura bakufu's headquarters in Kyoto. His break with Go-Daigo in 1335 and his subsequent entry into Kyoto in 1336, covered in Chapter 28, left him at the head of the largest warrior following in the capital region. What he did next mattered as much as what he had done to reach that position. Rather than returning to the east, he stayed in Kyoto, backed a Northern Court emperor, and in the eleventh month of 1336 issued the Kenmu shikimoku, a brief statement of governing principles discussed in the Primary Source Window below. In 1338 the Northern Court emperor appointed him shōgun, though it is important to remember that the Ashikaga were exercising governing functions well before the title was formally conferred.

The early regime was not a tidy institution. Throughout the 1330s and 1340s Takauji fought to hold Kyoto and to bring warriors in the provinces to his side. Historians often describe the arrangement between Takauji and his brother Tadayoshi as a kind of dual government. In an influential formulation, Takauji held the personal, military relationship of lord and vassal, granting land and office in return for service, while Tadayoshi handled territorial administration, including the adjudication of land disputes. This description simplifies a complicated situation, but it captures a real distinction: the shōgun's power rested partly on personal bonds with followers and partly on administrative and judicial functions that could be delegated.

The relationship between the brothers, and between their supporters, eventually broke down. In the Kannō Disturbance of 1350–1352, the regime divided between Takauji and his powerful steward Kō no Moronao on one side and Tadayoshi and his allies on the other. The conflict drew in the Southern Court, with which Tadayoshi briefly cooperated, and it did not end until Tadayoshi's death in 1352, which tradition attributes to poison, though the circumstances remain unclear. The episode matters for this chapter not only as narrative but as evidence. It showed that warrior loyalty could shift according to local advantage, that the Southern Court could revive whenever Ashikaga solidarity faltered, and that the regime's main threats came from within its own leadership as much as from its declared enemies.

The regime also needed to justify itself. The Ashikaga sought religious as well as political legitimacy, and in the 1340s Takauji and Tadayoshi, advised by the Zen monk Musō Soseki, promoted the founding of a national network of provincial temples and pagodas (the ankokuji and rishōtō) to pray for the war dead, and built Tenryūji in Kyoto, associated with the memory of Go-Daigo. These initiatives, examined further in Chapter 30, tied the regime to Buddhist institutions and to a language of reconciliation at the very moment it was extending its authority. They also illustrate how Ashikaga governance combined coercion, patronage, and religious politics from the beginning.

By the time the Ashikaga were fully established in Kyoto, in the 1350s and 1360s, they had settled several basic features that distinguished their government. Its seat was the capital. Its head held a court title and increasingly participated in court ceremonial. It relied on military governors to bring provincial warriors into line. And its authority in the eastern provinces was exercised by a second Ashikaga branch, discussed in Section 29.2.

Primary Source Window

The Kenmu shikimoku (1336)

Creator: Issued in the name of Ashikaga Takauji, with the involvement of legal specialists among his advisers · Date: eleventh month of 1336 · Place: Kyoto · Type: Statement of governing principles, often called a code, in a preface-and-articles format

Context: Takauji issued the Kenmu shikimoku soon after occupying Kyoto and installing a Northern Court emperor, while Go-Daigo's court at Yoshino remained a rival. The document is short. It opens by addressing a practical question, namely where the warrior government should be seated, and then sets out a series of policy articles, traditionally counted as seventeen, on matters including the conduct of government, the treatment of religious institutions, the behavior of warriors, and the handling of legal cases.

Paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation: The document treats the location of the government as a matter to be settled by considering the wishes of the realm, and it presents the early Kamakura shogunate as a model of good governance whose practices the new regime intends to follow, while adding policy guidance suited to the circumstances of 1336.

Analysis: The document is not a comprehensive legal code on the model of the Jōei code of 1232. It is better read as a political programme issued at a moment of uncertainty. Its willingness to treat the location of the regime as an open question, and its evident decision to remain in Kyoto, shows that the Ashikaga understood the choice of capital as a political decision. Its invocation of the earlier Kamakura model presented the Ashikaga as legitimate inheritors of warrior government rather than as innovators. Its concern with religious institutions, with lawsuits, and with the conduct of warriors shows what the new regime wished to be seen to regulate. Historians should also note what the document does not show: it does not describe how the regime actually worked in practice, and as a text issued in the name of the leader, it represents the perspective of the Ashikaga leadership rather than of the warriors, temples, courtiers, and villagers who would live under it.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the location and purpose of the new government?
  2. Who issued it, when, and what political circumstances might explain its content?
  3. What can historians learn from it about how the Ashikaga wished to present their regime?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about how Ashikaga government actually worked?
Section 29.2

Kyoto, Kamakura, and the Geography of Shogunal Rule

The most obvious difference between the Kamakura and Ashikaga bakufu was location. The Kamakura bakufu had developed in the eastern warrior center of Kamakura, physically separated from the imperial court in Kyoto: warrior government coexisted with the court but did not sit inside it. The Ashikaga reversed that arrangement. Their principal seat was Kyoto, where they were exposed to the pressures of the court, the great temples, the capital's merchants, and the warrior houses that maintained residences there. The bakufu's later name comes from the Muromachi residence, popularly known as the "Flower Palace" (Hana no gosho), which Yoshimitsu built from about 1378 and occupied from 1381. Its position near the imperial palace, and its scale, were a statement about the relationship between the two.

Residence in Kyoto carried practical consequences. The shōgun took over many of the capital's policing and judicial functions from the older court offices, and the Samurai-dokoro, originally an office for supervising warriors, became responsible for maintaining order in Kyoto and adjudicating certain cases there. The bakufu also gained access to sources of revenue in the capital region, notably the taxation of moneylenders, sake brewers, and other commercial enterprises that were concentrated there, a subject examined in Section 29.5 and in Chapter 31. In effect, the shogunate became the dominant warrior authority for policing and adjudication in Kyoto, a role the Kamakura bakufu, seated in the east, had never held.

Kyoto residence also meant that provincial warriors had to come to the capital. The major shugo were normally expected to maintain households in Kyoto, where they attended the shōgun, took part in ceremonies, and served on shogunal councils, while deputies (shugodai) managed their provinces. Historians have often seen this arrangement as a mechanism of control, since it kept powerful men close to the shōgun, and also as a source of vulnerability, since absent governors depended on deputies whose loyalty could itself be uncertain. When the shōgun's authority faltered, the capital became a place where rival shugo, all armed and all present, could turn against one another.

Government of the east worked differently. In the 1340s and afterwards, Takauji placed his son Motouji, and then Motouji's descendants, in Kamakura as the Kantō kubō, an Ashikaga deputy with authority over the Kantō provinces, and over time also over neighboring provinces. The Kantō kubō had his own administrative machinery, an office of deputy (the Kantō kanrei, held by the Uesugi family), and considerable independence. The arrangement had a strategic logic, given the size and importance of the eastern warrior population, but it also created a second Ashikaga center whose head could and sometimes did resist Kyoto. Its later history, culminating in the conflicts of the 1430s and 1440s discussed in Section 29.8, shows the structural tension in the arrangement.

Elsewhere, the shogunate relied on deputies sent to key regions. A Kyushu deputy (tandai) was established to bring the island's warriors into the Ashikaga camp, and under Imagawa Ryōshun in the 1370s and 1380s this office was notably effective, before Yoshimitsu removed him in the mid-1390s. The northeast (Ōshū and Dewa) was managed through regional deputies and powerful local houses. In each case the shogunate worked through an intermediary whose interests did not always coincide with Kyoto's.

Section 29.3

The Shōgun and the Machinery of Government

At the center of the system stood the shōgun, whose authority combined several distinct components. He was the lord to whom warriors owed service in exchange for confirmation of land and office, and this personal relationship remained the foundation of Ashikaga power. He held court rank and office, which lent him ceremonial and legal status in the capital. He was, according to Ashikaga claims, the head of the Minamoto lineage. And he controlled a body of direct retainers and directly held lands that provided some material support. The distinction between these components explains why shogunal power could vary so much from reign to reign: a shōgun who commanded personal loyalty could compel obedience that a shōgun who merely held office could not.

Below the shōgun, the machinery of government developed over several decades and did not reach a settled form until the later fourteenth century. The most important figure was the shōgun's deputy, originally known as the shitsuji and, from the 1360s, as the kanrei. The kanrei presided over deliberations, transmitted the shōgun's decisions, and, especially when the shōgun was young or inattentive, managed government in his name. Hosokawa Yoriyuki, who served as kanrei during Yoshimitsu's youth, was the most notable early example. From the later fourteenth century the office was normally filled from three great houses, the Shiba, Hatakeyama, and Hosokawa, which historians commonly call the three kanrei houses.

Historical Vocabulary
Kanrei

The kanrei was the shōgun's chief deputy in the Muromachi bakufu, a post that grew out of the earlier office of shitsuji. The kanrei presided over central decision-making, conveyed shogunal orders, and mediated between the shōgun and the shugo. A parallel office, the Kantō kanrei, served the Kantō kubō in Kamakura, and was held by the Uesugi family.

The word should not be confused with regency in the Heian sense, in which a Fujiwara regent governed on behalf of an emperor. The kanrei served at the shōgun's pleasure and drew power from support of the shōgun and from his own house's strength. Because the kanrei was normally also a leading shugo, the office illustrates a central feature of the system: the man who helped run the government was also the head of one of the powerful regional houses the government had to manage.

The regime's administrative offices developed alongside the kanrei. The Samurai-dokoro, as noted, took charge of policing and criminal matters in Kyoto and was headed by a member of one of four families: the Isshiki, Akamatsu, Kyōgoku, and Yamana. Historians call these four houses the shishoku and, together with the three kanrei houses, they are sometimes described collectively as the group of seven great houses (sankan shishoku) that dominated the highest levels of the regime. The Mandokoro, headed by a hereditary administrative family, the Ise, managed finances and property. The Monchūjo handled documents, records, and legal proceedings, and the Hikitsukeshū, an adjudicating council of the early regime, dealt with land disputes before being replaced by other arrangements. Around the shōgun were his direct retainers, the hōkōshū, who provided military guard, staffed offices, and served as a counterweight to the great shugo, and who were closely tied to the shōgun's own lands and income.

These institutions merit attention because they show what kind of regime this was. The offices rotated among a limited set of families, which gave the leading shugo a stake in the system and a direct role in its operation. Administrative specialists from hereditary families gave continuity and expertise. Direct retainers gave the shōgun some independent force. The structure resembled a coalition more than a hierarchy, and the shōgun's task was to keep it together. A capable shōgun could play the great houses against each other, as Yoshimitsu did in the 1390s. A weaker shōgun found himself dependent on whichever coalition of houses controlled the capital.

Decision-making in this system was also partly consultative. Important matters were discussed among the shōgun, the kanrei, and other senior figures, and shugo participation gave decisions a consensual quality that could make them more durable, though also slower and more open to obstruction. Decisions concerning war, succession, and the disposal of shugo offices were among the most sensitive, since they directly affected the balance among the great houses.

Section 29.4

The Shugo and the Making of Regional Power

No feature of Ashikaga government has received more scholarly attention than the transformation of the shugo. Under the Kamakura bakufu, shugo had been military governors whose responsibilities were narrowly defined: they mobilized the region's housemen for guard duty at Kyoto, suppressed rebels, and pursued certain serious crimes. They did not, in principle, hold general authority over land or taxation, and estate proprietors, local stewards, and the bakufu itself retained their own jurisdictions. The Ashikaga era was different, and the change occurred largely because of the demands of the civil wars.

The shogunate needed men in the provinces who could raise armies, enforce decisions, and keep local warriors from defecting to the Southern Court. It therefore extended the shugo's powers by stages. In the 1340s it authorized shugo to deal with the seizure of standing crops during disputes, a practice that had become a common instrument of warrior pressure, and to carry out enforcement of judgments in land cases. In 1352 it permitted shugo in Ōmi, Mino, and Owari to claim half the proceeds of estate revenues for a year to supply their troops, the first hanzei, or "half-tax." Although intended as a temporary wartime measure, the practice was extended to other provinces and, in practice, became a means by which shugo took over income that had gone to court nobles and religious institutions. Shugo also acquired the ability to levy extraordinary taxes and services, to take on the collection of estate revenues under contract (shugo-uke), and to assign lands to local warriors in return for service.

These powers gave shugo a genuine territorial authority. They became lords of provinces in something close to the ordinary sense of the word, holding lands, commanding local warriors, and administering justice and revenue. Historians commonly use the term shugo daimyō for shugo who had developed this kind of regional lordship, though scholars debate how far the term applies uniformly and how closely to date the phenomenon.

Historical Vocabulary
Shugo daimyō

The term shugo daimyō is a modern scholarly label for shugo who acquired lordship over a province or group of provinces during the Muromachi period, combining the shugo office with military command, taxation, land control, and vassal relationships with local warriors. It is not a term used by contemporaries in this sense, and it should be distinguished from the sengoku daimyō of the sixteenth century, discussed in Chapter 35, who emerged from varied backgrounds—including shugo, shugodai, kokujin, and other established warrior houses—and increasingly built territorial regimes that did not depend on effective Ashikaga control.

The label matters because it highlights that Ashikaga government was, to an important degree, a coalition of regional lords who owed their positions partly to the shōgun and partly to their own resources. The shogunate could grant, confirm, or reassign shugo offices, but the shugo's real power rested on local support that the shōgun did not control.

Shugo power was nevertheless not unlimited. Many shugo were absent from their provinces, residing in Kyoto and governing through deputies. Local warriors, the kokujin, retained their own lands and ambitions, and often formed leagues (ikki) to protect their interests against shugo as well as against other threats. Estate proprietors resisted encroachment, sometimes successfully. Some provinces, such as Yamato, where the temple Kōfukuji was the dominant authority, had no shugo of the ordinary kind. In practice, a shugo's authority depended on his ability to build a following among provincial warriors, and the loyalty of those warriors was frequently negotiated rather than assumed.

The greatest houses accumulated multiple provinces. The Yamana, at their height in the late fourteenth century, were said to hold eleven provinces, roughly a sixth of the country, a fact that made them the object of Yoshimitsu's attention. In 1391 he exploited a division within the Yamana family to provoke the Meitoku Rebellion, in which Yamana forces were defeated and their holdings sharply reduced. In 1399 he moved against Ōuchi Yoshihiro, who held provinces in western Honshu and had built a powerful position that included influence over trade with Korea, and Yoshihiro was killed at Sakai in the Ōei Disturbance. These episodes demonstrate that a determined shōgun could cut down the greatest houses, but also that their power had reached a level at which such interventions were necessary and risky.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Shugo Power

Long-term causes

The erosion of the estate system, the growth of local warrior power, and the Kamakura bakufu's earlier experience of using shugo for military purposes created conditions in which provincial lordship could expand.

Short-term causes

The civil wars of the 1330s–1350s required the shogunate to reward supporters, raise armies quickly, and prevent defection, leading it to grant shugo enforcement powers and temporary fiscal privileges.

Event or transformation

Shugo acquired judicial, fiscal, and landholding powers, taking over income from estates and building networks of local warriors as retainers.

Immediate effects

The shogunate obtained a workable provincial administration and military base, while noble and temple proprietors saw their revenues eroded.

Long-term consequences

The great shugo houses became indispensable to the regime and, when the center weakened, capable of acting as rivals; their disputes fed into the crises of the fifteenth century.

Historical complexity

The shogunate did not lose power to the shugo in a simple transfer. It chose to delegate powers, gained real benefits, and sometimes reversed the delegation, as Yoshimitsu did against the Yamana and Ōuchi. Shugo power was also constrained from below by kokujin and villages.

Historical complexity: The rise of the shugo cannot be reduced to a single cause or to a single policy decision. It grew out of wartime needs, gradual grants of authority, local conditions, and the decisions of individual houses.

Section 29.5

Law, Land, Money, and the Limits of Revenue

The Ashikaga regime generated a great deal of law, but most of it took the form of ordinances, judgments, and instructions rather than a single comprehensive code. The Kenmu shikimoku provided a framework, and the bakufu supplemented it with supplementary regulations (tsuikahō) that dealt with specific problems. Land disputes were the most common business of the courts. Warriors who claimed rights to land sought confirmation from the bakufu, and the bakufu's judgments, enforced in the provinces by shugo, were a principal mechanism by which the regime maintained the loyalty of its followers. In 1368, under the direction of Hosokawa Yoriyuki, the bakufu issued the Ōan half-tax ordinance, which attempted to regulate the use of hanzei and to protect certain categories of holdings, especially those of the imperial family and major temples and shrines, from seizure. The ordinance shows that the bakufu was trying to steer shugo power, though its effectiveness is debated.

The shogunate's finances were a subject of constant improvisation. Unlike a later regime such as the Tokugawa, the Ashikaga did not hold vast directly controlled lands or collect a regular land tax throughout the realm. The shōgun possessed scattered domains, which supported the retainers who managed them, and he could call on contributions from shugo. The bakufu also drew on the commercial economy of Kyoto: taxes on moneylenders and sake brewers (the dosō and sakaya), levies on houses, and fees at barriers and ports. In addition, special levies (tansen) were imposed for particular purposes, such as rebuilding temples or holding imperial ceremonies. Many of the moneylenders were connected to the great temples, Enryakuji in particular, which illustrates how religious institutions, commerce, and government overlapped.

Foreign trade gave the regime another source of revenue and prestige. Yoshimitsu reopened formal relations with Ming China beginning in 1401, received Ming recognition as "King of Japan" during the diplomatic exchanges that followed, and inaugurated the formal tally-trade system in 1404. Some historians have seen this as evidence of Yoshimitsu's pragmatic pursuit of trade profits and prestige; others have stressed that the title's implications for his standing at home were controversial, and Yoshimitsu's successor Yoshimochi suspended the missions in 1411. They were resumed under Yoshinori in 1432. These relations, and the wider maritime networks they belonged to, are examined in Chapters 31 and 32, and they matter here mainly because Yoshimitsu placed the shogunate within a formal Ming diplomatic and tributary framework that differed substantially from the foreign relations of the Kamakura bakufu, tying official diplomacy, commercial privilege, and shogunal prestige together.

These arrangements made the regime dependent on the goodwill of those who paid its bills. Shugo contributions could be delayed or withheld. Kyoto's merchants and moneylenders could be taxed but also alienated. And the wider population could resist, as the armed uprisings of the fifteenth century demonstrated. When bakufu revenues fell short, the shogunate could offer titles, confirm land grants, or make political concessions, but it could not readily compel payment. Financial weakness was thus not incidental to decentralization; it was one of its expressions.

The bakufu also became involved in the management of debt. By the fifteenth century, large numbers of peasants, warriors, and townspeople owed money to moneylenders, and communities sometimes demanded that debts be canceled. In 1428 a wave of uprisings in the Kyoto region and beyond made such demands, and in 1441, following the murder of Yoshinori, armed protesters occupied parts of Kyoto and forced the Muromachi bakufu to issue its first broad debt-cancellation edict (tokusei-rei) in response to a popular uprising. The Kamakura bakufu had issued a debt-cancellation measure of a different kind, the Einin edict of 1297, aimed narrowly at relieving indebted housemen; the 1441 Kakitsu edict was a broader concession wrung from the bakufu by an armed urban and rural movement rather than a policy adopted on the government's own initiative. The government's need to respond to popular pressure and its effort to profit from the resulting fees on moneylenders illustrate the ways in which ordinary people shaped government policy, an issue discussed further in Section 29.7.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Kamakura Bakufu to the Muromachi Bakufu

What Changed

  • The seat of government moved from Kamakura to Kyoto, bringing the warrior regime into the imperial capital
  • The shogunate absorbed police, judicial, and fiscal functions that had belonged to the court in the capital region
  • Shugo gained judicial, fiscal, and landholding powers far beyond their Kamakura duties
  • A second Ashikaga center emerged in Kamakura, governing the east through the Kantō kubō
  • The regime drew on commerce and foreign relations to a degree that Kamakura had not

What Remained Continuous

  • Warrior government coexisting with the imperial court, with the shōgun appointed through court authority
  • The bond of lord and vassal, expressed through confirmation of land and office, as the basis of shogunal power
  • The importance of adjudicating land disputes as a principal function of government
  • The use of an inherited legal tradition, with the Jōei code as a reference point

Who experienced change most directly: Court nobles and temple proprietors, whose income was eroded by shugo, and residents of Kyoto, who now lived under warrior police and taxation. Who experienced greater continuity: Many villagers, whose daily obligations and routines changed more gradually, though their communities became more organized and assertive.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Muromachi bakufu should be understood as a distinct system rather than a version of Kamakura relocated, and that the differences arose from the circumstances of the civil wars and the choice of Kyoto as a seat.

Section 29.6

Shōgun and Court: Overlapping Authority

The court did not disappear under Ashikaga rule. Emperors continued to reign, the court continued to confer ranks and offices, and ceremonies continued to define the annual calendar of the capital. What changed was the balance of power in Kyoto. The court lost much of its independent revenue and much of its police and judicial authority to the bakufu, and it came to depend on shogunal support for funding ceremonies, repairing buildings, and maintaining its households. Yet the shōgun also depended on the court. Only the court could appoint a shōgun, confer the ranks that raised the Ashikaga above their fellow warriors, and supply the imperial legitimacy that distinguished the regime from a purely military power.

This mutual dependence produced a distinctive political culture. Ashikaga shōguns participated in court ceremonial, held court offices, and intermarried with court families. Yoshimitsu, the most prominent example, became minister of the left in 1382 and in 1394, having resigned as shōgun in favor of his young son Yoshimochi, was appointed grand minister (daijō-daijin), the highest court office, before withdrawing from it and taking Buddhist vows in 1395. He continued to dominate politics from his Kitayama residence, receiving emperors as guests and directing bakufu affairs. His wife, Hino Yasuko, received the honorary status of a quasi-mother of the emperor in 1406. These arrangements show a warrior ruler embedding himself in the court's status system, a process that strengthened his standing and blurred the boundary between warrior and court authority.

It is important not to assume that the court was merely a passive instrument. Court nobles served as advisers to the shōguns, drafted documents, and provided expertise on ceremony and precedent, and some, such as the poet and regent Nijō Yoshimoto, were influential figures in Yoshimitsu's circle. The emperors themselves, though politically constrained, retained symbolic weight. Their consent, or at least their acquiescence, was needed for decisions about succession and for legitimating the shogunate's actions. The reunification of the courts in 1392, in which the Southern Court's emperor surrendered the regalia to the Northern line, gave Yoshimitsu a moment of triumph, though the promises made about alternating succession were not kept, a source of continuing grievance among supporters of the Southern line.

The relationship therefore combined cooperation, dependence, negotiation, and occasional tension. The shōgun protected and funded the court while restricting its independence; the court legitimized the shōgun while resisting some of his claims. The result was a Kyoto that contained overlapping centers of authority: the emperor as a ritual sovereign, the courtiers as keepers of precedent and office, the shōgun as the head of the warrior order and increasingly of the capital's administration, and the great temples as independent powers. The imperial institution survived, and its long-term survival across the turbulence of the following century owed something to this accommodation.

Historical Debate

Did Yoshimitsu Aim at the Throne?

The question: Yoshimitsu's accumulation of court honors and his treatment of his family have led some historians to ask whether he sought to place his own line in a position equal to or above the imperial house, perhaps by having his younger son Yoshitsugu enthroned or treated as an emperor. What does the evidence support?

Interpretation A — Imatani's usurpation thesis: The historian Imatani Akira argued in the 1990s that Yoshimitsu was moving toward taking over the imperial position. Supporting points include Yoshimitsu's grand-minister appointment, his wife's quasi-motherhood of the emperor, his treatment as a quasi-retired sovereign at Kitayama, the imperial-style coming-of-age ceremony held for Yoshitsugu in 1408, and his acceptance of the title "King of Japan" from Ming China.
Interpretation B — Yoshimitsu sought a dominant position within the existing order: Many other historians consider this reading too speculative. They point out that the evidence is circumstantial, that the same behavior is consistent with a warrior ruler seeking to integrate himself into the court hierarchy and to secure the Ashikaga house's place within it, that Yoshimitsu died in 1408 before any decisive act, and that his heir Yoshimochi declined the court's offer of a posthumous honor for him, suggesting that the honors did not necessarily reflect a settled dynastic plan.

Evidence: The debate depends on court diaries, records of ceremonies, and the interpretation of Yoshimitsu's Ming correspondence. Each item can be read in more than one way, and no source states Yoshimitsu's intention directly.

Why it matters: If the usurpation interpretation is accepted, Yoshimitsu's death in 1408 interrupted a potentially much deeper transformation of imperial–shogunal relations, and the shogunate was more ambitious than its structure would otherwise suggest. If it is not accepted, the episode instead shows how far a shōgun could go in blending warrior and court authority without challenging the imperial institution itself.

Section 29.7

Temples, Local Warriors, Villages, and Frontiers

A government seated in Kyoto had to deal with power holders that were neither shugo nor courtiers. The great temples were the most conspicuous. Enryakuji on Mt. Hiei, the headquarters of the Tendai school, maintained armed monks, controlled lands and toll barriers, and was closely tied to the moneylenders and brewers of Kyoto. Kōfukuji in Nara, associated with the Fujiwara, was the effective ruler of Yamato province. Shōguns sought to manage these institutions through patronage, appointments, and, at times, force. Yoshinori, who had himself been a Tendai abbot before becoming shōgun, took a hard line with Enryakuji in the 1430s, an episode that ended with several monks committing suicide in the burning of a hall on Mt. Hiei in 1435. The point for the present chapter is that religious institutions were political powers and had to be dealt with as such. Their religious ideas and cultural role are treated in Chapter 30.

Local warriors and villages were the base of all the rest. The kokujin, provincial warriors with local roots, might serve a shugo, hold land directly from the shōgun, or ally with other kokujin in leagues sworn by written oaths. These leagues, called ikki, regulated disputes among members and defended collective interests, and they show that warriors in the provinces did not always regard shugo as their natural lords. Village communities (sō) also became more organized in this period, holding assemblies, making local rules, managing common resources, and negotiating tax obligations collectively. In some regions, they could mobilize for petitions or armed action, as the debt-cancellation uprisings of 1428 and 1441 demonstrated. Historians disagree about the composition of these movements, which included peasants, transport workers, small warriors, and townspeople, but the fact of their impact is clear.

Beyond the central regions, the shogunate's reach depended on local circumstances. In the northeast, the Ōshū and Dewa deputies operated with limited resources alongside powerful local houses. In the far north, the Andō family, based in Tsugaru and with a tradition of involvement in trade across the Tsugaru Strait, maintained connections to Ezo, where Ainu communities engaged in extensive trade and had their own political organization. Pressure from the Nanbu family in the early fifteenth century pushed the Andō to cross to southern Ezo, and Wajin (ethnic Japanese) settlers established fortified settlements along the southern coast. This history of trade, settlement, and conflict, which would produce a major Ainu–Wajin war in 1457, belongs mostly to later chapters, but it shows that Ashikaga authority reached Ezo only indirectly, through local intermediaries. The Ainu were neither subjects of the Ashikaga nor participants in their political system; they were the inhabitants of a region into which Wajin power was gradually expanding.

Relations with the southwest were similarly indirect. In Kyushu, the deputy Imagawa Ryōshun's success in the 1370s and 1380s depended on cooperation with local houses, and his removal in 1395 showed both Yoshimitsu's willingness to reorganize and the fluidity of arrangements. The southern provinces were dominated by the Shimazu, whose relationship to Ryukyu grew increasingly significant in later centuries. A tradition, invoked much later by the Shimazu, holds that Yoshinori in 1441 granted them authority over Ryukyu; scholars treat the historicity and the meaning of this claim with caution, and it should not be read as evidence that the Ashikaga governed Ryukyu. The Ryukyu Kingdom, examined in Chapter 32, was an independent state with its own relations with China and other partners.

Multiple Perspectives

The Half-Tax: One Policy, Five Points of View

Officials also wanted to limit it, as the Ōan ordinance of 1368 shows, because unchecked shugo appropriation threatened both the regime's reputation and its relations with the court and temples.

Everyday Life and Society

Living Under a Decentralized Government

For most people, the shogunate was distant. Their daily experience of authority came from village leaders, local warriors, temple proprietors, and shugo deputies, and their obligations depended on who held the land. Tax collection, labor service, and the enforcement of judgments were handled locally, and competing claimants to income were a recurring source of dispute. Village assemblies, written regulations, and collective petitions became tools for managing these pressures.

Warrior households changed as well. The Kamakura practice of dividing inheritance among several children, including daughters, gave way in many warrior families to inheritance by a single heir, since fragmentation of land weakened the military capacity of a house. This shift narrowed the economic independence of many warrior women, though women of high status could still exert influence, as Hino Yasuko's position suggests, and later, in the crisis preceding the Ōnin War, as Hino Tomiko's role in Yoshimasa's household would show. In Kyoto, residents lived among the households of shugo and retainers, under the policing of the Samurai-dokoro, and were subject to levies and to the risk that political conflict among armed houses would spill into the streets.

These were not uniform experiences. Kyoto merchants and moneylenders gained from proximity to the government and suffered from its taxes; villagers in shugo provinces faced different pressures than those on temple estates; and communities on the frontiers of Ezo and Kyushu lived in worlds shaped as much by trade and local rivalry as by Kyoto's policies.

Section 29.8

Rulers and Turning Points: Takauji to Yoshimasa

The reigns of the shōguns illustrate the shifting balance in the system, and are most useful when read as evidence about institutions rather than as biography. Takauji (shōgun 1338–1358) established the regime under war conditions. His government was improvisational, his authority depended heavily on personal relationships, and its stability was threatened by internal division, as the Kannō Disturbance showed. His son Yoshiakira (shōgun 1359–1367) consolidated control in Kyoto and defended the regime against Southern Court and internal challenges, but died young.

Yoshimitsu (shōgun 1368–1394, then dominant until 1408) came to power as a child, and the early years were guided by the kanrei Hosokawa Yoriyuki. Yoriyuki was dismissed in 1379 in a factional struggle, and thereafter Yoshimitsu took a more direct role. The results were a reordering of the great houses, the Meitoku and Ōei actions against the Yamana and Ōuchi, the removal of the Kyushu deputy, the reunification of the courts, and a close association with the court. Yoshimitsu was also a major patron of culture, building the Kitayama villa and supporting Zen institutions and performing arts, subjects discussed in Chapter 30. His era is often treated as the apogee of Ashikaga power, and in Kyoto and among the great houses it was. But his methods, which depended on skill in manipulating rivalries and on personal prestige, were not institutional reforms, and they could not readily be transmitted to successors.

Yoshimochi (shōgun 1394–1423) governed in a manner that leaned on consultation with the great houses and on cooperation with the kanrei, and he distanced himself from some of his father's initiatives, including the Ming missions. His reign saw the Uesugi Zenshū rebellion in the Kantō in 1416, in which the Kantō kubō Mochiuji was challenged by a former Kantō kanrei and the bakufu became involved, showing how the eastern arrangement could produce instability. After his son and successor Yoshikazu died in 1425 and Yoshimochi himself died in 1428 without an adult heir, the leading figures of the regime held a drawing of lots before the god at the Iwashimizu Hachiman shrine among Yoshimochi's brothers who were in religious orders. The choice fell on Gien, who became Yoshinori. The use of a lottery, whatever its religious meaning, reveals the reliance on senior retainers and the absence of a secure succession mechanism.

Yoshinori (shōgun 1429–1441) was more assertive than his predecessor. He resumed the Ming missions in 1432, confronted Enryakuji, pressed shugo houses over succession disputes and appointments, and moved against the Kantō kubō Mochiuji, who was defeated and forced to commit suicide in the Eikyō conflict of 1438–1439. By these measures he strengthened the center, at least temporarily. His methods, however, alarmed the great houses, and in 1441 Akamatsu Mitsusuke, fearing for his position, invited the shōgun to a banquet at his residence and had him killed. This event, the Kakitsu Incident, showed that shogunal power had grown enough to threaten the great houses and that the same houses could eliminate a shōgun. The bakufu's response, including the defeat of Mitsusuke by other shugo forces, demonstrated the continued vitality of the coalition, but it also left the shogunate under the influence of the houses that had defeated him. The unrest of 1441, including the armed demand for debt cancellation, added another dimension.

After Yoshinori, Yoshikatsu (shōgun 1442–1443) died as a child, and Yoshimasa (shōgun 1449–1473) took office at a young age. Under his reign, disputes over succession within the shōgun's own family and among the great houses accumulated, and bakufu authority in the provinces continued to erode. The conflicts that followed, beginning in 1467, are the subject of Chapter 34; this chapter stops at the point where the tensions described above begin to burst the system's bounds. In the Kantō, the defeat of Mochiuji and the temporary collapse of the Kamakura-fu after 1439 were followed by the restoration of his son Ashikaga Shigeuji as Kantō kubō in 1449, only for renewed conflict in the mid-1450s to drive Shigeuji from Kamakura and split authority in the east still further, evidence that the region was already moving beyond Kyoto's reach. The Ashikaga would continue to hold the title of shōgun until 1573, but their ability to command the great houses would never again match what it had been in Yoshimitsu's time.

Section 29.9

Evidence, Method, and the Sources of Muromachi Government

Historians of the Ashikaga period work with a rich but uneven documentary record. Documents produced by government offices, including ordinances, judgments, land confirmations, and orders to shugo, survive in large numbers, though many exist only as copies preserved in temple, shrine, and family archives. They are especially valuable for tracing how institutions actually operated and how the shugo acquired their powers. But they usually record the outcome of disputes rather than the full story behind them, and they reflect the perspective of the office that issued them and the party that preserved them.

Diaries offer a complementary view. The diary of Mansai, a Buddhist prelate who was an adviser to Yoshimochi and Yoshinori, and the diary of Prince Sadafusa, an imperial relative of the early fifteenth century, provide detailed observations of political life in Kyoto, including relations among shōguns, courtiers, and temples. They give access to gossip, ceremony, and the reactions of insiders, but each diarist wrote from a particular position and with particular concerns. Sworn oaths of local warrior leagues and village regulations provide rare glimpses of how provincial communities organized themselves, though they survive unevenly.

Narrative sources also matter but must be treated carefully. The war tale Taiheiki, which recounts the conflicts of the fourteenth century, was compiled during or shortly after the events, but it is a literary work with dramatic and moral aims, and it shaped later images of the period, including the nineteenth-century image of the Southern Court as loyal and the Ashikaga as usurpers. Historical narratives such as the Baishōron, written from a perspective sympathetic to the Ashikaga, offer a different view. Neither can be treated as a transparent record of events.

Foreign sources supplement the picture. Chinese Ming records document Yoshimitsu's diplomacy and the terms of the tally trade, and Korean records mention contacts with Japanese houses, especially in the west. These sources allow historians to see the regime from outside and to test claims made in Japanese documents, though they too reflect the assumptions of the states that produced them, notably the Ming tributary framework, which treated diplomatic exchange as submission.

The general lesson is that any account of Ashikaga government depends on triangulating sources with different origins and viewpoints. The absence of sources from many ordinary people and from the Ainu and other communities beyond the central regions means that some voices are missing and must be inferred, when possible, from indirect evidence.

Section 29.10

Decentralized Rule and the Central Paradox

The preceding sections allow a more precise answer to the chapter's central question. The Ashikaga shogunate governed by managing a coalition. Its head held formal authority, court rank, the loyalty of a network of retainers, and control of key institutions in Kyoto. Across many provinces, shugo exercised substantial authority, but their power depended on their own resources and on negotiated relationships with local warriors, estate interests, temples, and communities; local warriors and villages organized themselves, and temples and shrines acted as political powers in their own right. The shōgun's task was to keep these actors within the system by distributing offices, confirming holdings, adjudicating disputes, and, when necessary, using force against those who threatened the balance. This is a form of government, and not a mere absence of government. It worked for long stretches and it could act decisively, as the actions of Yoshimitsu and Yoshinori show.

Describing this system as failed centralization would misread it, but so would describing shogunal authority as uniformly weak. The evidence does not allow historians to say with confidence what the Ashikaga leadership intended their state to become; what can be said is that the institutional structure that actually developed combined zones of considerable direct authority with zones of delegated and negotiated authority. In Kyoto and the surrounding Kinai provinces, especially at the regime's fifteenth-century height under Yoshimitsu and again briefly under Yoshinori, the shōgun could exercise unusually strong and direct control: appointing and dismissing officials, adjudicating disputes with real coercive force behind the judgment, taxing the capital's commercial economy, and disciplining even the greatest shugo houses when necessary. Beyond this core, and especially in the Kantō, Kyushu, and the northeast, authority operated through intermediaries whose cooperation had to be negotiated rather than commanded. The conditions that might have supported uniform direct rule across the whole archipelago, including a reliable nationwide tax base and a standing military independent of the great houses, did not exist, and the regime built its institutions to work with the resources actually at hand. Its decentralization beyond the core region was therefore a source of strength, in that it drew provincial elites into the regime and allowed flexibility, and of weakness, in that it left the shōgun dependent on the cooperation of those elites. The same structure that made the regime durable in the late fourteenth century made it vulnerable to the tensions of the fifteenth.

Those tensions were visible well before the mid-fifteenth century. Succession was uncertain, as the lottery of 1428 showed. Rivalry among the great houses, sharpened by the concentration of armed retainers in Kyoto, made political conflict potentially explosive. The Kantō kubō's independence created a standing challenge to Kyoto. Popular uprisings and debt disputes showed that revenue and legitimacy could be contested from below. And the coercive power of the shōgun was limited: Yoshinori's assassination demonstrated that a forceful ruler could be eliminated by those he threatened. The accumulating pressures set the stage for Chapter 34, which will explain how they combined in the Ōnin War and the transition toward the Sengoku order.

The central paradox, then, is that the Ashikaga shogunate was a durable warrior regime capable of exceptionally strong and direct leadership in Kyoto and the Kinai, and of meaningful cultural patronage, diplomacy, and coercion beyond it, yet it never established uniform direct control across the archipelago as a whole. That combination of strength at the center and negotiated authority at the margins is the key to Muromachi Japan as Part V presents it: a society that was politically decentralized beyond its core region but institutionally and culturally dynamic, connected by commerce and diplomacy, and organized around negotiation among many power holders. Chapters 30 through 34 will explore its culture, commerce, and maritime connections, and its eventual breakdown.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Ashikaga shogunate as a political system rather than as a sequence of rulers. Its central argument is that the shogunate governed by managing a coalition in which the shōgun was powerful but authority remained dispersed among the court, the great shugo houses, local warriors, temples, and village communities.

The regime emerged from the civil wars that followed the fall of the Kamakura bakufu. Ashikaga Takauji, having broken with Go-Daigo, entered Kyoto in 1336, supported a Northern Court emperor, issued the Kenmu shikimoku, and was appointed shōgun in 1338. The early regime was improvisational, and the Kannō Disturbance showed that its principal dangers came from within its own leadership.

The Muromachi bakufu differed from Kamakura in fundamental ways. It was seated in the capital, took over police, judicial, and fiscal functions in Kyoto that had belonged to the court, and drew revenue from the capital's commercial economy. It governed the east through a separate Ashikaga branch in Kamakura and other regions through deputies. It was a warrior government, but not Kamakura rebuilt in Kyoto.

The shōgun's authority combined personal lordship over retainers, court rank and office, and control of central institutions. Those institutions, including the kanrei, the Samurai-dokoro, the Mandokoro, the Monchūjo, and the direct retainers, were staffed largely by a limited set of great houses and hereditary administrative families. They functioned as a coalition in which the shōgun had to keep the great houses within the system.

The shugo were the pivot of the system. Their powers expanded from limited Kamakura duties to include judicial enforcement, fiscal privileges such as the half-tax, tax contracting, and control of provincial warriors. They became provincial lords whose power was essential to the regime and could constrain it. Yoshimitsu showed that a shōgun could cut down overmighty houses such as the Yamana and Ōuchi, but at high risk.

The relationship between shōgun and court was one of mutual dependence. The court supplied legitimacy, titles, and ritual authority, while the shōgun supplied protection and funds and absorbed the court's practical functions. Yoshimitsu embedded himself in the court hierarchy to an unprecedented degree, and historians debate whether this reflected a plan to displace the imperial line.

Temples, local warriors, and villages were also active participants in the political order. Enryakuji and Kōfukuji acted as regional powers; kokujin leagues and village communities organized themselves, negotiated, and at times resisted. Popular uprisings in 1428 and 1441 forced the bakufu to respond, and outlying regions were reached in different ways: Kyushu through an actual deputy and substantial Ashikaga intervention, and the far north only indirectly, through local houses such as the Andō well beyond the bounds of direct bakufu administration. The Ainu and Ryukyuans were not part of the Ashikaga system.

The reigns of individual shōguns marked shifts in the balance of power. Takauji built the regime, Yoshimitsu raised it to a peak of prestige and control, Yoshimochi ruled through consultation, and Yoshinori attempted a more forceful policy that ended in his assassination in 1441. By the reign of Yoshimasa, unresolved succession problems, great-house rivalry, and Kantō independence were accumulating.

That combination of strength at the center and negotiated authority at the margins is the central paradox this chapter has traced, and it rested on evidence of several kinds—government documents, diaries, war tales, and foreign records—each with its own limits. The chapter provides the political framework for Chapters 30 through 34, which turn to culture, commerce, Ryukyu, and the Ōnin War.

Key Terms

Bakufu
"Tent government," the warrior administration headed by the shōgun; the Ashikaga bakufu is also called the Muromachi bakufu.
Shōgun
A court title (seii taishōgun) held by the head of the warrior government, conferred through the imperial court.
Muromachi
The Kyoto street where Yoshimitsu built his residence; the name of the period and regime.
Kenmu shikimoku
The 1336 statement of governing principles issued in Takauji's name, not a comprehensive code.
Shugo
Provincial military governors, whose powers grew from Kamakura-era police duties into regional lordship.
Shugo daimyō
A modern scholarly label for shugo who combined the office with broad control of a province's land, taxes, and warriors.
Shugodai
A shugo's deputy who governed the province while the shugo resided in Kyoto.
Kokujin
Provincial warriors with local roots, who might serve shugo or the shōgun or act independently.
Kanrei
The shōgun's chief deputy, normally drawn from the Shiba, Hatakeyama, or Hosokawa houses.
Shitsuji
The earlier title of the shōgun's chief steward, from which the kanrei office developed.
Samurai-dokoro
The bakufu office responsible for policing and criminal matters in Kyoto.
Mandokoro
The bakufu's finance and property office, headed by the Ise family.
Monchūjo
The bakufu's records and legal-proceedings office.
Hikitsukeshū
An early adjudicating council that handled land disputes.
Hōkōshū
The shōgun's direct retainers, who provided guard and administrative service and some independent strength.
Sankan shishoku
The seven great houses: the three kanrei houses and the four shishoku houses that dominated senior offices.
Kantō kubō
The Ashikaga deputy based in Kamakura, responsible for the eastern provinces.
Tandai
A regional deputy of the bakufu, such as the Kyushu deputy.
Hanzei
The "half-tax," a wartime grant allowing shugo to claim half of estate revenues, first issued in 1352 and extended thereafter.
Shugo-uke
Shugo collection of estate revenues under contract with proprietors.
Shōen
Private estates whose proprietors' revenues were increasingly appropriated by warriors.
Ikki
A league sworn by oath among local warriors, villagers, or others, for mutual aid and regulation; also a term for armed uprising.
Sō
A self-governing village community with its own assemblies, rules, and collective identity.
Dosō
Moneylenders, often associated with temples, who were taxed by the bakufu.
Tansen
Special levies imposed for particular purposes, such as temple rebuilding or ceremonies.
Tsuchi ikki / Tokusei-rei
Uprisings demanding debt cancellation, and the debt-cancellation edicts they provoked.
Northern and Southern Courts
The rival imperial lines from 1336, reunified in 1392 on terms that were not fully honored.
Gozan
The "Five Mountains," a ranked system of Zen temples supported by the bakufu; treated in Chapter 30.
Ashikaga Yoshimitsu's Ming investiture
Yoshimitsu's acceptance of the title "King of Japan" from Ming China following the 1401 mission, leading to the inauguration of tally trade in 1404.

Chapter Reflection

The Ashikaga shogunate matters because it shows that a durable government need not be a centralized one. The shogunate endured for more than two centuries, although its capacity to provide political leadership, adjudicate disputes, conduct diplomacy, and compel regional actors changed dramatically over that span; at its height it sustained a remarkable cultural life while sharing authority with regional lords, temples, and self-governing communities. Understanding how that worked corrects the habit of judging medieval Japan against the standards of the Nara state or the Tokugawa order.

The chapter also reveals how power in Japan was layered. Imperial legitimacy, court authority, shogunal authority, provincial lordship, and local self-government coexisted and sometimes conflicted. This layering shaped later developments, including the survival of the imperial institution and the emergence of the territorial lords of the Sengoku period, who would inherit and transform the regional power that the shugo had built.

It also connects regions and societies. The shogunate's relations with Kantō, Kyushu, and the northern frontier show the archipelago as a set of regions with distinct political worlds. Its diplomacy with Ming China and its reliance on commerce show Japan's connection to a wider East Asian system, and the presence of the Ainu and Ryukyuans on the edges of the story is a reminder that the shogunate did not govern all who lived in and around the islands.

The chapters that follow build on this framework. The cultural achievements of Chapter 30, the commerce of Chapter 31, and the maritime world of Chapters 32 and 33 all developed within the political order described here, and Chapter 34 will show how the tensions of that order broke into open conflict. Students who understand the Ashikaga system as a functioning coalition, with its own logic and its own vulnerabilities, will be better prepared to understand both the achievements of Muromachi Japan and the transformations that ended it.
