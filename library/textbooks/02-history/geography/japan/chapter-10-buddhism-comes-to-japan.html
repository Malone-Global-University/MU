Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan
10

Buddhism Comes to Japan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Long before any Yamato ruler formally received a gilded image of the Buddha from a Baekje king, the ships and sea routes described in the previous chapter were already carrying more than iron, horses, and diplomatic gifts. They were carrying people — scribes, artisans, and travelers from the Korean peninsula — some of whom almost certainly knew how to chant a sutra, revere an image, or explain the workings of karma and rebirth long before any chronicle recorded a single "official" date for the introduction of Buddhism to the Yamato court.

Chapter 9 traced the dense web of ships, envoys, migrants, and exchanged goods that connected the Yamato polity to Baekje, Silla, Goguryeo, and the great dynastic centers of China. This chapter asks what happened when one particular body of ideas, texts, images, and specialists moved through those same channels and took root, however unevenly, in the Yamato world: Buddhism. It is tempting to imagine Buddhism's arrival in Japan as a single dramatic event — a ship arriving from Baekje, a golden image unwrapped before a startled court, a date fixed forever in memory. Later tradition, in fact, preserves exactly this kind of scene. But the history beneath that memory is considerably more layered, and considerably more interesting, than any single date can capture.

Consider, instead, several overlapping and only partly documented processes unfolding across the sixth century. Along the shores of northern Kyushu and the Inland Sea, communities with generations of contact with the peninsula almost certainly included people who had already encountered Buddhist images, chants, or teachings through ordinary migration, trade, and travel — long before any court chronicle thought the moment worth recording. At the Baekje court, meanwhile, Buddhism had already been an established royal religion for well over a century, woven into the diplomatic and cultural vocabulary Baekje used in its dealings with neighboring states. And at the Yamato court itself, a ruling lineage increasingly interested in continental prestige, literacy, and the trappings of sophisticated statecraft was, by the middle of the sixth century, unusually well positioned to receive a formal Buddhist mission and to treat that mission as something worth taking seriously.

This chapter is about the meeting of those processes: informal, migrant-carried religious knowledge; an already Buddhist Baekje court pursuing its own diplomatic interests; and a Yamato elite newly attentive to what continental religious and political culture had to offer. It examines what Buddhism actually was as a religious system — its teachings about suffering, karma, and liberation, its scriptures, its images, and its communities of monks and nuns — and explains why a religion built around these things looked so different from, and yet did not simply replace, the existing kami worship, ancestral rites, and lineage rituals that already structured Yamato religious life. It also confronts directly one of the most famous chronological puzzles in early Japanese history: whether Buddhism's formal court reception should be dated to 538 or to 552, and why historians increasingly regard the search for a single correct date as somewhat beside the point.

By the chapter's end, readers should understand Buddhism's arrival not as an instantaneous replacement of one religion by another, but as the beginning of a long, uneven, and often contested process by which a transregional religious system — with its own scriptures, clergy, images, and institutions — became part of the Yamato religious landscape. The chapter closes at Asukadera, the archipelago's first full-scale Buddhist temple complex, whose construction beginning in 588 shows that Buddhism had moved, within a few decades, from a diplomatic gift wrapped in cloth to a permanent institution built in wood, tile, and stone. It deliberately stops short of explaining exactly how that institutional foothold was won at court — a story of rivalry among powerful lineages that belongs to Chapter 11 — so that this chapter can concentrate fully on what Buddhism was, how it traveled, and why its arrival mattered on its own terms.

Two cautions should guide the whole discussion. First, nothing in this chapter should be read as claiming that Buddhism suddenly displaced an already unified, institutional religion called "Shinto." No such single institution yet existed in the sixth century; kami worship, ancestral ritual, and locally rooted sacred practice were diverse, decentralized, and continued alongside Buddhism for centuries, in relationships of coexistence and mutual adaptation rather than simple substitution. Second, nothing here should be read as claiming that Buddhism arrived in the archipelago fully formed from India by way of an unbroken, unchanged relay through China and Korea. By the time it reached Yamato, Buddhism had already been transformed by centuries of adaptation across Central Asia, China, and the Korean peninsula, and it would be transformed again, in distinctive ways, within the archipelago itself.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Buddhism's transmission to Yamato depended on the continental networks of migration, diplomacy, and exchange described in Chapter 9.
  2. Describe the core teachings of Buddhism relevant to its sixth-century East Asian context, including the Buddha, karma, rebirth, liberation, and the Buddha-Dharma-Sangha framework.
  3. Trace how Buddhism changed as it moved from South Asia through Central Asia, China, and the Korean kingdoms of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla.
  4. Distinguish informal, migrant-carried awareness of Buddhism from the formal court reception associated with the traditional dates of 538 and 552.
  5. Evaluate why historians treat 538 and 552 as competing traditions about formal court presentation rather than as settled facts about Buddhism's first appearance in the archipelago.
  6. Analyze the diplomatic and political context in which Baekje's King Seong sent Buddhist objects to the Yamato court.
  7. Identify the material and institutional requirements of Buddhism — images, sutras, relics, monastics, and temple architecture — and explain why these differed from existing ritual practice.
  8. Assess multiple, coexisting motives for elite adoption of Buddhism, including religious conviction, ritual efficacy, diplomatic prestige, and access to continental expertise.
  9. Explain why Buddhism should not be described as replacing or directly confronting a unified religion called "Shinto" in the sixth century.
  10. Describe the construction of Asukadera as evidence of Buddhism's transition from an imported gift to a durable institution.
  11. Use the Nihon Shoki's account of Buddhism's presentation as a historical source while distinguishing its eighth-century composition from the sixth-century events it describes.
  12. Contextualize Buddhism's arrival within the broader pattern of religious transmission across premodern East Asia.

  2. What did Buddhism actually teach, and why did those teachings represent something genuinely new in the archipelago's religious landscape?
  3. How had Buddhism already changed by the time it reached the Korean peninsula and, later, Yamato?

Historical Context

Chapter 8 traced the emergence of the Yamato polity as a loose but increasingly coordinated alliance of powerful kin-groups, or uji, under a paramount ruling lineage whose authority rested on a combination of military alliance, ritual prestige, and control over strategically important resources and titles. Chapter 9 then placed this polity within a dense continental world: Yamato rulers exchanged envoys, gifts, technical specialists, and strategic support with Baekje, Silla, and Goguryeo on the Korean peninsula, while more indirect but still significant connections extended to the Chinese dynastic centers whose administrative models, writing systems, and material culture radiated outward across East Asia. Migrants from the peninsula — sometimes called toraijin in later scholarship — brought ironworking, sericulture, horse breeding, scribal literacy, and other specialized knowledge into the archipelago, and many settled permanently among Yamato's ruling lineages, some rising to considerable prominence within the court's administrative and ritual life.

This chapter assumes that continental context rather than re-explaining it. Its task is to show what happened when one further body of continental culture — an organized, literate, image-based religious tradition — moved along the same channels that had already been carrying ironworkers, horse trainers, and diplomatic gifts for generations. Readers should keep two things from earlier chapters firmly in mind. First, "Yamato" in the sixth century was not a unified, centralized state in any modern sense; it was a coalition of powerful lineages whose paramount ruler exercised real but limited and contested authority. Decisions about whether and how to support an unfamiliar continental religion were, from the outset, decisions made within — and potentially disruptive to — this delicate balance of lineage power, a theme this chapter sets up but leaves for Chapter 11 to resolve. Second, Baekje's relationship with Yamato was not simply cultural generosity; it was a strategic partnership in which Baekje, frequently under military pressure from Goguryeo and Silla, sought Yamato military and diplomatic support, and offered access to prestigious continental culture — very much including Buddhism — as part of that exchange.

It is also necessary to say a brief word about religious life in the archipelago before Buddhism's arrival, since the chapters that follow will repeatedly return to the relationship between Buddhism and existing practice. Sixth-century communities across the archipelago maintained diverse traditions of kami worship, ancestral ritual, agricultural rites tied to the planting and harvest cycle, and reverence for particular sacred places — mountains, springs, groves, and rocks understood to be inhabited by or associated with kami. These practices were not organized into a single institutional religion with fixed doctrine, clergy, or scripture; they were embedded in local communities, lineage traditions, and the ritual responsibilities of particular uji, including the Yamato royal lineage itself, whose claims to authority were bound up with ritual roles that later tradition would associate with the sun deity Amaterasu. Historians today generally avoid calling this diverse constellation of practices "Shinto" for the sixth century. The term shintō itself does appear in the Nihon Shoki, in its account of this same general period, but there it functions simply to distinguish kami worship from the newly arrived Buddhism, not to name a unified institutional religion comparable to the more self-consciously organized Shinto tradition that developed over later centuries, partly in response to Buddhism's own institutional example. This chapter uses more specific terms — kami worship, ancestral ritual, lineage rites — to keep this distinction clear.

Section 10.1

From Continental Exchange to Religious Transformation

The ships that carried horses, iron ingots, woven silk, and diplomatic letters between the Korean peninsula and the Yamato court, described in detail in Chapter 9, were never carrying only material goods. Every voyage also carried people — envoys, hostages, refugees, artisans, and ordinary travelers — and every person carried, along with their skills and possessions, some portion of the beliefs, practices, and assumptions of the society they came from. It is within this broader movement of people and ideas, not as an isolated or separate event, that Buddhism's transmission to the Yamato world must be understood.

This point matters because popular retellings of Buddhism's "introduction to Japan" often imagine it as a discrete, self-contained episode: a single embassy, a single gift, a single date. Such retellings are not entirely wrong — a formal embassy bearing Buddhist objects almost certainly did occur, and the chapter will examine it closely — but they risk detaching that episode from the far larger and longer process of continental exchange that made it possible and gave it meaning. Buddhism could become diplomatically significant at the Yamato court only because Yamato already valued continental prestige, technical expertise, and literacy; specialists trained in Buddhist ritual, art, and scripture could settle and work in the archipelago only because migration channels already existed to bring them there; and the Yamato court could plausibly imagine adopting an elaborate new religious system only because it was already accustomed to receiving, evaluating, and selectively incorporating continental innovations in metalwork, sericulture, horse breeding, and administrative practice.

Buddhism, in other words, traveled the same roads as iron and silk, carried by many of the same kinds of people, and evaluated by many of the same court and lineage interests that evaluated every other piece of continental culture reaching Yamato shores. What made Buddhism different from a new weaving technique or a new style of horse harness was the scale of what it asked of those who adopted it: not simply a new technique to be learned, but a new set of teachings about the nature of existence, a new class of religious specialists, a new body of sacred texts, and, eventually, entirely new kinds of buildings unlike anything the archipelago had built before. The remainder of this chapter examines what exactly Buddhism was, how it had already changed by the time it reached Baekje and Yamato, and why its particular combination of religious depth and institutional weight made its reception at court a matter of real historical consequence.

Section 10.2

What Was Buddhism?

Buddhism began in South Asia with the teachings traditionally attributed to Siddhartha Gautama, a teacher whose dates remain genuinely disputed among scholars. Traditional chronologies, still widely repeated, often place his life in the sixth to fifth centuries BCE; much modern scholarship, however, favors a somewhat later chronology, with some specialists placing his death closer to 400 BCE. This chapter treats his precise dates as unresolved and historically secondary to the content of his teaching, which is the proper subject of this section. Gautama came to be known as the Buddha, meaning "the awakened one," after he was understood to have achieved a profound insight into the nature of suffering and its resolution. His teachings, transmitted orally for centuries before being written down, addressed a set of interlocking ideas that would travel, in various forms, across most of Asia over the following thousand years.

At the center of these teachings stood the problem of suffering, or dukkha — not merely physical pain, but the pervasive unsatisfactoriness of ordinary existence, rooted in attachment, craving, and ignorance of the true nature of reality. Buddhist teaching held that beings are caught in an ongoing cycle of death and rebirth, samsara, in which the moral quality of one's actions — karma — shapes the conditions of future existence. Liberation from this cycle, called nirvana, was understood as the extinguishing of the craving and ignorance that drove rebirth, achievable through ethical conduct, meditative discipline, and insight into the impermanent, interdependent nature of all things. These teachings were preserved, elaborated, and transmitted through what Buddhist tradition calls the "three treasures": the Buddha himself, the Dharma (his teaching), and the Sangha (the community of monks and nuns who preserved, practiced, and taught that teaching).

By the time Buddhism had spread across Central Asia and into China — a process examined further in Section 10.3 — it had diversified considerably, and the tradition that eventually reached Korea and Japan belonged predominantly to the broad current of Buddhism known as Mahayana, or the "great vehicle." Mahayana traditions emphasized the aspiration to liberate all sentient beings, not only oneself, embodied in the ideal of the bodhisattva — a being who postpones final liberation, or who works actively within the world, in order to guide others toward awakening. Mahayana traditions also developed an enormously rich cosmology of buddhas and bodhisattvas understood to offer compassionate assistance, protection, and merit to those who venerated them, alongside a large and continually expanding body of written sutras attributed, in varying degrees of directness, to the Buddha's own teaching.

For a sixth-century Yamato audience, several features of this religious system would have stood out as genuinely unfamiliar. Buddhism offered a universal explanation of suffering and moral causation applicable to all beings everywhere, rather than a set of practices rooted in particular lineages, places, or ancestral relationships. It came bundled with written scripture, requiring literacy to access directly. It required a specialized, trained religious profession — monks and nuns living under formal codes of conduct — rather than ritual roles integrated into ordinary kinship and political structures. And it arrived accompanied by a rich visual and material culture: images of the Buddha and bodhisattvas, ritual implements, and eventually distinctive architectural forms, all of which carried associations with the wealth, learning, and sophistication of the great continental courts from which they came.

Historical Vocabulary
Mahayana Buddhism

Mahayana ("great vehicle") is a broad current within Buddhism that emphasizes the bodhisattva ideal — the aspiration to liberate all beings from suffering, not only oneself — and that developed an expansive body of scripture, cosmology, and devotional practice centered on numerous buddhas and bodhisattvas understood to offer compassionate aid to devotees. By the time Buddhism reached the Korean peninsula and, subsequently, Yamato, Mahayana traditions had become dominant across most of East Asia, shaping the specific images, texts, and practices that would become influential in the archipelago.

Students should understand that "Buddhism" was never a single, uniform system as it moved across Asia. Distinguishing Mahayana traditions from other Buddhist currents helps explain why the Buddhism that reached Yamato carried particular emphases — merit-making, devotional image veneration, and elaborate cosmological schemes — that shaped how it was understood and adapted at the Yamato court.

Section 10.3

Buddhism Across China and the Korean Peninsula

Between the Buddha's teachings in South Asia and their eventual presentation at the Yamato court lay many centuries and thousands of miles of transmission, translation, and transformation. Buddhism moved out of South Asia into Central Asia along trade and pilgrimage routes beginning in the centuries around the turn of the common era, reaching China by at least the first century CE and gradually establishing itself there over the following several centuries through the patient work of translators, missionary monks, and, eventually, imperial and regional patronage. By the time Buddhism became prominent in China, it had already absorbed substantial influence from Central Asian Buddhist communities and had begun to be reshaped by Chinese intellectual and religious categories, including engagement and, at times, tension with existing Confucian and Daoist traditions.

From China, Buddhism moved onward to the Korean peninsula, where it became established as a royally sponsored tradition in each of the peninsula's three major kingdoms, though not simultaneously or in identical form. Goguryeo, in the north, adopted Buddhism as a state-sponsored tradition by the fourth century CE, drawing directly on Chinese Buddhist institutions and networks given its geographic proximity to Chinese dynastic centers. Baekje, in the southwest, adopted Buddhism not long afterward, and by the sixth century had developed a well-established royal Buddhist tradition of its own, complete with temples, monastic communities, and continuing exchange with Chinese Buddhist centers to the west. Silla, in the southeast, adopted Buddhism somewhat later and, in its own traditional accounts, only after considerable internal resistance from officials attached to existing religious and political traditions — a reminder, relevant to Yamato's own experience examined in Chapter 11, that Buddhism's adoption was rarely uncontested even within societies that eventually embraced it enthusiastically.

This regional history matters for understanding Yamato's later reception of Buddhism in at least two important ways. First, it means that the Buddhism that eventually reached Yamato was not a direct transmission from India, nor even from China, but specifically a Baekje-mediated, and more broadly Korean-peninsula-mediated, version of Buddhism — one already shaped by more than a century of adaptation to Korean court society, ritual practice, and artistic convention. Historians should therefore resist any simplified formula in which "India invented Buddhism, China passed it along, and Korea merely relayed it to Japan." Each society that received Buddhism adapted it according to its own political needs, artistic traditions, and existing religious landscape, and Baekje's Buddhism was itself a distinctively Baekje religious and artistic tradition, not an unmodified copy of a Chinese original.

Second, it means that by the time Buddhism reached Yamato, it arrived already bundled with more than a century of accumulated experience — among peninsula elites — in using Buddhist patronage to project royal legitimacy, secure protective merit for the state, and participate in a shared, prestigious religious culture recognized across East Asia's major courts. Baekje's decision to present Buddhist objects to Yamato in the sixth century, examined closely in Section 10.6, therefore drew on a diplomatic and religious vocabulary Baekje had already used domestically and in its dealings with other regional powers. Yamato was being invited to join a religious and cultural conversation already well underway elsewhere in East Asia, not to originate an entirely unprecedented undertaking.

Section 10.4

Buddhism Before the "Official Introduction"

Perhaps no point in this chapter deserves more careful emphasis than the distinction between Buddhism's first appearance in the archipelago and its formal presentation at the Yamato court. These are not the same historical event, and collapsing them into a single "introduction of Buddhism to Japan" obscures more than it reveals.

Given the volume and duration of migration between the Korean peninsula and the archipelago documented in Chapter 9 — a movement of artisans, scribes, refugees, and travelers stretching back well before the sixth century — it would be surprising if no one among these migrant communities carried any knowledge of Buddhism before any court chronicle thought to record a formal presentation. Buddhism had, after all, been an established, visible, state-sponsored tradition in Baekje and Goguryeo for well over a century by the time the traditional presentation narratives are set. Migrants moving between these Buddhist societies and the archipelago plausibly brought private devotional objects, personal practice, or simply general familiarity with Buddhist images and ideas well before any court-level reception occurred. Modern museum and reference scholarship on early Japanese Buddhism generally acknowledges this likelihood, noting that the Yamato court probably had some awareness of Buddhism through Korean and Chinese migrants and traders before the conventionally cited introduction dates.

It is important to be precise about what can and cannot be claimed here. Historians can reasonably infer that informal awareness, and quite possibly informal private practice, of Buddhism existed in migrant communities in the archipelago before any formal court reception. What the evidence does not permit is a confident reconstruction of exactly when, where, or among whom this informal awareness first appeared, since by its nature such private or small-scale practice left little trace in the kind of court-centered documentary record that survives from this period. The distinction this section asks students to hold in mind, therefore, has three separate levels: first, informal awareness or private practice among migrants and their descendants, likely present for some time before any recorded court event; second, formal presentation to the Yamato court, the event traditionally dated to 538 or 552 and examined in the next section; and third, sustained institutional adoption — temples, ordained clergy, court-sponsored image-making — which took further decades to develop and which this chapter traces through to the construction of Asukadera beginning in 588.

Treating these three levels as a single undifferentiated "introduction" flattens a genuinely interesting historical process into a single legendary moment. It also risks obscuring an important truth about religious transmission generally: formal, documented, elite-level religious change is very often preceded by less visible, less documented forms of contact and practice among ordinary people, migrants, and travelers whose experiences rarely survive in official chronicles built around the doings of courts and rulers.

Section 10.5

538 or 552? Dating Buddhism's Formal Reception

Two dates recur throughout modern accounts of Buddhism's introduction to Japan: 538 and 552. These dates should not be treated as fully equal, unresolved alternatives; among historians of early Japanese Buddhism, 538 is now the more commonly favored date for the formal court transmission, while 552 remains the highly influential date preserved in the Nihon Shoki. Even so, both figures describe the same kind of event — a formal court-level presentation — and neither should be mistaken for the moment Buddhism first appeared in the archipelago.

The year 552 derives from the chronology recorded in the Nihon Shoki, the official court chronicle compiled in the early eighth century and examined closely in this chapter's Primary Source Window. According to that chronicle's internal dating system, it was in this year that King Seong of Baekje sent an embassy to the court of the Yamato ruler Kinmei bearing a bronze or gilt-bronze image of the Buddha, a number of Buddhist sutras, and various ritual banners and implements, along with a message recommending the new religion's benefits. The year 538, by contrast, derives from an earlier and, in the view of many modern historians, more reliable chronological tradition, preserved in sources such as the Jōgū Shōtoku Hōō Teisetsu and the Gangōji garan engi — early temple- and biography-related records distinct from the Nihon Shoki's own chronology. This 538 tradition is the date used in some modern reference chronologies of early Japanese Buddhist history, including that maintained by Nara's national research institute for cultural properties.

Historians examining this discrepancy generally conclude that it reflects a genuine conflict between distinct early chronological and textual traditions, compiled using different systems for reckoning reign years or correlating Yamato and Baekje chronology, rather than a simple factual error with an easily recoverable original. The precise origin of the fourteen-year discrepancy between the two traditions cannot be securely reconstructed from the surviving sources, and this chapter does not attempt to adjudicate it further; students should understand that the disagreement is a documented feature of the early source record itself, not a puzzle this textbook can resolve.

The most important lesson of this dating puzzle is not simply which number is better supported, but what kind of historical claim either number can support. Even a preferred date of 538 should not be treated as marking the moment Buddhism "first arrived" in the archipelago, for the reasons discussed in Section 10.4. At most, each date marks a tradition about a specific act of formal court presentation — a diplomatically significant, and probably at least loosely historical, event in which Baekje self-consciously offered Buddhist objects and a persuasive message to the Yamato court, in a form intended to be remembered, recorded, and acted upon. Students should therefore learn to distinguish clearly between "arrival," "formal court presentation," "elite adoption," and "institutional establishment" as different historical processes unfolding over different timescales, rather than collapsing them into a single anchoring date.

Section 10.6

Baekje, King Seong, and the Buddhist Mission

Whichever date is preferred, the traditional account of Baekje's Buddhist mission to Yamato should be read, first and foremost, as an act of diplomacy conducted through religious means, not as a spontaneous act of religious generosity detached from strategic interest. Baekje in the sixth century occupied a difficult position on the Korean peninsula, frequently under military pressure from its rival kingdoms Goguryeo and, increasingly, Silla, and Baekje's rulers had long cultivated a close relationship with Yamato as a source of military assistance, diplomatic support, and access to the archipelago's resources and manpower.

King Seong, who reigned over Baekje through much of the second quarter of the sixth century, is traditionally credited with sending the Buddhist mission described in the Nihon Shoki. According to that chronicle's account, examined closely below, Seong's embassy presented a Buddhist image, sutras, and ritual implements to the Yamato court along with a message praising the merits of the new religion — its capacity to generate boundless good fortune and religious merit for those who venerated it, expressed in language drawing on Buddhist devotional vocabulary already established within Baekje's own court culture. This was not a uniquely spontaneous gesture; Baekje had, by this point, been sending various forms of continental culture and expertise to Yamato for generations, and the Buddhist mission fits within this broader, long-running pattern of prestige exchange examined in Chapter 9. The mission occurred within a diplomatic relationship in which Baekje was, at the same time, separately seeking Yamato's military and political backing against its peninsular rivals; the surviving sources do not allow historians to reconstruct a precise, direct exchange of Buddhist gift for military pledge, and this chapter avoids implying so simple a transaction.

From Baekje's perspective, offering Buddhism to Yamato served several purposes simultaneously. It reinforced the broader relationship of mutual obligation and prestige exchange that underpinned Baekje's requests for Yamato military support against Goguryeo and Silla. It extended Baekje's own religious and cultural influence into a partner state, in a manner comparable to how other East Asian courts used Buddhist patronage to project soft power and cultural leadership. And it offered Yamato's rulers a religious and artistic vocabulary already associated, throughout much of East Asia, with sophisticated, legitimate, and successful royal government — an association Yamato's own rulers, increasingly interested in continental models of authority, had every reason to find appealing.

It would be a mistake, however, to see the mission purely as Baekje's initiative, with Yamato as a passive recipient. Yamato's willingness to receive, consider, and eventually patronize Buddhist objects reflected the archipelago's own internal political dynamics — particularly the interests of certain uji lineages, including those connected to continental migrant communities, who stood to gain prestige, expertise, and political advantage from association with the new religion. The mission's ultimate significance therefore depended as much on how Yamato elites chose to respond to it as on Baekje's motives in sending it — a theme this chapter develops further in Section 10.8 and one that becomes the central subject of Chapter 11's account of court conflict over Buddhist patronage.

Section 10.7

Images, Sutras, Relics, Monks, and Ritual

One of the most important things for students to understand about Buddhism's arrival in Yamato is that accepting Buddhism was never simply a matter of accepting a new set of private beliefs. while informal, private devotion to a Buddhist image was plausible without much supporting infrastructure, sustained, institutional Buddhism — the kind of Buddhism that could train new practitioners, perform elaborate ritual, and endure across generations — required an expanding apparatus of specialized objects, texts, and personnel, and building that apparatus in the archipelago required sustained investment, continental expertise, and institutional commitment far beyond anything demanded by earlier forms of continental cultural borrowing.

At the most basic level, Buddhist practice centered on images — sculpted or cast representations of the Buddha and various bodhisattvas, intended as objects of veneration, devotion, and ritual offering. Producing such images at a level of craftsmanship considered appropriate required specialized casting, carving, and painting techniques that had to be either imported directly, as finished objects, or taught to local craftspeople by continental specialists, many of them migrants from Baekje. Buddhist practice also depended on sutras — written scriptures recording the Buddha's teachings — which required not only the physical technology of writing and copying already partially established in Yamato through earlier continental contact, but also literate specialists capable of reading, chanting, copying, and eventually interpreting these texts, most of which arrived in Chinese translation rather than in any language already spoken in the archipelago.

Buddhist practice further involved relics — physical remains or objects traditionally associated with the Buddha or other venerated figures — which were treated with extraordinary ritual importance and were, in continental Buddhist practice, often ceremonially interred within the foundations of pagodas as part of a temple's consecration. And Buddhist practice depended fundamentally on the Sangha: communities of monks and nuns trained in monastic discipline, liturgical performance, and doctrinal instruction, without whom the other elements of Buddhist practice could not be properly conducted or transmitted to new generations of practitioners. Early Yamato Buddhism relied heavily on monks and nuns of continental background, and early monastic training and ordination for Yamato ordinands depended heavily on continental religious expertise, including specialists from Goguryeo and Baekje, a process examined further in Chapter 11.

Finally, sustained Buddhist practice eventually required distinctive architecture: temple compounds combining halls for image worship, structures for housing sutras and ritual implements, and pagodas designed to enshrine relics. Temple planning, tiled roofs, specialized kilns for producing those tiles, and certain foundation-platform techniques introduced major new monumental building forms drawn from continental, chiefly Baekje, models, though the continental builders and specialists who introduced them also worked alongside and adapted existing archipelagic timber-working traditions rather than replacing them wholesale. Taken together, these requirements meant that adopting Buddhism in any serious, sustained way was never simply a private religious choice; it was, from the outset, a commitment to importing, training, housing, and supporting an entire new category of specialized personnel, objects, and buildings. This is precisely why Buddhist patronage became so closely entangled with political power and lineage competition at the Yamato court, a dynamic this chapter has deliberately left for Chapter 11 to explore in full.

Section 10.8

Why Buddhism Appealed to Yamato Elites

Given the substantial cost and complexity of establishing Buddhist practice described in the previous section, why did any Yamato elites choose to support it at all? Historians have generally resisted reducing the answer to any single motive, and this chapter follows that caution by presenting several overlapping appeals that likely operated simultaneously, in varying combinations, among different individuals and lineages.

Some elites almost certainly found genuine religious and ritual appeal in Buddhism's promises. Buddhist teaching offered explanations for suffering, misfortune, illness, and death that existing kami worship and ancestral ritual did not address in quite the same way, and Buddhist ritual specialists claimed the ability to generate merit, secure protection, and promote healing through prescribed devotional practices. For elites navigating the genuine uncertainties of illness, political rivalry, and mortality, these claims of ritual efficacy carried real weight, whatever mixture of sincere belief and practical hope motivated any particular individual's interest.

Buddhism also carried unmistakable diplomatic and cultural prestige. By the sixth century, Buddhism was the shared religious vocabulary of major and prestigious courts across East Asia — Baekje, Goguryeo, Silla, and the great Chinese dynastic centers beyond them. Adopting and patronizing Buddhism allowed Yamato elites to participate visibly in this shared prestige culture, signaling their court's sophistication, wealth, and worthiness of inclusion among the region's leading powers. This motive did not require cynicism; genuine religious interest and genuine concern for diplomatic standing could, and very likely did, coexist within the same individuals and lineages.

Buddhist patronage also offered specific political advantages within the internal competition among Yamato's ruling lineages. Uji families with strong continental migrant connections, and consequently privileged access to Buddhist images, texts, and specialists, could use Buddhist patronage as a distinctive source of prestige and influence not equally available to rival lineages whose status rested more heavily on older claims to ritual authority over kami worship and ancestral rites. Buddhist patronage, in other words, offered a new arena of competition and advancement within Yamato's existing lineage politics — a dynamic this chapter flags but leaves for Chapter 11 to trace in detail through the conflict between the Soga and Mononobe lineages.

Finally, Buddhism came bundled with practical continental expertise that had value entirely apart from religious devotion: literacy, sophisticated calendrical and astronomical knowledge maintained within monastic communities, medical and pharmacological knowledge associated with continental religious specialists, and advanced techniques in sculpture, casting, and architecture. Sponsoring Buddhist institutions was, among other things, a way of securing ongoing access to a broader package of continental learning and craftsmanship, whose value to an ambitious court extended well beyond specifically religious concerns.

The strongest and most historically responsible way to understand elite adoption of Buddhism, therefore, treats religious conviction, ritual hope, diplomatic prestige, lineage competition, and access to continental expertise not as competing explanations from which historians must choose one, but as genuinely overlapping motives that reinforced one another and that likely operated with different relative weight for different individuals, lineages, and moments across the second half of the sixth century.

Section 10.9

Buddhism and Existing Ritual Traditions

It is tempting, and historically inaccurate, to describe Buddhism's arrival as a confrontation between two rival, fully formed religions — "Buddhism versus Shinto." This framing should be avoided throughout this chapter and, indeed, throughout this textbook's treatment of the following several centuries, for a simple reason: no single, unified, institutionally organized religion called Shinto yet existed in the sixth century. What existed instead was a diverse constellation of locally rooted practices — kami worship tied to particular sacred places, ancestral rites maintained within individual uji lineages, agricultural rituals tied to the planting and harvest calendar, and purification practices addressing ritual pollution — none of which were unified under a single doctrine, priesthood, or set of sacred texts comparable to what Buddhism brought with it.

Understanding sixth-century religious life this way changes how students should think about Buddhism's reception. Rather than displacing an existing organized religion, Buddhism entered an already rich and varied ritual landscape as something genuinely new: a religion built around universal doctrine, written scripture, professional monastic specialists, and elaborate images, none of which had close equivalents within existing kami worship or ancestral practice. Existing practices continued to serve the purposes they had always served — maintaining relationships with ancestors, marking agricultural cycles, and legitimating the ritual authority of particular lineages, including royal ritual roles that later court tradition connected to solar and ancestral deities — while Buddhism offered additional, largely complementary resources: new explanations of suffering and rebirth, new forms of merit-making, and new claims to protective and healing power.

This does not mean the relationship between Buddhism and existing practice was free of tension. As Chapter 11 will examine in detail, some at the Yamato court worried that patronizing an unfamiliar foreign religion risked offending the kami whose goodwill was believed necessary for the community's health and prosperity, and this concern became entangled with lineage rivalry in ways that produced real political conflict. But even this conflict, properly understood, was not a wholesale contest between two organized religious systems; it was a dispute, conducted largely in political and ritual-efficacy terms, over whether and how a new religious practice should be incorporated into an already functioning, locally rooted ritual world — a dispute resolved, in the end, not by the wholesale victory of one religion over another, but by the gradual, uneven, and often locally negotiated coexistence of Buddhist and kami-centered practice that would characterize Japanese religious life for centuries afterward.

Students encountering later Japanese history should therefore resist projecting a later, more institutionally developed relationship between Buddhism and kami worship — including practices of shinbutsu shūgō, or the deliberate combination of kami and Buddhist worship, examined in later chapters — backward onto the sixth century. In this earliest period, Buddhism was best understood not as replacing an existing religion, but as adding an entirely new religious system, with its own distinctive institutional demands, onto an already diverse and decentralized ritual landscape.

Historical Vocabulary
Uji and Kami

Uji refers to the powerful kin-based political and social groupings that structured Yamato society, each typically associated with particular hereditary titles, ritual responsibilities, and claims of descent, sometimes from a particular kami. Kami refers to the spirits, deities, or sacred forces venerated within existing Yamato ritual practice, often associated with particular natural features, ancestors, or aspects of the agricultural cycle.

Neither uji-based social organization nor kami worship constituted a single, unified religious institution in the sixth century; both were diverse, locally variable, and closely bound up with lineage identity and political authority. Understanding this diversity helps explain why Buddhism's arrival did not simply confront a single rival religious system, but entered a decentralized landscape of practices in which different lineages held different ritual roles and different stakes in how a new religion might be received.

Section 10.10

From Court Gift to Buddhist Institution

The clearest material evidence that Buddhism had moved beyond a diplomatic gift and private elite curiosity, and had become a durable institutional presence in the Yamato heartland, is the construction of Asukadera, also known by its formal temple name Hōkōji, in the Asuka region that gives its name to this period of Yamato history. Traditional accounts place the beginning of construction in 588, a date that follows shortly after a decisive court conflict traditionally dated to 587 — a conflict entangled with imperial succession as well as with rival positions toward Buddhist patronage, and one this chapter has deliberately not narrated in detail, since it belongs properly to Chapter 11's account of the struggle between the Soga and Mononobe lineages.

What can be said here, without anticipating that conflict's causes or course, is simply this: by 588, a Yamato lineage with sufficient political power and continental connections was in a position to sponsor the construction of the archipelago's first full-scale Buddhist temple complex, and it did so using specialists brought directly from Baekje. Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that Baekje craftsmen participated directly in Asukadera's planning and construction, contributing expertise in temple layout, roof-tile production, and image-making that no Yamato workshop yet possessed independently. Nara's national research institute for cultural properties, whose archaeological investigations at the Asuka site have been extensive, identifies Asukadera as the earliest full-scale Buddhist temple built in Japan and documents this Baekje craftsmanship in detail.

Construction proceeded over several years. Early temple annals associate 593 with the foundation and ceremonial relic-installation for the temple's central pagoda, traditionally recorded as having taken place in the first month of that year, with the pagoda's central pillar raised the following day — a ritual sequence modeled directly on continental Buddhist practice, in which relics understood to be connected to the Buddha were ceremonially placed within the pagoda's foundation as part of its consecration. By 596, the main temple complex is traditionally recorded as complete, comprising image halls, a pagoda, and supporting structures arranged according to continental temple planning conventions, staffed by monks trained in Buddhist liturgy and doctrine.

Asukadera's significance for this chapter lies precisely in what it demonstrates about the pace and nature of Buddhism's institutional establishment. In the space of roughly four decades — from a traditionally dated formal court presentation in the middle of the sixth century to a fully built temple complex by its final years — Buddhism in the archipelago had moved from a set of portable objects carried by a single embassy to a permanent institution built in wood, tile, and stone, staffed by trained religious specialists, and capable of sustaining ongoing ritual practice, education, and craft production on a continuing basis. This transition from gift to institution is the chapter's proper endpoint. The political struggle that made Asukadera's construction possible — who supported it, who opposed it, and why the conflict resolved as it did — is the subject to which the textbook now turns.

Primary Source Window

The Nihon Shoki Account of the Presentation of Buddhism

Creator: Compilers of the Yamato/early Japanese court, working under official sponsorship · Date of composition: completed 720, describing events traditionally placed in the sixth century · Type: Official court chronicle, written in a mixture of historical record, court tradition, and retrospective narrative

Context: The Nihon Shoki is one of the earliest official chronicles compiled at the Yamato/early Japanese court, completed in 720 — roughly a century and a half after the events it describes for this period. Its account of Buddhism's presentation states that King Seong of Baekje sent an embassy bearing a Buddhist image, a number of sutras, and ceremonial banners and implements to the court of the ruler Kinmei, accompanied by a message recommending the new religion's spiritual benefits.

Paraphrase of the chronicle's account (not a verbatim quotation): The chronicle describes Baekje's king sending envoys with a Buddhist image and sacred texts, along with a message explaining that this teaching surpassed all other doctrines in its power to generate merit and protection, and recommending that the Yamato court adopt and venerate it accordingly.

Analysis: This account should be read as a retrospective court narrative composed generations after the event it describes, drawing on earlier records, family traditions, and possibly Baekje-derived materials no longer independently available to modern historians. It reflects how the eighth-century Yamato court wished to remember and present Buddhism's arrival — as a deliberate, diplomatically framed gift from a respected foreign king — rather than serving as a contemporary, eyewitness record of the sixth-century event itself. Its emphasis on Buddhism's superior merit-generating power may preserve material derived from earlier Buddhist or Baekje-associated traditions, but the surviving eighth-century chronicle does not allow historians to reconstruct King Seong's original message with confidence, and students should be cautious about treating its specific wording as a reliable transcript of sixth-century diplomacy.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say Baekje sent to the Yamato court, and how does it describe Buddhism's benefits?
  2. Why might Baekje's king have wanted Yamato to adopt this new religion?
  3. What does this account suggest about how the eighth-century compilers of the Nihon Shoki wished to remember Buddhism's arrival?
  4. What can this source not prove about the actual first presence of Buddhism in the archipelago?
  5. Why does the chronicle's date of 552 differ from the alternative tradition of 538, and what does this difference suggest about using a single chronicle to fix an exact date?
Historical Debate

When Did Buddhism Come to Japan?

The question: Reference works, textbooks, and museum chronologies variously cite 538 or 552 as the date Buddhism "came to Japan." Should historians treat this as a single question with a single correct answer, or as several distinct questions that have been collapsed together?

Before 538 — Informal knowledge and practice: Given the scale of migration between Baekje and the archipelago, and the century-plus history of established Buddhism in Baekje and Goguryeo before either traditional date, it is likely that some migrants and their descendants in the archipelago possessed private knowledge of, or even engaged in private practice of, Buddhism before any court-level presentation.
538 — One tradition for formal court presentation: Some reference chronologies, including that maintained by Nara's national research institute for cultural properties, place Baekje's formal presentation of Buddhist objects to the Yamato court in this year.
552 — The Nihon Shoki chronology: The Nihon Shoki's own internal dating places what is generally understood to be the same, or a closely related, presentation embassy in this later year, according to its account of King Seong's mission to Kinmei's court.

Evidence: Both traditions draw on early chronicle material compiled well after the events described, using different underlying sources or different conventions for correlating reign years across Baekje and Yamato. Neither can be verified against a contemporary sixth-century document.

Why it matters: Treating this as a single, resolvable factual question about "the" date of Buddhism's introduction encourages a misleadingly simple narrative of religious change. Recognizing multiple, overlapping historical processes — informal awareness, formal presentation, and institutional establishment — produces a more historically accurate, and more interesting, account.

Student task: Explain why a textbook, museum, or reference work might choose to cite one date over the other, and propose a way of phrasing Buddhism's "introduction" to the Yamato court that avoids implying a single, uncontested date.

Historical Debate

Why Did Yamato Elites Adopt Buddhism?

The question: Historians have proposed several explanations for why some Yamato elites chose to support and patronize the new religion. Should any one explanation be treated as primary, or do the explanations work best together?

Interpretation A — Emphasis on religious and ritual motives: Some historians emphasize that Buddhism's promises of merit, protection, healing, and explanations of suffering and rebirth addressed genuine spiritual and practical concerns not fully met by existing kami worship and ancestral ritual, and that sincere religious interest should not be dismissed as secondary to political calculation.
Interpretation B — Emphasis on diplomatic, political, and cultural motives: Other historians emphasize that Buddhist patronage offered diplomatic prestige, access to continental expertise, and a new field of competition among rival uji lineages, and that these political and cultural incentives provide the more historically decisive explanation for why particular lineages invested in Buddhism when and how they did.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same limited body of evidence — chronicle accounts, the material record of early Buddhist patronage, and comparative evidence from Baekje's own use of Buddhism in statecraft — but weigh the relative importance of religious sincerity versus political calculation differently.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they interpret the court conflict over Buddhist patronage examined in Chapter 11, and more broadly shapes how students think about the relationship between religious belief and political interest throughout the textbook.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that could support each interpretation, and explain why treating religious and political motives as complementary, rather than competing, may offer the most historically responsible conclusion.

Continuity and Change Over Time

An Expanding Ritual World

What Changed

  • A new body of written scripture, requiring literacy and specialized training to access, entered the archipelago's religious landscape
  • A new class of professional, celibate religious specialists — monks and nuns — began to be trained and supported by elite patrons
  • New forms of religious imagery, relic veneration, and eventually temple architecture appeared, modeled on continental design
  • Buddhist patronage became a new arena of competition and prestige among rival Yamato lineages

What Stayed the Same

  • Kami worship, ancestral ritual, and agricultural rites continued to be practiced throughout communities across the archipelago
  • Uji lineages continued to derive political and ritual authority from existing claims of descent and ritual role
  • Reliance on continental migrant specialists for advanced technical and religious knowledge, already established before Buddhism's arrival, continued and intensified
  • Buddhism's practice, in this earliest period, remained heavily concentrated among a small circle of court-connected elites rather than becoming a broadly popular religion

Why the pattern matters: Treating Buddhism's arrival as an addition to, rather than a replacement of, the existing ritual landscape helps students understand later centuries of Japanese religious history, in which Buddhist and kami-centered practice developed increasingly interconnected relationships rather than remaining locked in permanent opposition.

Cause and Effect

From Networks to Institutions

Long-term causes

Centuries of Buddhist adaptation and royal sponsorship across Central Asia, China, and the Korean peninsula produced a mature, well-resourced religious tradition ready for further transmission by the sixth century.

Short-term causes

Sustained migration and diplomatic exchange between Baekje and Yamato, combined with Baekje's strategic need for Yamato support against Goguryeo and Silla, created both the channels and the motive for a formal Buddhist mission to the Yamato court.

Effect

Yamato elites, particularly lineages with continental connections, began experimenting with Buddhist image worship and patronage, generating both genuine religious interest and new avenues of political competition.

Long-term consequence

Within roughly four decades, Buddhist patronage produced a permanent institutional foothold at Asukadera, setting the stage for the court conflict, statecraft, and religious policy examined in the chapters that follow.

Historical complexity: Continental networks and diplomatic motives did not mechanically produce Buddhist adoption; Yamato elites made their own decisions about whether, how, and how far to support the new religion, decisions shaped by competing religious, political, and cultural considerations examined throughout this chapter.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Buddhism, one of the most consequential bodies of continental culture to reach the Yamato world, traveled along the same networks of migration, diplomacy, and exchange described in Chapter 9, and why its arrival became a matter of real historical significance at the Yamato court. Buddhism was never simply an isolated "religious import"; it moved through the same ships, the same migrant communities, and the same diplomatic relationships that carried iron, horses, and continental expertise into the archipelago across the fifth and sixth centuries.

The chapter has explained Buddhism's core teachings — the Buddha's insight into suffering, the workings of karma and rebirth, the aspiration toward liberation, and the framework of the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha — and has shown how these teachings had already been substantially transformed through centuries of transmission across Central Asia, China, and the Korean kingdoms of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla before ever reaching Yamato. By the sixth century, the Buddhism poised to enter the archipelago was specifically a Baekje-mediated Mahayana tradition, shaped by more than a century of Korean peninsula court patronage, rather than an unmodified transmission from India or China.

This chapter has also insisted on a crucial distinction between informal awareness of Buddhism, likely present among migrant communities in the archipelago well before any court-level event, and the formal court presentation traditionally dated to either 538 or 552. Rather than resolving this dating puzzle in favor of one date, the chapter has treated the disagreement itself as an important lesson: both dates rest on later chronological traditions compiled generations after the events they describe, and neither should be understood as marking Buddhism's actual first appearance in the archipelago.

The traditional account of Baekje's King Seong sending a Buddhist image, sutras, and ritual objects to the Yamato court has been examined as an act of diplomacy inseparable from Baekje's strategic interest in securing Yamato support against rival Korean kingdoms, and inseparable from Yamato's own developing interest in continental prestige and expertise. The chapter has emphasized that adopting Buddhism required far more than a private change of belief: it required images, sutras, relics, trained monks and nuns, and eventually distinctive temple architecture, all of which had to be imported, taught, or built using continental techniques largely unprecedented in the archipelago.

Yamato elites' interest in Buddhism has been explained through several overlapping motives rather than any single cause: genuine religious and ritual appeal, diplomatic and cultural prestige, new opportunities for political competition among rival lineages, and access to a broader package of continental learning and craftsmanship. The chapter has taken particular care to explain that Buddhism did not confront a unified, institutionally organized rival religion in the sixth century; existing kami worship, ancestral ritual, and agricultural rites remained diverse and locally rooted, and Buddhism entered this landscape as something genuinely new rather than as a direct substitute for an existing organized religion later known as Shinto.

The chapter has closed with the construction of Asukadera, beginning in 588 and traditionally recorded as complete by 596, as clear material evidence that Buddhism had moved, within a few decades of its formal court presentation, from a diplomatic gift to a permanent institution staffed by trained religious specialists and built using continental architectural expertise. The political conflict that made this institutional achievement possible — the struggle among Yamato's most powerful lineages over Buddhist patronage — has been deliberately left for the next chapter, so that this chapter could concentrate fully on what Buddhism was, how it traveled, and why its arrival mattered on its own historical terms.

Students who understand Buddhism's arrival as a layered, uneven process — involving informal migrant knowledge, formal diplomatic presentation, contested elite adoption, and eventual institutional establishment — are well prepared to move into Chapter 11, where the political struggle over Buddhist patronage reshapes the balance of power at the Yamato court, and into Chapter 12, where Buddhist patronage becomes entwined with broader projects of statecraft and diplomacy under Prince Shōtoku.

Nihon Shoki
An early eighth-century official court chronicle preserving the most detailed traditional account of Buddhism's formal presentation to the Yamato court.
Asukadera (Hōkōji)
The first full-scale Buddhist temple complex built in the archipelago, constructed beginning in 588 with Baekje craftsmen and traditionally recorded as completed by 596.
Formal reception
The diplomatically significant, court-level presentation of Buddhist objects, distinguished in this chapter from earlier informal awareness and later institutional establishment.
Institutional Buddhism
Buddhism supported by durable temples, trained clergy, and sustained patronage, as opposed to informal private knowledge or a single diplomatic gift.
Syncretism
The coexistence, blending, or mutual adaptation of distinct religious traditions, relevant to the long-term relationship between Buddhism and kami worship in Japan.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept or category to an earlier period; relevant here to describing sixth-century kami worship as a unified religion called "Shinto."
King Seong
The Baekje ruler traditionally credited with sending the Buddhist mission to the Yamato court within a broader diplomatic relationship in which Baekje was also seeking Yamato military and political support.
Kinmei
The Yamato paramount ruler traditionally associated with receiving Baekje's Buddhist mission.

Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha)

The Korean kingdom through which Buddhism was formally presented to Yamato, and a long-standing strategic partner seeking Yamato's military and political support.

The Yamato paramount ruler traditionally associated with the reception of Baekje's Buddhist mission.

Baekje's rival Korean kingdoms, both of which had also adopted Buddhism as a royally sponsored tradition on their own timelines.

Migrant communities from the Korean peninsula and broader continent whose knowledge, skills, and possibly private religious practice preceded formal court-level Buddhist reception.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the continental networks introduced in Chapters 8 and 9 into their most religiously consequential episode yet: the transmission of Buddhism into the Yamato world. It has shown that even a religious transformation as significant as this one did not arrive as a single, self-contained event, but emerged from decades of migration, diplomacy, and gradual institutional investment — a pattern of layered, uneven change that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of later religious and cultural developments.

The chapter's insistence on distinguishing informal awareness, formal presentation, and institutional establishment matters well beyond this specific case. It offers students a model for thinking about cultural and religious change generally: dramatic, memorable moments recorded in chronicles are very often preceded by less visible processes of contact and adaptation among ordinary people, migrants, and travelers, and followed by long, uneven periods of institutional consolidation that chronicles compress into a much shorter and tidier narrative than the underlying history actually was.

The chapter has also planted an important seed for the chapters that follow. Buddhism's arrival did not resolve into an immediate, uncontested elite consensus; it entered a court already structured by rivalry among powerful lineages, and its patronage became, almost immediately, entangled with the competition for power that Chapter 11 examines directly. Understanding Buddhism's teachings, its material and institutional requirements, and its complex relationship with existing kami worship and ancestral ritual — the work of this chapter — provides the necessary foundation for understanding why its patronage became so fiercely contested at court, and why that contest, once resolved, opened the way toward the more systematic statecraft examined in Chapters 12 and 13.

Students who understand Buddhism's arrival as a genuine, evidence-based historical process — shaped by continental migration, diplomatic calculation, religious conviction, and institutional ambition all at once — are well prepared to move forward into the court politics, statecraft, and religious policy that the following chapters will trace across the remainder of Yamato and early imperial Japanese history.
