MacArthur, SCAP, and the Government of Occupied Japan

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On September 2, 1945, aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, Japanese representatives signed the Instrument of Surrender before delegates of nine Allied powers. The document did not abolish the Japanese government. It placed the authority of the emperor and the government under the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, and that sentence defined the strange constitutional condition of Japan for the next six and a half years.

The ceremony is a useful way into this chapter because it shows, in miniature, the problem the chapter must explain. Japan had lost a war, its empire had collapsed, and foreign troops were about to take up positions across the home islands. Yet the Japanese cabinet, the ministries, the prefectural governors, the police, and the emperor all remained in place. When the Japanese delegation signed, it did so on the authority of an imperial command. When the occupation began to issue orders, those orders were overwhelmingly delivered to Japanese officials, who translated them into Japanese law and carried them out. Who, then, governed Japan between 1945 and 1952?

The short answer is that two systems of authority were layered on one another. At the top stood a military occupation dominated by the United States and personified by General Douglas MacArthur, who held the title of Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP). Beneath and alongside that command, Japanese institutions continued to draft regulations, collect taxes, allocate rice, run schools, and police streets. SCAP could dictate, veto, dismiss, and censor. It rarely administered directly. This arrangement, usually called indirect rule, shaped every reform of the period and also determined which parts of the prewar state survived. This chapter treats it as the institutional foundation for the whole occupation sequence that runs through Chapter 90.

The chapter's scope is deliberately institutional. Chapter 81 explained how Japan surrendered, how its overseas empire dissolved, and how repatriation began. Chapter 83 will examine war responsibility, the emperor's wartime role, and the Tokyo Trials. Chapter 84 will treat the 1947 Constitution, Article 9, women's suffrage, and the substantive democratic reforms. Chapters 85 through 88 will take students into hunger, black markets, the Recreation and Amusement Association, crime, and occupation culture. The present chapter asks a prior question: through what machinery were these things done? It examines the Allied bodies that claimed to direct the occupation, the staff sections of General Headquarters (GHQ), the directives that flowed from GHQ to Japanese ministries, the military government teams that watched the prefectures, the decision to retain the emperor, the dismantling of the political police, the purges, and the system of censorship that limited public speech even as political life reopened.

Three themes recur. The first is that the occupation was not a single will. Washington, GHQ, the Allied councils, rival SCAP sections, Japanese politicians, and career civil servants each had distinct interests, and policy emerged from their negotiation. The second is the coexistence of liberalization and control: the same headquarters that ordered political prisoners released also read private mail and removed whole categories of subject matter from newspapers. The third is continuity. Japanese bureaucratic expertise, local administrative habits, and the imperial institution survived because the occupation needed them, and their survival conditioned everything that followed. Students should avoid two simple stories: one in which an American general single-handedly remade Japan, and one in which Japanese institutions merely absorbed foreign orders unchanged.

The evidence for this subject is unusually rich and unusually contested. It includes the texts of the Potsdam Declaration and the Instrument of Surrender, American policy directives, the numbered SCAP Instructions to the Japanese Government (SCAPINs), the records of GHQ sections held in the U.S. National Archives (Record Group 331) and the National Diet Library, Japanese cabinet papers and the Official Gazette, newspapers, the diaries and memoirs of officials on both sides, and the huge collection of censored publications and monitored communications preserved in the Gordon W. Prange Collection at the University of Maryland. Each of these records a particular vantage point. MacArthur's own memoirs, for example, are valuable but self-presenting, and occupation documents often describe what SCAP intended rather than what local officials actually did.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to describe the system of occupation government with precision, explain why it took the indirect form that it did, and assess how that form helped some reforms and limited others. The chapter's central question is how a government of supreme foreign authority and continuing Japanese administration should be understood, and how the balance between the two shifted as the occupation aged.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Potsdam Declaration and the Instrument of Surrender placed Japanese governmental authority under SCAP while leaving the Japanese state in existence.
  2. Trace the arrival of American forces and the establishment of GHQ in 1945, distinguishing the military landing from the later institutional machinery.
  3. Distinguish the roles of Washington, SCAP, the Far Eastern Commission, and the Allied Council for Japan in making and overseeing occupation policy.
  4. Describe the internal organization of GHQ/SCAP and explain why it cannot be treated as a single unified office.
  5. Analyze how directives moved from SCAP through the Central Liaison Office, the cabinet, and the ministries to prefectures and municipalities.
  6. Assess the political reasoning behind the retention of Emperor Hirohito and the changing public position of the imperial institution.
  7. Explain the October 1945 Civil Liberties Directive and its consequences for the Special Higher Police, the Home Ministry, and political prisoners.
  8. Evaluate the purge program and the extent to which the civil bureaucracy survived the occupation.
  9. Analyze occupation censorship, including the Press Code and the Civil Censorship Detachment, as a contradiction within democratization.
  10. Compare the experiences of SCAP officers, Japanese officials, local administrators, activists, and Korean residents under occupation governance.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of indirect rule and avoid treating either American control or Japanese continuity as the whole story.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits, and identify whose perspectives they omit.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What was the legal and political basis of SCAP's authority, and why did the occupation not begin as a full military government of the kind imagined in earlier plans?
  2. How did American predominance coexist with the formal claims of other Allied governments, and what did the Far Eastern Commission and the Allied Council actually accomplish?
  3. How did a directive issued in English at GHQ become a binding rule in a Japanese village, and where could it be delayed, softened, or redirected along the way?
  4. Why did American and Allied officials decide to retain the emperor, and how did that decision shape every other institutional choice?
  5. What did the occupation abolish, what did it merely supervise, and what did it leave largely untouched?
  6. How should historians weigh the liberating effects of the Civil Liberties Directive against the censorship regime that operated at the same time?
  7. Were Japanese officials passive instruments of occupation policy, or did they exercise real agency in shaping its content and implementation?
  8. How should historians describe the government of occupied Japan: as American rule, as Japanese government under supervision, or as something that changed over time?

Historical Context

The government that surrendered in August 1945 was the product of the Meiji constitutional order described in Chapters 59 and 74. The emperor was sovereign under the 1889 Constitution; the cabinet was not responsible to the Diet; the army and navy ministers had to be serving officers; the Home Ministry controlled the police and appointed prefectural governors; and a web of wartime control associations, neighborhood associations, and censorship offices reached into every community (see Chapter 77). The end of the war did not remove this apparatus. It was the apparatus through which Japan accepted surrender, demobilized millions of soldiers, and attempted to prevent disorder, and it was the only machinery of national administration then available.

Chapter 81 described the surrender decision of August 14–15, 1945, the emperor's radio broadcast, the dissolution of the overseas empire, and the first stages of repatriation. Two points from that chapter are essential here. First, Japanese leaders hoped, above all, to preserve the national polity (kokutai), a goal they associated with the survival of the imperial institution, and the Allied reply of August 11 left the question deliberately ambiguous. Second, the surrender was accepted through imperial authority, which meant that the emperor's voice, not an Allied declaration alone, ended the fighting and instructed army commanders, many of them still armed and overseas, to comply.

Allied planning for the occupation had begun long before the surrender. American officials in the State Department, the War Department, and the Navy Department had studied Japan since 1942, and they disagreed about whether to preserve the emperor, how far to restructure Japanese society, and whether to govern directly or through existing institutions. The Cairo Declaration of 1943, the Yalta agreements of February 1945, and the Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945, issued by the United States, Britain, and China and joined by the Soviet Union in August, provided the public framework: occupation until the objectives were met, the removal of those who had misled the Japanese people into war, the limitation of Japanese sovereignty to the four main islands and designated minor ones, and the establishment of freedom of speech, religion, thought, and respect for fundamental human rights. Germany's experience was an important contrast. There, the Allies had declared the absence of a central government and assumed supreme authority themselves; Japan, by contrast, still had a functioning central state.

The occupation also inherited the immediate conditions described in Chapters 80 and 81: destroyed cities, collapsed transport, severe food shortages, millions of demobilized soldiers and repatriates, and a population that had been told for years that Allied victory would mean brutal treatment. Those conditions gave the Japanese government practical leverage, since the occupation could not afford the collapse of food distribution or public order, and they gave ordinary people strong reasons to cooperate. This chapter begins from that combination of overwhelming military authority and practical dependence on the defeated government's administrative capacity.

Section 82.1 · Division I — Occupation Authority, Objectives, and Arrival

From Surrender to Occupation: Sovereignty, Authority, and the Potsdam Settlement

The legal basis of the occupation was a chain of documents rather than a single treaty. The Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945 offered Japan terms of surrender and promised that Allied forces would remain until their objectives were achieved. When the Japanese government indicated on August 10 that it would accept the Declaration on the understanding that the emperor's prerogatives were not prejudiced, the Allied reply of August 11, drafted by U.S. Secretary of State James Byrnes, said in substance that the authority of the emperor and the Japanese government to rule would be subject to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, and that Japan's final form of government would be established by the freely expressed will of the Japanese people. Japan accepted on that basis. The Instrument of Surrender of September 2 repeated the subordination: the emperor and the government were to take such steps as the Supreme Commander required to give effect to the surrender terms.

Three features of this settlement matter. First, it was a military occupation, not an annexation. Japan remained a state with a government, a territory (reduced by the loss of its empire), and a legal personality, even though the exercise of its powers was suspended in important respects, notably in foreign relations, which were effectively placed under SCAP. Second, the Meiji Constitution remained formally in force until May 3, 1947, so every occupation reform of 1945 and 1946 had to be implemented through legal forms the old constitution recognized. Third, the Japanese government itself provided the legal bridge. On September 20, 1945, an imperial ordinance (No. 542), often called the Potsdam Ordinance, authorized the government to issue orders implementing the demands of the Allied powers without prior Diet consultation. In practice this placed SCAP's directives above ordinary statute and gave them a Japanese legal form.

Historians therefore distinguish between SCAP's authority as a matter of power and as a matter of law. As a matter of power it was close to absolute: Japan had no army, no independent foreign policy, and no way to refuse. As a matter of law it operated through the surrender instrument and Japanese enabling instruments, which is why scholars debate whether the occupation is better described as the replacement of Japanese sovereignty or its suspension. The contrast with Germany is instructive. In June 1945 the four powers had declared that no central German government existed and had assumed supreme authority, dividing the country into zones. Nothing similar happened in Japan, where the emperor's broadcast had ended the fighting and where American planners had concluded that a functioning government was worth preserving.

The occupation's legal order cannot be understood by imagining that an Allied constitution replaced the old one on September 2. Japanese courts continued to decide ordinary cases, ministries still collected revenue, and elected members of the Diet could debate laws. Yet the Supreme Commander could direct these institutions in matters he considered necessary to execute the surrender, and Japanese authorities could not invoke domestic constitutional restrictions as a final answer to his demands. A dispute over a statute or an officeholder could therefore become a dispute about the scope of superior occupation authority. The Japanese government remained recognizable while its independence of decision was radically limited. That asymmetry explains why an instruction issued by a foreign military headquarters could become an imperial ordinance, ministerial regulation, or local administrative practice without passing through the ordinary process of an independent country's lawmaking.

The distinction also helps students identify several meanings of sovereignty. Internationally, Japan remained a state rather than being absorbed into the United States; domestically, its governing offices remained staffed by Japanese personnel. But practical authority over security, the boundaries of governmental competence, major political change, and foreign relations lay with the occupation. Scholars can accordingly describe the period as a suspension or severe restriction of sovereign powers without implying that Japan ceased to exist. The September 20 enabling ordinance was consequential precisely because it offered a way to turn outside commands into Japanese legal instruments. Legal continuity, in this case, made far-reaching discontinuity possible.

Neither the surrender instrument nor subsequent directives fixed every detail of the future political settlement. The Potsdam Declaration envisaged a Japan whose political order ultimately reflected popular wishes, but it did not specify how existing ministries should be supervised in the meantime. Those unanswered operational questions left room for MacArthur's staff, Washington agencies, Japanese cabinet ministers, and other Allied representatives to contest policies. The working structure of occupation emerged through such contests, not simply by reading one legal document in isolation.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Indirect Rule and SCAP Terminology

SCAP stands for Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers. Strictly, it was the title of one person, MacArthur; in everyday usage, both Japanese and English speakers also used it for the whole headquarters, which is formally GHQ/SCAP. From January 1947 MacArthur also commanded U.S. forces in the Far East, so a separate American command (the Far East Command) existed alongside the occupation headquarters. Do not confuse it with the Far Eastern Commission, the Allied policy body in Washington.

Indirect rule means governing through the existing local government rather than replacing it with foreign administrators. Direct military government would have meant Allied officers issuing proclamations, running courts, and administering ministries. Early American contingency plans anticipated parts of the latter, but the occupation as implemented used the Japanese government. The term should not be read as "mild": indirect rule could be coercive, since SCAP could dismiss officials and compel compliance, and it left Japanese ministries with substantial discretion in how they executed orders.

Section 82.2 · Division I

MacArthur's Arrival and the Establishment of the Occupation

The physical occupation began cautiously. American planners worried that fanatical army or navy units might resist, and the landing plan, code-named Blacklist, was designed for opposition that did not occur on a large scale. An advance party flew to Atsugi airfield near Yokohama on August 28, 1945. Some officers there had initially resisted the surrender, and Japanese authorities had to persuade or suppress them before the landing. On August 30, MacArthur arrived at Atsugi, while Eighth Army units under General Robert Eichelberger came ashore at Yokohama and naval infantry occupied the Yokosuka base. On September 8 MacArthur entered Tokyo, American flags were raised at the embassy, and over the following weeks occupation forces spread through the main islands. American troop numbers were several hundred thousand in the first autumn and fell steadily, to roughly one hundred thousand by the late 1940s, as demobilization pressure at home reduced forces.

The September 2 surrender was followed by an immediate test of the occupation’s form. SCAP staff had prepared three proclamations contemplating comprehensive military-government authority, military trials for occupation offenses, and circulation of supplemental B-marked military currency. Japanese representatives objected that direct occupation administration could undermine their government’s ability to maintain public order. Foreign Minister Shigemitsu Mamoru, who had signed the surrender instrument for Japan, met MacArthur on September 3. After Japanese representations, MacArthur withdrew the proposed proclamations, while reserving the overriding authority of the occupation. The episode is documented in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs files catalogued by JACAR, reference B17070000700. It shows Japanese officials influencing how the occupation was conducted without eliminating their subordinate position.

GHQ was first located in Yokohama and moved to Tokyo, where the National Diet Library records that it was formally established on October 2, 1945. It occupied the Dai-Ichi Seimei Building across from the Imperial Palace moat, a choice that was noticed by contemporaries, since the headquarters of the supreme authority literally looked at the residence of the emperor. MacArthur lived in the American Embassy residence and was known for working a punishing schedule and for limited personal contact outside a small circle, which contributed to the image, cultivated by his staff, of an aloof proconsul. Historians treat that image cautiously. It served the occupation's authority, but it can mislead students into attributing every policy to one man, when a large staff, instructions from Washington, and Japanese actors all shaped outcomes.

The evidence for the arrival is mostly visual and official: photographs, newsreels, GHQ records, and Japanese diaries and newspaper accounts. Both sides had reasons to stage the event. American communications emphasized orderliness and victory; Japanese official communications emphasized calm and obedience, since the government feared incidents between civilians and troops. Students should read such sources for what each side wished to show.

The arrival proceeded through Japanese channels as well as American deployment plans. Japanese officers and civil officials prepared airfields, transport, billets, and communications for men who had recently been enemies. American forces feared isolated resistance or sabotage; the Japanese leadership feared that one confrontation might provoke a far wider breakdown of public order. The absence of general armed resistance did not mean these worries were imaginary, since surrender orders had reached units at different speeds and some soldiers still believed they were obliged to resist. The careful sequencing of reconnaissance, advance detachments, and larger landings was a military precaution and a political demonstration that the surrender could be enforced.

The confrontation over proposed proclamations on September 2–3 reveals more about the outcome than the familiar photographs of the landing. Archival files reproduced by the Japan Center for Asian Historical Records describe three drafts: a proclamation asserting comprehensive authority of the occupation commander, one concerning military tribunals, and another giving legal-tender status to supplemental military currency. Japanese officials understood that the drafts could bypass and marginalize their government. After immediate representations through the liaison organization, Foreign Minister Shigemitsu met MacArthur on September 3. The proclamations were withdrawn or suspended; the decision was contingent upon Japanese cooperation rather than a renunciation of occupation supremacy. The issue of English as a formal language of rule must not be read into these Japanese drafts merely because a separate American proclamation governing Korea used such a clause. The documentary distinction prevents a persuasive but misleading parallel.

The GHQ building, nearby embassy residence, and meeting places were more than a commander's backdrop. Documents had to circulate among offices in Tokyo and Yokohama, often in two languages, while regional military headquarters dealt with conditions far from the capital. Occupation correspondence recorded directives, requests for clarification, summaries of reports, and disputes over precedence. The physical concentration of senior officials in Tokyo encouraged rapid contacts with central ministries but could also make provincial circumstances appear remote. That was one of the structural reasons local military-government teams mattered.

Section 82.3 · Division I

Washington's Occupation Policy: Truman, the Joint Chiefs, and Initial Directives

MacArthur commanded the occupation, but he did not originate its basic objectives. Policy was prepared in Washington by an interdepartmental body, the State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee (SWNCC), approved by the president, and transmitted to MacArthur through the Joint Chiefs of Staff. The statement known as the Initial Post-Surrender Policy for Japan was approved by President Truman in early September 1945 and published on September 22. It declared the ultimate objectives to be ensuring that Japan would not again threaten the peace and establishing a peaceful and responsible government that respected the rights of other states and supported the principles of the United Nations, ideally one based on the freely expressed will of the people. It did not commit the United States to retain the emperor or the existing governmental structure.

Washington issued two important but distinct statements of occupation authority. SWNCC 181/2, transmitted on September 6, 1945 as JCS 1380/6, expressly rejected any interpretation that relations with Japan rested on a contractual equality, and permitted control through Japanese governmental machinery so long as it produced satisfactory results. The later Basic Initial Post-Surrender Directive, JCS 1380/15, dated November 3, organized the policy in greater detail and affirmed SCAP’s supreme authority to execute surrender, while generally favoring indirect control through the emperor and Japanese government unless circumstances demanded otherwise. The two directives should not be conflated: the September message stated the noncontractual principle expressly; the November document gave it a broader administrative framework.

In practice the relationship between Washington and Tokyo was looser than the chain of command suggests. MacArthur had enormous prestige, a staff loyal to him, and the advantage of being on the spot. Washington's directives were often general, and MacArthur's headquarters frequently acted first and reported afterward. Some of the most consequential initiatives of the early occupation, including the Civil Liberties Directive of October 1945, came from GHQ rather than from instructions from the Joint Chiefs. At the same time, MacArthur could not ignore Washington. Budgets, shipping, troop levels, food imports, and the diplomatic management of the Allies all required American government support, and the State Department's political adviser to SCAP represented a separate channel of influence. Historians therefore describe American policy as a negotiated product of the president, the departments, and the commander, in which the commander held unusual autonomy, especially in 1945–1947.

It is essential to separate two closely related Washington documents. The September 6, 1945 instruction, SWNCC 181/2, transmitted as JCS 1380/6, stated explicitly that relations with Japan did not rest upon a contractual relationship and that control should proceed through the Japanese government only so long as that arrangement produced satisfactory results. The detailed November 3 directive, JCS 1380/15, then organized the occupation's general political, economic, civilian-supply, and financial responsibilities. Both affirm superior authority and conditional indirect rule, but they are not interchangeable texts. The distinction also demonstrates how a government can issue an early declaration of command principle and later spell out operational instructions after occupation experience has already begun.

Washington's early choices were shaped by competing fears. Demilitarization required the removal of institutions that had facilitated expansion and suppression; economic collapse threatened hunger, disorder, and the feasibility of occupation itself. A large foreign administrative bureaucracy would have been costly and slow to assemble, particularly amid American demobilization. The same officials who wanted to eliminate militarist control therefore had reasons to retain the Japanese tax system, budget offices, transportation departments, and personnel able to identify local problems. Military security and political reconstruction could not be treated as separate undertakings. For example, disarming units and returning personnel depended on record keeping and transportation facilities that were overwhelmingly Japanese.

MacArthur's discretion was real but bounded. Washington controlled the purposes of occupation, major diplomatic decisions, the deployment and provisioning of American forces, and increasingly the economic resources needed to keep Japan functioning. GHQ could propose policy and sometimes establish facts rapidly, but decisions that affected international trade, fiscal support, and American relations with the Soviet Union could not be insulated from Washington. Institutional history therefore requires two directions of analysis: the instructions descending from the president and Joint Chiefs, and the information and proposals moving back from occupation headquarters through American political and military channels. A directive alone discloses an authorized policy, not the debates that produced it or the compromises that followed.

Section 82.4 · Division I

The Far Eastern Commission, the Allied Council, and American Predominance

The occupation was formally Allied but practically American. The Soviet Union, Britain, China, Australia, and other governments that had fought Japan contested the idea that the United States alone should shape postwar Japan, and the arrangement they negotiated at the Moscow conference of foreign ministers in December 1945 was a compromise. It created two bodies. The Far Eastern Commission (FEC), which began meeting in Washington in February 1946, was a policy-making body that initially included eleven governments, among them the Soviet Union, Britain, China, France, the Netherlands, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India, the Philippines, and the United States, and later more. The Allied Council for Japan (ACJ), which began meeting in Tokyo in April 1946, was a four-member advisory body representing the United States, the Soviet Union, China, and the British Commonwealth, with an American chair.

Neither body displaced SCAP. The FEC could formulate policy and review directives, but the United States held a veto among the leading powers and, crucially, the right to issue interim directives when the Commission had not agreed. The ACJ could consult and advise but had no authority to instruct MacArthur, who remained responsible to the American government and who took a restrictive view of its role. Soviet representatives used the Council as a platform for criticism of American policy and for requests for greater Soviet participation, and tensions there foreshadowed the Cold War conflicts of later chapters. The Commission occasionally influenced outcomes, particularly in the review of the new constitution, but it more often served as a forum in which allies recorded disagreement than as an effective check.

Allied participation also took military and territorial forms. Australia, Britain, New Zealand, and India contributed the British Commonwealth Occupation Force (BCOF), which from early 1946 occupied part of western Honshu and Shikoku under an Australian commander. The Soviet Union requested an occupation zone in northern Hokkaido in August 1945; Truman refused, and no Soviet zone existed in the home islands, although the Soviet Union occupied the Kuril Islands and southern Sakhalin. China and the Philippines pressed claims for reparations and for stricter treatment, while Australia, in particular, pressed the case for placing the emperor on trial, a matter taken up in Chapter 83. These facts matter because they show that American predominance was a political outcome rather than a pre-existing legal fact. Americans defended it by pointing to the scale of their military effort in the Pacific and the practical need for a unified command, and critics, including some Allied governments, saw it as a unilateral occupation conducted under an Allied label.

The Far Eastern Commission's limitations deserve more careful definition than the word "advisory" suggests. It possessed a policy-formulating role under the Moscow arrangements, while the Allied Council in Tokyo was consultative. But the FEC did not administer Japan or deliver daily instructions to Japanese ministries, and American control of interim action, combined with rules governing major-power agreement, sharply constrained its capacity to compel a change of course. MacArthur could also resist interpretations he considered intrusive into his command. Students should therefore distinguish a body's legal competence from its ability to translate a decision into immediate action. A commission minutes book may document genuine Allied disagreement without showing an enforceable instruction at GHQ.

British Commonwealth participation makes the distinction particularly clear. Australian-led BCOF personnel served in assigned sectors of western Japan and undertook occupation duties, including overseeing demilitarization, but they did so under the integrated occupation command rather than as the government of an independent national zone. Commonwealth disagreements over emperor accountability and security therefore mattered politically even when they did not result in separate territorial administration. The absence of a Soviet occupation zone in Hokkaido is equally important: the Soviet Union was an Allied power and member of oversight bodies, but its occupation of southern Sakhalin and the Kurils did not give it a zone in the main Japanese islands.

Other Allies did not all speak with one voice. China was emerging from war with Japan into renewed internal conflict; Australia and the Philippines gave priority to security, reparations, and responsibility; Britain also had commercial and imperial concerns; Soviet representatives sought influence in a settlement increasingly dominated by the United States. These differences are explanations for both the existence and the weakness of collective machinery. If Japan's occupation is called "Allied," that term describes the coalition's claimed authority and multiple national contributions. If it is called "American," that describes the nationality of the dominant command, the decisive financial and military resources, and the main channels of implementation. The two descriptions answer different questions.

Section 82.5 · Division I

The Geography of Occupation: Japan's Home Islands, Prefectures, and Separate Territories

SCAP's authority was geographically specific. In early 1946 the Eighth Army, under Eichelberger, took responsibility for the entire home islands, replacing the Sixth Army, which had initially occupied western Japan. American divisions and corps were distributed across Honshu, Kyushu, and Hokkaido, with headquarters and military government teams in each prefecture, while BCOF held its zone around Hiroshima and Kure and on Shikoku. The pattern concentrated the greatest American presence around Tokyo and Yokohama, the port and base cities, and the former military installations.

The definition of "Japan" itself was an act of occupation policy. On January 29, 1946, SCAPIN-677 separated from Japanese governmental and administrative authority a number of areas: the Ryukyu Islands south of latitude 30 degrees north (including Okinawa and Amami), the Izu islands south of a specified line, the Bonin (Ogasawara) islands, the Volcano islands, the Kuril Islands, and the Habomai and Shikotan islands, among others. This directive was stated not to be a final determination of Japan's boundaries, but it determined where the Japanese government could act. The overseas empire had already been removed from Japanese administration: Taiwan was taken over by the Republic of China in October 1945, Korea was divided at the 38th parallel into a Soviet zone in the north and an American military government in the south, Manchuria was occupied by the Soviet Union, and southern Sakhalin (Karafuto) was seized by Soviet forces.

Okinawa and the Ryukyus deserve particular attention. Their experience of the Battle of Okinawa (Chapter 80) had left a devastated population, and the islands came under an American military government that was far more direct than anything in the home islands. The Okinawan population did not experience "indirect rule" at all, and the separation from Japanese administration would be a persistent source of political conflict until reversion in 1972. Hokkaido, meanwhile, was affected by Soviet claims and, for the Ainu community, by the persistence of earlier land and assimilation policies; the Hokkaido Ainu Association was organized in 1946, and Ainu communities would be affected by the farmland reform discussed in Chapter 84. The map study below asks students to attend to these differences.

The spatial arrangement of rule changed over time. Military personnel were concentrated around ports, communications routes, former bases, and major urban centers, while small teams had to cover extensive rural jurisdictions. The visibility of Allied troops was therefore uneven. A resident of central Tokyo might encounter American soldiers, requisitioned buildings, and censorship institutions regularly; a resident of a remote mountain village might chiefly encounter changes transmitted through familiar prefectural and municipal offices. This unequal distribution of personnel was consistent with indirect rule, in which occupation officers monitored systems they did not staff in full.

SCAPIN-677 also demonstrates why administration and title to territory must be separated. It defined places where the Japanese government was not authorized to exercise governmental functions; the order explicitly avoided deciding ultimate sovereignty. An excluded island could thus be under a different military administration while its final legal status remained open. The distinction mattered to residents who faced different legal systems, access to mainland travel, and educational and economic regimes. It also prevents the hindsight error of applying a later treaty settlement automatically to every decision made in January 1946.

For Okinawans, whose society and infrastructure had been devastated in 1945, separation from mainland Japanese administration was more than an abstract map line. American military authorities made distinct decisions about police, schools, land, and public health; communication and travel with the main islands were constrained. The boundaries of Chapter 82 require only an outline of these institutions, not a comprehensive history of American rule in the Ryukyus. Yet leaving Okinawa off the occupation map would reproduce the Tokyo-centered assumption that the governance of all people previously ruled by Japan followed the same chronology. A map study must therefore place separate administrations beside the GHQ system rather than shading all former imperial possessions as one occupation unit.

Section 82.6 · Division II — The Machinery of Indirect Rule

Inside GHQ/SCAP: Command, Staff Sections, and Administrative Responsibilities

GHQ was organized as a military headquarters with specialized civil staff sections added to handle governance. The general staff sections, which handled personnel, intelligence, operations, and supply, were supplemented by special staff sections that corresponded roughly to the fields of civil government. The most important for this chapter were the Government Section (GS), headed by Brigadier General Courtney Whitney with Colonel Charles Kades as a principal deputy, responsible for political reorganization, elections, the civil service, and local government; the Economic and Scientific Section (ESS), responsible for industry, finance, trade, and labor; the Civil Information and Education Section (CIE), responsible for media, schools, and religion; the Public Health and Welfare Section (PHW), responsible for health, sanitation, and relief; the Legal Section; the Natural Resources Section; and the intelligence organizations under the Chief of the G-2 section, General Charles Willoughby, which included the Counter Intelligence Section and the Civil Censorship Detachment.

The headquarters was shaped by MacArthur's personnel. Senior positions were held by a circle of officers who had served with him in the Philippines, sometimes called the "Bataan Gang," and by civilians recruited from the American government, universities, and business, many of whom had worked on New Deal programs or on wartime planning. This produced identifiable tensions. The Government Section, which included reform-minded lawyers and administrators, repeatedly clashed with the intelligence staff under Willoughby, who was more conservative and more concerned with communism. Because MacArthur preferred to maintain tight central control and to receive recommendations from his subordinates separately, he sometimes allowed sections to pursue competing policies. Historians such as Eiji Takemae emphasize that the staff was a world of rivalries, personal loyalties, and shifting alliances.

SCAP's civil staff was also small relative to the task. A few thousand people, military and civilian, supervised a country of some seventy million. Very few spoke Japanese fluently; translation, interpretation, and the drafting of Japanese-language orders depended heavily on Japanese staff and on Japanese-American language specialists. That dependence on language mediators is one structural reason why indirect rule was necessary. A staff that could not read Japanese administrative documents could not govern directly, and it also could not always verify whether its orders were implemented as intended. The section system gave GHQ expertise but also fragmentation, and students should therefore picture SCAP as a collection of semi-autonomous bureaucracies speaking through one commander's name.

The internal hierarchy of GHQ divided policy drafting from field supervision. Government Section dealt with elections, political institutions, and administrative reform; the Economic and Scientific Section addressed industrial and financial policy; Civil Information and Education worked with schools, publishers, broadcasting, and civic instruction; Public Health and Welfare handled disease control and welfare coordination; intelligence and censorship units assessed security and controlled communications. These labels did not remove competition. A proposal concerning textbooks might have implications for constitutional principles, public opinion, occupation security, and the staffing of schools. A dispute over a civil servant could raise both purge policy and the practical need for expertise. Officials therefore negotiated across sections and did not always arrive at a single consistent approach.

Staffing produced its own limitations. American officers often held important powers with little prior experience of Japanese administrative law or the particular prefecture assigned to them. Japanese officials supplied translations, histories of institutions, statistics, and practical explanations. That dependence could help reforms succeed by making them intelligible to the people expected to implement them, but it could also privilege the bureaucracies capable of presenting their own interests as necessities. When sources describe a Japanese ministry as having "cooperated," historians should ask which office cooperated, whether the ministry proposed modifications, and whether another Japanese group opposed the result.

Policy also changed with personnel. MacArthur's commanding reputation did not eliminate the work of section chiefs, legal officers, interpreters, political advisers, and younger professionals who produced drafts and investigated conditions. The existence of an authoritative SCAPIN tells a historian little about the internal disagreement preceding it. Staff histories, working memoranda, and correspondence are needed to distinguish MacArthur's own intervention from policy developed by subordinates. This is particularly important for the later assertion that reform was either imposed entirely by Americans or generated mainly by Japanese initiative. The answer differs by issue and by stage of drafting.

Section 82.7 · Division II

SCAPINs and the Administrative Chain of Command

The characteristic instrument of occupation governance was the SCAPIN, an abbreviation for Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers Index Number, usually translated as an instruction or memorandum to the Japanese government. More than two thousand numbered SCAPINs were issued between 1945 and 1952, and they covered subjects from demobilization and the abolition of ultranationalist organizations to price controls, customs, and the registration of publications. Not all occupation instructions took this form. Some were issued as numbered memoranda, some as oral instructions delivered through liaison officers, some as "suggestions" whose force was understood, and some as directives that were never published. For that reason, a researcher cannot assume that the numbered series is complete, a limitation that matters for any statistical analysis of directives.

The chain ran as follows. A SCAP section drafted an instruction after consultation within GHQ, and it was reviewed by the staff and issued over the authority of the Supreme Commander. It was delivered in English with a Japanese translation to the Japanese government through the Central Liaison Office. The cabinet, with the relevant ministry, then decided how to execute it. Until the new constitution, the executing instrument was typically a Potsdam imperial ordinance, cabinet order, or ministerial ordinance based on the September 1945 enabling ordinance, which converted the instruction into Japanese law. The ministry then transmitted notifications to prefectural governors, who in turn informed municipalities and local organizations. Compliance was monitored by SCAP section staff, by Eighth Army military government teams, and by intelligence and censorship units.

Each link in that chain offered opportunities for modification. Ministries translated phrases, defined terms, and chose enforcement priorities. They could ask SCAP for clarification, propose alternative methods, or seek exceptions, and in some cases GHQ accepted Japanese counterproposals. They could also delay, comply narrowly, or comply in letter while undermining the purpose. Historians use the term "implementation gap" for the difference between the instruction as issued and its application on the ground. The documents available to historians, including the SCAPIN-DB collection and the National Archives' Record Group 331, mostly record the top of the chain; evidence of implementation must be pieced together from ministerial files, prefectural archives, military government reports, newspapers, and testimonies.

An order's lifecycle can be reconstructed from the surviving paperwork. Consider the public-assistance memorandum SCAPIN-775 of February 27, 1946. It was sent to the Japanese government through the Central Liaison Office and required a national agency to furnish basic relief through prefectural and local channels without preferential treatment. It also required periodic reports: monthly figures, beginning with March 1946, on families and individuals assisted and funds spent, broken down by prefecture. Thus SCAP was not merely announcing a social goal. It was specifying who should administer assistance, which populations should be counted, and what information would come back for inspection. The Japanese government's response required both national rules and offices capable of recording individual cases.

That example reveals several sites where the official design might encounter reality. The central ministry could interpret eligibility language; prefectural offices could ask whether their budgets or records were adequate; municipal officers might know which households had lost documentation, returned from overseas, or moved between districts. A monthly return could show that a local office reported many recipients without proving that assistance was timely or sufficient. Conversely, low reported coverage might reflect weak reporting rather than a low level of need. Historians must compare the SCAPIN with cabinet ordinances, ministry circulars, budget appropriations, local registers, and complaints before judging implementation.

A second example, the December 15, 1945 State Shinto directive (SCAPIN-448), specified the end of governmental sponsorship and official affiliation with Shinto institutions. Execution required education administrators, shrine-related offices, and local governments to disentangle public funds and official functions from religious practice. Its results could not be measured merely by a formal declaration: a scholar would need municipal expenditures, school circulars, and reports about continuing practices. Placed together, the welfare and religious directives show why the SCAPIN system was administrative as well as ideological. The crucial historical question was not simply what occupation headquarters desired but which Japanese procedures made that desire effective, partial, or contested.

Section 82.8 · Division II

The Japanese Cabinet, Central Liaison Office, and Surviving Bureaucracy

The Central Liaison Office (Shūsen Renraku Chūō Jimukyoku) was established in late August 1945 under the Foreign Ministry as the sole official channel between GHQ and the Japanese government, with local liaison offices later created in the prefectures. Staffed by diplomats and officials who could use English, it was the means by which instructions were received, queries raised, and negotiations conducted. The office did not simply pass paper. Its officers, including figures who would later become prominent in postwar politics, were skilled in deciding how to present Japanese positions to American officers, and the office became a locus of what historians call negotiated compliance. Foreign Minister Yoshida Shigeru, who became prime minister in 1946, drew on these networks.

Behind the cabinet stood the civil bureaucracy. The Finance, Commerce and Industry, Agriculture and Forestry, Welfare, Transport, and Education Ministries, and the Home Ministry until its dissolution, retained their career staffs, their procedures, and their records. Several wartime institutions were abolished: the Imperial General Headquarters was dissolved in September 1945, the Army and Navy Ministries were replaced in December 1945 by demobilization ministries and then absorbed, and the Greater East Asia Ministry was eliminated. But the bulk of the civil service survived. The reasons were practical. SCAP needed the officials to administer rationing, taxation, transport, and demobilization, and it could not replace tens of thousands of trained administrators with Americans who lacked Japanese. Many bureaucrats also calculated that cooperation would protect their institutions and enable them to influence reform.

The results varied. In some fields, such as labor and local government, SCAP pressed reforms that officials disliked. In others, such as industrial policy and economic planning, officials maintained wartime habits of administrative guidance and reconstituted them for reconstruction. Agencies such as the Economic Stabilization Board, created in 1946, served as Japanese-run instruments that coordinated recovery programs under SCAP supervision. Historians of the postwar economy have argued that the continuity of the bureaucracy, and of practices developed in the wartime control economy, helps explain later institutional patterns discussed in Chapters 89 and 91, while others stress how much the occupation changed the rules within which bureaucrats operated. The question of what bureaucrats were free to do, and what they actually chose to do, is the most important interpretive issue in this section, and the evidence is stronger for formal reorganization than for informal practice.

Japanese participation should not be confused with complete freedom to set policy. Cabinet ministers negotiated as representatives of a defeated government and could be dismissed or overruled. Yet the system assigned them responsibilities without which occupation directives could not operate. The Ministry of Finance remained central to national budgets, the Transport Ministry to trains and ports, and the Welfare Ministry to public assistance and health systems. Experienced officials could identify contradictions among directives and recommend schedules that reduced disruption. In doing so, they sometimes preserved policies or institutional habits that had developed before 1945. Bureaucratic survival was therefore both a practical resource and a limitation upon radical reconstruction.

The Central Liaison Office illustrates that influence in a form easy to overlook. Its personnel transmitted English-language memoranda, negotiated phrasing, assembled Japanese responses, and managed access between foreign headquarters and domestic officials. Translation could change the perceived scope of a rule even without deliberate obstruction. If a directive used a broad word such as "militarist," officials needed categories suitable for personnel decisions. If it used "public assistance," administrators needed definitions of household, need, and the responsible agency. The job of mediating these categories could influence who lost a position or qualified for help. That is why liaison correspondence, not only the final directive, belongs in a serious study of occupation governance.

The Diet and cabinet also provided forums in which the Japanese government explained policies to the public, including actions compelled by SCAP. Debates and official announcements might describe a measure as a domestic reform, a response to Allied orders, or an unavoidable administrative necessity. These competing presentations shaped legitimacy. Citizens who petitioned their representatives or approached a ministry were not necessarily dealing with the Americans directly, but they were acting within an institutional system constrained by American power. A history interested only in orders descending from Tokyo's occupation headquarters would miss the negotiations and public claims that gave the orders a recognizable Japanese political life.

Section 82.9 · Division II

Military Government, Prefectures, and Local Administration

Below Tokyo, occupation governance depended on the field organization of the Eighth Army. Military government teams were assigned to each prefecture (and, for a time, to major cities), consisting of American officers and enlisted staff who observed the work of the prefectural governor and officials, reported on compliance, investigated complaints, and passed instructions. Their formal role was supervisory. They did not take over the prefectural office, and they relied on interpreters and on the cooperation of Japanese staff. They reported regularly to higher headquarters on matters such as food collection, public health, demobilization, political activity, labor disputes, and the progress of reforms. Records of these teams are held in Record Group 331 of the U.S. National Archives and in some prefectural repositories, and they are among the best sources for the difference between policy and practice. In 1949, as the occupation's emphasis shifted, military government became "civil affairs," with teams and sections renamed to reflect a reduced supervisory role.

The local picture was shaped by Japanese institutional changes. Prefectural governors were still appointed by the Home Ministry until local autonomy reforms in 1947 introduced elected governors; municipal officials were also subject to central direction. The neighborhood associations (chōnaikai and tonarigumi) that had distributed rations and organized mobilization were abolished in 1947, which removed a layer of local control but also disrupted a channel for distributing goods. The police, which until 1948 were organized under the Home Ministry and then reformed into a decentralized system under the 1947 Police Law, remained the principal enforcement agents of local order.

Regional variation was considerable. Military government teams differed in personnel, interest, and style; some officers were energetic reformers and others administered cautiously. Large cities with greater American presence experienced the occupation differently from rural districts where few soldiers appeared. The first postwar elections, local autonomy, and public health campaigns each reached different communities at different speeds. Because the evidence for local administration is dispersed and uneven, historians generalize cautiously. A student reading Tokyo sources alone would overestimate the centralization of occupation government and underestimate the variety of local experiences.

Military government teams performed a checking function that the central headquarters could not undertake by itself. A team might inspect records in a prefectural office, examine public-health provisions, attend meetings on elections or education, and report obstacles to regional command. It was not ordinarily designed to replace all local officials or operate the prefecture's routine services. The National Diet Library's guide to World War II military-government operations records also shows administrative reorganization: regional and prefectural military-government arrangements were standardized in July 1946, after the earliest occupation detachments had been demobilized or reorganized. These organizational shifts matter because an officer's reports from autumn 1945 and mid-1947 may have traveled through different chains.

Field officers often confronted a gap between the apparent simplicity of a headquarters directive and a local government's resources. A ministry could issue a vaccination campaign, but a prefecture needed clinicians, supplies, transport, refrigeration, publicity, and data collection. A reform creating elected prefectural governors could change the position of local executives without removing civil servants who understood revenue collection or road maintenance. Military government reports are particularly valuable for such differences between institutional design and local capacity. They are also partial records: American officers tended to report problems visible to their unit, and language barriers sometimes filtered what Japanese subordinates and residents could convey.

Social position and geography made encounters unequal. A Japanese governor could seek a meeting and negotiate through an interpreter; a Korean resident facing registration or policing might have neither comparable access nor protection. Women who organized civic activities sometimes found new opportunities to approach officials while encountering assumptions about gender that both Japanese and Americans carried into the period. The occupation's administrative reach thus rested on different relationships of authority at national, prefectural, and household levels. Explaining this layered structure is more informative than referring generally to "the occupation" as if its officials were present with equal power and visibility everywhere.

Section 82.10 · Division III — The Emperor, Japanese Politics, and Administrative Reconstruction

Why the Emperor Was Retained: Imperial Legitimacy and Occupation Strategy

The decision to retain Hirohito as emperor was one of the most consequential choices of the occupation, and it was made gradually rather than in a single act. Within American policy circles, officials such as Joseph Grew, who had served as ambassador to Japan, argued that the imperial institution was a stabilizing force and that its abolition would provoke resistance and make surrender harder to enforce. Others believed the institution was the foundation of militarism and ultranationalism and should be eliminated or fundamentally changed. The Potsdam Declaration and the August 11 reply left the question open, and the September 1945 policy statement declined to commit the United States either way.

The practical reasons for retention were strong in the autumn of 1945. The emperor's broadcast had persuaded army and navy commands, including large forces overseas, to lay down arms, and several imperial princes were dispatched to commands in China, Southeast Asia, and Manchuria to reinforce the order. GHQ saw the throne as a means to secure compliance with demobilization, the Allied landing, and subsequent reforms without major violence. MacArthur and his staff were also concerned about the possibility of guerrilla resistance and collapse of public order if the emperor were removed or tried. In a January 1946 message to Washington, MacArthur argued that prosecuting the emperor would provoke upheaval and require very large reinforcements, an argument historians have examined critically because it was a forceful statement of a position that served his own preferences.

The decision was contested then and since. Some Allied governments, notably Australia, and many Japanese and foreign critics argued that the emperor bore responsibility for the war and should be held accountable. Historians such as Herbert Bix have emphasized that the occupation's protection of the emperor required shaping the historical record and limiting inquiry into his role, while others, including John Dower, have emphasized that retention produced a collaborative relationship between SCAP and the throne that was useful but morally and historically costly. Chapter 83 examines the question of responsibility directly. The point for this chapter is institutional: retention meant that occupation government relied on imperial authority to legitimate its orders, and that the transformation of the imperial institution became a project carried out inside the occupation rather than against it.

Arguments about retaining Hirohito concerned more than his personal popularity. The emperor stood at the junction of constitutional authority, military obedience, and civic ritual. In August 1945 the government had used imperial instruction to end resistance, and occupation planners feared that openly humiliating or immediately removing the monarch could weaken a command structure still needed to demobilize forces and maintain public order. Some American policymakers believed the throne might serve as an anchor for a transformed constitutional order. Those calculations rested on assumptions about Japanese society that must be assessed rather than repeated as inevitable facts: loyalty to the emperor varied across individuals and regions, and the institution's prewar role could not be separated from its legitimating function in wartime mobilization.

Preserving an institution did not mean preserving its previous powers or protecting every associated organization. SCAP's authority could override imperial commands, and the Imperial Household Ministry and related institutions faced restructuring. The emperor's speeches and appearances changed as occupation authorities considered the political effects of royal symbolism. The decision not to prosecute Hirohito was especially consequential because it affected the range of questions likely to be examined in the war-crimes process. Chapter 83 treats that issue directly; here the point is how a contested choice concerning accountability helped determine which authority could remain visibly continuous while the administrative state was being transformed.

A comparison of alternatives is useful if carefully framed. Removal of the emperor would not automatically have produced either anarchy or democracy, and retention did not automatically guarantee a peaceful occupation. Supporters of retention pointed to the practical advantage of a known symbol and a familiar channel of obedience. Critics argued that permitting the institution to survive risked obscuring responsibility and validating parts of the political hierarchy that had supported war. Contemporary memoranda, the public pronouncements of SCAP, Japanese diaries, and the interventions of other Allied governments illuminate these competing judgments; none supplies direct access to the opinions of all Japanese people.

Section 82.11 · Division III

MacArthur, Hirohito, and the Changing Public Position of the Emperor

On September 27, 1945, Hirohito paid a visit to MacArthur at the American Embassy residence, the first of several meetings. The conversation took place with only interpreters present, and its content is disputed. MacArthur later wrote in his memoirs that the emperor took responsibility for the actions of those who served him; Japanese accounts differ in emphasis, and an interpreter's memorandum that became available decades afterward has been used to support more cautious readings. Historians therefore treat the substance of the talk as uncertain, while treating the event's symbolic significance as clear.

That significance was amplified by a photograph taken on the occasion, showing the emperor in formal morning dress standing stiffly beside MacArthur, who wore an open-collared uniform without a tie. When Japanese newspapers published the picture on September 29, the Home Ministry attempted to prohibit its distribution. SCAP ordered the restrictions on the press lifted, and the picture circulated widely. For many Japanese readers, the image conveyed the relationship of the new order: the emperor had come to the foreign commander, not the reverse. For American and Allied audiences it conveyed the dominance of the occupation and the continuity of the throne. The image is a good example of a source whose meaning depends on its audience.

The imperial rescript of January 1, 1946 is often called the "declaration of humanity" (ningen sengen), a label that was applied later. The text, prepared with the involvement of Japanese officials and SCAP staff, reaffirmed the Charter Oath of 1868, which Japanese drafters hoped would show that democratic principles were part of Japan's own tradition, and stated that the ties between the emperor and his people rested on mutual trust and affection and not merely on the legend that the emperor was a divine being or that the Japanese were superior to other peoples. It did not repudiate the throne's claimed descent or formally abolish imperial sovereignty, and its precise meaning is still debated: some historians view it as an explicit renunciation of divinity, others as a carefully limited statement that preserved the dynasty's mythological status. MacArthur welcomed it publicly. The emperor was subsequently presented to the public in a new way through tours of the country beginning in 1946. Chapter 84 examines how the 1947 Constitution then redefined the emperor as a symbol of the state and the unity of the people, with no powers related to government.

The imperial household itself was also reorganized. Imperial property was frozen and later reduced, most collateral branches of the imperial family were removed from the imperial house in 1947, and the Imperial Household Ministry was reduced and eventually reorganized as an agency. These changes reduced the institution's economic and political reach while leaving the monarchy in place.

The famous photograph of MacArthur and Hirohito is evidence about deliberately staged political communication, not a complete account of their conversation. The contrast in dress, physical posture, and setting made the relationship of authority visible in a way that detailed diplomatic memoranda could not. Japanese newspapers' publication of the photograph raised the question of who could control images of the emperor. The shock it caused for some observers was not universal, and any historical interpretation should distinguish press reaction and later memory from the intentions of the participants. The photographic encounter helped redefine the emperor's public status precisely because imperial authority had previously been communicated through carefully managed rituals of distance and reverence.

The January 1, 1946 rescript was likewise a political text produced within a contested cultural setting. Its often-used label, the "humanity declaration," can be misleading if taken as a simple confession that the emperor was an ordinary person. The text criticized the notion that the relationship between monarch and subjects rested upon myths of divinity or racial superiority, while also invoking the Five Charter Oath of 1868. This invoked an older Japanese reform tradition to situate change in a national historical vocabulary. Later accounts of the rescript's meaning often emphasize the separation of political legitimacy from divine descent, but the precise relationship between the Japanese wording, the emperor's status in Shinto traditions, and later constitutional symbolism requires care.

There were also administrative reasons the emperor's position mattered. The imperial household had property, ceremonial personnel, public finances, and relations with ministries and political elites. Reforming those connections required rules affecting appropriations, the scope of household offices, and the boundary between public functions and private ritual. Keeping Hirohito on the throne did not preserve the old institutional system intact; it allowed occupation authorities and Japanese officials to rework the monarchy from within. The 1947 Constitution would later define the emperor as the symbol of the state and unity of the people, but this chapter confines itself to the institutional transition and reserves constitutional provisions for Chapter 84.

Section 82.12 · Division III

Occupation-Era Cabinets, the Diet, and Political Parties

Between 1945 and 1952 Japan had a succession of cabinets that governed under external supervision. The first, headed by Prince Higashikuni Naruhiko, was formed on August 17, 1945 to manage the surrender and was dominated by traditional elites. It resigned on October 5, in the wake of the Civil Liberties Directive, which the government considered impossible to carry out because it required dismissing the Home Minister and police leadership. It was replaced on October 9 by a cabinet under Shidehara Kijūrō, a former foreign minister of the 1920s associated with international cooperation, whose government was more acceptable to GHQ and presided over the first reforms of the labor, electoral, and educational systems. After the April 1946 election, Yoshida Shigeru formed a cabinet in May, and he led the government through much of the occupation, with an interruption from May 1947 to October 1948 during which coalitions under the Socialist Katayama Tetsu and the Democrat Ashida Hitoshi governed.

Political parties reappeared quickly. By the end of 1945 the Liberal Party under Hatoyama Ichirō, the Progressive Party, the Socialist Party, and the Communist Party, the last led by Tokuda Kyūichi, who had been released from prison, and Nosaka Sanzō, who returned from China, were active, joined by smaller groups such as the Cooperative Party. Some were direct successors of prewar parties; others were new organizations of labor and the left. The Diet, which continued as the Imperial Diet, revised the electoral law in December 1945, extending the vote to women, and was dissolved for the general election of April 10, 1946, in which the Liberals won a plurality, the Progressives and Socialists each won around ninety seats, and the Communists won a handful. Chapter 84 examines the significance of women's participation.

Occupation oversight of politics took formal and informal forms. SCAP did not choose prime ministers, but it could and did influence their selection. In May 1946, on the eve of forming a government, Hatoyama was purged by SCAP order, an intervention that opened the premiership to Yoshida. The Government Section monitored elections, supplied "suggestions" on legislation, and reviewed bills before they went to the Diet. The politics of the period was therefore an unusual blend of competitive elections, vigorous parliamentary debate, and a supervising authority whose preferences all politicians had to estimate. Politicians who understood the occupation's priorities, as Yoshida did, could use them to their own advantage, and those who misjudged them, as Hatoyama did, could find themselves removed.

Political activity revived within the inherited framework of a cabinet and a Diet whose authority remained subject to military occupation. Parties could organize, publish platforms, nominate candidates, and contest elections, but the selection of leaders was not simply an autonomous domestic process. The purge could exclude an otherwise viable candidate, and SCAP's reactions could constrain cabinet formation or legislative proposals. The removal of Hatoyama Ichirō from public office just before he might have formed a cabinet in 1946 illustrates how legal electoral procedures and occupation personnel policy intersected. Yoshida Shigeru's rise must therefore be understood through both party alignments in Japan and an externally imposed screening regime.

These parties should not be reduced to a single "democratic" camp. Conservatives differed over the extent of reform, unions and Socialist politicians pressed for labor rights and social security, Communists reclaimed a legal political presence after repression, and women participated as voters and candidates under a changed electoral order. The political field remained unsettled, and policies often drew criticism from several directions. Local political organizations and newspapers provided routes through which social demands reached the cabinet, even as censorship and occupation vetoes limited what could be said and done. Political liberalization thus expanded the number of actors who could make claims without removing the fact of military supremacy.

Institutional continuity also shaped the technical conduct of elections. Electoral rolls, district administration, polling places, police arrangements, and certification were handled by Japanese authorities. SCAP officers observed and pressed reforms, but they were not numerous enough to perform all this work directly. An electoral directive's implementation must therefore be evaluated through the local apparatus that registered candidates and counted ballots. The distinction is analytically important: an election held under occupation can create meaningful political competition, yet its rules and eligibility requirements may still reflect the preferences of a foreign authority. The chapter should analyze that duality rather than classify the election as either wholly imposed or wholly free.

Section 82.13 · Division III

Civil Liberties, Political Prisoners, and the Dismantling of the Repressive State

The most dramatic early use of SCAP's authority came on October 4, 1945, with the memorandum known as SCAPIN-93, "Removal of Restrictions on Political, Civil, and Religious Liberties." It directed the Japanese government to abolish all laws and regulations that restricted freedom of thought, religion, assembly, and speech, to release all persons detained under such laws within days, to abolish the Special Higher Police and any other agencies that had suppressed such liberties, and to dismiss the Home Minister, the heads of the police bureau, and other officials associated with that apparatus. The cabinet's response illustrates the interplay of authority and capacity. Home Minister Yamazaki Iwao had told a foreign journalist days earlier that the police would continue to suppress communists and critics of the throne, and the cabinet decided it could not carry out the directive; it resigned on October 5.

The consequences were immediate and symbolic. Political prisoners, including Communist leaders who had spent as long as eighteen years in prison, were released on October 10, and crowds greeted them. The Peace Preservation Law, the Public Peace Police Law, and related instruments, laws described in Chapter 69 as the legal structure of political restriction in the 1920s, were repealed in the weeks following. The Special Higher Police (Tokkō) was dissolved, as were thought-surveillance bodies, and the military police (Kenpeitai) disappeared with the armed forces. The Home Ministry, which had overseen the police, local government, and censorship for decades, lost its police functions and was dissolved at the end of 1947. A 1947 Police Law, effective in 1948, replaced the centralized system with a decentralized structure of municipal police and national rural police under public safety commissions.

Students should note the limits. Dismantling the legal and institutional apparatus did not remove its personnel from society. Former Special Higher Police and other officials were purged from their posts, but their skills in surveillance did not vanish, and later occupation intelligence operations and the Japanese police relied again on some of them. The repeal of repressive laws also coexisted with SCAP's own controls on expression, discussed in Division IV. Finally, the release of prisoners and the end of legal restrictions made possible an explosion of organizing: unions, parties, newspapers, and associations appeared in a matter of months, including Korean organizations whose members had been among those held or watched by the prewar police.

The October 4 directive was unusually forceful because the machinery of political repression lay deep within ordinary administrative life. The Special Higher Police investigated political and religious organizations, but their activities depended upon prosecutors, police stations, official records, school officials, and informants. Ending an agency's formal existence did not erase every file, habit of surveillance, or official who had worked within it. Nor did the release of political prisoners automatically restore their health, livelihoods, or public reputation. The immediate opening of political life was genuine, but what remained in the bureaucracy complicated later efforts to measure the depth of change.

One of the directive's effects was to bring the Japanese cabinet into direct conflict with the occupation. Higashikuni's government could not reconcile SCAP's demands with its existing political assumptions and resigned; the Shidehara cabinet formed amid the ensuing transition. This was a demonstration of indirect rule at its most coercive: SCAP did not have to replace ministries with American offices to make the continuation of a cabinet impossible. The same command could liberate long-imprisoned dissidents and compel a new Japanese administration to issue the implementing orders. Students should distinguish the American instruction, the Japanese legal abolition of repressive organizations, the release of named detainees, and the longer transformation of policing.

The relationship between civil liberty and institutional capacity is also crucial. Groups previously banned had to rebuild membership networks and publications; returning activists could clash with established political leaders; local police still dealt with demonstrations and property conflicts. Freedom of association meant that occupations, labor disputes, and public gatherings could become visible in ways they had not been during wartime mobilization. Yet the new political space was not beyond surveillance. Occupation intelligence and censorship bodies simultaneously monitored activity, and officers could interpret left-wing militancy as a security threat. The result was not a neat transition from repression to unfettered liberty, but a period in which significant rights were reopened inside a system of exceptional command power.

Section 82.14 · Division III

Political Purges, Disqualification, and Bureaucratic Continuity

The purge program was the occupation's principal instrument for removing the people thought responsible for aggressive war. On January 4, 1946, SCAP issued directives (SCAPIN-548 and SCAPIN-550) dissolving ultranationalist organizations and requiring the removal of undesirable persons from public office. Seven categories, usually labeled A through G, defined those to be excluded: war criminal suspects; career military officers and certain officials of the military; leaders of ultranationalist and terrorist societies; officials of organizations that supported expansion, such as the Imperial Rule Assistance Association; leaders of financial and development institutions involved in expansion abroad; governors of occupied territories; and a residual category of other militarists and ultranationalists. In 1947 the program was extended to economic, media, and local government leaders. Scholars commonly estimate that roughly two hundred thousand persons were designated or excluded by the end of the program, a large majority of them former military personnel.

The purge touched politics directly. It removed many Diet members elected in the wartime 1942 election and decimated the leadership of the older parties, producing the generational turnover that brought new politicians into office, and it removed Hatoyama Ichirō on the eve of his premiership. At the same time, its application was uneven. Japanese screening committees administered much of the program, and the career civil bureaucracy was affected far less than the military, politicians, and business and media leaders. Most senior officials who had served in wartime ministries remained, because SCAP needed administrators and because screening focused on those with visible ideological or leadership roles. Beginning in 1950, as Cold War priorities changed, tens of thousands of purged persons were gradually released from exclusion, and many returned to public life, including figures who later became prime ministers.

Historians therefore describe the purge as both significant and limited. It broke the political and military leadership of the wartime state, and it removed individuals from posts for years. It did not fundamentally restructure the civil service, and it did not eliminate the personal and institutional networks that connected the wartime and postwar eras. The reasons were simultaneously pragmatic, ideological, and bureaucratic, and the comparison of the purge in different sectors, such as the military, the economy, education, and the media, is a major theme of later chapters.

The purge system was more complicated than the removal of a list of well-known wartime leaders. Occupation directives defined categories of disqualification and directed Japanese screening bodies to investigate careers and affiliations. The Japanese state retained much of the paperwork necessary to classify candidates for office, but its bureaucrats were also capable of protecting colleagues, supplying narrow interpretations, or treating ambiguous service histories inconsistently. A screening decision might depend on the survival of a personnel file, the individual's ability to explain former duties, and the political urgency attached to a particular office. Official totals therefore have to be read with attention to who was covered, which phase of screening they measure, and whether disqualification meant permanent removal or temporary exclusion.

The distinction between eliminating organizations and evaluating individual people matters. SCAPIN-548 concerned the dissolution of certain organizations associated with militarism and ultranationalism, while SCAPIN-550 addressed the removal of undesirable persons from public office. Both were issued on January 4, 1946, but they had different subjects and different implementing problems. Disbanding an organization did not make all its former members automatically ineligible for every job; conversely, an individual could be disqualified from public office regardless of whether an association still existed. This explains why Japanese administrative committees and the occupation's Government Section had to devote sustained attention to personnel questions.

Continuity was therefore selective, not accidental. The occupation could remove high-profile political and military figures while leaving technical expertise in ministries and local government intact. Advocates viewed this as essential to reconstruction; critics saw opportunities for former wartime administrators to retain influence under new institutional labels. Later relaxations and reversals of exclusion rules should be connected to broader political changes, but the full narrative of the Cold War Reverse Course and subsequent Red Purge belongs to Chapter 89. Chapter 82 should establish the early legal architecture and the inherent tension between comprehensive political cleansing and the practical administration of a large country.

Section 82.15 · Division III

Governing Democratic Reform: Japanese Initiative and Occupation Oversight

It is misleading to describe the democratic reforms as simply imposed from above. The typical process involved a combination of SCAP initiative, Japanese drafting, bargaining, and legislation. A SCAP section would identify a reform objective and communicate it, often as a "suggestion." Japanese ministries or committees would draft a text. The Government Section or another unit would review it, ask for changes, and sometimes substitute its own language. The cabinet would then submit it to the Diet, where it might be amended. The Trade Union Law of December 1945, for example, was drafted by Japanese officials and reformers with SCAP encouragement and later replaced by a more comprehensive system. The Constitution of 1946 is the exception that proves the pattern: SCAP's Government Section wrote a model draft in February 1946 after the Japanese government's own proposals were judged inadequate, but even then the Japanese government and the Diet revised and adopted the text through the existing legal procedures (Chapter 84).

Japanese society also supplied its own energy. Labor unions grew within two years to millions of members. Women's organizations, such as those led by Ichikawa Fusae, who had been prominent in the suffrage campaign described in Chapter 69, resumed activity almost immediately after the surrender. Socialists, liberals, and some conservatives proposed their own reforms, and teachers, students, and writers formed associations and publications. These groups used the new openness to press their agendas, sometimes beyond what SCAP wished, as in the labor movement's escalating strikes in 1946 and 1947. The occupation, in short, created an opening and then had to manage what came through it. It could and did limit movements it considered threats, a pattern discussed in Section 82.19.

For historians, the central question is how to assign credit and responsibility. Some emphasize SCAP's role as the engine of reform, arguing that without foreign authority the changes would not have occurred. Others emphasize preexisting Japanese reform currents, from the Taishō movements to wartime bureaucratic planning, and stress that Japanese actors adapted and sometimes diluted SCAP's initiatives. The debate below presents the principal positions.

The occupation's claim to democratize Japan reached through several different institutions. Government Section worked on political administration and constitutional questions; Civil Information and Education influenced the educational and informational environment; public-health staff negotiated with welfare and medical officials; economic administrators worked with financial and industrial ministries. Japanese ministries translated these demands into bills, ordinances, budgets, manuals, and instructions. In some cases Japanese reformers had advocated comparable changes long before 1945. It is therefore inaccurate to equate the American origin of a directive with the American origin of every idea contained in the reform it produced.

A practical illustration is the problem of local-government change. An occupation instruction favoring local autonomy meant little until responsibility for the governor's office, the police, schools, and prefectural finances was specified in Japanese law. Elected officials would have to work with career administrators and tax structures built under earlier governments. Reform involved assigning functions and resources as well as proclaiming values. In education and labor, too, the ability of a ministry to issue instructions was only the beginning: teachers, employers, workers, and local councils could interpret or resist the new arrangements. These are matters of implementation that Chapter 82 should demonstrate without retelling the full social and legal history assigned to Chapter 84.

One reason indirect rule survived was that both sides could claim a degree of authorship. GHQ could report success in achieving occupation objectives, while Japanese cabinets could present reforms as national choices expressed through domestic institutions. This overlap helped make some innovations durable, yet it could conceal unequal power and marginalize people who lacked access to officials. The historian's task is not to distribute credit mechanically between foreign and domestic actors. It is to ask which alternative proposals existed, whose language entered the final rule, who controlled the schedule, how the rule was funded, and which groups experienced the resulting institution.

Historical Debate

Did Indirect Rule Facilitate Reform or Preserve Too Much of the Prewar State?

The question: By governing through the Japanese state, did the occupation enable rapid and legitimate reform, or did it compromise reform by leaving the bureaucracy, the throne, and many elites intact?

Interpretation A — Indirect rule as enabling: Some historians argue that governing through Japanese institutions made reform possible at low cost and with Japanese ownership. It gave reforms a Japanese legal form, drew on administrative expertise, prevented collapse, and allowed Japanese reformers to participate. They emphasize that a purely foreign government would have lacked the capacity and legitimacy to carry out so many changes.
Interpretation B — Indirect rule as conserving: Other historians argue that reliance on the existing state, the retention of the emperor, and the limited purge of the civil service preserved structures and personnel that shaped postwar politics in conservative directions. They point to the survival of ministerial power, the later return of purged leaders, and the narrowing of war-responsibility inquiry.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same sources: SCAP and ministry records, purge statistics, accounts of drafting processes, and comparisons with Germany and the colonies. They differ over counterfactuals, since neither can demonstrate what direct rule would have produced.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians evaluate the occupation's achievements, the origins of postwar political and economic institutions, and the continuing debates over war memory and the imperial institution.

Section 82.16 · Division IV — Censorship, Information, Coercion, and the Limits of Occupation Government

Occupation Censorship: Newspapers, Radio, Mail, Film, and the Press Code

While SCAP ordered the end of Japanese censorship and the political police, it created its own system of control over public communication. On September 19, 1945, SCAPIN-33 promulgated a Press Code for Japan, and a Radio Code followed within days. The Press Code, ostensibly intended to promote truthful reporting and to prevent disturbance of public tranquility, targeted false or destructive criticism of the Allied powers, destructive criticism of occupation troops, unauthorized troop-movement reports, and news deemed misleading or disturbing to public tranquility. Additional restrictions against criticism of SCAP policy developed through internal censorship guidance and enforcement rather than being written as a general prohibition on all criticism in the public Press Code. The code was elaborated in internal guidance used by censors. Scholars note that the working lists of prohibited topics, sometimes described as about thirty categories, included criticism of the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, Koreans, and Chinese; discussion of the Tokyo trials in a critical vein; criticism of the new constitution's drafting; discussion of censorship itself; and certain accounts of the atomic bombings.

The agency responsible was the Civil Censorship Detachment, part of the intelligence apparatus under General Willoughby. It employed thousands of Japanese staff, many of them educated people who needed work, to read publications, mail, telegrams, and telephone conversations. Newspapers and magazines were at first subject to pre-publication review, which required submission of proofs, and later to post-publication review, which required publication followed by monitoring and the threat of suspension. Books, films, and broadcasts were controlled in parallel, with the Civil Information and Education Section supervising radio and film. The Asahi shimbun and the Dōmei news agency were temporarily suspended in September 1945, an early demonstration of enforcement. In stages in 1948 and 1949, pre-censorship was relaxed, and the censorship apparatus was dismantled by the end of 1949, although other forms of supervision continued.

Occupation censorship also reached culture. Plays and films that glorified feudal loyalty or revenge, including some staples of kabuki and swordplay cinema, were discouraged or banned for a time because of their association with militarism, and thousands of titles were removed from circulation as ultranationalist or militaristic. These decisions reflected SCAP's judgments about what was dangerous, and they were applied in a society that had experienced years of Japanese wartime censorship, an irony that Japanese writers and critics noted. The extent of the system was concealed: occupation censorship practice restricted disclosure of censorship interventions, so readers often did not know what had been removed. Recovering what was censored is the work of historians who use the Prange Collection, where the marked proofs survive. The system suggests that the occupation's commitment to free expression was real but conditional.

The Press Code's wording requires precise reading. SCAPIN-33 of September 19, 1945 did not literally prohibit every critical statement about the Allies. Its provisions prohibited "false or destructive" criticism of the Allied powers and "destructive" criticism of occupation forces, along with material judged capable of disturbing public tranquility, unauthorized reports on troop movements, propaganda-like distortion, and certain editorializing in purported news. Those terms were elastic and could support extensive restriction. Broader prohibitions in actual practice—including sensitivity about criticism of occupation policy and subjects touching atomic-bomb suffering—must be explained through internal Civil Censorship Detachment instructions, screening practices, and later evidence, not misquoted as if each appeared verbatim in the ten-paragraph public code.

The difference between open rules and concealed enforcement created a specific problem for researchers and journalists. Publications could be held up, edited, or refused while readers were not necessarily told that an occupation censor had intervened. Editors learned to anticipate restricted subjects and might omit them before a submission. That anticipation is difficult to quantify: a surviving marked page shows intervention, whereas an article never written or a topic avoided may leave little documentary trace. The Prange Collection preserves an unusually rich body of publications submitted for screening, but it is a record of a control process, not a complete census of everything ordinary readers were prevented from considering.

Censorship was also institutionally distinct from CIE's efforts to influence public opinion. Both could affect the same newspapers or broadcasts, but a campaign encouraging democratic civic instruction and an intelligence unit suppressing material were not identical functions. The occupation sometimes presented itself as the liberator of expression from Japanese militarist controls while restricting communication about its own operations. The contradiction was not merely rhetorical. Newspaper editors, writers, teachers, and readers lived with a wider political field and new boundaries drawn by an outside authority. A careful chapter should preserve both sides of that experience without assuming that a constitutional promise of free speech immediately ended all forms of censorship.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Censorship and Political Authority

Pre-censorship required publishers to submit material before publication so that censors could approve or delete passages; post-censorship permitted publication and reviewed it afterward, leaving publishers to anticipate what would be punished. The latter often produced self-censorship, since writers learned the boundaries by guesswork. Kenetsu is the Japanese term for censorship, and Japanese critics contrasted SCAP's kenetsu with the wartime Home Ministry's.

The concept is important because the occupation presented itself as bringing freedom of speech under the Potsdam Declaration while imposing restrictions in the name of security. The two commitments were reconciled in SCAP's view by a distinction between legitimate political debate and material that threatened the occupation's mission. Whether that distinction could be drawn neutrally is one of the central questions of the chapter.

Section 82.17 · Division IV

Civil Information and Education: Managing Public Opinion and Political Reorientation

The Civil Information and Education Section (CIE) was SCAP's instrument for shaping what Japanese people were told and taught. Its divisions dealt with the press, radio, film, publications, education, religion, and the arts, and its staff included officers, educators, journalists, and specialists in Japanese culture. CIE supervised the national broadcaster NHK, directed programming, required the broadcast of material it wished to convey, and reviewed and approved film production. It carried out much of the work described in Chapter 84 on education reform and the separation of state and Shinto, which followed the December 1945 directive on state support for Shinto.

Some of CIE's early campaigns were explicit efforts to alter public understanding of the war. In December 1945, newspapers ran a series on the history of the Pacific War supplied by CIE, and NHK began a radio program, "The Truth Is This" (Shinsō wa kōda), which presented the wartime leadership's actions and, for the first time for many listeners, accounts of atrocities and of how the war had been conducted. Listeners reacted in different ways, from shock to disbelief to resentment, and later critics argued that the programs were propaganda. Some writers, notably the critic Etō Jun, interpreted such efforts as a systematic "war guilt information program" intended to instill guilt. Other historians, including John Dower, regard the evidence for a single coordinated program as limited, while acknowledging that CIE worked deliberately to change attitudes. The existence and scale of such a program remain contested, and students should distinguish the documented campaigns from broader claims about their intent and effect.

CIE also conducted public-opinion research, consulted with Japanese educators, and tried to promote civic education. Its efforts depended on Japanese collaborators, such as writers, broadcasters, and educators, some of whom were enthusiastic and others reluctant. The Section's work illustrates a theme of the chapter: the occupation sought to use its authority to promote democratic values, but the method, directive and supervised communication, sat uneasily with the content, free and pluralistic debate. Japanese audiences were not passive. They selected, interpreted, and criticized what they received, and many embraced the new freedoms while remaining skeptical of the occupier's motives.

CIE operated by encouraging, commissioning, supervising, and sometimes rejecting work created by Japanese institutions. Its staff could influence which topics schools emphasized, how broadcasts presented the recent war, and what civic language appeared in public-information campaigns. Yet Japanese journalists, educators, publishers, and radio personnel supplied much of the actual content. A national campaign could sound different when introduced by an announcer familiar to a local audience or explained by a teacher who had formerly worked under wartime ideological restrictions. The intermediary's experience and credibility shaped the result. Therefore the sources needed for analysis are not only GHQ memoranda but also Japanese scripts, school publications, newspaper debates, and responses from audiences.

The War Guilt Information Program is especially instructive. Occupation officials promoted accounts of the conduct and consequences of the war intended to weaken militarist justifications and encourage a different public memory. Some publications and broadcasts exposed information that wartime censorship had kept from the population. Yet the selection, tone, and timing of information were shaped by an occupying power with its own political interests. This does not render the information necessarily false, nor make every reader passive. It does require students to separate factual historical evidence from a state-sponsored attempt to organize how evidence would be interpreted.

CIE's work with education likewise had institutional limits. School curricula, textbooks, and teacher training could be supervised from Tokyo, but implementation occurred in classrooms whose buildings, staffing, and materials varied widely. Teachers sometimes embraced change, sometimes adapted it to their circumstances, and sometimes struggled simply to operate amid shortages. Teachers' unions, school principals, parents, and pupils had different interests. Religious and political questions could also cross departmental boundaries. The State Shinto directive, for instance, required changes in government-supported educational practice while affecting shrines and local public finance. This was governance through information and institutions, not a cultural transformation that could be accomplished by producing slogans. The extensive later history of cinema, broadcasting, and jazz belongs to Chapter 88.

Section 82.18 · Division IV

Everyday Administration: Health, Welfare, Policing, and Local Authority

For most Japanese, the occupation was experienced less through grand directives than through routine administration. The Public Health and Welfare Section, directed by Colonel Crawford Sams, oversaw campaigns against infectious disease in a country with damaged sanitation, large numbers of returning soldiers and repatriates, and outbreaks of typhus, cholera, and smallpox. Methods included mass vaccination, quarantine of arriving ships, and the dusting of people and housing with DDT to kill lice. Japanese health officials, public health nurses, and local authorities carried out these measures, and surviving records show a pattern of American direction and Japanese implementation. The section also pushed social welfare policy: SCAPIN-775 of February 1946 required a system of public assistance that was not discriminatory and that made the state responsible for relief, which led to the Daily Life Security Law, administered through local welfare offices.

Occupation forces also appeared in everyday life through requisition. American agencies took over office buildings, hotels, theaters, department stores, and housing, displacing Japanese tenants and owners. In Tokyo, for example, the Dai-Ichi Seimei Building housed GHQ, and parts of the Ginza became post exchanges and clubs for Allied personnel, while family housing districts were constructed for American dependents. Japan bore the cost through payments for occupation expenses, which consumed a substantial share of government revenue in the early years. Local governments were required to supply labor, buildings, interpreters, and materials. Signs marked areas off-limits to Japanese, and Japanese women's and men's contact with soldiers was shaped by rules that Chapter 86 examines in detail.

Police work was a third point of contact. Japanese police continued to enforce ordinary law and, until the 1947 reform, worked within a centralized hierarchy; American military police and the Counter Intelligence Corps operated alongside them, and Allied personnel were generally subject to their own courts, not Japanese jurisdiction. This jurisdictional asymmetry was a persistent source of resentment, because Japanese authorities could not prosecute crimes by Allied personnel in the same way, a theme developed in Chapter 86 and in the later history of the American military presence in Okinawa and on the main islands.

The experience of public health gives an unusually concrete view of how headquarters instructions met ordinary administration. Under Crawford Sams, SCAP's Public Health and Welfare Section advised and supervised Japanese national authorities, which transmitted measures through prefectural hygiene departments. Military-government public-health officers monitored local conditions and sent reports through the occupation chain. The arrangement involved overlapping lines of supervision: a Japanese civil servant might take formal instruction from a Japanese superior while an American officer evaluated whether the prefecture complied with SCAP policy. The distinction mattered for responsibility, particularly when resources were lacking or a campaign harmed the people it was intended to protect.

Kyoto offers a documented example that should not be generalized automatically to all Japan. Physician John D. Glismann arrived in September 1947 to work with the Kyoto Military Government Team and the prefectural Hygiene Department headed by Tadayoshi Tsuchiya. Historian Sey Nishimura reconstructs their interactions from monthly administrative reports, Glismann's correspondence, interviews, and photographs. Their work addressed infectious disease, sanitation, hospital conditions, and maternal and child health. These records show how an American officer could make recommendations while Japanese experts supplied experience, budgets, personnel, and direct contact with residents. The program's possibilities also depended on civic organizations and the willingness of households to participate.

The example exposes limitations rather than only achievements. The November 1948 diphtheria vaccination campaign in Kyoto suffered a grave accident after defective vaccine production and sampling; Nishimura's study documents 68 children who died. The episode cannot be used as evidence that public-health reform was uniformly beneficial. It shows why administrative accountability included medical manufacturing, laboratory certification, the prefectural chain of responsibility, and the occupation's supervisory role. In another dimension of coercion, local measures against prostitution and venereal disease brought surveillance and detention of women under suspicion, and public-health personnel disagreed over such tactics. A government may pursue disease prevention while imposing unequal or harmful measures. Such documented contradictions are more instructive than a fictional composite in which a prefectural officer performs a routine inspection.

Everyday Life and Society

Kyoto Military Government Team and Prefectural Public Health, 1947–1949

The case is documented, not hypothetical. Sey Nishimura examined reports of the Kyoto Military Government Team, prefectural records, letters, interviews, and photographs to reconstruct the work of American physician John D. Glismann and Japanese colleagues in Kyoto. Glismann arrived in September 1947 and worked with the prefectural Hygiene Department led by Dr. Tadayoshi Tsuchiya. Their responsibilities included infectious-disease prevention, hospital inspection, food conditions, and health education. Although Glismann had supervisory influence as a military-government medical officer, prefectural employees and Japanese medical professionals carried out much of the everyday work.

The cooperation was neither effortless nor uniformly successful. Glismann worked with Japanese officials on medical supply shortages, tuberculosis and other illness, and responses to the June 1948 Fukui earthquake. The administration also encountered coercive measures associated with policing prostitution and health certification; occupation personnel disagreed over those practices. In November 1948 a serious failure of vaccine production and testing in a diphtheria inoculation campaign killed 68 children, according to Nishimura’s reconstruction. The tragedy exposes the limitations of supervision shared among military health advisers, manufacturers, laboratories, and local officials.

The Kyoto evidence makes indirect rule visible: an American medical officer could set priorities and inspect, but Japanese doctors, bureaucrats, laboratories, and families determined whether instructions became effective public-health practice. It also shows why one cannot infer benefits from the issue of a policy directive alone. Kyoto’s conditions and the unusually detailed surviving correspondence make it informative but not representative of every prefecture.

Section 82.19 · Division IV

Contradictions of Occupation: Democratization, Coercion, Continuity, and Resistance

The occupation was built on several tensions that its officers recognized and its Japanese interlocutors exploited. The first was between democratization and military supremacy: SCAP required Japan to adopt a democratic system through an undemocratic process. The second was between free expression and censorship, discussed above. The third was between purges and continuity: SCAP removed leaders while relying on administrators. The fourth was between American predominance and Allied participation. And the fifth was between the occupation's reforming mission and its security and logistical interests, which became more prominent as the Cold War developed.

Resistance and limits appeared in several forms. Japanese officials negotiated and delayed. Labor unions pressed beyond what SCAP wished, culminating in the planned general strike of February 1, 1947, which MacArthur prohibited the day before. In July 1948 MacArthur wrote to Prime Minister Ashida that public employees should not have the right to strike, and a cabinet ordinance restricted their rights, an episode treated in Chapters 89 and 90 as part of the Reverse Course. Korean residents, who were treated by SCAP as "liberated people" in some respects and as Japanese nationals subject to Japanese law in others, protested in April 1948 in the Hanshin region against a Japanese order closing ethnic Korean schools, and the Eighth Army declared a state of emergency in Kobe, the only such declaration of the occupation. These cases show that the occupation could be contested and also that SCAP would use force to limit challenges.

The changing international environment altered the balance. By 1947 and 1948, American officials increasingly saw Japan as a potential partner in containing communism, rather than as a defeated enemy to be reformed. Policy emphasis moved toward economic recovery, internal security, and eventually rearmament, and the Soviet representatives on the Allied Council became more openly adversarial. Chapters 89 and 90 treat these changes. For this chapter, it is enough to note that the governing system created in 1945 had to accommodate a different set of priorities from those that had motivated it, and that the indirect structure, with its ministries and conservative politicians, made that adaptation easier.

The planned general strike of February 1, 1947 illustrates the limits of political reopening under foreign military authority. Postwar workers organized on a scale previously obstructed, and union leaders pressed the government over wages, subsistence, and political direction. MacArthur prohibited the proposed general strike, demonstrating that reform commitments were conditional when the command judged public order or the occupation's program to be endangered. The episode should not be treated merely as a prelude to the later Red Purge. In its own moment it revealed how far workers could mobilize and how decisively the occupation commander could intervene in domestic political conflict.

Other inconsistencies were built into the administrative structure. Allied bodies represented more than one country, but Americans made most consequential executive choices. Japanese officials continued to govern, but could not insist on sovereign equality. Political detainees were released, while intelligence officers monitored publications and associations. The emperor was retained as an instrument of continuity as institutions closely linked to the old order were dismantled. These tensions were not random failures of execution; they followed from governing a defeated country through institutions whose survival was useful and whose wartime conduct had made them targets of reform.

As the political environment changed, judgments about which institutions were dependable also changed. American officials increasingly considered the perceived threat of communism and the economic viability of Japan in East Asian security policy. Japanese conservative politicians and administrators found new opportunities in that shift, while unions and left-wing organizations faced changing official attitudes. The point is not that democratization simply ended in 1947. Political freedoms, local institutions, and new forms of public debate survived, but the occupying authority reassessed the risks it was willing to tolerate. Chapter 89 examines the economic and political details of the Reverse Course; here the task is to demonstrate that military supremacy allowed the governing program to be revised even after reforms had been announced as general principles.

Cause and Effect

Military Authority, Indirect Governance, and Administrative Transformation

Long-term causes

Wartime American planning that favored using Japanese institutions; the existence of a capable central bureaucracy; the emperor's authority over the armed forces; Japan's lack of a collapsed state, unlike Germany.

Short-term causes

The surrender through imperial authority; the shortage of American personnel with Japanese skills; the urgency of food, demobilization, and order; Shigemitsu's protest against direct-rule proclamations; the Potsdam ordinance.

Event or transformation

SCAP governed through the Japanese state, issuing directives that ministries implemented, while abolishing military and police institutions and supervising political change.

Immediate effects

Rapid reform in selected areas; retention of civil administration; preservation of the monarchy in new form; dependence of SCAP on Japanese implementation; censorship alongside liberalization.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

The method made the occupation efficient and enabled later restoration of sovereignty in 1952 with a functioning government, but it also preserved bureaucratic power, narrowed inquiry into the throne, and left the question of how far democracy had been voluntary. No single cause explains the pattern: American strategy, Japanese choices, Allied politics, and the changing Cold War all contributed, and the effects differed by region and social group.

There was another boundary to the language of democracy: not all residents stood in the same relationship to the state being reformed. Koreans living in Japan had experienced empire and mobilization under Japanese rule, and the end of colonial authority created difficult questions of registration, political activity, and status. The occupation and Japanese authorities approached those questions through security, nationality, and domestic-administration practices that did not necessarily match the inclusive language of reform. Outside the main islands, Okinawans remained under a separate American military administration whose institutions did not follow the same constitutional timetable as Tokyo. These differences complicate any statement that a single set of reforms transformed the governing position of everyone once subject to the Japanese empire. They also show why institutional comparison must be spatial: the authority to command, the agency that implemented the command, and the rights available to residents differed according to jurisdiction and the political categories imposed upon them.

Section 82.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Review

Who Governed Occupied Japan? Military Authority, Japanese Institutions, and the Paradoxes of Reform

If MacArthur and SCAP possessed supreme authority but Japanese institutions carried out most governmental administration, whose government was occupied Japan? The evidence in this chapter supports an answer that is neither simple nor static. In law and in power, SCAP was supreme: the Instrument of Surrender subordinated the emperor and the government to the Supreme Commander, and Japan had no ability to refuse. In daily administration, however, the Japanese cabinet, ministries, prefectures, and local governments governed, because the occupation chose and needed to work through them. The government of occupied Japan was therefore a joint but unequal enterprise, one in which one party could command and the other held the means of execution.

This partnership operated within a larger structure. Washington set the framework, and the Joint Chiefs transmitted it; the Far Eastern Commission and the Allied Council gave other Allies a voice without a veto over American executive action; and within GHQ, rival sections pursued different aims. The emperor was retained because he was useful to the occupation and because the American government chose not to remove him, and his transformation from sovereign to symbol was carried out with the throne's own participation. The political police were abolished and political prisoners freed, while an occupation censorship system and later restrictions on strikes limited some forms of public action. Purges removed military and political leaders, yet the civil bureaucracy largely survived. Each of these is a paradox of reform: change achieved through continuity, liberty alongside control.

The balance shifted over time. In 1945 and 1946, when institutions were new and American confidence was high, SCAP intervened often and visibly. After 1947, as the constitution took effect, Japanese governments gained experience, and the Cold War shifted priorities, SCAP intervened less in the structure of government and more in matters of security, labor, and the economy. By 1950 and 1951 Japanese leaders were negotiating the terms of restored sovereignty. The occupation did not so much hand over power as become increasingly conditional on Japanese cooperation, which the governing partnership had cultivated from the start.

These findings frame the chapters that follow. Chapter 83 turns to the question that the retention of the emperor left open: who bore responsibility for the war, how the Tokyo Trials addressed it, and how the institutions studied here shaped what was asked and what was not.

The public-assistance directive of February 1946 supplies a compact example of the system in action. An occupation section prepared requirements for a national relief program, and SCAPIN-775 transmitted them through the Central Liaison Office to the Japanese government. The responsible ministry then had to organize legal authority, budgets, and communication with prefectural and municipal officials. Those offices processed applicants and compiled monthly figures for return to GHQ. At each stage a policy meant to reach needy households could be modified by staffing, competing responsibilities, administrative categories, and the availability of funds. The directive proves what SCAP demanded; prefectural returns and residents' experiences are needed to judge what it accomplished. This was neither a direct American welfare service nor an independently designed Japanese scheme.

Kyoto's public-health records provide a second, more local perspective. American medical supervision and Japanese prefectural expertise could produce cooperative disease-control efforts, but the 1948 diphtheria inoculation disaster also exposed failure in the production and inspection of vaccine. Administration was therefore not just a hierarchy of signatures. It was a set of human decisions whose consequences could be beneficial, coercive, or fatal. The documentary record must be evaluated from the standpoint of residents as well as officials.

The answer to the governing question is consequently historical rather than categorical. Occupied Japan was administered by Japanese institutions under overriding American-led Allied military authority; over time the relative initiative of the actors and the occupation's priorities changed. The system allowed rapid commands to reach a whole country, yet gave existing officials influence in interpreting those commands. It enlarged political life while limiting challenges to the occupation itself. Recognizing these simultaneous realities explains both the durability of some reforms and the unresolved conflicts examined in the following chapters.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan was governed between the surrender of 1945 and the end of the occupation in 1952, with its principal attention on 1945–1947. Its central argument is that the occupation was a military occupation dominated by the United States and led by General MacArthur as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, but that it generally operated indirectly, through the emperor, cabinet, ministries, prefectures, and local authorities. That arrangement produced a distinctive condition in which Japan was not fully sovereign, yet its government continued to function.

The legal foundations were the Potsdam Declaration, the Allied reply of August 11, and the Instrument of Surrender of September 2, which placed the emperor's and the government's authority under SCAP. The Meiji Constitution remained formally in force until May 1947, and the Potsdam ordinance of September 20, 1945, gave SCAP's directives a Japanese legal form. Planned proclamations of direct military rule were withdrawn after Foreign Minister Shigemitsu's protest on the day of the surrender, one of the earliest documented instances of Japanese influence on the occupation's form.

American policy came from Washington. The Initial Post-Surrender Policy of September 1945 and the Basic Initial Directive, JCS 1380/15, of November 3, defined SCAP's supreme authority and the conditional use of Japanese governmental machinery. MacArthur nonetheless enjoyed wide latitude, and many early initiatives originated at GHQ. The Far Eastern Commission in Washington and the Allied Council for Japan in Tokyo gave Britain, the Soviet Union, China, and other governments a voice, but the United States retained decisive control, and no Soviet occupation zone was created in the home islands.

GHQ/SCAP itself was a layered bureaucracy of staff sections, including Government, Economic and Scientific, Civil Information and Education, Public Health and Welfare, and intelligence units, with rivalries among them and with limited Japanese-language expertise. Its numbered directives, the SCAPINs, passed through the Central Liaison Office, the cabinet, and the ministries, which converted them into Japanese law and instructed prefectural and local governments. At each stage Japanese officials could clarify, negotiate, delay, or reshape. The Eighth Army's military government teams supervised the prefectures, and the geography of authority was defined by SCAPIN-677, which separated Okinawa and other territories from Japanese administration.

The emperor was retained for reasons of stability, compliance, and American strategy, despite objections from some Allied governments and from critics who held him responsible for the war. The September 27 meeting with MacArthur, the photograph, and the rescript of January 1, 1946 changed the public presentation of the throne, which was further redefined by the Constitution. The imperial household was reduced in property and membership but remained intact.

The Civil Liberties Directive of October 4, 1945 forced the resignation of the Higashikuni cabinet, released political prisoners, and abolished the Special Higher Police, leading to the dissolution of the Home Ministry and decentralization of the police. The purge directives of January 1946 removed roughly two hundred thousand people, mostly military personnel, from public life, but left the civil bureaucracy largely intact. Cabinets under Shidehara and Yoshida governed under SCAP supervision, and elections took place alongside interventions such as the removal of Hatoyama.

The occupation also imposed censorship. The Press Code of September 19, 1945 and the work of the Civil Censorship Detachment restricted criticism of the occupation and the Allies, monitored private communications, and removed material considered militaristic or dangerous. The Civil Information and Education Section sought to reorient public opinion through radio, press, film, and education, in campaigns whose interpretation remains debated. Everyday administration, from health campaigns to requisition and policing, shaped how ordinary people experienced the occupation, and the experiences of Okinawans, Koreans, and others differed sharply from those in the central ministries.

Historians disagree over whether indirect rule enabled reform or preserved too much of the prewar state, and the better view is that it did both. The occupation was most interventionist in 1945–1946 and became more conditional as the constitution took effect and the Cold War shifted priorities. The government of occupied Japan is best understood as a joint but unequal enterprise in which one party commanded and the other executed, a structure that Chapter 83 now tests against the question of responsibility for the war.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains the machinery behind everything else the occupation did. The reforms, trials, shortages, and cultural changes examined in the following chapters all passed through the governing system described here: a supreme foreign command, working through a defeated but functioning state. Understanding that structure prevents two common errors, the view that the occupation was a simple American imposition and the view that Japan merely continued under new labels.

The chapter also reveals how power operates in unequal partnerships. SCAP could issue orders, but it depended on translators, ministries, and local officials to carry them out, and those officials used their position to shape results. Japanese leaders such as Shigemitsu and Yoshida showed that a defeated government could exert influence through timing, interpretation, and negotiation. At the same time, the censorship system, the purge, and the treatment of Okinawa and Korean residents show that the partnership was never equal, and that its costs fell unevenly.

The institutions studied here shaped later history. The survival of the bureaucracy and the throne, the dissolution of the Home Ministry and the political police, the creation of elected local government, and the habit of negotiating with an American authority all affected postwar politics, the restoration of sovereignty in 1952, and the enduring debates about war responsibility, the American military presence, and the nature of Japanese democracy. They also connect Japan to a wider world, since the structure was devised in Washington, argued over by the Allies, and compared with Germany and the colonies.

Students who understand occupation government as layered, negotiated, and changing are prepared for Chapter 83, where the retention of the emperor and the limits of inquiry become the starting point for a direct examination of war responsibility and the Tokyo Trials.
