Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution
52

Treaty Ports, Sonnō Jōi, and Political Violence

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On a snowy morning in March 1860, the senior minister of the Tokugawa government was carried in a palanquin toward the gates of Edo Castle. A small group of armed men, most of them former retainers of the Mito domain, attacked the procession outside the Sakuradamon gate. Within minutes the man who had signed Japan’s commercial treaties with the Western powers was dead. The shogunate had survived Commodore Perry. It was now discovering that treaties could be as dangerous as warships.

Chapter 51 traced how the arrival of Perry’s squadrons in 1853–1854 turned a diplomatic problem into a problem of governing authority. The Convention of Kanagawa opened two ports to American ships for provisioning and rescue, but it did not create general commercial relations. This chapter begins where that limited arrangement ended. Between about 1856 and 1866, the Tokugawa bakufu negotiated, and then signed, treaties that permitted foreign residence and regulated trade at designated ports. Those treaties changed the economy of the ports and their hinterlands, provoked a sustained argument about who had the right to decide Japan’s foreign policy, and helped turn the political culture of the 1850s into a decade of assassination, armed demonstration, naval bombardment, and domain-level conflict.

The chapter has three intertwined subjects. The first is the treaty system itself: the negotiations led by the American consul Townsend Harris, the Treaty of Amity and Commerce of 1858 and the parallel agreements with the Netherlands, Russia, Britain, and France, and the new treaty ports, especially Yokohama, Nagasaki, and Hakodate. The second is the movement known as sonnō jōi, “revere the emperor, expel the barbarians,” a slogan that proved adaptable to many different political purposes. The third is political violence, which took many forms: purges by the shogunate, assassinations by shishi (activists of purpose), attacks on foreigners, domain-level coups, naval bombardments, and civil war in miniature in Kyoto and western Japan.

The chapter does not treat these developments as the inevitable prelude to the Meiji Restoration. In 1860, few observers, Japanese or foreign, expected the Tokugawa house to be overthrown within a decade. Even in 1866 the shogunate retained the largest single concentration of land, revenue, and troops in the country, and it was investing in new naval and military capacity with French assistance. What changed was the political environment. The imperial court, long a ceremonial and legitimating institution, was drawn into the center of foreign-policy decisions. Powerful domains, especially Satsuma and Chōshū, developed foreign policies and military organizations of their own. And a generation of samurai, many of them low-ranking and few of them wealthy, found in the language of loyalty to the emperor a way to criticize the shogunate without formally rejecting the order of which they were part.

It is equally important to recognize that “Japan” did not respond to the treaties with a single voice. Shogunal officials wrestled with the legal and military limits of their position. Daimyō and domain councilors argued with one another and with the bakufu. Court nobles in Kyoto, usually remote from government, issued edicts and manipulated factions. Merchants in Yokohama and silk-producing villages in the interior responded to new markets. Farmers and townspeople bore the consequences of rising prices. Ainu communities around Hakodate and Ryukyuan officials in Naha, whose homelands were also pulled into the treaty system, saw these events from yet other vantage points. Foreign diplomats, traders, interpreters, missionaries, and naval officers, for their part, were not a united bloc but competing parties with different aims.

The evidence for this decade is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Historians draw on bakufu records and diplomatic correspondence, domain papers, the diaries and letters of samurai activists, court documents, treaty texts in several languages, British, American, French, and Dutch diplomatic reports, newspapers published in the Yokohama settlement, memoirs written by foreigners and Japanese, woodblock prints of the treaty ports, and the vast body of commemorative literature produced after 1868. That last category requires special caution. The victors of the Restoration wrote the history of the 1860s, and the figures later honored as loyalist heroes were often more ambivalent, more divided, and more flexible than later memory suggested.

This chapter connects directly to its neighbors. Chapter 51 supplies the opening crisis; Chapter 53 will examine how the shogunate was overthrown and a new government created in 1867–1868. Chapter 52 explains the years between: the period in which the problem of the foreign treaties and the problem of political legitimacy became one problem. It ends in 1866, with the shogunate militarily humbled in the field, the treaties increasingly confirmed, and the question of who would govern Japan still open.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the negotiation of the Shimoda Convention (1857) and the Treaty of Amity and Commerce (1858), and explain the roles of Townsend Harris, Hotta Masayoshi, and Ii Naosuke.
  2. Explain why the question of imperial sanction for the treaties became a political crisis, and what it revealed about the relationship among shogun, court, and domains.
  3. Identify the principal provisions of the 1858 treaties, including treaty ports, extraterritoriality, and fixed tariffs, and distinguish them from the Convention of Kanagawa.
  4. Describe the treaty ports of Yokohama, Nagasaki, and Hakodate as social, economic, and legal spaces.
  5. Analyze the economic effects of foreign trade, including silk and tea exports, currency disruption, and price inflation, for different regions and social groups.
  6. Distinguish the intellectual roots and shifting political meanings of sonnō jōi, and compare the aims of different activist groups and domains.
  7. Evaluate the causes and consequences of political violence, including the Ansei Purge, the Sakuradamon Incident, attacks on foreigners, and the Namamugi Incident.
  8. Assess the policy of court–bakufu union (kōbu gattai) and the Bunkyū reforms of 1862 as attempts to restore shogunal legitimacy.
  9. Compare the bombardments of Kagoshima (1863) and Shimonoseki (1863–1864) and explain how each altered Satsuma and Chōshū policy.
  10. Trace the sequence from the coup of 1863 to the Kinmon Incident, the Chōshū expeditions, and the Satsuma–Chōshū alliance, without assuming that the shogunate’s fall was inevitable.
  11. Interpret primary and secondary sources on this period, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later heroic memory.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a diplomatic agreement that the shogunate considered unavoidable become a source of political attack on the shogunate itself?
  2. Why did Hotta Masayoshi seek imperial approval in 1858, and how did that decision change the political role of the court?
  3. In what ways did the commercial treaties alter the economies of ports, farming regions, and cities, and who gained or lost?
  4. Did sonnō jōi express a consistent ideology, or did different groups use the slogan for different goals?
  5. Why did assassination and other forms of political violence become so frequent in the early 1860s, and who were the victims?
  6. How successful were the shogunate’s efforts after 1860 to rebuild its authority through reform, alliance with the court, and military modernization?
  7. What did the naval bombardments of Kagoshima and Shimonoseki teach Satsuma and Chōshū, and how did those lessons differ?
  8. How should historians weigh later Meiji-era memory of the “loyalist heroes” against evidence from the 1860s themselves?

Historical Context

By 1855 the Tokugawa political order had endured for more than two and a half centuries. Under the bakuhan system described in Chapter 42, the shogun controlled the largest domain, the major cities, key mines and ports, and the foreign-policy powers formerly exercised through the Nagasaki, Tsushima, Satsuma, and Matsumae channels described in Chapter 48. The roughly 260 daimyō domains (han) governed their own territories, maintained their own armies and finances, and owed the shogun military service, ceremonial attendance, and compliance with bakufu law. The emperor in Kyoto, supported by a small income and a court nobility largely excluded from policy, conferred the title of shogun and remained a source of ritual legitimacy. Before the 1850s, there was no regular procedure for the court to rule on foreign policy.

Chapter 50 examined the intellectual and administrative background: the spread of literacy and publishing, Dutch learning (rangaku), reform efforts, and the stresses of famine and fiscal strain. Chapter 51 then traced the arrival of the American squadrons, Russian and British approaches, the shogunate’s unusual decision to consult the daimyō, and the 1854–1855 agreements. Those events left several unresolved problems. The Convention of Kanagawa was limited, but American and European governments expected more, especially after Britain and France forced new concessions from Qing China during the Second Opium War (the Arrow War, 1856–1860). Within Japan, Abe Masahiro’s consultation of daimyō had weakened the assumption that foreign policy belonged exclusively to the shogun’s inner circle, and it left the bakufu’s authority to decide for itself more contested than before.

Two further background elements matter for this chapter. First, the domains of Satsuma in southern Kyushu and Chōshū (Hagi) at the western end of Honshu were large, financially resourceful, and geographically exposed to maritime traffic. Satsuma also controlled the Ryukyu Kingdom’s foreign trade through an arrangement of long standing, while Chōshū commanded the Shimonoseki Strait, the gateway between the Inland Sea and the Sea of Japan. Second, the Mito domain, a Tokugawa collateral house, had developed a distinctive school of thought (Mitogaku) that fused Confucian ethics, loyalty to the emperor, and anxiety about foreign threats. These conditions help explain why opposition to the shogunate’s foreign policy would be so geographically and intellectually diverse.

Section 52.1

From Convention to Commerce: The Treaties Come Due

The agreements of 1854–1855 had not created a stable framework. The Convention of Kanagawa, discussed in Chapter 51, provided for ship provisioning, assistance to shipwrecked sailors, and access to Shimoda and Hakodate, with provision for a consul at Shimoda. Bakufu negotiators had accepted it as a limited concession that would leave the core of Tokugawa foreign policy intact. American officials saw it differently. For them it was a starting point. They wanted regular commerce, resident diplomats, secure treatment of their citizens, and a Japan fully integrated into the Pacific trade that linked California, Hawaii, and Chinese ports. The British, French, Dutch, and Russians pursued comparable aims and watched each other for advantage.

The key distinction for this chapter is between a port-access agreement and a commercial treaty regime. The former allowed ships to call. The latter established permanent foreign communities, set the terms of trade, defined legal jurisdiction over foreigners, and bound Japan to obligations that would last for decades. The shift from one to the other occurred in just four years, and it occurred under mounting international pressure, an unsettled domestic politics, and a shogunate that was itself divided over how much to concede.

Historians have long debated how to describe the bakufu’s behavior. Some emphasize weakness and capitulation, pointing to Japan’s inability to match Western naval power. Others stress calculation: bakufu officials such as Iwase Tadanari, Inoue Kiyonao, and later reformers sought to preserve the shogunate by extracting what concessions they could, buying time to build strength, and avoiding the fate of China. This chapter adopts a third approach, which treats the treaties as the outcome of unequal but real bargaining in which Japanese officials bargained skillfully on particular points, such as the limitation on opening cities and the exclusion of opium, even as they accepted terms that later generations would call unequal. The more consequential question is not whether the bakufu bargained, but what its decisions did to its authority at home.

Section 52.2

Townsend Harris and the Shimoda Convention

Townsend Harris, a New York merchant with experience in the East Asian trade, arrived at Shimoda in August 1856 as American consul general. His position was awkward. Bakufu officials in Shimoda questioned the need for a consul at all, restricted his movements, and delayed his requests to travel to Edo. Harris, in turn, pressed his hosts with persistence and, at times, with exaggeration of American power. Few American warships were available to back him, and the United States, in the mid-1850s, had no appetite for a Pacific war. His leverage was less naval strength than timing, namely the news arriving from China.

On June 17, 1857 (Ansei 4/5/26) the two sides concluded the Shimoda Convention. It permitted American residence at Shimoda and Hakodate, opened Nagasaki to American ships for supplies, and, importantly, established in its Article IV that Americans accused of offenses in Japan would be tried by the American consul under American law. This extraterritorial principle, drawn from precedent in the Ottoman Empire and China, was not part of the 1854 convention. It became a lasting feature of Japan’s relations with the Western powers.

Harris then pushed for a full commercial treaty and for permission to reside in Edo. After months of delay, he was received in Edo in late 1857 and had an audience with Shogun Tokugawa Iesada. He then delivered a lengthy account of world affairs to the senior councilor Hotta Masayoshi, who was responsible for foreign relations. The core of Harris’s argument was that Britain and France, after the Second Opium War, would soon turn toward Japan. A treaty negotiated peacefully with the United States, he said, would be a lighter alternative to one imposed by force. Historians treat this argument as both a real reading of the international situation and a negotiating tool. It was not baseless: the British and French did pursue treaties in Japan shortly afterward. But it also served American commercial aims.

Hotta, a reform-minded official sympathetic to Dutch learning and persuaded that Japan could not hold the line, accepted the logic. Negotiators Iwase Tadanari and Inoue Kiyonao worked through the text with Harris early in 1858. The draft was substantially agreed by February. The difficult question was no longer content but authority: could the bakufu sign without the approval of the emperor and the opinion of the daimyō?

Section 52.3

Hotta Masayoshi, Kyoto, and the Question of Imperial Sanction

Before 1853, no Tokugawa foreign-policy decision had required the emperor’s approval. Policy toward Dutch and Chinese traders, Korea, and Ryukyu was conducted by the bakufu under its delegated authority to govern. Perry’s arrival changed practice: the shogunate informed the court of the American demands and, under Abe Masahiro, consulted daimyō about its response. These steps were intended to build consensus and spread responsibility. They also established, almost unintentionally, that the treaty question was larger than the bakufu’s own household.

In early 1858, Hotta traveled to Kyoto to obtain imperial endorsement for the draft treaty. He expected the court to defer to the shogunate’s judgment, as it had in earlier decades. Emperor Kōmei, however, was deeply averse to opening the country to foreigners, and many courtiers resisted. Dozens of lesser nobles also staged a protest, an unprecedented act of political assertion by a court that had been largely silent for generations. The court did not unequivocally reject the treaty, but it demanded further deliberation and consultation with the daimyō. Hotta returned to Edo without the sanction he had sought.

Why did the court refuse? Hotta had assumed that a request for approval would be a formality. The senior court official, the kampaku Kujō Hisatada, was inclined to accommodate the bakufu, but the emperor’s aversion to opening Japan was strong and the lesser nobles’ protest made it politically costly for the court to concede. The court’s answer was a compromise that satisfied no one: it did not forbid a treaty outright, but it asked the bakufu to consult the great daimyō again and to reconsider. For Hotta this was a defeat. He had risked his own standing on a journey whose logic rested on a precedent that no longer held, and his enemies in Edo could now claim that his judgment had failed.

The refusal also changed what each side could say. Supporters of the bakufu would later argue that governing authority, including the conduct of foreign relations, had been entrusted by the court to the shogunate for centuries, an argument sometimes called the doctrine of delegated rule (taisei inin). Critics could answer that the bakufu itself had undercut that position by asking for approval it did not obtain. Either way, the treaty decision could no longer be presented as a routine administrative matter. It was now a test of whose will counted.

The consequence was far-reaching. By going to Kyoto, the bakufu had acknowledged that the court’s opinion mattered. By failing to obtain approval, it exposed a gap between its practical needs and the court’s symbolic authority. Anyone who opposed the treaty could now invoke the emperor’s will as a higher standard, and anyone who defended the shogunate would have to reckon with the charge of acting against the court. The distinction among imperial legitimacy (the court’s ritual and symbolic standing), shogunal authority (the bakufu’s delegated right to govern), and governing capacity (the actual ability to carry out policy) became politically crucial.

Section 52.4

Ii Naosuke and the Treaties of 1858

Hotta’s failure coincided with a change in the shogunate’s leadership. On June 4, 1858 (Ansei 5/4/23 in the lunisolar calendar then in use), Ii Naosuke, lord of Hikone, one of the leading fudai (hereditary vassal) daimyō, was appointed tairō, an exceptional senior post filled only in times of crisis. Ii was a conservative defender of shogunal prerogatives. He believed that the bakufu, not the court or the daimyō at large, should decide foreign policy, and that the Tokugawa house should choose its own heir without outside interference. Hotta was soon dismissed.

In the summer of 1858, news reached Japan that Britain and France had forced the Treaty of Tianjin on China. Harris relayed it to the Japanese side, warning that an Anglo-French fleet might soon appear. Ii instructed the negotiators to seek postponement if possible, but allowed them to sign if postponement proved impossible. On July 29, 1858, aboard the American steam frigate Powhatan off Kanagawa, the Treaty of Amity and Commerce was signed. Within weeks, similar treaties followed with the Netherlands, Russia, and Britain (August), and France (October). Collectively these are sometimes called the Ansei Five-Power Treaties, after the Japanese era name Ansei (1854–1860).

The signing took place without imperial approval. Ii reported it to Kyoto afterward and defended it as a necessary measure, but the court, and many daimyō, regarded it as a breach of faith. In the autumn the emperor’s displeasure was expressed in a secret edict sent to the Mito domain, an unprecedented bypass of the bakufu. That edict became a central document of the unfolding crisis and a justification for Ii’s repression.

Ii’s reasoning, as far as it can be reconstructed from his decisions and from the records of the officials around him, rested on a hard comparison of risks. Refusing the treaty, in his view, invited a war the bakufu could not win and might lose its capital to a blockade; signing without the court’s consent invited criticism but kept the initiative in bakufu hands. The negotiators, Inoue Kiyonao and Iwase Tadanari, made a similar calculation when they decided that further delay was not available. The political cost, however, fell immediately. Within days of the signing, leading members of the Hitotsubashi faction made a series of unauthorized visits to Edo Castle to protest. Hitotsubashi Yoshinobu confronted Ii on the twenty-third day of the sixth lunar month (Ansei 5/6/23); on the following day Tokugawa Nariaki, the Owari lord Tokugawa Yoshikatsu, and others protested in turn, and Matsudaira Shungaku of Echizen was also involved in challenging Ii’s decisions. Ii treated the visits as breaches of established procedure and punished the men involved. That response linked the treaty dispute to the succession dispute, and it established Ii’s method: to answer opposition with institutional discipline rather than consultation. The same logic, applied on a larger scale, produced the Ansei Purge described below.

Historical Vocabulary
“Unequal Treaties” (fubyōdō jōyaku)

The phrase refers to treaties that included extraterritoriality (foreign nationals tried by their own consuls), fixed tariffs that Japan could not alter on its own, and one-sided or non-reciprocal clauses. It is a retrospective label: the term became standard only after the treaties had become a grievance of the Meiji government and public, which spent decades seeking revision. Treaty revision was achieved in stages between 1894 and 1911.

Students should note that contemporary Japanese officials in the 1850s did not use the concept as later generations did. Some bakufu officials regarded the treaties as a reasonable price for avoiding war, and some modern historians emphasize that the arrangement also included provisions that protected Japanese interests, such as the ban on opium imports. The label is useful for understanding long-term consequences but should not be read back as a uniform contemporary judgment.

Primary Source Window

The Treaty of Amity and Commerce between the United States and Japan, 1858

Creator: American and Japanese negotiators, principally Townsend Harris and Inoue Kiyonao and Iwase Tadanari · Date: signed July 29, 1858 · Type: Treaty text, with versions prepared in several languages

Where to read the text: The full historical text is available in the University of Tokyo’s “The World and Japan” database: 1858 Treaty of Amity and Commerce. Students should read the articles named below directly rather than relying on the paraphrase.

Context: The treaty was negotiated in a language environment shaped by interpreters, notably the Dutch-speaking intermediaries through whom much of the Harris–Japanese conversation passed. Translation was itself part of the diplomatic process, and differing versions could leave room for dispute. The text was concluded amid mounting concern about an Anglo-French arrival and without the emperor’s endorsement.

Paraphrase of selected provisions (not a verbatim quotation): The treaty provided for additional open ports (Kanagawa, Nagasaki, Hakodate, and, on a later schedule, Niigata and Hyōgo) and for foreign residence and trade in designated places, including later access to Edo and Osaka. Article III concerns the opening of ports and the places of residence and trade. Article IV concerns duties, which were set by an attached schedule rather than left to Japanese authorities, and the same article expressly prohibits opium imports. Article VI provided that Americans accused of offenses against Japanese would be tried by the American consul under American law. Article VIII protected the freedom of American residents to worship and ended certain restrictions related to Christian practice for them. Article XIII provided for a possible review of the terms after a fixed number of years.

Analysis: The treaty is a clear statement of what the American side sought: access, commerce, and legal protection for its nationals. It also reveals the specific concessions that later became grievances: jurisdiction and tariff control. Historians read it for what it says and for what it leaves out. The text says little about reciprocity, because it assumes no Japanese residents in the United States. It does not reflect the debates inside Japan, the court’s objections, or the pressure from other powers. It also cannot show how the clauses were applied in practice, which depended on consular behavior, local officials, and merchant practice.

Questions for students:

  1. What does Article III establish about ports and residence, and what does Article VI establish about jurisdiction?
  2. Who created the treaty and under what pressures, and how might the Article VIII provisions on religion have been understood in Japan?
  3. What can historians learn from Articles IV and XIII about the long-term economic and legal position of Japan under the treaty?
  4. What can historians not establish from the text alone about the treaty’s effects on ordinary Japanese people?
Section 52.5

Succession, Purge, and the Assassination at Sakuradamon

The treaty crisis overlapped with a separate succession dispute. Shogun Iesada was ill and had no heir. One faction, supported by reform-minded daimyō such as Tokugawa Nariaki of Mito, Matsudaira Shungaku of Echizen, and Shimazu Nariakira of Satsuma, favored Hitotsubashi Yoshinobu, Nariaki’s son, who was an adult and regarded as capable. The opposing faction, centered on senior bakufu officials and traditional fudai leaders, preferred the young Tokugawa Yoshitomi of Kii, closer in blood to the main line. Ii Naosuke supported Yoshitomi. The decision was made during the summer of 1858, and Yoshitomi became the fourteenth shogun, Iemochi.

Ii then moved against those who had challenged him. In what is known as the Ansei Purge (1858–1859), he forced Tokugawa Nariaki into confinement, compelled Yoshinobu and Matsudaira Shungaku into retirement or restriction, and punished many officials, courtiers, and domain samurai who had agitated against the treaty or for the Hitotsubashi candidacy. More than a hundred people were penalized in some way, and a number were executed, including the Chōshū teacher Yoshida Shōin and the Echizen scholar Hashimoto Sanai. The purge extended to Kyoto, where courtiers lost office and where agents of the bakufu pursued activists.

Ii’s purpose was to assert shogunal authority. Its effect was to enlarge the opposition. It created martyrs, bound together disparate critics, and showed that the bakufu would use coercion against not only commoners but members of the samurai elite and the court. Many of Ii’s adversaries were themselves loyal to the Tokugawa order, but the purge pushed some to conclude that the shogunate would not tolerate loyal dissent.

On March 24, 1860, a group of about eighteen swordsmen, seventeen of them former Mito retainers and one from Satsuma, attacked Ii’s procession outside the Sakuradamon gate of Edo Castle. Ii’s escort, hindered by snow and by rain gear covering their weapons, was overwhelmed. Ii was killed. The assassins, acting in defense of what they understood as the court’s will and the honor of the Mito lord, were largely killed or executed, or committed suicide in the aftermath. The bakufu concealed the death for a time, announcing that Ii was injured, an act that itself shows how seriously the regime regarded the damage.

The Sakuradamon Incident marked a turning point. A leading minister had been assassinated at the gates of the shogun’s castle by retainers of a Tokugawa collateral domain. The bakufu’s subsequent leaders, Andō Nobumasa and Kuze Hirochika, adopted a more conciliatory approach toward the court and the daimyō. The shogunate had not collapsed, but its aura of command was severely weakened, and political killing had gained a precedent.

Section 52.6

The Treaty Ports: Yokohama, Nagasaki, and Hakodate

On July 1, 1859, the ports of Kanagawa, Nagasaki, and Hakodate opened to foreign trade. (The American treaty had named July 4 as the opening date, but the earlier date in the Russian treaty was the one put into effect.) Niigata and Hyōgo (near Kobe) were scheduled to follow, and Edo and Osaka were to admit foreign residents and merchants at later dates, but the bakufu repeatedly sought delay, fearing the political consequences of foreigners in the capital cities and, especially, near Kyoto. A protocol negotiated in London in 1862 postponed the opening of Edo, Osaka, Hyōgo, and Niigata until 1868. The ports that actually opened therefore carried the burden of the treaty regime during its first decade.

Yokohama. Treaty text referred to Kanagawa, a post station on the Tōkaidō highway. The bakufu chose instead to build the foreign settlement across a bay at Yokohama, a small fishing village. The choice was strategic: it separated foreigners from the busy highway and placed them in a space the bakufu could police. Within a few years Yokohama became the principal center of foreign trade. A foreign settlement (kyoryūchi) was laid out beside a separate Japanese district. Merchants from Britain, the United States, the Netherlands, France, Germany, and elsewhere set up firms, joined by Chinese traders and compradors who had gained experience in Shanghai and Hong Kong and who acted as intermediaries. Japanese merchants from Edo and the silk-producing regions moved into the port, some quickly rich, some quickly bankrupt. The bakufu’s officials, interpreters, and guards were an additional presence. Yokohama also gave rise to a visual culture: Yokohama-e, woodblock prints depicting foreigners, ships, and settlement life, sold to a curious Japanese public.

Nagasaki. Nagasaki already had a long history of regulated foreign contact through the Dutch at Dejima and Chinese traders. After 1859, new foreign merchants, Britain’s Glover and Company among them, took up residence on the hillside Ōura district. The port became a center of arms and ship trade, a point of interaction between Western merchants and southwestern domains like Satsuma, Chōshū, Saga, and Tosa, and the site of the shogunate’s naval training under Dutch instruction in the 1850s. Foreign religion also entered: Catholic priests built the Ōura Church in the 1860s, and in March 1865 local descendants of earlier Christians who had maintained their faith in secret approached one of the priests. This “discovery of the hidden Christians” would have severe consequences in the early Meiji years (see Chapter 55).

Hakodate. Opened first under the Convention of Kanagawa, Hakodate in Ezo (Hokkaido) served as a provisioning port for American whalers and merchant ships, and soon as a place of Russian, British, and American residence. The bakufu took the main Ezo coastline and the Hakodate area under direct control in 1855, replacing the Matsumae domain in many roles, and it began construction of the Goryōkaku, a Western-style star-shaped fortress designed by Takeda Ayasaburō, who had studied European fortification. The Ainu communities of Ezo were not parties to the treaties or to bakufu decisions about their land. They lived in a region where Japanese fishing contractors, shogunal officials, and foreign ships all made claims on resources and labor. The Ainu are discussed more fully in later chapters on Hokkaido’s nineteenth-century colonization, but this was the decade in which Ezo became more directly tied to national and international strategy.

Niigata and Hyōgo were deferred and so remain outside this chapter’s detailed narrative. The ports that did open show that the treaty regime was not uniform. Yokohama was a new, trade-driven city under heavy bakufu supervision. Nagasaki was an old port with layers of established relationships. Hakodate was a frontier outpost with strategic and fishing interests. Local conditions shaped how treaty clauses were applied.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living Beside the Settlements

For many people, the treaty ports meant new work and new risk. Day laborers, carpenters, porters, and servants found employment in Yokohama, and merchants supplied foreign households and ships. Some Japanese women worked in or around the settlements. The term rashamen was applied to Japanese women, often from the licensed quarters, who were associated with foreign men; the word carried stigma and was used both by observers and, at times, as an insult. A licensed quarter at Miyozaki served the port’s male population, and an establishment known as the Gankirō became celebrated among foreign residents. Evidence about the women’s own views is limited, as most surviving sources were written by foreign men or Japanese commentators.

Foreign residents lived in a restricted space and traveled outside it only within set distances. They depended on Japanese officials for security, and their presence was resented by some of their neighbors. Rumor shaped perceptions on both sides. Foreigners feared swordsmen who might strike without warning; Japanese commoners and samurai circulated stories about foreigners’ appetites, habits, and intentions. The 1858 cholera epidemic, which spread after ships from abroad called at Nagasaki, deepened the association between foreigners and misfortune in the popular mind, though estimates of its death toll vary widely and the causal story is more complicated than contemporary rumor suggested.

Section 52.7

Silk, Gold, Prices, and Disease: Trade and Economic Shock

The commercial effects of the treaties were large, uneven, and quickly visible. Within a few years, raw silk became Japan’s dominant export, with tea second. European silk production had been hit by a silkworm disease, creating demand for Japanese silkworm eggs and raw silk. Villages in silk-producing regions of the interior, such as parts of Kōzuke, Shinano, Musashi, and Ōu, increased production, and rural merchants tied villages to Yokohama through new commercial channels. Households that raised silkworms, reeled thread, or brokered cocoons saw income rise. Regional elites, including some wealthy commoners, gained capital and influence.

The same trade disrupted older patterns. The bakufu tried to regulate the flow of goods, for instance through an edict of 1860 requiring certain commodities (grains, oil, wax, cotton cloth, and raw silk) to pass through Edo wholesalers, partly to protect supply and customary guild arrangements. Foreign merchants and treaty provisions resisted such controls, and enforcement was inconsistent. Imported cotton cloth and yarn competed with domestic producers in some areas. At the same time, export demand pulled goods out of domestic markets, helping push up prices.

Currency was a separate and acute problem. Japan’s gold-to-silver ratio differed sharply from the international market’s, and the treaty’s terms for exchanging foreign dollars for Japanese silver coin allowed traders to convert silver to gold and carry gold out of the country at a considerable profit. The outflow of gold alarmed the bakufu, which responded in 1860 by recoining the gold kōban with a lower gold content. This stopped the drain but fueled inflation. Rice and other staple prices rose steeply in the 1860s. Samurai on fixed stipends, urban wage earners, and rural families who purchased grain were hit hardest. The sense of material pressure fed grievances that political activists could harness, though the relationship between economic distress and political action is complex and varied by region.

Not all effects were harmful. Exporters, rural silk merchants, and certain port-city businesses prospered, and Japan’s ability to purchase ships, arms, and machines came through the same channels. Historians disagree about how deeply the early treaty trade transformed the economy. Some stress damage to domestic industries and the loss of tariff autonomy. Others stress adaptation and note that the Japanese economy retained control over much of its internal commerce, unlike some other treaty-port economies in Asia. Chapter 56 returns to these questions in discussing the foundations of industrial development.

Section 52.8

Sonnō Jōi: Ideas, Networks, and Shishi

“Revere the emperor, expel the barbarians” (sonnō jōi) combined two ideas that predated the Perry crisis. Sonnō, respect for the emperor as the source of legitimacy, had roots in Confucian discussions of hegemons and kings and in the nativist scholarship (kokugaku) that glorified the imperial line. Jōi, driving out foreign intruders, drew on a long tradition of regarding foreign religion and trade with suspicion. The Mito school (Mitogaku) combined both in an influential form. Aizawa Seishisai’s Shinron (New Theses), written in 1825, argued that the Tokugawa order should be defended by uniting loyalty to the emperor with preparedness against Western encroachment. It circulated widely in manuscript, and its themes were absorbed by many readers.

After 1853 the slogan took on new urgency, and it was never a single doctrine. For some, such as Tokugawa Nariaki, it meant strengthening the bakufu and the defense of the realm under shogunal leadership. For others, it was a means to demand that the court have a say in policy. For radical samurai from Chōshū, Tosa, Satsuma, and Mito, it increasingly meant that the shogunate’s concessions disqualified it from rule. For some, jōi was literal: foreigners should be driven away by force. For others it was a rhetorical stance, a way of insisting that Japan negotiate from strength and eventually from equality. The shift from jōi to opening could occur quickly, and several prominent activists later became architects of the Meiji state’s openness to Western learning.

Historical Vocabulary
Sonnō Jōi (尊王攘夷)

Sonnō jōi is usually translated “revere the emperor, expel the barbarians.” It combines a statement about political loyalty with a statement about foreign policy. Its meaning depended on who spoke. A bakufu-supporting official might use it to call for national unity; a domain activist might use it to criticize shogunal weakness; a court noble might use it to claim a role in decisions; a samurai assassin might use it to justify killing.

Students should not treat it as the ideology of a single movement or as an early form of modern nationalism. It was a language of political contest that evolved with events, and its “expel the barbarians” half was largely abandoned by many of its supporters once they saw Western military capacity firsthand.

The social base of the activists is important. Many shishi were low- or middle-ranking samurai, rōshi (masterless samurai), village leaders, scholars, and some commoners, often educated in domain schools or private academies. They had literacy, networks, and a sense of mission, but lacked the standing to influence policy through ordinary channels. Yoshida Shōin’s private academy in Chōshū, the Shōka Sonjuku, trained a number of future leaders. Shōin himself, who had tried unsuccessfully to board Perry’s ship in 1854, was executed in 1859 during the Ansei Purge, becoming a symbol for later admirers. Letters, travel between domains, and shared teachers created a network that crossed domain boundaries even as domain governments tried to control their members.

Activists also operated within domain politics, where factions competed for influence over the daimyō. In Tosa, a loyalist party under Takechi Hanpeita (Zuizan) organized sword-wielding followers and assassinated the domain’s reform minister Yoshida Tōyō in 1862. In Chōshū, loyalists moved from advocating court–bakufu cooperation to demanding expulsion of foreigners. In Satsuma, a different dynamic prevailed: the domain’s leadership, particularly Shimazu Hisamitsu, sought to guide national politics through the court and bakufu while suppressing radicals in its own ranks.

Historical Debate

Sonnō Jōi: Conviction or Strategy?

The question: Was sonnō jōi primarily an expression of genuine ideological and anti-foreign conviction, or was it chiefly a tool used by domains and factions to pursue power and challenge the bakufu?

Interpretation A — Conviction and ideology: Some scholars emphasize the intellectual depth of Mitogaku and nativism and the sincerity of activists who risked, and often lost, their lives. They point to diaries, letters, and poems showing genuine fear of foreign domination and devotion to the imperial institution, and to the willingness of activists to die for their beliefs.
Interpretation B — Strategy and political opportunity: Other scholars emphasize the changeability of the slogan. Domains such as Satsuma and Chōshū quickly abandoned expulsion when confronted with Western naval power, and many activists later served an open-door government. They argue that sonnō jōi served as a framework for competing groups to attack rivals, claim legitimacy, and gain leverage over the bakufu, often while recognizing that expulsion was unrealistic.

Evidence: Both sides draw on activist writings and domain records. The difficulty is that much evidence was produced or preserved after 1868, when former activists had reasons to present their past in particular lights. Behavior after 1863, especially in Satsuma and Chōshū, is often cited as showing pragmatism, but sincere belief and pragmatism can coexist.

Why it matters: If the movement was mainly ideological, the Restoration might be explained as the outcome of a coherent loyalist program. If it was mainly strategic, the story becomes one of power politics reshaped by foreign pressure and domestic crisis. Most historians now see an interaction of both.

Student task: Identify one example from this chapter that supports the conviction interpretation and one that supports the strategy interpretation, and explain why the two interpretations may not be mutually exclusive.

Section 52.9

Attacks on Foreigners and the Namamugi Incident

From 1859 onward, foreigners were targeted in a series of attacks. In 1859, Russian naval personnel were killed in Yokohama. In January 1861, Henry Heusken, the Dutch-American interpreter who served Harris, was murdered in Edo. In July 1861, a party of former Mito retainers attacked the British legation housed in the Tōzenji temple, wounding two British officials, and a second attack on the legation followed in 1862. These attacks were committed by activists who saw the foreign presence as a violation of the realm and the treaties as a betrayal. They also embarrassed the bakufu, which had promised to protect foreigners and was held responsible by Western diplomats.

The bakufu was therefore caught between two demands. Foreign ministers, notably Britain’s Rutherford Alcock, insisted that Japan fulfill its treaty duties, protect foreigners, and pay indemnities. Domestic opponents insisted that it not yield further. Bakufu officials improved guard arrangements, relocated the legations, and paid compensation, but the incidents continued, and each reinforced the foreigners’ belief that bakufu authority was unreliable and Japan’s countryside dangerous.

On September 14, 1862, a party of British residents riding on the Tōkaidō near Namamugi, outside Yokohama, encountered the procession of Shimazu Hisamitsu, returning from Edo to Satsuma with a large armed retinue. Under the etiquette of daimyō processions, bystanders were expected to dismount, step aside, and show respect. The Britons did not, or did not do so adequately; accounts differ about what happened at the first moment. Retainers of Hisamitsu attacked the group, killing Charles Lennox Richardson and wounding two others. A fourth member of the party, a woman, escaped.

The incident exposed a legal and cultural collision. For the Satsuma retinue, the act was an enforcement of status etiquette, to which they thought all had to conform. For the British, it was an unprovoked killing of treaty-protected nationals. The British government demanded an indemnity from the bakufu and the surrender and punishment of the killers from Satsuma. The bakufu, under pressure and with limited control over Satsuma, eventually paid a large indemnity in 1863. Satsuma’s own response was less compliant, leading to the Anglo-Satsuma confrontation described in Section 52.12.

Multiple Perspectives

Treaty Trade and Violence: Who Saw What

Section 52.10

Kōbu Gattai and the Bunkyū Reforms

After Ii Naosuke’s death, the bakufu sought to repair its standing. Its leaders, Andō Nobumasa and Kuze Hirochika, pursued kōbu gattai, a policy of union between court and shogunate, intended to show that the shogun acted with imperial approval and that the court participated in national policy. The cornerstone was a marriage: in 1862 Princess Kazunomiya, sister of Emperor Kōmei, wed the young Shogun Iemochi. The court agreed to the match partly on the promise that the bakufu would work toward ending the treaties or at least restoring the pre-treaty situation within a period of years, a promise that proved impossible to keep and that haunted subsequent negotiations. Andō was himself wounded in an attack outside Edo Castle in early 1862 and soon lost power.

Historical Vocabulary
Kōbu Gattai (公武合体)

Kōbu gattai, “union of court and military government,” described several related proposals to strengthen the shogunate by binding it to the court and, in some versions, widening decision-making to include leading daimyō. It could mean a marriage alliance, a reform of institutions, or a negotiated balance of power. Different advocates imagined different arrangements, and radicals eventually rejected it as insufficient.

The concept shows that the shogunate’s critics were not all anti-Tokugawa. Many sought to reform the system rather than abolish it.

The more consequential reforms came in the summer and autumn of 1862 and are called the Bunkyū reforms after the era name. They were driven in part by Shimazu Hisamitsu, who traveled to Kyoto with a large Satsuma force in the spring of 1862, suppressed radicals in his own domain at the Teradaya Inn in Fushimi, and then escorted an imperial envoy to Edo to press for changes. Hisamitsu’s aim was to guide national politics through moderate reform, without allowing the radicals to seize the initiative. The shogunate agreed to appoint Matsudaira Shungaku as chief political adviser (seiji sōsai) and Hitotsubashi Yoshinobu as guardian of the shogun (shōgun kōken-shoku), restoring to office two of the men purged in 1858. It also created the post of Kyoto Protector (shugoshoku), given to Matsudaira Katamori of Aizu, to maintain order in the imperial capital. This appointment was to prove consequential, since Aizu troops would soon stand against Chōshū and radical activism in Kyoto.

One of the reform measures changed the foundation of Tokugawa control. The system of sankin-kōtai, alternate-year attendance by daimyō in Edo with their families kept as hostages in the city, had been central to the bakufu’s supervision of the domains since the seventeenth century. In 1862 the requirement was reduced to attendance every third year for roughly one hundred days, and families were permitted to return to their home domains. The change lessened the financial burden on the daimyō and reduced the bakufu’s grip. It also reduced Edo’s role as a political and economic center, since many domain residents left the city. The bakufu hoped to gain goodwill; instead, it gave up one of its main levers of control at a moment when it needed them most.

The bakufu also looked abroad for expertise. It had already dispatched a mission to the United States in 1860, accompanied by the steam corvette Kanrin Maru, with naval officers such as Katsu Kaishū and passengers such as Fukuzawa Yukichi. In 1862 it sent a mission to Europe to negotiate the postponement of the opening of additional ports. It created Western-style infantry and naval units, and with French assistance, started the Yokosuka arsenal in 1865. These efforts show that the shogunate pursued modernization before the Restoration, not merely afterward.

Section 52.11

Kyoto, Tenchū, and the Order to Expel Foreigners

By late 1862 Kyoto had become the center of national politics, to a degree unseen in the Tokugawa period. Chōshū, Tosa, and other domains sent representatives, and activists crowded the city. Court nobles sympathetic to radical views, notably Sanjō Sanetomi, gained influence with the young Emperor Kōmei. Imperial envoys delivered messages to Edo demanding action on expulsion. The court, for its part, was not a monolith: some nobles favored cooperation with the bakufu, others pressed for more drastic steps, and the emperor’s own views, firmly opposed to opening the country but wary of war, were interpreted differently by competing factions.

Political violence now took place inside the imperial capital. Activists carried out tenchū (“heavenly punishment”) killings of people they regarded as collaborators with the bakufu, including officials and informants. In early 1863 some radicals decapitated wooden statues of Ashikaga shoguns in a Kyoto temple and displayed the heads, a symbolic attack on the idea of warrior rulers who had slighted the throne. The shogunate responded by recruiting groups of masterless samurai (rōshi) to maintain order, among them the units that became the Shinsengumi under Kondō Isami and Hijikata Toshizō, who served under the Aizu domain’s authority as Kyoto Protector. The Shinsengumi later became a famous symbol in popular culture, but contemporary sources show them as a controversial police force whose methods were harsh and whose members came from varied backgrounds.

In the spring of 1863, Shogun Iemochi traveled to Kyoto, the first shogunal visit since the 1630s. The visit fulfilled the logic of kōbu gattai but also revealed the shogunate’s weakness: the shogun was required to attend the emperor’s shrine visits and to answer demands for a date for expelling foreigners. Under pressure, the bakufu agreed to the date of June 25, 1863 (the tenth day of the fifth lunar month). It apparently regarded the pledge as a means to gain time and avoid immediate confrontation, intending to negotiate with the foreign powers rather than attack them. The court and radical domains, however, took it literally. Chōshū, in particular, announced that it would act.

The episode illustrates a recurring pattern: the shogunate’s promises in Kyoto were not compatible with its obligations in Yokohama. The bakufu could not satisfy both audiences, and each attempt to reassure one angered the other.

Section 52.12

Kagoshima and Shimonoseki: Expulsion Meets Naval Power

Chōshū and the Shimonoseki Strait. On June 25, 1863, Chōshū gun batteries and warships fired on an American merchant ship, the Pembroke, in the Shimonoseki Strait. In the following weeks they attacked French and Dutch vessels. The Western powers responded: an American warship and a French warship bombarded Chōshū positions in July 1863. Chōshū’s forts were damaged, and its small navy suffered, but the domain rebuilt the batteries and continued to threaten passing traffic. The strait is a bottleneck for shipping between the Inland Sea and the Sea of Japan, and Chōshū’s closure of it also disrupted Japanese trade.

In September 1864, after diplomatic efforts had failed, an allied squadron of British, French, Dutch, and American ships, seventeen in all, bombarded and destroyed the Shimonoseki batteries and landed forces to disable them. Chōshū’s negotiator, the young Takasugi Shinsaku, concluded the settlement. The powers demanded an indemnity, and because Chōshū was a domain, not a treaty state, they largely directed the demand at the bakufu, which was obliged to pay or to concede additional measures. The episode highlighted the confusion over who exactly was responsible for what: the bakufu answered for a domain that was defying it.

Satsuma and Kagoshima. Britain demanded that Satsuma punish the killers of Richardson and pay an indemnity. When Satsuma stalled, a British squadron under Admiral Kuper sailed into Kagoshima Bay in August 1863. In the battle that followed, Satsuma’s shore batteries damaged British ships and killed officers, while the British bombarded the batteries and set parts of the city on fire. Both sides suffered losses, and each could claim a degree of resilience. The two sides then negotiated, and Satsuma agreed to pay an indemnity and to address the issue of the killers.

The consequences were surprising. Rather than increasing hostility, the clash led to a practical relationship between Satsuma and Britain. Satsuma officials, impressed by British naval power, sought technology and trade connections and bought ships and arms. The domain’s industrial program was not a product of the war: Shimazu Nariakira had begun the Shūseikan works in Kagoshima in the early 1850s, building on interest in Western-style shipbuilding, cannon founding, and manufacturing. Much of the original complex was destroyed in the 1863 bombardment, and Satsuma rebuilt and expanded its machinery facilities in 1864–1865, now with more direct engagement with British technology. In 1865 the domain sent a group of students, along with officials, to study in Britain. Chōshū pursued a similar path: even before the bombardments, a small party of Chōshū students, including Itō Hirobumi and Inoue Kaoru, had gone to Britain in 1863. These men returned convinced that expulsion was impractical, though they remained committed to the political struggle against the bakufu.

Satsuma’s position also affected Ryukyu. The kingdom’s trade and diplomacy were overseen by Satsuma, which used Ryukyuan channels to obtain information and goods while avoiding open violation of bakufu rules. The kingdom was not an ordinary Japanese domain. After its 1854 agreement with the United States, it signed Friendship Conventions with France (November 24, 1855) and the Netherlands (July 6, 1859), negotiating in its own name while subject to Satsuma’s influence and maintaining its relationship with Qing China. Neither the French nor the Dutch government ultimately ratified its convention. Ryukyuan officials, caught between Satsuma, Qing China, and Western ships, pursued their own strategies. These relationships appear only indirectly in bakufu records but are important to understanding how Satsuma could prepare for the foreign confrontation.

Section 52.13

The Coup of 1863, Ikedaya, Kinmon, and the First Chōshū Expedition

In the political arena of Kyoto, Chōshū’s radical influence reached its height in mid-1863. Radical nobles and domain activists were pressing for dramatic gestures, including a plan for the emperor to visit the Yamato region and personally take up the cause of expelling foreigners. To moderates at court, and to Aizu and Satsuma, the plan looked less like loyalty than like the seizure of the emperor’s authority by a single domain. Emperor Kōmei, who opposed the treaties but did not want to be a pawn, was apparently receptive to those who offered a way out. Their opening came on the eighteenth day of the eighth lunar month of Bunkyū 3, corresponding to September 30, 1863, which is why the event is traditionally called the August Eighteenth Coup. Aizu and Satsuma forces, with moderate court nobles and the emperor’s apparent acquiescence, sealed the palace gates and removed Chōshū troops from guard duty. Sanjō Sanetomi and six other court nobles who had backed the radicals fled to Chōshū (the “Flight of the Seven Nobles”). Radical court policy collapsed, and the order to expel foreigners lost its momentum, though the emperor’s opposition to opening the country remained. Note the sequence in Gregorian terms: the Kagoshima bombardment (August 15–17) preceded the coup, and the Chōshū clashes at Shimonoseki (from June 25) preceded both. The 1863 coup should not be confused with the Kinmon Incident of 1864.

Chōshū activists tried to return. Chōshū leaders argued that their domain had been slandered and that expelling foreigners had been the emperor’s own order. Their position in Kyoto, however, was now weak, and Aizu, as Kyoto Protector, was determined to keep order. In the summer of 1864, the Shinsengumi attacked a gathering of Chōshū and other activists at the Ikedaya inn in Kyoto on July 8, killing or arresting a number of them. The raid reportedly disrupted plans for violent action in the capital, but it also enraged Chōshū hardliners, who had been divided over whether to march. Shortly afterward, Chōshū forces advanced on Kyoto to petition for the restoration of the domain’s standing and the return of its lord’s privileges, with the implied threat of force if the petition was refused. On August 20, 1864, they fought Aizu, Satsuma, and other forces near the Hamaguri Gate of the imperial palace, in an engagement known as the Kinmon (Forbidden Gate) Incident. The Chōshū force was defeated, and Kusaka Genzui, a leading activist, died. Fires set during the fighting burned large parts of Kyoto, destroying thousands of houses and making it one of the most destructive episodes in the city’s history since the Ōnin War. Satsuma, which had joined Aizu in defending the palace, was at this point still acting against Chōshū; its later realignment (Section 52.14) was not foreordained.

The court declared Chōshū an enemy of the court (chōteki), and the bakufu organized a punitive expedition. The First Chōshū Expedition was assembled in the autumn of 1864 from the forces of many domains, but it ended in negotiation rather than major battle. Saigō Takamori of Satsuma played a key role in encouraging a moderate settlement, partly because he doubted that crushing Chōshū would benefit anyone except the bakufu. Chōshū’s conservative leadership, which had taken over, executed or forced the suicide of its chief councilors blamed for the attack on the capital, dismantled some defenses, and submitted in late 1864. The bakufu then disbanded the army without occupying the domain. Many in Edo, including those who wanted a harsher outcome, regarded the settlement as incomplete; many in the domains regarded the mobilization as costly and the aims as unclear.

For the bakufu, the expedition seemed a victory. In reality, it exposed two problems. First, the shogunate depended on domain troops it could not always control. Second, the settlement did not resolve Chōshū’s internal conflict. A few weeks after the expedition ended, a radical faction in the domain rose against the conservatives.

Section 52.14

Realignment: Chōshū’s Radicals, Satsuma, and the Second Chōshū Expedition

In late 1864, Takasugi Shinsaku, who had earlier organized the Kiheitai, an irregular force that drew members from commoners as well as samurai, launched an uprising from Shimonoseki against the conservative leadership. After a brief civil war within the domain, the radicals regained control in early 1865. They then reorganized Chōshū’s military along Western lines, supported by figures such as Ōmura Masujirō, whose military reforms were influential. The domain’s armed forces were no longer solely a hereditary samurai structure. They incorporated rifle-armed units and a new sense of political mission.

Meanwhile, Satsuma’s attitude toward the bakufu changed. Its leaders, Saigō Takamori and Ōkubo Toshimichi, found that the shogunate’s drive to punish Chōshū again threatened to strengthen the bakufu at the expense of the domains. Mediators from Tosa, including Sakamoto Ryōma and Nakaoka Shintarō, worked to bring Satsuma and Chōshū together. On Keiō 2/1/21 (March 7, 1866) the two domains concluded a secret agreement in Kyoto, the Satsuma–Chōshū alliance, whose terms emphasized mutual support and the restoration of Chōshū’s standing; Sakamoto Ryōma later wrote a confirmation of its terms. It was a pact of mutual support, not a plan for the immediate overthrow of the shogunate, and its content has been debated, but it linked the two most powerful opponents of the shogunate’s current policy. Chōshū also obtained modern rifles, in part through Nagasaki intermediaries, including merchants such as Thomas Glover, with Satsuma serving as a nominal purchaser, evading the bakufu’s embargo on arms sales to Chōshū.

The bakufu, however, was not idle. It continued reforms and pursued a closer relationship with France. The French minister Léon Roches encouraged the bakufu and supplied military advisers and technical assistance, and the Yokosuka arsenal proceeded. In November 1865, after allied ships appeared off Hyōgo, the court finally gave its approval to the existing treaties, though not to the opening of Hyōgo, which remained a point of dispute. In June 1866 the powers and the bakufu signed a tariff convention that reduced import duties to roughly five percent, consolidating the treaty system’s commercial terms.

In the summer of 1866, the shogunate launched the Second Chōshū Expedition. It was a military failure. Chōshū’s modernized forces defeated bakufu and allied troops on several fronts, including in the Inland Sea and in northern Kyushu near Kokura. Several domains declined to participate fully, and the Satsuma domain refused to send troops. In August 1866, Shogun Iemochi died at Osaka Castle, and the campaign was halted. Hitotsubashi Yoshinobu soon succeeded to leadership of the Tokugawa house, and became shogun on Keiō 2/12/5 (January 10, 1867). How he attempted to recover the shogunate’s position, and how his opponents responded, belongs to Chapter 53.

The expedition occurred amid severe social strain. In 1866, rice prices soared, and the year saw a wave of urban riots (uchikowashi, “smashings”) in Osaka and Edo and large rural uprisings, many framed as demands for “world renewal” (yonaoshi). These protests were not directed primarily at foreign policy, and many targeted local rice dealers, moneylenders, and village officials. But the combination of inflation, military expense, and the failure of the campaign deepened the sense that the shogunate could neither protect nor provision its people.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Authority and Foreign Policy, 1853–1866

What Changed

  • Foreign policy, once a bakufu prerogative, became a matter on which court, domains, and activists claimed a voice
  • The court became an active political arena; the emperor’s statements gained decisive symbolic weight
  • Political violence, including assassination, became a recognized method of contest
  • Powerful domains developed autonomous military and diplomatic capacities, including purchases of foreign arms
  • Ports with foreign residents and foreign law (extraterritoriality) became part of Japan’s landscape

What Stayed the Same

  • The status system and the domain structure remained in place
  • The shogunate retained substantial resources and many loyal domains
  • Most activists and domains still described their aims as serving the emperor and the realm, not abolishing the shogunate outright
  • Agricultural village life and rural taxation continued, now with new pressures from trade and inflation

Who experienced change most directly: Port residents, silk-region households, activist samurai, and officials who had to implement the treaties. Who experienced greater continuity: Many villages and many domains remote from the ports, at least before 1866. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a political order can lose legitimacy before its institutions are formally dismantled.

Cause and Effect

From Treaty Crisis to Civil Conflict

Long-term causes

Fiscal strain on the bakufu and domains; the growth of commerce and literacy; the Mito and nativist traditions of loyalty to the emperor; the decentralized bakuhan structure; Western expansion in Asia.

Short-term causes

The Perry expeditions; the Second Opium War and the pressure it created; Hotta’s and Ii’s decisions on the 1858 treaties; the succession dispute; Ii’s purge.

Immediate effects

Treaty ports opened; trade and currency disruption; assassination of Ii; attacks on foreigners; bakufu reform attempts; the imperial court’s entry into policy-making.

Long-term consequences

Military and political empowerment of Satsuma and Chōshū; a split between shogunate and court-aligned domains; entrenchment of the treaty system; conditions for the political crisis examined in Chapter 53.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced the escalation. Foreign pressure created the crisis, but the response depended on domestic institutions, factional rivalries, economic stress, and the choices of individuals. Had the court granted its approval in 1858, or had Ii survived, events might have taken different paths.

Section 52.15

Evidence, Memory, and the Politics of Political Violence

Writing about political violence in the 1860s requires awareness of how memory shaped the record. After 1868, the Meiji government and its supporters honored many of the activists who had fought the shogunate, building monuments, enshrining the dead, and publishing biographies. The enshrinement of the war dead at Tokyo’s Shōkonsha, which later became Yasukuni Shrine, began with those who died for the imperial cause in the Restoration conflicts. Lives that had been messy and contradictory were rewritten as coherent stories of loyalty. Those who had worked for the shogunate, such as Ii Naosuke, or who had fought on its side, such as Aizu’s Matsudaira Katamori, were often portrayed as villains or losers, although more recent scholarship has restored complexity to their stories.

Popular culture also reshaped the period. Novels, theater, and, in the twentieth century, film and television made figures like the Shinsengumi and Sakamoto Ryōma into romantic heroes. These portrayals are part of cultural history and deserve study, but they are not evidence for what happened. Historians instead use bakufu and domain archives, court documents, activist diaries and letters, foreign diplomatic reports, and contemporary prints, comparing them to detect bias. Foreign accounts offer valuable observations of events such as Namamugi and Kagoshima but reflect the assumptions of their authors. Japanese sources, including official records of the shogunate, reflect the perspectives of administrators and elites. Commoners, women, Ainu, and Ryukyuan people appear mainly through the eyes of others.

Finally, the violence of the period should be seen in proportion and in context. Assassination and street attacks were significant and symbolically powerful, but they were carried out by small groups and affected a limited number of victims compared with the mass deaths of war or famine. Their importance lay in what they revealed and what they changed: the fragility of bakufu authority, the willingness of activists to use terror, and the intersection of international pressure with domestic politics. Later chapters on the Boshin War and Meiji state-building will show how this conflict developed into armed struggle on a far larger scale.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the decade between the limited Convention of Kanagawa and the shogunate’s military humiliation in 1866. Its central argument is that the commercial treaties of 1858 converted a diplomatic crisis into a lasting crisis of political authority. The shogunate did not collapse in these years, and its fall was not inevitable, but its ability to claim exclusive control over foreign policy, and with it much of its legitimacy, steadily eroded.

Townsend Harris used the example of Anglo-French pressure on China to press for a treaty beyond the 1854 convention. The Shimoda Convention of 1857 introduced extraterritoriality, and the Treaty of Amity and Commerce of July 29, 1858, followed by agreements with the Netherlands, Russia, Britain, and France, established treaty ports, fixed tariffs, and foreign residence. Hotta Masayoshi’s failed attempt to obtain imperial sanction and Ii Naosuke’s decision to sign without it made the treaty an issue of who had the right to decide.

Ii’s answer was repression. The Ansei Purge punished critics of the treaty and supporters of the Hitotsubashi candidacy, turning policy disagreement into persecution, and his assassination at Sakuradamon in 1860 showed that the regime could not protect even its chief minister. Political killing, once started, became a tool for many factions.

The treaty ports of Yokohama, Nagasaki, and Hakodate opened in 1859 and had distinct characters. Trade brought prosperity to silk and tea producers and port merchants, but also currency disruption, inflation, and distress for others. The ports were also places where Japanese and foreigners met in work, in licensed quarters, and in religious and cultural exchange, including the 1865 appearance of hidden Christians in Nagasaki. Ainu and Ryukyuan communities, as well as Satsuma’s management of Ryukyu’s external relations, show that the treaty system reached beyond the main islands.

Sonnō jōi gave voice to opposition but never formed a single program. Mitogaku, nativism, and domain-based networks of shishi produced a flexible language that Chōshū radicals, Satsuma leaders, court nobles, and even some bakufu supporters could use for different ends. Attacks on foreigners, including the Namamugi Incident, forced the bakufu to defend a treaty system it did not control against enemies it could not suppress.

Reform and alliance were the shogunate’s answers after 1860. Court–bakufu union, the marriage of Princess Kazunomiya to Iemochi, the Bunkyū reforms, the relaxation of sankin-kōtai, and the creation of the Kyoto Protector aimed to restore consensus but, in several respects, reduced bakufu control. At the same time the shogunate pursued military and naval modernization with Dutch and French help.

The bombardments of Kagoshima and Shimonoseki showed that expulsion was militarily unrealistic. They did not, however, end opposition to the bakufu. Satsuma and Chōshū turned to Western technology and trade, and both sent students abroad. The coup of 1863, Ikedaya, the Kinmon Incident, and the First Chōshū Expedition brought domain conflict into Kyoto and into the shogunate’s military practice.

By 1866, Chōshū’s radicals had regained power and modernized their forces, Satsuma and Chōshū had allied, the court had accepted the treaties in principle, and a tariff convention had consolidated their economic terms. The Second Chōshū Expedition failed, Shogun Iemochi died, and social unrest spread. The decade left unsettled the question that Chapter 53 will address: who would govern Japan, and in whose name.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how an international agreement can become a domestic political crisis. The treaties of 1858 were negotiated to avoid war, and in that narrow sense they succeeded. But they also forced the question of who spoke for Japan, a question that the Tokugawa order had never needed to answer so explicitly. The answer was contested in Kyoto, in domain castles, in the treaty ports, and in the streets.

It also shows the range of people affected. Silk producers, port merchants, samurai on fixed stipends, women working in and around the settlements, Ainu communities in Ezo, Ryukyuan officials, and hidden Christians in Nagasaki all experienced the opening of Japan differently. A history of the decade that includes only diplomats and assassins would miss most of this.

Many features of this period shaped what followed. The court’s new political role, the military and technological capacity of Satsuma and Chōshū, the experience of studying abroad, and the memory of foreign naval power all carried into the Meiji era. So did the grievance of the treaties themselves, whose revision became a central goal of the new government and is taken up in later chapters on constitutional government and empire.

Students who understand this chapter are prepared for Chapter 53, where the shogunate’s final years, the Boshin War, and the creation of a new central government unfold. They should carry with them the lesson of this decade: the end of the Tokugawa order was not the result of a single event, and it was not preordained, but the outcome of interacting pressures, decisions, and contingencies.
