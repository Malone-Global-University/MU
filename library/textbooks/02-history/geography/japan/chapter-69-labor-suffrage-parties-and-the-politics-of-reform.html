Part X — Mass Society, Democracy, and Crisis

Labor, Suffrage, Parties, and the Politics of Reform

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On May 2, 1920, thousands of workers assembled in Tokyo's Ueno Park for what organizers presented as Japan's first major May Day demonstration. Nothing about the gathering was a spontaneous riot. It had a program, sponsoring organizations, speakers, and demands that could be written down and presented to the public: demands about working hours, wages, the right to organize, and the place of workers in national life.

The scene in Ueno Park is a useful way into this chapter because it marks a change in the form of popular politics. Two years earlier, in the summer of 1918, crowds in fishing towns, cities, and mining districts had attacked rice dealers and clashed with police, as Chapter 68 explained. Those events showed the depth of popular grievance, but the crowds disbanded once the immediate crisis passed. The organizations examined in this chapter were built to last. They collected dues, published newspapers, drafted programs, negotiated or tried to negotiate with employers and landlords, lobbied politicians, and argued among themselves about strategy. The central question of the chapter is how social grievances over wages, rents, hours, and political exclusion were converted into organized demands for institutional change.

That conversion produced real results. Between 1912 and 1928 the Japanese labor movement grew from a small mutual-aid society into a set of competing union federations. Tenant farmers formed a national union and fought thousands of local disputes. Socialists, anarchists, and communists tried to rebuild a political left that the state had crushed in 1910–1911. A coalition of unions, students, journalists, and party politicians campaigned for universal male suffrage, and in 1925 the Diet abolished the tax qualification for voting. Women organized a suffrage league, and, although they won no vote, they changed the terms of debate. In 1926 the government repealed the notorious restriction on strike-related incitement and created conciliation machinery for labor disputes. On February 20, 1928, some twelve million men were eligible to vote in the first national election under the new law; roughly four in five of them did, and a handful of candidates from labor and farmer parties won seats in the House of Representatives.

The same years also produced the Peace Preservation Law of 1925, which made it a crime to organize associations aimed at altering the national polity (kokutai) or denying the private property system. This was not an accident of timing. The chapter's central argument is that Taishō-era reform was neither a gift from enlightened leaders nor the triumph of a unified democratic movement. It emerged from sustained competition among workers, tenant farmers, party politicians, socialists, women's associations, intellectuals, employers, landlords, and state officials. Its defining contradiction was that the state widened electoral participation and made limited concessions to organized labor while preserving, and sharpening, instruments for restricting and repressing political movements. Students should resist two simple stories: one in which reform was inevitably headed toward democratic success, and one in which every concession was a mere cover for repression. The evidence supports neither.

The people involved were not a single "working class" or "the people." Skilled male machinists in Kobe shipyards, young women in textile mills housed in company dormitories, coal miners in Kyushu and Hokkaido, tenant households paying roughly half their crop in rent, Christian reformers in the Women's Christian Temperance Union, Marxist intellectuals writing in clandestine circumstances, Buraku activists who founded the Suiheisha in 1922, and Korean laborers who had migrated to Japanese cities each experienced the period differently. Their interests sometimes aligned and often did not. Korea and Taiwan had no constituencies electing representatives to the Imperial Diet, even though decisions made in Tokyo shaped colonial lives. The expanded 1928 electorate was organized in metropolitan constituencies; qualified Korean and Taiwanese men resident in Japan proper could vote and stand for election under the 1925 law, a limited exception that did not confer representative institutions on the colonies. Chapter 67 should be consulted for the constitutional framework and the history of cabinets; this chapter concerns those who organized to change specific conditions.

The evidence available to historians is unusually varied for this period. It includes statutes published in the Official Gazette, Home Ministry and police surveillance reports, union newspapers, the proceedings of the Diet, labor yearbooks compiled by the Ōhara Institute for Social Research, election manuals, party platforms, memoirs by organizers such as Suzuki Bunji and Ichikawa Fusae, and the writings of intellectuals such as Yamakawa Hitoshi, Yamakawa Kikue, and Ōsugi Sakae. It also includes statistics, which must be read critically: official counts of strikes, union members, and tenancy disputes were compiled by agencies with their own definitions and purposes, and the figures given in this chapter are approximations drawn from standard scholarly summaries. Wherever the text gives a figure, students should treat it as an estimate unless the text says otherwise.

Chapter 69 builds directly on Chapter 63's history of industrial labor and on Chapter 64's treatment of women's changing social roles, and it overlaps with the Rice Riots (Chapter 68) and the civil-society history of Chapter 67. It looks ahead to Chapter 71's account of the 1923 earthquake, Chapter 73's treatment of rural crisis and financial instability, and Chapter 74's account of the collapse of parliamentary restraint. The chapter ends in 1928 with a mixed assessment: a much broader electorate and a set of organized social movements that had won real concessions, but also a political order that had not accepted an unrestricted right to organize, to protest, or to pursue radical political alternatives.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the legal framework of the Public Peace Police Law constrained union organizing, strikes, and political association, and how enforcement varied.
  2. Trace the development of Japanese unionism from the Yūaikai of 1912 to the Sōdōmei and the left-wing Hyōgikai, distinguishing the strategies of each organization.
  3. Analyze the 1920 May Day gathering and the 1921 Kobe shipyard strikes as evidence of changing relations among workers, union leaders, employers, and police.
  4. Compare urban industrial organizing with tenant-farmer organizing in terms of grievances, bargaining power, leadership, and legal constraints.
  5. Distinguish among social reformism, social democracy, Marxism, anarchism, and syndicalism, and the distinct institutions (unions, parties, the clandestine Communist Party) that carried them.
  6. Evaluate why the tax qualification for voting became difficult to defend and how the universal male suffrage movement developed between 1919 and 1925.
  7. Assess what the Universal Male Suffrage Law of 1925 did and did not accomplish, including its exclusions of women, welfare recipients, and colonial subjects.
  8. Interpret the Women's Suffrage League of 1924 and the divisions among liberal, Christian, socialist, working, and professional women.
  9. Explain why the Peace Preservation Law of 1925 and the Labor Disputes Conciliation Law of 1926 belong to the same political moment as electoral reform.
  10. Distinguish the Rōdō Nōmintō, Nihon Rōnōtō, and Shakai Minshūtō, and explain their relation to unions and to the 1928 election.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Taishō reform and avoid treating the later authoritarian turn as inevitable.
  12. Evaluate primary sources for their creators, purposes, and limits, and recognize whose perspectives are missing.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did social grievances after 1918 become lasting organizations with programs, finances, and leadership, and what changed when protest became institutionalized?
  2. Why did Japanese unionism move from a cooperative, mutual-aid model in 1912 toward militancy and then fragment into rival federations by the mid-1920s?
  3. In what ways were tenant-farmer movements similar to, and different from, industrial unionism, and why did cooperation between the two prove attractive yet difficult?
  4. How did socialists, anarchists, syndicalists, and communists differ about how political change should be pursued, and how did state surveillance shape those disputes?
  5. Why did the tax qualification for voting become politically unsustainable, and who deserves credit for the 1925 reform: movements, parties, or officials?
  6. Why could the same Diet and cabinet that widened the electorate also pass the Peace Preservation Law, and what does this suggest about the nature of Taishō political reform?
  7. How did women's exclusion from the vote shape women's activism, and how did class, religion, and occupation divide women reformers?
  8. What does the 1928 election reveal about the achievements and the limits of the Taishō reform movements, and how should historians avoid reading the later authoritarian turn backward into it?

Historical Context

By the end of the First World War, Japan was an industrial and imperial power whose political structure had been designed in the 1880s. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 created an elected House of Representatives, but it limited the franchise to a small number of male taxpayers, placed the cabinet outside parliamentary control, and gave the House of Peers and the military important independent powers (see Chapter 59). During the 1910s and 1920s the parties in the lower house increased their influence, especially after 1918, when Hara Takashi became the first prime minister to head a cabinet drawn mostly from the majority party in the lower house (Chapter 67). But the electorate remained small, and the legal environment for collective organization was restrictive.

The First World War transformed the economic setting. Japanese exports surged, shipbuilding and heavy industry expanded, factory employment rose, and consumer prices climbed, culminating in the rice price spike of 1918. Workers' wages did not generally keep pace with inflation, and the postwar recession that began in 1920 brought layoffs and wage reductions. Chapter 63 described the early history of industrial labor, including the mines, textile mills, and the first unions; Chapter 68 described the Rice Riots. This chapter assumes that background and asks what organized movements did next.

The international setting also mattered. The Russian Revolution of 1917, the creation of the Comintern in 1919, and the founding of the International Labour Organization in the same year all influenced how Japanese activists and officials thought about labor questions. The suppression of the March First Movement in Korea in 1919 and Japan's intervention in Siberia (1918–1922) reminded contemporaries that Japan was an imperial power as well as an industrializing one. Meanwhile universal or near-universal male suffrage was being adopted or expanded in many countries, and Japanese advocates pointed to these examples while their opponents pointed to the dangers of foreign radicalism.

Finally, the political left had been severely damaged before this chapter begins. After the High Treason Incident of 1910–1911, in which twelve people were executed, including the anarchist Kōtoku Shūsui, the government created a specialized political police and drove open socialism to the margins. The 1910s are often called the "winter period" of Japanese socialism. The reorganization of the left after 1918 therefore had to proceed under conditions of surveillance, and the legal organizations examined here, such as unions and legal parties, always had to anticipate police action.

Section 69.1 · Division I — Economic Change, Political Exclusion, and the Foundations of Organized Reform

Japan after the Rice Riots: The Expansion of Organized Political Demands

The Rice Riots of 1918, treated in Chapter 68, left an ambiguous legacy. They forced the resignation of the Terauchi cabinet and helped bring Hara Takashi to office, but they also showed officials how quickly local disturbances could spread. For many reformers, the lesson was different: if crowds could act without leaders, then organized associations might give grievances a durable and negotiable form. The years immediately after the riots saw a dramatic increase in the number of unions, study groups, and political associations. University students formed societies such as the Shinjinkai at the Imperial University of Tokyo (founded in December 1918), and intellectuals such as Yoshino Sakuzō organized discussion groups that linked academic debate to public campaigns. Chapter 67 discusses these civic associations; here the emphasis falls on organizations that sought concrete changes in wages, working conditions, land rents, and the franchise.

Economic conditions pushed in two directions. The wartime boom had expanded industrial employment and reinforced workers' sense that their labor was essential to national power. But the boom also generated inflation, speculation, and visible wealth among profiteers, while the recession that followed the financial panic of 1920 produced layoffs and wage cuts. Workers could therefore claim both that they had contributed to national prosperity and that they had not received a fair share of it. The international environment added further encouragement. The Treaty of Versailles established the International Labour Organization in 1919, and its first conference, held in Washington, adopted standards on working hours that Japanese labor leaders cited. The controversy over who would represent Japanese workers at that conference, since the government chose a delegate whom labor organizations did not accept, became an early lesson in how labor's claim to representation was contested.

The setting after 1918 thus encouraged lasting organization rather than only temporary protest. Chronic grievances were joined by new opportunities: more urban literate workers, more newspapers willing to cover labor, a government that had begun to create social-affairs bureaucracies, and a political climate in which the language of democracy and "reconstruction" (kaizō) had public currency. The organizations that resulted were nevertheless constrained by a legal order that had been written to prevent exactly this kind of collective action, and the next section turns to that framework.

Section 69.2 · Division I

The Legal and Institutional Barriers to Collective Action

The principal legal barrier to unions and strikes was the Public Peace Police Law (Chian Keisatsu Hō) of 1900. The statute regulated political associations and public meetings, required notification to the police, and allowed officials to prohibit gatherings and dissolve associations. It also forbade certain categories of persons, among them soldiers, police, teachers, students, minors, and women, from joining political associations, and it barred women and minors from attending political meetings (a restriction on women that was modified in 1922, discussed in Chapter 67 and Section 69.28). Its most notorious clause for labor was Article 17, which prohibited the use of violence, threats, or what the statute called seduction and incitement to induce others to join or not join a union or a collective work stoppage, or to press demands about wages. Because the language about "incitement" was broad, a labor organizer who urged coworkers to join a union or strike could be prosecuted. The statute did not formally outlaw unions or strikes as such; it criminalized the activities by which unions typically grew and strikes were typically organized.

Enforcement was uneven. Strikes and unions existed throughout the 1900s and 1910s, and prosecutions under Article 17 were not constant. Police could choose when to tolerate a dispute and when to intervene, and officials sometimes acted as informal mediators. This discretion mattered: it created a situation in which organizers could never be sure what was permitted and in which the state held a standing threat over any dispute. After the High Treason Incident of 1910–1911, the Tokyo Metropolitan Police created a Special Higher Police (Tokubetsu Kōtō Keisatsu) to monitor political dissent, and similar units were later established elsewhere. Surveillance of unions, student groups, and socialist associations became routine.

The protective side of the legal order was thin. The Factory Law, enacted in 1911 and put into force in 1916, set limits on hours for women and young workers, restricted night work with long-term exemptions, and created minimum standards for factory safety and compensation. But it covered only factories above a minimum size, allowed numerous exceptions, and was enforced by a small inspectorate. It did not recognize unions, collective bargaining, or the right to strike. In the aftermath of the First World War, the Home Ministry developed a social-affairs bureaucracy and reformers inside the government began to discuss labor legislation, but those discussions took place alongside ongoing suspicion of organized labor. The legal framework therefore shaped the strategies of the movements discussed below: some groups emphasized cooperation and legality, others tested the limits through direct action, and nearly all had to calculate the costs of police attention.

Primary Source Window

Article 17 of the Public Peace Police Law, 1900

Creator: The Japanese government (Cabinet and Ministry of Home Affairs), promulgated under the Meiji Constitution · Date: March 1900 (Law No. 36) · Type: Statute · Provenance: Published in the Official Gazette (Kanpō); the National Archives of Japan preserves the original statute as accession 御04291100 (https://www.digital.archives.go.jp/file/152693.html); the National Diet Library indexes it as Law No. 36 of March 10, 1900. This teaching window remains a paraphrase, not a transcription.

Context: The law was drafted after the first stirrings of an organized labor movement in the 1890s, and it consolidated earlier regulations on assemblies and associations. Government officials argued that public order and the freedom of workers who did not want to join unions or strikes required protection from coercion. Labor activists and later reformers argued that the article made ordinary organizing a crime.

Paraphrase, not a verbatim translation: Article 17 forbade attempts, by violence, threats, or by inciting or seducing others, to induce people to join or not join an organization formed to pursue a collective work stoppage or to press demands about wages. Penalties for violating the article, set in a separate provision, were short terms of imprisonment or fines. For exact wording, consult the gazette text or a scholarly translation.

Analysis: The article's importance lies less in any single prosecution than in its structure. By phrasing the prohibition around "incitement," it let police and prosecutors treat any recruitment drive or strike appeal as potentially criminal while formally leaving the existence of unions undefined. The text reflects the perspective of the state and of employers; it says nothing about the perspective of workers who sought to organize. The source cannot tell historians how often the article was actually enforced, which requires police and court records.

Section 69.3 · Division I

War, Industrial Expansion, and the Changing Composition of Labor

The industrial workforce of the late 1910s was far from uniform. In the textile industry, which remained the largest employer of factory labor, a majority of workers were young women, many of them daughters of rural households recruited on fixed-term contracts and housed in company dormitories. Chapter 63 described the origins of these arrangements. In shipbuilding, steel, machinery, and electrical equipment, by contrast, the workforce was largely male and included skilled workers who had considerable mobility between firms and a tradition of craft pride. In coal and metal mining, workers lived in company-controlled communities and faced danger, debt, and subcontracted supervision. Railway, tram, and port workers were in a different position again, because their work was essential to commerce and difficult to replace quickly.

Skill, gender, and workplace control shaped the possibilities of collective action. Skilled male workers in heavy industry, as historians such as Andrew Gordon have emphasized, increasingly resented the authority of foremen and the arbitrary power of managers, and they demanded recognition as responsible members of the enterprise, a claim that could be expressed in the language of "personality" or dignity as well as wages. Women textile workers had fewer opportunities to bargain: contracts, dormitory rules, and the pressure of family debt limited their freedom to leave, and the male-dominated unions of the period paid little attention to them. Miners faced employers and subcontractors who combined economic and personal authority over entire settlements. A shared vocabulary of "labor" or "workers" therefore concealed major differences in leverage and goals, and any account of organized reform must keep those differences in view.

The war years increased the size of the industrial workforce, according to Home Ministry and Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce surveys, by well over half in the course of the decade, though the precise numbers depend on how small workshops are counted. Wages rose in nominal terms, but inflation was severe, especially in 1917–1918. The economic downturn after 1920 and the structural problem of an agricultural sector sending young people to cities and mills created a persistent sense of insecurity. This was the world in which the first nationwide labor federations took shape, and in which a large proportion of industrial disputes arose from the specific grievances of one workplace rather than from a doctrine.

Section 69.4 · Division I

From Local Grievances to National Reform Movements

Reform in the Taishō era took several distinct forms, and it is useful to separate them. A union was an organization of workers in a trade, firm, or industry that collected dues and sought to bargain or to support strikes. A tenant association performed similar functions among farmers who rented land. A political association or party sought to influence legislation or win seats. A suffrage league campaigned for a specific change in the law. A demonstration or petition was a tactic that any of these groups might use. Many individuals belonged to more than one organization, and leaders such as Suzuki Bunji, Kagawa Toyohiko, or Ichikawa Fusae moved among forms. But the organizations had different resources, faced different legal constraints, and were accountable to different constituencies, and treating them as a single "movement" obscures the choices their leaders had to make.

The central analytical problem is how social demands became demands for institutional reform. A worker who wanted a wage increase might at first appeal to a foreman or manager. If the appeal failed, collective action followed. If collective action was criminalized by Article 17, the demand for a legal right to organize followed. If the right to organize required legislation, then representation in the Diet mattered, and the franchise became a labor issue. A tenant who wanted a rent reduction faced a similar chain: the landlord's refusal, the village's pressure, the court's reluctance, the demand for tenancy legislation, and finally the question of who in the Diet represented tenants rather than landlords. This is why the same organizations often moved from workplace or village issues to the campaign for universal male suffrage, and why the franchise campaign cannot be understood apart from the social movements that supported it.

At the same time, the movements often disagreed about how to proceed. Some leaders favored negotiated agreements and incremental legislation; others favored strikes, direct action, or revolutionary politics. State authorities and party politicians sometimes encouraged the moderates and treated the radicals as threats. The remaining sections of the chapter follow these developments in turn: unions and industrial labor (Division II), rural tenancy (Division III), socialist and radical thought (Division IV), the franchise campaign (Division V), women's political rights (Division VI), and the parties and legislation that culminated in the 1928 election (Division VII).

Section 69.5 · Division II — Trade Unions, Strikes, and the Politics of Industrial Labor

Suzuki Bunji and the Founding of the Yūaikai, 1912

The Yūaikai (the "Friendly Society") was founded in Tokyo on August 1, 1912, by Suzuki Bunji, a Tokyo Imperial University graduate who had worked as a journalist and as a social worker associated with the Unitarian movement in Japan. The first meeting was small, reportedly about fifteen people, and the founding took place in a Unitarian meeting hall. Suzuki's own background shaped the organization's early character. He was influenced by Christian social reform and by the model of American craft unionism, and in 1915 he traveled to the United States, where he met leaders of the American Federation of Labor. The Yūaikai's initial program emphasized mutual aid (such as sickness and funeral benefits), moral and educational improvement for workers, and cooperation between labor and capital (rōshi kyōchō). Suzuki and his colleagues presented the organization as a loyal, legal association that would make workers better citizens and better employees, an approach that helped it avoid immediate suppression and attract support from some business and government figures, including, according to historians, financial assistance from the industrialist Shibusawa Eiichi.

The strategy was gradualist but not passive. The Yūaikai published a newspaper, offered lectures, organized branches, and sought to improve workers' conditions through persuasion and public opinion. In its early years it avoided strikes and rejected socialism, while still advocating for legislation and for recognition of workers' dignity. Stephen Large's study of the organization emphasizes that this approach reflected both Suzuki's convictions and a calculation about what was feasible under the Public Peace Police Law. The model also reflected a particular conception of the worker: a skilled, steady, family-supporting male breadwinner whose self-respect could be strengthened through education and mutual help. Women and unskilled laborers were present but rarely at the center of early planning.

Membership grew modestly in the first years and then rapidly in the war economy. By 1919 the Yūaikai counted tens of thousands of members, a figure that should be treated as approximate because organizational and official statistics differed in how they counted dues-paying and nominal members. Its geographical base was concentrated in the Tokyo region, the Osaka–Kobe region, and industrial centers such as Yawata in northern Kyushu. The organization that Suzuki founded as a mutual-aid association thus became a mass organization whose members increasingly expected it to act as a union.

Section 69.6 · Division II

From Mutual Aid to Collective Bargaining: Labor Organization during the War

As the wartime economy expanded, the Yūaikai's branches multiplied, and the organization increasingly found itself involved in disputes it had not planned. Members facing wage cuts, dismissals, or abusive supervisors turned to local officers for help, and local officers often had little choice but to press their employers or support work stoppages. The number of recorded labor disputes in Japan rose sharply from 1917 through 1919, according to Home Ministry statistics, and these disputes included an increasing number of strikes in heavy industry and mining as well as in textiles. Many were short and local, but the pattern signaled that workers were using collective action as a regular tool.

This created a tension within the Yūaikai. Suzuki's gradualist approach, built around legality, persuasion, and cooperation, came under pressure from members who wanted the organization to behave more like a bargaining agent and from younger activists influenced by socialist ideas and by the Russian Revolution. Historians have emphasized the importance of distinguishing between demands that came from rank-and-file workers and decisions made by union leaders and sympathetic intellectuals. Rank-and-file workers often pressed for practical gains: higher wages, shorter hours, protection against arbitrary dismissal, and respect from supervisors. Leaders and intellectuals framed these demands in broader language, speaking of workers' personality, democracy, or class. The two did not always match, and neither was static. The union's Kansai branches, for example, were often more militant than its Tokyo headquarters.

The Yūaikai also expanded its membership beyond skilled men. It established a women's section during the war years and attempted to recruit among textile workers, though women's participation remained limited, as Section 69.10 explains. By 1918–1919, under the impact of price inflation and the Rice Riots, the organization recognized that its earlier strategy no longer matched its situation. The next stage, from 1919 to 1921, was a formal transformation in name, structure, and program.

Section 69.7 · Division II

From Yūaikai to Sōdōmei: The Transformation of Unionism, 1919–1921

In 1919 the Yūaikai adopted the longer name Dai Nihon Rōdō Sōdōmei Yūaikai ("Greater Japan General Federation of Labor, Friendly Society"), a transitional name that signaled a shift from a friendly society toward a federation of labor unions. In 1921 it adopted the shorter name Nihon Rōdō Sōdōmei ("Japan Federation of Labour"), usually abbreviated as the Sōdōmei, dropping the older "friendly society" label. The change was more than cosmetic. The organization's program increasingly emphasized unions as bargaining agents, the right to organize and strike, and political demands including universal male suffrage. Its structure moved from individual membership in a society toward affiliation by unions, and its tactics moved from persuasion to negotiation backed by strikes.

The reasons for this transformation were both external and internal. Externally, the ILO's emphasis on workers' representation and the postwar atmosphere encouraged language about rights; the recession of 1920 and its layoffs prompted defensive struggles; and the example of strikes in other countries, including in Britain and the United States, circulated through the press. Internally, local unions in the shipyards and heavy industries were demanding recognition and bargaining rights. At the same time, the Sōdōmei did not become a uniformly militant body. It remained a federation of unions with different orientations, and its leadership, drawn increasingly from a younger generation including Matsuoka Komakichi and Nishio Suehiro, sought a pragmatic balance between pressure and negotiation. Students should avoid treating the Yūaikai of 1912 and the Sōdōmei of 1921 as identical organizations with unchanged strategies: the later body was larger, more contentious, more political, and internally more divided.

The transformation also had consequences outside the labor movement. Employers who had been willing to tolerate a cooperative society were less willing to bargain with a federation that endorsed strikes. Police attention increased. And the Sōdōmei's growing political commitments, especially its support for universal suffrage, tied it to the broader reform campaigns described in Division V.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Labor and Legal Terminology

Rōdō kumiai ("labor union") referred to an organization of workers for mutual benefit and bargaining. In this period it had no clear legal status; unions existed in a gray area, neither recognized by statute nor formally banned. Sōdōmei means "general federation" and, as a proper name, refers to the Nihon Rōdō Sōdōmei. Rōshi kyōchō ("labor–capital cooperation") described the idea that employers and workers shared interests and should resolve conflicts through harmony; it was both a sincere reformist philosophy and a slogan used by employers. Sōgi means "dispute" and includes strikes and lockouts; chōtei means "conciliation" or "mediation," the term used in the 1924 and 1926 laws. Chian Keisatsu Hō is the Public Peace Police Law, with its Article 17.

These terms matter because modern English words like "union" and "strike" carry assumptions about legal rights that did not exist in prewar Japan. A "union" in 1920 might be recognized by an employer informally, tolerated by police, or targeted for suppression, but no law guaranteed it a right to bargain. Likewise, "conciliation" in 1926 did not mean that employers were obliged to bargain with unions; it meant that the state offered a procedure for mediating disputes.

Section 69.8 · Division II

Japan's First May Day, May 2, 1920: Labor Enters the National Public Sphere

May Day, the international labor holiday, had been celebrated in Japan in small socialist gatherings in the 1900s, but the gathering held in Tokyo's Ueno Park on May 2, 1920, is widely regarded as the first major May Day in Japan. It was organized by a coalition of labor groups, including the Yūaikai and other unions such as the printers' union Shin'yūkai, along with other labor and intellectual allies. Contemporary newspaper reports and later scholarship commonly place attendance at around ten thousand, though estimates vary with the source and with the counting of those who joined marches. The gathering took place on May 2 rather than on May 1, the international date; scholars link such scheduling choices to the practical constraints on workers' free time and to negotiations with the police.

The speeches and resolutions at the gathering articulated an agenda that combined economic and political demands: an eight-hour working day, protections against unemployment, a minimum wage, recognition of the right to organize, repeal of the restrictions of the Public Peace Police Law, and universal male suffrage. Police were present in large numbers. Although the organized Ueno Park gathering took place, after it ended demonstrators demanding the release of detained participants clashed with police. The distinction between the rally itself and the subsequent confrontation matters for understanding its policing. The press covered it extensively, and its presence in newspapers signaled that the labor movement had become a feature of the national public sphere. Unlike a strike, which could be dismissed as a local dispute, a May Day parade with a published program presented labor as a national constituency with national demands.

The event also shows the diversity of the movement. Moderate unionists, socialists, and intellectuals shared the platform, and the participation of different groups showed both the strength of the coalition and the seeds of later divisions. The 1921 and subsequent May Days were more tightly policed, and in some years authorities banned or restricted marches. Historians treat the 1920 gathering as an indicator of the movement's confidence at its postwar peak and, at the same time, as a reminder that public demonstrations required the tolerance, however grudging, of the police.

Section 69.9 · Division II

The Kawasaki and Mitsubishi Shipyard Strikes of 1921

The largest industrial confrontation of the early 1920s took place in the shipyards of Kobe in the summer of 1921. Kobe was a major port and shipbuilding center, with two large employers: the Kawasaki Dockyard and the Mitsubishi shipyard. The postwar slump in shipbuilding had led to wage restraint and threats of dismissals, while workers had been building organizations with support from the Sōdōmei and from Christian reformers such as Kagawa Toyohiko, who lived and worked in Kobe. The immediate dispute at Kawasaki arose over demands that included recognition of a workers' organization's right to bargain collectively, mechanisms for worker representation in the workplace such as factory committees, and improvements in wages and conditions. Management, headed by the firm's leadership, refused to recognize outside unions as bargaining agents.

The strikes that followed involved tens of thousands of workers across the two yards, making them among the largest industrial disputes in prewar Japan up to that time. Workers marched through Kobe in large demonstrations, and the movement adopted tactics including slowdowns and attempts to take control of aspects of production, which drew on both syndicalist ideas and workers' own initiatives. The Mitsubishi and Kawasaki disputes were related but not identical: they involved different workforces, different employers, and different histories of organization, and their paths diverged as the summer progressed. Employers responded by dismissals, lockouts, and appeals to police; authorities arrested union leaders, and troops were dispatched to maintain order. Within weeks the strikes collapsed in defeat, with many workers dismissed or forced to return on the employers' terms.

The defeat was significant. It showed the limits of union strength when facing large employers backed by the state, and it fueled debates about strategy within the labor movement. Some leaders concluded that direct action had been premature and that the union needed to focus on organization and legislation. Others concluded that the strike's failure demonstrated the need for more radical strategies or for political action. The Kobe disputes also illustrate the tension among rank-and-file workers, whose determination sustained the strikes; professional union leaders, who sought negotiated outcomes; and intellectuals and activists, who interpreted the disputes through larger ideological lenses.

Section 69.10 · Division II

Women Workers, Factory Discipline, and the Limits of Union Representation

Women made up a majority of the workforce in the textile industry, and textile workers outnumbered workers in any other single factory sector. Their situation differed sharply from that of male skilled workers. Many were recruited from rural households, sometimes through labor brokers who advanced money to families, and lived in company dormitories where meals, movement, and correspondence were regulated. Work schedules in spinning and weaving mills often involved long shifts and night work, and wages were low and frequently tied to production quotas and fines. The Factory Law of 1911 imposed limits on hours and night work for women and young workers, but it contained long-term exemptions, and a revision enacted in 1923 raised the minimum age and shortened hours only gradually; the ban on night work for women and young workers did not take effect until 1929. Reformers and journalists documented the conditions of textile workers, and a vivid and widely read account of them is Hosoi Wakizō's Jokō aishi ("The Sad History of Factory Girls"), published in 1925 by a former mill worker turned labor organizer. Such accounts are valuable evidence, but they were written by observers with reformist aims, and they should be read alongside government surveys and company records.

Women workers did organize and strike. Strikes in textile mills and silk-reeling factories had occurred since the 1880s, and in the 1920s women took part in disputes in textiles, tobacco, and other industries. The Yūaikai established a women's section in the war years, and the Sōdōmei and later federations included women's departments. But women's membership remained small relative to their share of the workforce. Obstacles included the dormitory system, which restricted contact with outside organizers; short employment terms, since many women worked only a few years before returning to rural households or marrying; legal limits on women's political association under the Public Peace Police Law; and the attitudes of male union leaders, who often treated women as temporary workers or as auxiliary members. Gendered wage differences were wide, and unions rarely made equal pay a priority.

Women's labor activism also intersected with other reform movements. Women reformers such as those in the Christian women's organizations investigated conditions in mills, and some socialist women, as Section 69.20 explains, argued that women's emancipation required organized class action. Yet women workers were seldom the leaders of these campaigns. Historians therefore face an evidentiary problem: much of what we know about women workers comes from observers and organizers rather than from the workers themselves, though diaries, songs, letters, and later oral histories offer glimpses of their views.

Section 69.11 · Division II

Reformists, Militants, Syndicalists, and the Divisions of Organized Labor

Disagreement within the labor movement concerned three connected questions: whether unions should concentrate on negotiation and legal reform or on direct action; whether unions should support political parties and, if so, which ones; and whether the goal of the movement was improvement within capitalism or its replacement. Reformists, associated with the Sōdōmei leadership, emphasized organization, negotiation, and legislation, and many supported a labor party that would operate within the parliamentary system. Syndicalists and anarchists, influenced by Ōsugi Sakae, argued that workers should rely on direct action and workplace organization rather than parliamentary politics. Marxists and communists, discussed in Division IV, argued for a disciplined political movement aimed at transforming society and increasingly sought to win influence in unions.

These disputes shaped the Sōdōmei's internal politics in the early 1920s. A conflict between anarcho-syndicalists and Bolsheviks in 1922–1923 weakened the left, and then the 1923 earthquake and its aftermath (Chapter 71) disrupted organization. By the mid-1920s the Sōdōmei's leadership was increasingly social-democratic in orientation, and left-wing unions inside it were pressing for more militant policies and for greater cooperation with communists. In 1925 the leadership expelled several left-wing unions, which then formed the Nihon Rōdō Kumiai Hyōgikai (the "Hyōgikai") in May of that year. Further divisions in 1926–1927 separated the remaining Sōdōmei into moderate and more centrist currents. The Hyōgikai led a number of high-profile strikes, including the Kyōdō Printing dispute in Tokyo in 1926 and the long strike at the Nippon Gakki musical-instrument factory in Hamamatsu in 1926, both of which ended in employer victories. Meanwhile the Sōdōmei supported the long dispute at the Noda soy-sauce factory in 1927–1928, which also did not achieve union recognition.

It would be misleading to suggest a uniform division across every workplace. Many local unions affiliated with one federation or another for reasons of personality, region, or opportunity, and many workers cared more about their own wages than about the ideological position of their federation. Moreover, by the late 1920s, official data show that only a small fraction of Japan's industrial workers belonged to any union: Home Ministry tallies of the period counted a little over a quarter of a million union members, a small share of the total industrial workforce. The labor movement's influence thus exceeded its numbers, because it was concentrated in key industries and in public debate, but it should not be mistaken for a mass movement of all workers.

Section 69.12 · Division III — Tenant Farmers, Rural Disputes, and Agrarian Organization

Landownership, Tenancy, Agricultural Rent, and Rural Inequality

Rural Japan in the 1910s and 1920s was not a world of independent smallholders. Since the Meiji land-tax reform of the 1870s, land had become private property that could be bought, sold, mortgaged, and rented, and over the following decades a substantial share of cultivated land passed into the hands of landlords. By the early twentieth century close to half of Japan's cultivated land was farmed by tenants, according to standard estimates, and a large proportion of farm households rented at least part of the land they worked. Many families were part-owners and part-tenants, which meant that the line between "landlord" and "tenant" was not always sharp. Landlords themselves ranged from large proprietors who lived on rents and engaged in local politics or business to small owners who rented out a few plots. Chapter 73 will examine the structural economic crisis of the later 1920s; this chapter focuses on the tenant disputes and organizations of the preceding decade.

Rent was commonly paid in kind, in rice, and commonly amounted to roughly half the main crop, though rates varied by region, soil quality, and local custom. Tenants paid rent regardless of the harvest unless a landlord agreed to a reduction, and in poor years the process of crop inspection and rent negotiation was a flashpoint. Many tenancy arrangements were based on oral agreements and customary expectations, including the expectation that a family would continue to rent the same land across generations; landlords, for their part, could terminate a lease or refuse to renew it. Households also depended on supplementary income from sericulture, handicrafts, wage labor, and migration, and when silk prices or wages fell, tenants had fewer resources with which to meet rent. The wartime boom raised the price of rice and benefited landlords who received rent in kind, but it did not necessarily improve tenant incomes in proportion, and the price fluctuations of the early 1920s unsettled expectations.

Rural inequality was therefore different from industrial inequality. A tenant's grievance was usually directed not at a factory manager but at a landlord who might be a neighbor, a relative, a village official, or a member of a local elite with ties to local government and the dominant parties. The village was a community of overlapping obligations as well as a site of economic exploitation, and organized resistance risked disrupting social relationships on which tenants also depended. These characteristics help explain why tenant movements adopted tactics and arguments different from those of industrial unions.

A decision to join a tenant association was not only an economic calculation but a social risk, since landlords controlled access to land, credit, and favors. The expansion of schooling and military service also exposed younger men to ideas and organizations beyond the village. These pressures help explain why tenant protest gathered pace in the late 1910s and 1920s.

Section 69.13 · Division III

Tenant Disputes and the Development of Organized Rural Resistance

Tenancy disputes (kosaku sōgi) became a recognized feature of rural life in the late 1910s. Tenants petitioned landlords for rent reductions, especially in years of poor harvest, and some refused to deliver rent or withheld part of it. Landlords sometimes responded by threatening eviction or terminating leases, and some hired substitute tenants or sought the help of police and local officials. Official counts compiled by the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce recorded a few dozen disputes in the mid-1910s, hundreds by 1920, well over a thousand a year in the early 1920s, and more than two thousand in 1926, although these figures depend on how disputes were defined and reported and should be treated as indicators of a trend rather than precise totals.

Disputes took many forms. Some were negotiated within a village through the mediation of local notables. Others involved formal associations of tenants who drew up demands, elected leaders, and coordinated actions such as refusing to pay rent or to harvest the landlord's share. Some became prolonged struggles with litigation, police involvement, and the formation of landlord organizations in reply. Regional variation was significant. Disputes were common in the commercialized agricultural districts of the Kinki region (around Osaka and Kyoto), in parts of Okayama and other western Honshu prefectures, and in rice-producing districts of Niigata and other regions of northern and central Honshu, and they appeared more slowly in some districts of the northeast, where landlord dominance and different farming conditions shaped the pattern. Historians therefore caution against a generalized story of rural rebellion: many districts saw no organized dispute, and many disputes ended in compromise or in the tenants' defeat.

The social base of resistance also varied. Some of the strongest tenant movements emerged where tenants were relatively literate, commercially oriented, and connected to nearby towns, and where local organizers, including teachers, Christian reformers, former soldiers, and young men with experience outside the village, could provide leadership. Elsewhere, village hierarchy suppressed organization. Women appear in the sources as participants in demonstrations and in negotiations, though male household heads usually signed documents. The next section examines the national organization that emerged from these local struggles.

Section 69.14 · Division III

The Japan Farmers' Union of 1922: Sugiyama Motojirō and Kagawa Toyohiko

The Japan Farmers' Union (Nihon Nōmin Kumiai) was founded on April 9, 1922, in Kobe, according to the Ōhara Institute for Social Research's documentation, and its central organizers included Sugiyama Motojirō and Kagawa Toyohiko. Kagawa, a Christian social reformer who had worked among Kobe's urban poor and in the labor movement, brought organizational experience from cooperatives and unions. Sugiyama, an organizer of tenant associations, brought connections to rural disputes. The choice of Kobe reflected the concentration there of activists with ties to both industrial labor and rural districts in the nearby prefectures.

The union began as a federation of local tenant associations and individuals, with a program that combined cooperative self-help, demands for rent reductions and secure tenure, and support for broader political reform, including universal suffrage. In its early years it emphasized organization, education, and legal advice, but as disputes multiplied and landlords resisted, the union became more directly involved in contentious tenant advocacy, sending organizers and supporters to disputes and publicizing them. Its growth was rapid in the Kinki region and in other districts where local associations affiliated, and its membership grew into the tens of thousands during the 1920s, though membership figures depend on whether local organizations or individuals are counted. Like the industrial unions, it experienced ideological divisions, between cooperative reformers, socialists, and more radical activists, and it split in the mid-1920s, with different groups forming rival federations.

The Farmers' Union is significant for several reasons. It gave tenants a national voice and an organizational network that connected local disputes. It linked rural grievances to national politics at the moment when the franchise campaign reached its height. And it shows the variety of leadership within the reform movements: Christian reformers, socialists, and local tenant organizers worked side by side, sometimes uneasily. Its limitations were equally clear. Tenants had little legal protection, landlords had considerable local power, and the union depended on small payments from poor households. Section 69.15 turns to the countermeasures landlords and the state adopted.

Section 69.15 · Division III

Landlords, Police, Courts, and the Politics of Tenancy Reform

Landlords did not stand idle. In many districts they organized associations to coordinate responses to tenant demands, shared information about activists, and pressed prefectural and national officials to protect their interests. They used legal and extralegal tools: eviction suits, the withdrawal of leases, social pressure, and sometimes the hiring of substitute tenants. Landlords also had influence over local government, including village councils, tax collection, and local police, and landlords were among the most important supporters of the major parties in rural constituencies, especially the Seiyūkai. When tenants collectively withheld rent, landlords could seek court enforcement, and the civil law of the time, which protected property rights and offered tenants little security of tenure, generally favored them.

Government officials, for their part, increasingly viewed tenancy conflict as a threat to rural stability. Some bureaucrats in the Ministry of Agriculture and Commerce and the Home Ministry believed that moderate reform could preserve social order by reducing the worst abuses of tenancy, and they worried that unresolved disputes would feed radicalism. This reasoning led to the Farm Tenancy Conciliation Law (Kosaku Chōtei Hō) of 1924. The law created a procedure under which local courts could appoint conciliation committees to mediate disputes between landlords and tenants. Mediation was meant to encourage compromise and to channel disputes away from direct action. It did not, however, give tenants new legal rights, such as security of tenure or a guaranteed rent level, and it relied on the willingness of landlords to participate. Efforts to pass a more comprehensive tenancy law in the 1920s did not succeed.

The effects of the conciliation system were mixed. Tenants did use the committees, and many disputes were resolved through them, sometimes with rent reductions or other concessions. But the process also gave landlords and officials a way to manage conflict without altering the underlying relationship, and it put pressure on tenant organizations to accept mediation rather than direct action. Radical tenant leaders criticized the system as a means of co-opting resistance. Historians generally interpret the law as an example of how the interwar state combined social reform with social control: it addressed a real problem while keeping rural property relations largely intact.

Section 69.16 · Division III

Urban and Rural Collective Action Compared

Urban industrial workers and rural tenants often spoke of one another as natural allies. Both groups lived with insecurity, both faced employers or landlords who held property and legal advantage, and both supported the extension of the franchise as a means of obtaining legislative remedies. Organizers such as Kagawa Toyohiko worked in both settings, and the Sōdōmei and the Farmers' Union cooperated in support of political campaigns and, from 1925–1926, in the formation of labor–farmer parties (Section 69.35). Cooperation had practical and symbolic appeal. A workers' movement allied with farmers could claim to represent the majority of the population, and political leaders in both camps recognized that a party based on a single group would be weak.

But cooperation was difficult. The economic relationships differed: a factory worker sold labor for wages to a firm, while a tenant paid rent in kind to a landlord and managed his own farm. Their bargaining power also differed. A strike of shipyard workers could halt production, while a rent strike by tenants threatened only the landlord's income and could be answered by eviction or the hire of replacements. Workers lived in concentrated settings in which organizers could easily meet them; tenants were dispersed across villages, where social hierarchy discouraged open dissent. Workers' organizations could draw on dues from wages, while tenants often had very little cash. Leaders frequently came from different backgrounds, urban intellectuals and Christians in one case and local organizers and teachers in the other. And the families concerned had different interests in specific policy questions, such as rice prices, on which urban consumers and rural producers could conflict.

Section 69.17 · Division IV — Socialist Thought, Radical Movements, and Competing Strategies for Reform

The Socialist Inheritance: Repression, Reform, and Political Reorganization

Japanese socialism had a brief public life in the early 1900s, with the Social Democratic Party of 1901 (banned almost immediately), the antiwar writing of Kōtoku Shūsui and Sakai Toshihiko during the Russo-Japanese War, and a short-lived socialist party in 1906–1907. After 1907 the government tightened controls, and in 1910 police arrested many anarchists and socialists on charges of plotting to assassinate the emperor. In the High Treason Incident, as it is known, twenty-four defendants were sentenced to death in January 1911 in a closed trial, and twelve were executed, including Kōtoku Shūsui and Kanno Suga. Most historians conclude that a small group had discussed an assassination plot, and that the state used the case to eliminate Kōtoku and to frighten the broader movement. The affair deepened a climate of fear around radical politics and prompted the creation of the Special Higher Police.

The period from 1911 to the mid-1910s is often called the "winter years" of Japanese socialism. Survivors such as Sakai Toshihiko and Yamakawa Hitoshi continued to write, often through commercial publishing ventures, and the anarchists Ōsugi Sakae and Arahata Kanson published a journal, Kindai Shisō ("Modern Thought"), from 1912 to 1914. But open political organization was nearly impossible. The reemergence of socialism after 1917–1918 was linked to several developments: the Russian Revolution, the labor unrest of the war years, the Rice Riots, and the energy of university students and young intellectuals. In December 1920 socialists, anarchists, and union activists formed the Nihon Shakaishugi Dōmei (Japan Socialist League), a broad coalition that the government dissolved in 1921. Its brief existence showed both that there was a constituency for socialist politics and that the state would not tolerate an open organization of this kind.

This inheritance shaped the left of the 1920s. Leaders of the older generation brought experience but also scars. Younger activists, many from universities, were influenced by new theoretical currents, and they inherited a movement that had to operate in the shadow of surveillance. The question of how to build a movement that could survive was central, and the answers differed sharply, as the following sections show.

Section 69.18 · Division IV

Anarchism, Syndicalism, and Ōsugi Sakae

Ōsugi Sakae (1885–1923) was the most prominent anarchist writer and activist of the Taishō years. He had been imprisoned several times and was spared the High Treason Incident only because he was already in jail on another charge. After 1912 he translated and wrote widely, advocating a philosophy that stressed individual autonomy, workers' self-organization, and distrust of both the state and political parties. In the early 1920s he was associated with journals devoted to labor affairs and with groups of syndicalist workers, especially in Tokyo and Osaka. Syndicalism, the strategy of building revolutionary unions that could take control of production through direct action and general strikes, rejected parliamentary politics as a distraction and often as a trap.

The anarchists' critique of parliamentary reform was pointed: voting would not change the structure of economic power, and entering electoral politics would draw workers into the state's framework and into compromise. This argument had real force in a country where the franchise was restricted and where the Diet was not sovereign. But the anarchists also faced weaknesses. They lacked the resources of larger federations, and they came into conflict with socialists who favored organization and with the Bolsheviks, who emphasized discipline and party leadership. In the "anarchist–Bolshevik dispute" of 1922–1923, the two currents clashed over unions and over the relationship between spontaneity and leadership, and the dispute weakened both. Anarcho-syndicalist influence persisted in some unions and in later federations, but it did not become the dominant force in the movement.

Ōsugi's death illustrates the dangers faced by radicals. On September 16, 1923, in the chaotic aftermath of the Great Kantō Earthquake, military police led by Captain Amakasu Masahiko arrested Ōsugi, his partner Itō Noe, and his young nephew and killed all three. The killings were exposed, and Amakasu was convicted by a military court but served a relatively short sentence. The incident was a case of state violence against known dissidents; the broader context of the earthquake, the massacre of Koreans and other violence, and the reconstruction of Tokyo belongs to Chapter 71, and here it is important only as a consequence for the movements discussed in this chapter. It removed a leading anarchist voice and heightened the sense that radical politics carried mortal danger.

Section 69.19 · Division IV

Marxism, Yamakawa Hitoshi, and the Clandestine Communist Movement

Marxist ideas gained ground among Japanese intellectuals and labor activists after 1917. Translations of Marx and Lenin circulated, student societies discussed them, and economists and sociologists drew on them to analyze Japanese capitalism. Yamakawa Hitoshi (1880–1958) became one of the most influential Marxist writers. In an essay of 1922 he urged the movement to shift its focus from small groups of committed radicals to the masses of workers, a reorientation often described as "a change of direction." His position, which favored working with legal organizations while building a political movement, differed from the more doctrinaire positions of some other communists and from the anarchists' rejection of political parties.

The Japanese Communist Party was founded in July 1922 as a clandestine organization affiliated with the Communist International (Comintern). Its founders included veterans of the earlier socialist movement such as Sakai Toshihiko and Yamakawa Hitoshi, along with younger activists, and the Comintern played an important role in its creation and direction. Because the party was illegal, its membership was small and its activities secret, and its history is difficult to reconstruct. Police uncovered the party in 1923 in what is called the First Communist Party Incident, arresting dozens of members, and in 1924 the party was dissolved by its own leaders, in part because of the disagreements over the strategies Yamakawa favored. It was reconstituted in late 1926 and came under the influence of theories associated with Fukumoto Kazuo, which emphasized a sharp separation of true Marxists from reformists, before the Comintern replaced that line in 1927 with new instructions.

It is crucial to distinguish three institutions: the clandestine Communist Party, the trade-union federations (such as the Sōdōmei and the Hyōgikai), and the legal labor–farmer parties. They overlapped in membership and sometimes in strategy, especially in the later 1920s when communists sought to influence the Hyōgikai and the Rōdō Nōmintō. But they were not identical, and treating them as one hides the fact that most union members, tenant farmers, and voters for proletarian parties were not communists. The government, however, tended to blur these lines, treating contact with communists as a pretext to suppress legal organizations, a pattern that culminated in the events of 1928.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Socialism, Syndicalism, Suffrage, and Reform

Shakai minshūshugi (social democracy) referred to socialist politics pursued through parliamentary participation and legal reform; its Japanese advocates, such as Abe Isoo, differed from communists. Sandikarizumu (syndicalism) emphasized unions as the means of revolutionary change through direct action. Museifushugi (anarchism) rejected the state. Kyōsanshugi (communism) in this context referred to the Marxist-Leninist approach affiliated with the Comintern. Fusen (a contraction of futsū senkyo) means universal suffrage; in the 1920s it normally meant universal male suffrage, and "fujin sanseiken" meant women's political rights. Kokutai ("national polity") was a contested term used by the state to refer to the emperor-centered political order, and its use in the Peace Preservation Law gave the state a flexible legal weapon. Musan seitō ("proletarian party") referred to parties claiming to represent workers and tenants.

Students should avoid assuming that modern English categories such as "left," "liberal," or "socialist" map neatly onto these Japanese movements. Many reformers who supported labor legislation did not consider themselves socialists, and many socialists distrusted both liberalism and communism.

Section 69.20 · Division IV

Socialist Women, Feminist Debates, and the Politics of Class

Socialist women formed a distinctive current within the reform movements. Yamakawa Kikue (1890–1980) became the most influential theorist of socialist feminism. She argued that women's emancipation could not be separated from the abolition of class inequality and criticized middle-class feminism for ignoring the situation of working women. In the "maternal protection debate" of 1918–1919, in which Hiratsuka Raichō, Yosano Akiko, and Yamakawa Kikue disagreed about whether women should seek state support for motherhood or economic independence, Yamakawa emphasized the importance of structural economic change. Itō Noe (1895–1923), who had edited the feminist journal Seitō and later became an anarchist, was another leading figure, known for her writing on freedom, marriage, and women's autonomy.

In April 1921 a group of socialist women including Yamakawa Kikue, Itō Noe, and Sakai Magara founded the Sekirankai ("Red Wave Society"), the first organization of socialist women in Japan. The society's activities were modest and short-lived, in part because of police pressure and the dissolution of the broader socialist coalition. But it brought a socialist critique into the public conversation about women's rights. Its members argued that women of the working classes needed economic and social changes more than the vote alone, and they were skeptical of suffrage organizations led by middle-class women, though they did not necessarily oppose women's political rights as such.

These debates should be distinguished from those of liberal suffrage organizations such as the New Women's Association and the Women's Suffrage League, discussed in Division VI. Liberal and Christian reformers generally believed that winning political rights would give women a voice on social problems. Socialist feminists generally believed that the vote would be an instrument but that deeper transformations were needed. Both groups criticized the limits of existing law, and they sometimes cooperated, but differences in class position, religion, and strategy complicated alliances. The conflict also reflected the broader debate in the labor movement about whether the franchise was a means or an end.

Section 69.21 · Division IV

Reform, Revolution, Internationalism, and the Limits of Political Alliance

The currents described above can be compared by their answers to three questions: how should power be changed, what role should the state play, and what is the proper form of organization. Social reformers and social democrats believed that existing institutions could be improved through legislation, negotiated agreements, and parliamentary participation. Anarchists and syndicalists believed that institutions of the state and of capitalism had to be bypassed or destroyed through direct action. Communists believed in a disciplined party leading a revolutionary transformation. None of these positions was held by every member of any organization, and individuals moved among them as circumstances changed.

International influences were also important. Japanese labor leaders engaged with the International Labour Organization, which provided both a forum and a source of standards, and some looked to the British Labour Party or the German Social Democratic Party as models. Communists looked to Moscow and the Comintern, which sent instructions, money, and ideological directives. Anarchists drew on European and American anarchist thought and on Chinese and Korean contacts. State authorities, by contrast, followed these connections closely and used them to justify surveillance. The government's concern about "foreign" ideas coexisted with its own selective borrowing of foreign social policy, such as the 1922 Health Insurance Law modeled partly on European precedents.

The limits of political alliance were evident throughout the 1920s. Cooperation was often possible on specific goals, such as suffrage, but fractured over strategy and doctrine. State surveillance and repression complicated matters, because informers and arrests created suspicion and because the line between legal and illegal activity was unclear. Internal disputes were not merely personal squabbles; they reflected genuine disagreements about how to achieve change in a political system that offered limited channels for dissent. The chapter's next divisions return to the legal and electoral arena in which those disagreements were tested.

Historical Debate

Reformism or Militancy: Which Strategy Served Labor Better?

The question: Historians have long debated whether the moderate, negotiation-oriented strategy of the Sōdōmei leadership or the more confrontational approach of left-wing unions was better suited to winning gains for workers in the 1920s.

Interpretation A — Reformist strategy as pragmatic: Some scholars argue that in a legal environment hostile to unions and in a state capable of repression, gradual organization, negotiation, and legislation offered the most realistic path. They point to the successes of the Sōdōmei in building a durable federation, to the role of union pressure in producing the 1926 Conciliation Law, and to the heavy costs of defeat in strikes such as Kobe in 1921.
Interpretation B — Militancy as necessary pressure: Other scholars argue that the state and employers conceded little unless pressed. They emphasize that militant disputes and the threat of radicalism pushed officials to enact reform, as the Home Ministry's interest in social legislation suggests, and that reformist leaders often compromised without winning union recognition.

Evidence: Both sides use strike outcomes, union membership data, the content and timing of legislation, police and ministry reports, and the writings of participants. Scholars such as Andrew Gordon, Stephen Large, and Sheldon Garon have examined how state policy, employers' strategies, and union choices interacted. Most recent work stresses that the strategies were interdependent rather than strictly alternative.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians judge both the labor movement's achievements and the causes of its fragmentation, and whether the failure to secure a comprehensive trade-union law reflects tactical errors or structural constraints.

Section 69.22 · Division V — The Universal Male Suffrage Movement and the Struggle over the Electorate

From Property-Qualified Voting to the Demand for Universal Male Suffrage

The Meiji Constitution established an elected House of Representatives but left the definition of the electorate to a separate election law. The first election law of 1889 limited the vote to men aged twenty-five and older who paid at least fifteen yen in direct national taxes, which gave the vote to roughly 450,000 men, about one percent of the population. The law of 1900 lowered the threshold to ten yen and approximately doubled the electorate, to a little under a million, or roughly two percent. In 1919 the Hara cabinet lowered the qualification to three yen and replaced large electoral districts with small, mostly single-member districts, which raised the electorate to about three million, around five and a half percent of the population. These figures are standard estimates; precise numbers vary by source and by how eligibility and registration were counted. Hara and his party argued that the reform would favor stable, propertied voters, especially in rural districts where landlords and local notables were strong, and that it would strengthen the Seiyūkai.

Advocacy of universal male suffrage had a long history. Liberals and early socialists campaigned for it in the 1890s and 1900s, associations were formed, and bills were introduced in the Diet. According to standard accounts, a bill passed the House of Representatives in 1911 but died in the House of Peers. The case for universal suffrage rested on several arguments: that taxation should not be the measure of political capacity; that the war and industrial expansion had made workers essential to national life; that military service was borne by men regardless of tax status; and that exclusion of workers from the electorate fostered resentment and radicalism. Opponents argued that universal suffrage was premature, that uneducated voters could be manipulated, that it would threaten the national polity, and that it would open the way to socialism.

By the late 1910s the tax qualification looked increasingly arbitrary. A skilled worker in a Kobe shipyard might earn more than a small landowner who qualified, yet he lacked the vote because he paid little direct tax. Tenants, who paid rent rather than land tax, and the growing urban middle class of salaried employees were also excluded. In an industrializing society where income was increasingly earned through wages and salaries, a franchise defined by direct tax payment excluded a large share of adult men by design. These tensions fed the coalition of the early 1920s examined in the next section.

Section 69.23 · Division V

The Nationwide Universal Manhood Suffrage Federation of 1920

In January 1920 supporters of universal male suffrage formed the Zenkoku Fusen Rengōkai, the Nationwide Universal Manhood Suffrage Federation. The National Diet Library's archival materials document its formation and identify a resolution adopted on February 22, 1920. The federation was a coalition. It included trade unions, including the Yūaikai, student groups, journalists, academics, and supporters from the opposition parties, among them members of the Kenseikai and the smaller Kokumintō, and politicians such as Ozaki Yukio and Inukai Tsuyoshi were prominent advocates. The coalition combined groups with different political outlooks: moderate reformers who saw suffrage as a step toward constitutional government, labor leaders who saw it as a means to pursue legislation, and socialists who disagreed over whether parliamentary participation was worthwhile.

The campaign's methods included public meetings, parades, petitions, and newspaper publicity. In February 1920 large demonstrations took place in Tokyo while opposition parties introduced suffrage bills in the Diet. The Kokumintō offered a bill for universal manhood suffrage, while the Kenseikai proposed a more limited measure that would extend the vote to men who were financially independent. Prime Minister Hara Takashi rejected immediate adoption, argued that the country was not yet ready, and dissolved the House of Representatives at the end of February. In the general election in May, the Seiyūkai won a commanding majority. The result was a setback for the campaign, since it showed that the government could use the restricted electorate to defeat a proposal to enlarge it.

The 1920 federation did not end the movement, but its history is instructive. It showed that broad coalitions could mobilize public attention but also that the issue divided parties, since the Seiyūkai opposed immediate reform and the Kenseikai wavered on the extent of reform. It also showed that labor and student groups could make suffrage a mass cause. And it raised a question that would recur: whether the popular movement or the party leaders, who could actually pass legislation, were the decisive force.

Section 69.24 · Division V

Party Competition and the Suffrage Question, 1919–1924

The positions of the parties on suffrage were shaped by electoral calculation as much as by principle. The Seiyūkai, which relied heavily on support in rural districts and on alliances with landlords and local notables, feared that a mass electorate would dilute its advantage and might empower labor and tenant movements. Hara's 1919 reform, with its small districts and modest tax threshold, was designed to expand the electorate carefully while preserving the party's base. The Kenseikai, whose support was stronger among urban businessmen, professionals, and some officials, was more open to extension of the franchise, but its leaders were cautious about radicalism and, in 1920, favored a limited measure. The smaller Kokumintō, led by Inukai Tsuyoshi, supported universal suffrage more consistently but lacked the numbers to enact it.

After Hara's assassination in November 1921, a series of short-lived cabinets (Chapter 67) struggled with questions of the franchise, military spending, and the aftermath of the 1923 earthquake. The cabinet of Yamamoto Gonnohyōe, formed in September 1923, reportedly considered a suffrage reform but fell after the Toranomon Incident of December 1923, an attempt on the life of the Prince Regent by a young man with radical sympathies. The subsequent Kiyoura cabinet, composed largely of members of the House of Peers, provoked a political backlash. The Kenseikai, the Rikken Seiyūkai led by Takahashi Korekiyo, and the Kakushin Kurabu (Reform Club) formed the pro-constitution three-party alliance in the second "movement to protect the constitution" and won the May 1924 election. The Seiyū Hontō was a separate organization associated with support for the Kiyoura cabinet, not a member of that constitutional-protection coalition. The resulting cabinet, headed by Katō Takaaki, committed itself to electoral reform.

The parties' record shows that party leaders accepted reform when it served their interests and when pressure was sufficient. The popular movement had raised the issue, kept it on the agenda, and made opposition costly; but the final decision depended on a parliamentary alliance and on negotiation with the House of Peers. Tensions between reform principle and party calculation remained: some party leaders also saw universal male suffrage as a way to bring workers into legal politics and thereby reduce radicalism, an argument that links the franchise to the repressive measures discussed in Division VII.

Section 69.25 · Division V

Demonstrations, Petitions, Newspapers, and the Popular Suffrage Campaign

The suffrage campaign used the full repertoire of popular politics available in the 1920s. Organizers held mass meetings in public halls and parks, led processions through city streets, collected signatures on petitions to the Diet, circulated pamphlets and handbills, and wrote for newspapers and magazines. The rapid expansion of the daily press, with circulation of the largest papers reaching the hundreds of thousands, gave the campaign a platform, and editors and journalists were among its most active supporters. The campaign also drew on lecture meetings at which professors, politicians, and labor leaders presented arguments to audiences of students, workers, and townspeople.

Suffrage organizers framed the franchise as a matter of political dignity and economic interest. They argued that men who bore the burdens of work, taxation in the form of consumption and rent, and military service deserved a voice in government, and that political rights would allow workers and tenants to press for social legislation. Some emphasized loyalty to the emperor and national unity, presenting universal suffrage as consistent with the constitution; others emphasized class interest. The diversity of rationales helped hold the coalition together but also created ambiguity about what the franchise would accomplish.

Historians should distinguish movement leaders from the broader public they claimed to represent. Organizers were often students, journalists, intellectuals, and union officials, who had more time, education, and organizational capacity than most workers or tenants. How many ordinary people supported universal suffrage actively is difficult to say; crowd estimates and petition counts are uncertain, and enthusiasm for the cause in a particular rally does not necessarily show durable commitment. At the same time, the extent of public meetings and the attention of the press show that the issue was a matter of national debate. The campaign's tone was also gendered: it was a campaign for male suffrage, and its leaders generally did not treat women's political rights as part of the same agenda, a point that Division VI takes up.

Section 69.26 · Division V

The Universal Male Suffrage Law of 1925: Provisions, Exclusions, and Consequences

The Diet passed the revised House of Representatives Election Law in March 1925, and it was promulgated on May 5, 1925. The law abolished the direct-tax qualification, so that all male subjects aged twenty-five or older who met residency requirements could vote, and it set the minimum age for candidates at thirty. It required a period of residence, generally one year, in the municipality. It also changed the structure of constituencies: the small single-member districts of 1919 were replaced by medium-sized districts electing three to five members each, with voters casting a single vote, a system that tended to make it easier for smaller parties to win seats. The law imposed a deposit on candidates, forfeited if a candidate failed to win a minimum share of votes, a significant burden for poorly funded proletarian candidates, and it restricted campaign practices, including a ban on door-to-door canvassing.

The word "universal" had to be understood with qualification. Women were excluded, as Division VI explains. Men who were receiving poor relief were excluded, as were certain others, such as those lacking a fixed residence or those disqualified for specified reasons, and active-duty military personnel did not vote. The law applied to the metropole; residents of the colonies of Korea and Taiwan, and of Karafuto (southern Sakhalin), had no representation in the Diet, although Korean and Taiwanese men who lived in Japan proper and met the residency requirements could in principle vote and stand for election. Because the 1925 reform was limited in these ways, it is more accurate to describe it as universal adult male suffrage for those in the metropole who met the conditions, rather than as a general franchise.

The consequences were large. The electorate rose from roughly three million to over twelve million, about a fifth of the population, and the parties had to compete for a mass constituency. The reform altered the strategic situation of the proletarian parties, which for the first time had a prospect of winning seats, and of the established parties, which had to build organizations capable of reaching millions of new voters. Yet the Diet enacted this law in the same legislative session as the Peace Preservation Law (Section 69.33), and the National Diet Library's archival history of the period emphasizes that the two measures belong together. The first national election under the new law was not held until February 1928, which is why the chapter continues through that election rather than ending in 1925.

Section 69.27 · Division V

Preparing a Mass Electorate, 1925–1927: Voter Education and Political Mobilization

Between the passage of the law and the first election three years later, parties, officials, and civic groups tried to prepare for a mass electorate. Election manuals explained the new rules for registration and voting, and officials and civic organizations urged voters to cast their ballots without bribery or coercion. Newspapers published guides, explained the districts, and discussed candidates. Public lectures and local meetings sought to educate new voters, and youth associations and reservist organizations, long used for local mobilization, became part of the effort. The Home Ministry played a central role in organizing elections and also, as the 1928 election would show, in attempting to influence them.

For the parties, the new situation required new organization. Both the Seiyūkai and the Kenseikai (which in 1927 merged with a faction of the Seiyū Hontō to form the Rikken Minseitō) expanded local branches, cultivated local notables, and sought to reach new groups of voters, including urban wage earners, shopkeepers, and rural tenants. Political advertising, posters, handbills, and speech meetings multiplied. Radio broadcasting, which began in Japan in 1925, was in its early years and played a limited role, but newspapers and magazines reached an increasingly national audience. The campaign restrictions, notably the limits on canvassing, shaped how parties could approach voters.

The proletarian parties faced special difficulties. They had little money, and the candidate deposit and campaign restrictions weighed heavily on them. Their organizations were divided (Section 69.35), and they faced the possibility of police interference. Yet they also saw a historic opportunity, since the new electorate included hundreds of thousands of workers and tenants who had never voted. Preparations in these years thus combined optimism and apprehension. The actual results of the 1928 election are the subject of Section 69.36.

Section 69.28 · Division VI — Women's Political Rights and the Boundaries of Reform

From the New Women's Association to the Demand for Enfranchisement

Chapter 67 described the New Women's Association (Shin Fujin Kyōkai), founded in 1920 by Hiratsuka Raichō, Ichikawa Fusae, and Oku Mumeo, and its campaign to amend Article 5 of the Public Peace Police Law, which barred women from attending political meetings and joining political associations. That campaign achieved a partial success in 1922, when the law was amended to allow women to attend political meetings; women were still barred from joining political parties. The campaign taught women activists the techniques of lobbying, petitioning, and public speaking, and it created a network of organizers. It also showed that legal restrictions on women's political participation could be changed through sustained organization.

The demand for the vote followed logically but required a different campaign. Meeting rights concerned the ability to attend political gatherings; suffrage concerned the right to vote and to stand for office. Women's organizations of various kinds, among them Christian reform societies such as the Japan Women's Christian Temperance Union and professional women's groups, had long discussed political rights, and the 1920s saw these discussions become more explicit. The movement for universal male suffrage was a catalyst: as the electorate for men widened, the exclusion of women became harder to treat as natural. At the same time, the male suffrage campaign did not treat women's inclusion as part of its agenda, and many male reformers regarded women's suffrage as a later or separate question.

The transition from meeting rights to enfranchisement thus involved a change in aims and in organizations. In the mid-1920s, a new association, described in the next section, assumed leadership of the campaign. Its history shows how women activists built on the experience of earlier groups while adopting a more focused goal and a more national scope.

The 1922 reform also needs to be read within a precise hierarchy of political rights. The legal ability to attend and sponsor a political meeting was not equivalent to membership in a political party, the right to nominate a candidate, or eligibility to cast a parliamentary ballot. Activists could become experienced public speakers and organizers while remaining excluded from the institution whose legislation they wanted to influence. This institutional contradiction gave women's suffrage advocates a practical argument: organized women could petition legislators and debate public policy, yet had no direct electoral means to reward or dismiss those who governed. Their campaign consequently addressed both legal discrimination and the legitimacy of a legislature speaking for citizens while excluding half the adult population from its electorate.

Section 69.29 · Division VI

Ichikawa Fusae and the Women's Suffrage League of 1924

Ichikawa Fusae (1893–1981) had been a teacher and journalist before joining the New Women's Association. She spent time in the United States between 1921 and 1924, where she observed American suffrage organizations and labor reform, and after returning she worked at the Tokyo office of the International Labour Organization. In December 1924 she joined other women, among them Kubushiro Ochimi of the Christian temperance movement, in founding a women's suffrage organization that was soon known as the Women's Suffrage League (Fusen Kakutoku Dōmei, "League for the Attainment of Women's Suffrage"). The Ichikawa Fusae Memorial Association's Center for Women and Politics dates the League's founding to 1924, and scholarly research places the organizational launch in December. The League's early leaders included both secular and Christian women, and its membership was drawn largely from educated, urban, middle-class women, along with teachers and professionals.

The League's methods resembled those of the broader suffrage movement but were adapted to women's circumstances. Members gathered signatures on petitions, lobbied members of the Diet, organized conferences and lectures, issued statements, and cultivated relationships with sympathetic politicians and journalists. Women could not vote or join parties, so the League's influence depended on persuasion and on the willingness of male legislators to introduce bills. Its arguments for women's political rights combined several themes: equality before the law; the idea that women's experience as mothers and household managers qualified them to contribute to public affairs; and the belief that women's participation would help address social problems such as child welfare, public health, and the regulation of prostitution. These arguments sometimes overlapped with prevailing ideals of women as "good wives and wise mothers" and sometimes challenged them.

The League did not win the vote in the 1920s. Bills for limited women's suffrage, such as municipal suffrage, were introduced in the Diet in the late 1920s and early 1930s, and one passed the House of Representatives in 1931 but was rejected by the House of Peers. Full women's suffrage came only after 1945, as Chapter 84 will explain. Nevertheless, the League had created a national movement, trained a generation of activists, and kept the issue in public debate during a period when the political system was moving in other directions.

Section 69.30 · Division VI

Class, Employment, and Ideological Differences within Women's Reform Movements

Women's reform in the 1920s was not a single movement. Liberal suffragists such as Ichikawa Fusae focused on winning political rights through lobbying and public education. Christian reformers, many of them associated with the Women's Christian Temperance Union and related societies, combined suffrage with campaigns against licensed prostitution, alcohol, and the double standard in marriage. Socialist feminists such as Yamakawa Kikue argued that class inequality lay at the root of women's subordination. Women workers, who experienced low pay and rigid discipline, had interests that overlapped with all of these groups but also distinct priorities, such as limits on night work and protection against dismissal. Professional women, among them teachers, physicians, and journalists, sought access to education and careers, and some saw the vote as part of a larger agenda of professional recognition.

There was cooperation across these lines. Women from different groups joined in petitions, protest meetings, and committees, and issues such as protective labor legislation for women and the regulation of prostitution drew wide interest. But divisions were also real. Socialist women criticized liberal suffragists for ignoring class; liberals feared that association with socialism would damage the cause. Christian reformers and secular activists disagreed about moral questions. Women workers were rarely leaders of suffrage organizations, and middle-class women sometimes spoke for working women without consulting them. The organizational weakness of women's labor activism, noted in Section 69.10, contributed to this imbalance.

Historians should therefore resist presenting women's activism as a single organization or a unified perspective. The suffrage campaign was important, but it was one strand among several. Treating women's labor organizing and women's suffrage organizing as connected but not identical helps explain why women who shared the goal of political rights could differ on strategy, priorities, and alliances.

Section 69.31 · Division VI

Who Remained Excluded? Gender, Poverty, Discrimination, and Empire

Even after the reform of 1925, large categories of people remained outside the electorate. Women, about half of the adult population, had no vote, no right to stand for office, and, until the 1922 amendment, no right even to attend political meetings. The poor were excluded in a distinct way: men receiving poor relief were barred from voting, a rule that tied citizenship to economic independence and suggested that dependency was a kind of political disqualification. Men without fixed residence or who moved frequently, a situation characteristic of itinerant laborers, also faced barriers. Soldiers on active duty were excluded.

Discrimination in social life also limited political participation. Communities classified as Buraku, descendants of groups once placed in outcast statuses (see Chapter 43), continued to face discrimination in marriage, employment, and daily life even after the legal abolition of status categories in 1871. In March 1922, activists founded the Suiheisha (the Levelers' Society) in Kyoto, a national organization that demanded an end to discrimination and asserted pride and equality. Its declaration is a major document in the history of human-rights movements in Japan. The Suiheisha's relationship with labor and tenant organizations was close in some regions, since many Buraku households were also tenants or laborers, and it participated in the broader movement for social reform, though it was a distinct movement with its own priorities. Men in Buraku communities who met the qualifications could vote after 1925, but social discrimination and economic marginality shaped how they could use that right.

The distinction between metropolitan representation and colonial political rights was equally important. Residents of Korea and Taiwan were subjects of the empire but had no representatives in the Imperial Diet. In Korea, the March First Movement of 1919 had demonstrated mass opposition to Japanese rule, and the colonial government responded with a mix of limited reform and continued repression. In Taiwan, activists associated with the Taiwan Cultural Association and with leaders such as Lin Hsien-t'ang petitioned the Imperial Diet repeatedly from 1921 for the creation of a Taiwanese legislative body, without success. Korean and Taiwanese men living in Japan proper could vote if they met the residency requirements, and Koreans formed a growing community of workers in Osaka, Tokyo, and other industrial cities, where they faced wage discrimination and often hostile treatment. Chapter 62 treats colonial rule in Korea, and later chapters examine the history of Zainichi Koreans. For present purposes, the point is that the "universal" electorate of 1925 was a metropolitan, male, economically self-supporting electorate, set within an empire in which millions of subjects had no vote at all. Ainu people in Hokkaido and Okinawans were formally included in the electorate on the same terms as other metropolitan men, but their incorporation into the national political system had been recent and unequal, and the effects of assimilation policy shaped their political voice.

Section 69.32 · Division VII — Political Parties, Legal Reform, Repression, and the First Mass Election

The Seiyūkai, Kenseikai, and the Political Calculation of Reform

The two major parties of the 1920s, the Seiyūkai and the Kenseikai, had different social bases but shared an interest in managing the political consequences of a larger electorate. The Seiyūkai, the older of the two, had built its strength through alliances with local notables, landlords, and the promise of public works such as railways, ports, and roads for rural districts. The Kenseikai drew support from urban business interests, professionals, and bureaucrats, and it had cultivated a reputation for administrative reform and fiscal discipline. Both parties had ties to large business: the Seiyūkai to Mitsui and others, the Kenseikai to Mitsubishi interests, as scholars have long noted. Neither was a labor party. Cabinet history is covered in Chapter 67; the concern here is how the parties responded to demands from workers, farmers, and new voters.

Their responses combined calculation, conviction, and administrative initiative. In the early 1920s, governments enacted measures that responded to labor and rural unrest in limited ways: the Health Insurance Law of 1922 (enforced only in 1927 because of delays following the earthquake), amendments to the Factory Law, and the Farm Tenancy Conciliation Law of 1924. The Home Ministry developed a social-affairs bureaucracy, and some officials argued that moderate reform would reduce the appeal of radicalism. At the same time, party politicians understood that the new electorate would include millions of wage earners and tenants. Both major parties added labor and social-policy planks to their platforms, though they remained cautious about legal recognition of unions and about measures that would disturb landlords or employers.

The party organizations also had to adapt. They created or strengthened local branches, recruited candidates who could appeal to broader constituencies, and invested in publicity. Some local party figures built relations with moderate labor or tenant leaders, while others viewed any organized movement as a threat. The parties' strategies suggest that the franchise reform did not simply produce new democratic institutions; it set off competition among older parties and new movements over who would organize the new voters.

Section 69.33 · Division VII

The Peace Preservation Law of 1925 and the Criminalization of Radical Politics

The Peace Preservation Law (Chian Ijihō) was promulgated on April 22, 1925, in the same legislative session that produced the electoral reform. It made it a crime to organize an association with the aim of altering the national polity (kokutai) or of denying the system of private property, and it made it a crime to join such an association knowingly. The original penalty was imprisonment at hard labor of up to ten years. The law was a successor to an earlier proposal, a bill for controlling "radical social movements" that had failed in the Diet in 1922 after opposition from many quarters. In 1925, the government's arguments included concern about communist propaganda following the establishment of diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union in January 1925, the experience of the 1923 earthquake period, and the expectation that an enlarged electorate would increase the opportunities for radical agitation. Officials in the Katō cabinet, including those in the Kenseikai, presented the law as directed at genuinely revolutionary conspiracy and not at legitimate reform movements.

The law's key weakness, from the perspective of civil liberties, lay in the vagueness of its central term. Kokutai had no precise legal definition, and prosecutors and courts could interpret it expansively. The law was extended to Korea and Taiwan by imperial ordinance shortly after its enactment, and it became a major instrument for suppressing independence movements as well as communism. Its first major application in the metropole was the Kyoto Gakuren Incident, which began in late 1925 with the arrest of students and others associated with a social-science student federation, and which led to prosecutions in 1926. After the mass arrests of March 1928, the law was amended in 1928, and an emergency ordinance raised the maximum penalty to death, a step discussed in Chapter 74.

The relationship between the franchise and the Peace Preservation Law has been central to debates about Taishō democracy. Some contemporaries and later historians have argued that the two were linked by a political bargain: conservatives, including members of the House of Peers and officials in the Home Ministry, would accept universal male suffrage if the state acquired tools to contain radicalism. Others argue that the measures arose from different considerations and were enacted by overlapping but not identical coalitions. It would be wrong to claim that every supporter of suffrage endorsed the repressive law; many liberals, journalists, and labor leaders opposed or criticized it. But it would also be wrong to treat the two as unrelated. They emerged from the same cabinet and the same Diet session, and together they defined the boundaries within which the expanded electorate could operate.

Section 69.34 · Division VII

The Labor Disputes Conciliation Law of 1926 and the Limits of Social Legislation

In 1926 the Diet enacted the Labor Disputes Conciliation Law (Rōdō Sōgi Chōtei Hō), which created official procedures for conciliation of labor disputes, with special provisions for disputes in public-utility industries. In the same legislative effort, the government replaced the strike-related provisions of Article 17 of the Public Peace Police Law, which were repealed, with a separate statute punishing violent acts and threats by groups. The repeal removed a legal instrument that had long been a symbol of labor's grievance. The Ōhara Institute's historians and others have documented this legislation and its relation to earlier proposals.

The new framework was important but limited. Conciliation procedures established a state-supervised forum for resolving disputes, but its formal use was exceptionally limited: the Ōhara Institute reports only six applications of the statute before its postwar repeal. The legislation therefore changed the formal framework more than it transformed day-to-day industrial dispute resolution. But the law did not require employers to recognize or bargain with unions, and it did not give unions a legal personality or protect them against dismissal or retaliation. The statute on violence created new risks for activists, since its definitions could be applied to strike pickets and demonstrations. A comprehensive trade-union law, which would have defined the legal status of unions, was debated in the mid-1920s but did not pass in that decade; later attempts, including a bill considered in 1931, also failed to secure a comprehensive statute. Historians therefore warn against describing the 1926 measures as comprehensive legal recognition of trade unions.

Interpretations of the law vary. Some historians, following the work of Sheldon Garon, emphasize the role of social bureaucrats who sought to integrate labor into a managed system of industrial relations, and see the conciliation law as part of a strategy of incorporation. Others emphasize that labor pressure and political calculation forced reluctant officials and legislators to concede something. The chapter's central argument accommodates both views: the legislation reflected genuine concessions and a determination to channel labor conflict into forms the state could supervise.

Section 69.35 · Division VII

Labor–Farmer Parties and the Fragmentation of Proletarian Politics, 1925–1927

The prospect of a mass electorate prompted unions and farmers' organizations to try to create legal political parties. The first attempt was the Nōmin Rōdōtō (Farmer-Labor Party), founded in December 1925 by the Sōdōmei, the Farmers' Union, and other groups, which the government ordered dissolved almost immediately because of the participation of left-wing groups with communist ties. The organizers regrouped, and in March 1926 they founded the Rōdō Nōmintō (Labor-Farmer Party), a party that attempted to unite moderate and left groups. The effort could not hold. Disagreement over the participation of the left-wing Hyōgikai and over relations with the communists led the Sōdōmei and its allies to leave, and in late 1926 the movement divided into three parties.

The Shakai Minshūtō (Social Democratic Party), founded in December 1926 under Abe Isoo, a Christian socialist and Waseda University professor, represented the moderate, social-democratic current and was supported by the Sōdōmei. The Nihon Rōnōtō (Japan Labor-Farmer Party), founded days later, occupied a middle position and drew support from some unions and tenant organizations. The Rōdō Nōmintō, now reorganized with its left wing, was closest to the communists and the Hyōgikai, and Ōyama Ikuo, a political scientist at Waseda University, became its best-known leader. These names are similar and the parties' constituencies overlapped, so students should take care to distinguish them: the Rōdō Nōmintō (left), the Nihon Rōnōtō (center), and the Shakai Minshūtō (moderate right of the proletarian camp). Distinct from all three were the clandestine Communist Party and the unions themselves.

Fragmentation had practical costs. Proletarian parties competed for the same limited pool of voters, candidates, and funds. The deposit requirement and the restrictions on campaigning magnified the disadvantage of dividing the movement. At the same time, the divisions were not merely tactical; they reflected genuine disagreements over legality, revolution, and the role of the state. The government, meanwhile, watched the left parties closely and was prepared to treat the Rōdō Nōmintō as a front for communists. The parties thus entered the election of 1928 divided and under pressure.

Section 69.36 · Division VII

The General Election of February 20, 1928: A New Electorate and Its Limits

The election of February 20, 1928, was the first national election held under the 1925 law. The House of Representatives had 466 seats, and roughly twelve million men were eligible to vote, about four times as many as in the previous election. Turnout was high, around four-fifths of eligible voters according to standard accounts. The Tanaka Giichi cabinet, formed by the Seiyūkai in 1927, faced the Rikken Minseitō, formed in June 1927 from the Kenseikai and a faction of the Seiyū Hontō. The result was extremely close: the Seiyūkai won about 217 seats and the Minseitō about 216, leaving the government with a narrow plurality and no secure majority. Historians have documented efforts by Home Ministry officials and police to influence the election in the government's favor, including interference with opposition campaigns, which was a notable feature of the contest.

The proletarian parties collectively won eight seats, according to the National Diet Library's records, out of 466, with about half going to the Shakai Minshūtō. The parties won roughly half a million votes, a modest share of the total. For the first time, however, candidates identified with workers and tenants sat in the Diet, and some used the platform to raise issues of labor law, tenancy, and civil liberties. The result showed that a mass electorate did not automatically produce a large labor vote. Many workers voted for the established parties, which had organization, money, and local patronage; others were indifferent or faced obstacles to registration and voting; and many tenants and rural voters were subject to the influence of landlords and local notables.

The weeks following the election revealed the limits of the new openness. On March 15, 1928, police carried out mass arrests of communists and suspected sympathizers across the country, detaining on the order of 1,600 people under the Peace Preservation Law, according to commonly cited figures. In April the government ordered the dissolution of the Rōdō Nōmintō, the Hyōgikai, and a left-wing youth league. These events, which belong to the transition described in Chapter 74, are mentioned here to show how the first election under universal male suffrage coexisted with large-scale repression. They do not mean that the later authoritarian turn was inevitable; the electoral system continued to function, and party cabinets remained in office for several more years. But they show that the right to vote and the right to organize were not equivalent.

Section 69.37 · Division VIII — Reform, Political Power, and the Meaning of Taishō Mass Politics

Comparing Reform Movements: Workers, Tenants, Suffragists, and Political Parties

The movements examined in this chapter shared a context but differed in resources, goals, and leverage. Unions had the clearest source of power, the ability to halt production, but they were also the most exposed to employer and police retaliation, and they remained a small minority of the workforce. Tenant organizations challenged landlords in places where landlords were socially and politically powerful, and they won concessions mainly where the state saw tenancy conflict as a threat to order. Suffrage organizations pursued legal change through persuasion, petition, and coalition, and their success depended on elite decisions; the men's campaign won in 1925, while the women's campaign did not. Socialist and communist groups offered ideological frameworks and, in some cases, organizational skills, but they were small and heavily policed. Political parties had access to legislation but pursued reform when their electoral interests required it.

Leadership profiles also differed. Union leaders came from skilled working-class backgrounds, from Christian reform circles, and from the intelligentsia. Tenant leaders were local organizers, teachers, and reformers. Suffrage leaders included party politicians, journalists, and academics, while women's leaders were typically educated urban women. Funding came from dues, donations, and, for parties, from business and landlord interests. The demands overlapped, including suffrage, social legislation, and civil liberties, but priorities diverged, and alliances were hard to sustain. Understanding reform as a set of interacting but distinct movements helps explain both the achievements, which required coalitions, and the failures, which often arose from fragmentation.

Cause and Effect

How Radicalization and State Intervention Shaped Reform Strategies

Long-term causes

A restrictive legal order from 1900; a narrow franchise; rapid industrialization and rural inequality; the memory of the 1910–1911 repression of socialism; wartime inflation and postwar recession; international examples from the Russian Revolution and the ILO.

Short-term causes

The Rice Riots; the strike wave of 1919–1921; the 1920 suffrage campaign; the defeat at Kobe in 1921; the founding of the Communist Party in 1922; the earthquake and the killing of Ōsugi in 1923; the 1925 expansion of the electorate.

Event or transformation

Movements pursued different strategies of negotiation, direct action, party politics, and revolution, while the state combined concessions (suffrage, conciliation, tenancy mediation) with repression (the Peace Preservation Law, arrests, bans).

Immediate effects

A broader electorate; limited labor and tenancy legislation; division of unions and parties into moderate, centrist, and left camps; surveillance and criminalization of radical politics; a first election in 1928 followed by mass arrests.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

Repression pushed some activists toward clandestine radicalism and others toward moderation, while concessions encouraged participation within the system. Neither radicalization nor state intervention operated alone: radical groups gave officials a reason to expand surveillance, and surveillance reinforced the isolation of radicals; moderates gained legislation partly because radicals existed, yet were weakened when the left was crushed. The pattern cannot be reduced to one cause, and later developments, including the crises of Chapters 73 and 74, were shaped by many factors beyond the reform movements.

It is equally important not to measure success only by whether an organization won a national statute. A failed strike could produce worker networks, shared experience, and new strategies, while a negotiated tenancy reduction could matter greatly to a village even without changing property law. A suffrage petition that failed in one Diet session could alter a later party's platform; conversely, a statutory reform could be weakly enforced or available only to part of the population. Comparing formal outcomes with organizational survival, access to officials, local bargains, and the political costs of repression yields a more accurate picture of reform. This comparison also prevents the history of the best-documented male party leaders from overshadowing women workers, poorer rural households, and colonial-origin residents whose political options were more constrained.

Section 69.38 · Division VIII

The Achievements and Limits of Taishō Reform: Rights, Representation, and Repression

By the late 1920s the reform movements had achieved substantial results. The electorate had grown about fourfold. Labor organizations had survived, established a presence in national life, and contributed to legislation that repealed Article 17 and created conciliation procedures. Tenant farmers had a national union and a state mediation system. Women had won the right to attend political meetings and had created a suffrage movement. Socialist and labor politicians sat in the Diet. Public debate about social inequality, political participation, and civil liberties was more open than it had been a decade earlier. These were real changes in the lives of millions of people.

Yet the limits were equally real. Unions had no comprehensive legal recognition, represented a small share of the workforce, and were divided. Tenants had mediation but not secure rights. Women had no vote. The colonies of Korea and Taiwan had no territorial Diet representation, although qualified Korean and Taiwanese men living in Japan proper could vote under the 1925 rules. The proletarian parties held eight seats. The state possessed a statute, the Peace Preservation Law, that could criminalize political organization, and it used its police powers against the left. The constitutional structure, in which the cabinet was not responsible to the Diet, the House of Peers could block legislation, and the military had independent standing, remained intact. Taishō reform therefore expanded participation without altering the sovereignty of the emperor-centered state.

Historians disagree about how to weigh these facts. Scholars have used the concept of "imperial democracy," developed notably by Andrew Gordon, to describe a political order in which popular participation expanded within an imperial framework. Others stress the durability of the conservative institutions of the Meiji system, and some emphasize the continuities between the reform of the 1920s and the wartime mobilization state of the 1930s, since the social bureaucracy that managed labor and rural relations in the 1920s later served the state's mobilization. A further group warns against teleology: the later authoritarian turn was not predetermined by the limits of Taishō reform, and in 1928 many contemporaries expected the parliamentary system to continue and to develop. The best historical account must hold these possibilities together.

Historical Debate

Did Taishō Reform Create Durable Democratic Institutions?

The question: Did the reforms of the 1920s create the foundations of a lasting democratic system that was later destroyed by militarism, or did they reveal the structural constraints of the imperial political system from the outset?

Interpretation A — Real but incomplete democratization: Some historians argue that the expansion of the electorate, the growth of parties and unions, and the vitality of public debate were genuine achievements, and that the later breakdown resulted from specific crises, including the economic depression, military insubordination, and political miscalculation. They emphasize contingency.
Interpretation B — Constraints built into the system: Others argue that the constitutional order, the Peace Preservation Law, the limited rights of unions, and the exclusion of women and colonial subjects show that reform was designed to contain and manage popular politics, so that the system's openness was always conditional. They emphasize structure.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same facts: the statutes of 1925–1926, election results, police records, party platforms, and the actions of cabinets. Work by Andrew Gordon, Sheldon Garon, and Stephen Large, among others, has shown that interpretations depend on whether historians emphasize the expansion of participation or the mechanisms of control. Many scholars now treat the two interpretations as complementary rather than exclusive.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes how they understand the origins of the 1930s, the relation between prewar and postwar democracy, and the significance of the movements that fought for reform.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence from this chapter that support Interpretation A, two that support B, and explain whether the interpretations can be reconciled.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Reform Movements, 1912–1928

What Changed

  • The electorate grew from about three million to about twelve million men.
  • Labor moved from a mutual-aid society to rival union federations and legal labor parties.
  • Tenants gained a national union and a conciliation system.
  • Women's political activity moved from meeting rights to a suffrage campaign.
  • The state added conciliation procedures and, at the same time, a law against political organization.

What Remained Continuous

  • The Meiji constitutional structure and the role of the House of Peers
  • Landlord influence in rural politics and the weak legal position of tenants
  • Police surveillance of the left
  • Women's exclusion from the vote and the exclusion of colonial subjects
  • The concentration of women in low-wage, tightly supervised factory work

Who experienced change most directly? Adult male wage earners and tenants in the metropole gained the most visible new rights and opportunities, and politicians and organizers on the left gained new platforms. Who experienced greater continuity? Women, colonial subjects, poor-relief recipients, and workers in small or unorganized workplaces. Why the pattern matters: It shows that reform was uneven and that the same period could be one of opening for some and of unchanged exclusion or intensified surveillance for others.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the organizations that, between 1912 and 1928, sought concrete changes in wages, working conditions, land rents, electoral participation, and political power. Its central argument is that Taishō-era reform was neither a gift from enlightened leaders nor the product of a single democratic movement. It emerged from sustained competition among workers, tenant farmers, party politicians, socialists, women's associations, intellectuals, employers, landlords, and state officials. Its defining contradiction was that Japan expanded electoral participation and conceded limited protections to organized labor while preserving and strengthening instruments to restrict and repress political movements.

The setting was a legal order, built around the Public Peace Police Law of 1900 and its Article 17, that criminalized the activities by which unions grew and strikes were organized, combined with an industrial and rural economy in transformation. The First World War expanded industrial employment and raised prices; the Rice Riots of 1918 revealed the depth of grievance; and the postwar recession exposed workers' insecurity. In these conditions, grievances that might have been resolved locally became demands for national institutional change. The shift from crowd protest to durable organization was the first major theme of the chapter.

In the labor movement, the Yūaikai, founded by Suzuki Bunji in 1912 as a mutual-aid society committed to cooperation with employers, became during and after the war a mass organization pressed by rank-and-file members toward collective bargaining. Renamed in 1919 and again in 1921, it became the Sōdōmei. The May Day of 1920 brought labor into the national public sphere, while the Kobe shipyard disputes of 1921 showed both workers' determination and the strength of employers backed by the state. Women workers, who dominated textile employment, were poorly represented in unions because of the dormitory system, short employment, legal limits, and the attitudes of male leaders. By 1925 the labor movement had divided into moderate, centrist, and left currents, with the Hyōgikai separating from the Sōdōmei.

Tenant farmers experienced a different structure of inequality. Close to half of cultivated land was rented, rents were commonly about half the crop, and landlords were embedded in village and local-government power. Tenancy disputes multiplied from the late 1910s, and in 1922 the Japan Farmers' Union was founded in Kobe by organizers including Sugiyama Motojirō and Kagawa Toyohiko. Landlords organized countermeasures, and the state responded in 1924 with a conciliation law that offered mediation but no new tenant rights. Comparing urban and rural organizing shows why alliance was attractive and why it was fragile.

The political left re-formed after the repression of 1910–1911, but it did so in several incompatible forms. Anarchists and syndicalists, led by Ōsugi Sakae, rejected parliamentary politics; Marxists, led by figures such as Yamakawa Hitoshi, built the clandestine Communist Party in 1922; social democrats such as Abe Isoo pursued legal reform. Socialist women such as Yamakawa Kikue and Itō Noe created the Sekirankai in 1921 and insisted on class analysis in debates about women's emancipation. The killing of Ōsugi and Itō in 1923 illustrated the physical danger faced by radicals. These currents should not be confused with each other or with the labor federations.

The campaign for universal male suffrage connected all of these movements. The tax qualification of 1889, lowered in 1900 and 1919, increasingly excluded the wage earners and tenants on whom industrial society depended. The Nationwide Universal Manhood Suffrage Federation of 1920 mobilized unions, students, journalists, and politicians, but the Hara cabinet dissolved the Diet and won the May 1920 election. The reform succeeded in 1925 only after a change in party alignments and negotiation with the House of Peers. The law removed the tax qualification but excluded women and recipients of certain forms of poor relief, and it gave no electoral districts to Korea or Taiwan; eligible Korean and Taiwanese men resident in the metropole could nevertheless vote. The first election under it took place not in 1925 but in February 1928.

Women's political activism moved from the campaign to amend Article 5 of the Public Peace Police Law (1922) to the Women's Suffrage League founded in December 1924, led by Ichikawa Fusae and Kubushiro Ochimi. Women reformers were divided by class, religion, and ideology, and the League won no vote in the 1920s. The Suiheisha, founded in 1922, and the petitions of colonial activists in Taiwan and Korea show other excluded groups pressing for equality or representation.

In 1925–1926 the state paired concession with control. The Peace Preservation Law criminalized organizing to alter the kokutai or to deny private property; the Labor Disputes Conciliation Law repealed Article 17 and created conciliation procedures but gave no comprehensive legal recognition to unions. The labor–farmer parties fragmented into the Rōdō Nōmintō, the Nihon Rōnōtō, and the Shakai Minshūtō, and in 1928 the proletarian parties won eight of 466 seats. The March 15, 1928, arrests showed that voting rights and the right to organize were not equivalent.

Historians interpret these developments differently. Some emphasize genuine democratization; others emphasize the structural constraints of the Meiji system; the concept of "imperial democracy" attempts to capture both. The chapter's position is that the reforms were real, that they were limited, and that the later authoritarian turn was not inevitable. The chapter therefore sets up the problems of mass society examined in Chapters 70 through 73 and the breakdown of parliamentary restraint examined in Chapter 74.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how political reform actually happened in a society that had no tradition of mass democratic politics. The movements of the Taishō years did not wait for permission from the state, but neither did they succeed by themselves. They created organizations, framed claims, tested legal boundaries, and negotiated, sometimes unwillingly, with parties and officials who calculated their own interests. The result was a political order that was more open than before and more heavily policed than before at the same time. That combination is the chapter's most important historical finding.

The chapter also reveals how unevenly the benefits of reform were distributed. Adult male wage earners and tenants in the metropole gained the vote and a presence in public life; women, the poor who depended on relief, Buraku communities facing discrimination, and the subjects of Korea and Taiwan did not gain the same rights. By keeping those groups in view, the chapter resists a story in which "the people" gained democracy in 1925. It also shows that organizing across lines of class, gender, region, and empire was difficult, and that fragmentation was not only a failure of leadership but a consequence of real differences in interest and circumstance.

The developments examined here shaped later history in several ways. The institutions of social management built in the 1920s, including conciliation procedures and a social-affairs bureaucracy, later served different purposes under wartime mobilization. The memory of union, tenant, and suffrage movements informed the reforms of the occupation period, when land reform, labor law, and women's suffrage came to be enacted. The Peace Preservation Law, whose scope expanded in 1928, became a key instrument of political repression in the 1930s. Chapters 73 and 74 return to the economic and political crises in which these structures were tested.

Finally, the chapter connects Japan to a wider world. Japanese reformers drew on ILO standards, American and European precedents, and the Russian Revolution, while the state studied foreign social policy and surveillance. And the unequal political rights of the empire remind students that the history of Japanese reform cannot be written only within the boundaries of the main islands. Students who understand these movements as contested, uneven, and open-ended are prepared to approach the cultural and urban transformations of Chapters 70 through 72 and the crisis of the early 1930s without assuming that the outcome was fixed.
