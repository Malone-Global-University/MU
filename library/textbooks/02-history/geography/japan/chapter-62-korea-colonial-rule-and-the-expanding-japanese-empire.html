Korea, Colonial Rule, and the Expanding Japanese Empire

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In late August 1910 a state that had existed in some form for more than five centuries disappeared from the diplomatic map. Its name, Korea (in Korean, Daehan Jeguk, the Korean Empire), was replaced in official use by "Chōsen," the Japanese reading of Joseon, and the peninsula became a territory governed by a Japanese Governor-General answerable to the emperor in Tokyo.

The annexation treaty was signed on 22 August 1910 and proclaimed on 29 August. Yet the act was the end of a process rather than its beginning. For five years before 1910, Japan had steadily dismantled the institutions through which Korea conducted its foreign relations, financed its government, policed its streets, commanded its soldiers, and administered its courts. Annexation formalized a loss of independence that had already been engineered through diplomatic pressure, military force, and the cooperation of some Korean officials. It also provoked responses that did not end in 1910: armed uprisings, new schools and newspapers, exile governments, nationwide demonstrations, labor and tenant organizing, literary and scholarly projects, and, among some Koreans, calculated cooperation with the new rulers.

This chapter traces Korea's history from the protectorate of 1905 to Japan's surrender in 1945. That is a span of four decades in which colonial rule was never a single, unchanging system. It began with the creation of a Residency-General and then a military-backed Government-General that relied heavily on gendarmerie police. After the March First Movement of 1919 it adopted a more flexible administrative style, often called "cultural rule" (bunka seiji), which expanded Korean-language publishing and some schooling while enlarging the police apparatus. From the 1930s, as Japan fought in Manchuria and China and then across the Pacific, it pressed Koreans toward assimilation as imperial subjects and drew on Korean labor, resources, and eventually soldiers. These three phases provide a useful framework, but policies overlapped and did not change uniformly at each date.

The chapter is organized in seven divisions. The first examines the destruction of Korean sovereignty between 1905 and 1910. The second explains the construction of the colonial state, including its administration, law, land survey, and economic foundations. The third is devoted to the March First Movement and its aftermath. The fourth turns to rural society, industry, schooling, religion, culture, and family life. The fifth examines Korean resistance, political movements, and the difficult question of collaboration and accommodation. The sixth traces assimilation and wartime coercion after 1931. The seventh places Korea within Japan's wider empire and considers its collapse and legacies.

Two cautions shape the whole chapter. First, colonial Korea must not be told only from the standpoint of the colonial government. Government-General statistics, ordinances, and reports are indispensable evidence, but they were produced by the ruling institution to administer, justify, and defend its rule. Korean newspapers, memoirs, court records, petitions, organizational documents, mission reports, and later oral testimony allow historians to ask whom colonial policy benefited, whom it burdened, and how people understood their circumstances. Second, Korean society should be understood as neither uniformly powerless nor uniformly resistant. Farmers, landlords, students, workers, Christians, intellectuals, women, police auxiliaries, businesspeople, and exiles faced different pressures and options, and their choices changed across time.

The chapter also asks students to hold together two statements that are sometimes presented as alternatives. Colonial rule coincided with railways, ports, factories, schools, new cities, and growing integration into an imperial economy. Those developments occurred within a political order in which Koreans lacked sovereignty, equal civil rights, and control over the institutions that governed them, and in which the purposes and distribution of economic change were shaped to serve the empire's interests. Historians disagree about how to weigh these facts, and this chapter presents that disagreement as part of the evidence.

Finally, this chapter fits within a divided but connected textbook. Chapters 60 and 61 explain how Japan acquired Taiwan and defeated Russia, creating the conditions for Korea's loss of independence. Chapters 63 to 65 treat labor, gender, education, and mass culture primarily within the Japanese metropole; here these topics are examined as Koreans experienced them under colonial rule. Chapter 71 will examine the 1923 Kantō earthquake and the massacres of Koreans, Chapter 75 the establishment of Manchukuo, and Chapter 79 the systematic wartime treatment of forced labor, sexual violence, and atrocities. This chapter establishes the colonial origins of those later developments without retelling them in full.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Explain the roles of the Residency-General and the Government-General and how each exercised power over Korean administration.
  3. Analyze the legal and political distinction between Japanese imperial nationality and equal citizenship in colonial Korea.
  4. Evaluate competing interpretations of the 1910–1918 land survey and of colonial economic development.
  5. Explain the causes, organization, geography, and repression of the March First Movement and its effect on colonial policy.
  6. Compare the main strands of Korean resistance, including armed, diplomatic, cultural, socialist, and mass movements.
  7. Distinguish collaboration, accommodation, constrained survival, and resistance, and assess the evidence needed to apply those categories.
  8. Describe how colonial rule shaped rural life, industry, education, religion, culture, and family life.
  9. Interpret wartime assimilation policies, including naisen ittai, language policy, name policy, labor mobilization, and military recruitment.
  10. Contextualize Korea's legal and administrative status among Japan's other imperial territories.
  11. Assess how colonial sources represent particular perspectives and identify Korean perspectives that official records may obscure.
  12. Connect colonial rule to the problems of liberation, division, and historical memory after 1945.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What choices did Koreans have between 1905 and 1910, and why did reform, diplomacy, and armed resistance all fail to prevent annexation?
  3. How did the colonial state actually operate in villages, courts, schools, and police stations, rather than only in Seoul and Tokyo?

  6. Why is it inadequate to divide colonial Koreans into "collaborators" and "patriots"?

  9. How do the sources available for colonial Korea shape what historians can know, and whose experiences are hardest to recover?
  10. In what ways did the legacies of colonial rule shape Korea after 1945 and relations between Japan and Korea thereafter?

Historical Context

By 1900 the Korean state was one of the oldest continuously governed polities in East Asia. The Joseon dynasty, founded in 1392, had ruled through a Confucian scholar-official bureaucracy, a hereditary status system, and a long tributary relationship with China that left Korea domestically autonomous. In the late nineteenth century this order was strained by population pressure, factional politics, peasant unrest such as the Donghak uprising of 1894, and the arrival of foreign powers. Japan forced the Treaty of Ganghwa on Korea in 1876, opening ports in an unequal agreement modeled on arrangements that Western powers had imposed on Japan. Korean officials, reformers, and monarchs then pursued varied strategies of modernization and balancing among China, Japan, and Russia. In 1897 King Gojong proclaimed the Korean Empire (Daehan Jeguk) and took the title of emperor, asserting formal equality with neighboring empires.

Japan's victory over Qing China in 1894–95 (Chapter 60) ended China's claim to suzerainty over Korea and made the peninsula a field of competition between Japan and Russia. The Russo-Japanese War (Chapter 61) resolved that competition. Japan had already imposed a protocol on Korea at the start of the war in February 1904, and an August 1904 agreement that placed Japanese advisers in Korean finance and diplomacy. Britain, in the revised Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1905, accepted Japan's right to take measures of guidance and protection in Korea. The United States signaled acquiescence in the Taft–Katsura memorandum of July 1905, a record of conversation rather than a treaty. The Treaty of Portsmouth in September 1905 required Russia to recognize Japan's paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea. By the time the protectorate agreement was imposed that November, Korea had few remaining sources of foreign support.

Japanese policy toward Korea was never made by one actor. The government in Tokyo, the army, the Foreign Ministry, financial interests, civilian advisers, Japanese residents in Korea, and leaders such as Itō Hirobumi and Terauchi Masatake held differing views on pace and methods. Korean society likewise included monarchists, Confucian scholars, reformist intellectuals, Christians, merchants, peasants, soldiers, and officials who favored cooperation with Japan on the grounds of modernization. This chapter follows these differences. It does not revisit the diplomacy of the Russo-Japanese War or the detailed 1895 Taiwan campaign, which belong to earlier chapters, and it leaves the later conquest and governance of Manchukuo to Chapter 75.

Section 62.1

The Eulsa Agreement of 1905 and the Loss of Korean Diplomatic Sovereignty

Japan's military position in Korea in 1905 was overwhelming. Japanese troops had occupied Seoul since the outbreak of war the previous year, and Japanese advisers already sat within Korean finance, diplomacy, and police affairs. After Portsmouth, Itō Hirobumi, a former prime minister and one of Japan's most senior statesmen, was sent to Seoul as a special envoy. He arrived in November with instructions to obtain Korean consent to a protectorate. He was accompanied by Hasegawa Yoshimichi, the commander of Japanese forces in Korea, and Japanese troops were visible in and around the palace district.

On 15 November Itō presented the proposed agreement to Emperor Gojong. Gojong declined to approve it, and over the following two days Japanese officials pressed the Korean cabinet in a series of meetings in which Japanese soldiers were present nearby. In the early hours of 18 November (the agreement is dated 17 November), a majority of the ministers signified assent. Han Gyu-seol, the prime minister, opposed it and was removed from the room. Five ministers—Yi Wan-yong, Yi Ji-yong, Yi Geun-taek, Gwon Jung-hyeon, and Foreign Minister Park Je-sun—are remembered in Korean historical memory as the "Five Eulsa Traitors." Park Je-sun's name and seal appear on the agreement as a signatory; their presence does not resolve the coercive circumstances of its conclusion or Gojong's refusal to approve it. Gojong never personally approved the text.

The agreement transferred control of Korea's foreign relations to Japan's Foreign Ministry, forbade Korea from concluding international agreements except through Japan, and provided for a Japanese Resident-General in Seoul to manage diplomatic matters and Japanese residents at the open ports. Foreign legations, including the American one, were withdrawn from Seoul. The Korean text bore no formal title, a fact Korean scholars cite among its procedural irregularities. In the months that followed, protest suicides by officials, including the senior statesman Min Yeong-hwan, and an editorial by the journalist Jang Ji-yeon lamenting the day became widely known; the newspaper Hwangseong Sinmun was suspended for publishing it.

The legality of the agreement has been disputed for more than a century. Korean historians and many international-law scholars emphasize duress against the sovereign and ministers, the absence of ratification by Gojong, and procedural defects, and they therefore argue that it was never legally valid. Japanese officials at the time and many later Japanese commentators argued that the agreement had been formally concluded by authorized ministers and that other powers accepted the arrangement. The 1965 treaty normalizing relations between Japan and South Korea stated that earlier treaties between the Korean Empire and Japan were "already null and void," a wording each government has interpreted differently. This chapter treats the contested legitimacy as a historical fact in itself: contemporaries made competing claims, and the dispute shaped later politics.

Primary Source Window

The Second Japan–Korea Convention of 17 November 1905 (the "Eulsa Agreement")

Signatories: Japanese Minister Hayashi Gonsuke and Korean Foreign Minister Park Je-sun; concluded under Japanese military pressure · Date: 17 November 1905 · Type: Diplomatic agreement

Context: The agreement was concluded in Seoul under Japanese military presence and without Gojong's personal approval. It was presented in Japanese as a protectorate arrangement ("protection" of Korea's external affairs).

Paraphrase of the main provisions (not a verbatim quotation): The articles provide that Japan's Foreign Ministry will supervise Korea's foreign relations and protect Korean subjects abroad; that Korea will make no international agreements of an international character except through Japan; that a Japanese Resident-General in Seoul will manage diplomatic affairs, with residents at the open ports; and that Japan will maintain the welfare and dignity of the Korean imperial house. For the exact text, see the English agreement printed in the U.S. Department of State’s Foreign Relations of the United States, 1905.

Analysis: The text reveals how Japan framed its action as protective and diplomatic while removing Korea's capacity to act as a sovereign state in international relations. Its limits matter as much: the document cannot show the pressure applied in the negotiation room, Gojong's refusal, or the ministers' reasons. Those details come from Korean court and ministerial accounts, foreign diplomats' reports, and later testimony, each with its own perspective.

Section 62.2

Itō Hirobumi and the Residency-General: Building the Machinery of Control

The Residency-General opened in Seoul in early 1906, and Itō took up his post as Resident-General in March. Although the 1905 agreement referred to a resident with powers over diplomacy, Itō and his successors extended their reach far beyond foreign affairs. The Resident-General was empowered to command Japanese troops in an emergency, to attend meetings of the Korean cabinet, and to propose reforms. In practice, he supervised nearly every area of Korean government through Japanese advisers placed in ministries and local posts. The system rested on a simple reality: Korean officials understood that Japan's army stood behind the Resident-General's advice.

Control of finance and communications came first. Japanese financial advisers had since 1904 been reorganizing Korean currency and taxation, with the Japanese bank Dai-Ichi Ginkō acting as a quasi-central bank, and Japan took over the Korean postal, telegraph, and telephone services in 1905. After 1906 Japanese officials oversaw budgets and loans, and loans from Japan increased Korea's indebtedness in ways that later became a nationalist grievance. Police power moved in the same direction: Japanese advisers expanded their control over the Korean police in 1906–1907, and in 1910, shortly before annexation, Korea's police administration was formally transferred to Japan. In 1909 Korea's judicial and prison administration was likewise placed under Japanese direction.

Itō's own approach was gradualist and paternalistic. He argued that Korea needed reform under Japanese tutelage, building schools, reorganizing administration, and promoting economic development, and he was, at least until 1909, cautious about outright annexation, which he thought would be costly and provoke resistance. These views did not prevent him from presiding over the removal of Korean autonomy. Japanese military and settler interests, as well as officials such as Hasegawa and later Terauchi, pushed for firmer control. The tension between "protectorate" reformism and full colonial assimilation recurred in Japanese policy throughout the colonial period.

The protectorate also changed who held power inside Korea. Korean officials who cooperated with the Residency-General gained influence, and organizations such as the Ilchinhoe, a pro-Japanese association founded in 1904 whose leaders included Song Byeong-jun and Yi Yong-gu, took part in efforts to legitimize Japanese guidance. For many ordinary Koreans, however, the visible signs of change were Japanese policemen, advisers, new taxes and currency arrangements, and settlers. Evidence for these years includes Residency-General annual reports on "Korean reform and progress," Korean newspapers before censorship tightened, diplomatic dispatches, and memoirs. Because the Residency-General's reports described its own work in terms of reform and development, historians read them alongside Korean and foreign accounts.

Section 62.3

Korean Responses: Constitutional Reform, Education, Economic Nationalism, and Protest

Korean responses to the protectorate did not begin with armed resistance. Many educated Koreans, believing that Korea had fallen behind because of weak institutions, supported a strategy often called "self-strengthening" (jaganghoe in the name of one major society) or enlightenment: build schools, spread literacy, develop commerce, and reform government so that independence could be recovered in the future. The Korean Self-Strengthening Society (Daehan Jaganghoe, 1906) campaigned for constitutional government and education until Japanese authorities pressed it to disband in 1907. Such associations coexisted with the Independence Club's earlier legacy from the 1890s and with a growing private-school movement. Schools such as Osan, founded by Yi Seung-hun, and Daesong, founded by An Chang-ho, taught modern subjects together with patriotic content.

The press was a central arena. The Daehan Maeil Sinbo, published under the British journalist Ernest Bethell, enjoyed a degree of protection from Korean and Japanese censors because of his foreign status, while the Hwangseong Sinmun and other papers published patriotic editorials. The Japanese authorities responded with the Newspaper Law of 1907 and the Publications Law of 1909, and in 1908 the Private School Ordinance required schools to be authorized and supervised. These measures show how Japanese policy tried to channel Korean education and speech.

Economic nationalism produced a mass movement in 1907. The National Debt Redemption Movement began in Daegu in early 1907, where Seo Sang-don and Kim Gwang-je proposed that Koreans repay the roughly 13 million won of debt owed to Japan through collective contributions, including men giving up tobacco. The campaign spread through newspapers and associations, and women's groups, including those who donated jewelry and household goods, took an active part. Japanese authorities opposed it and targeted its organizers and the newspapers that promoted it; the campaign did not raise enough to pay the debt. It nonetheless demonstrated the capacity of Koreans to organize across class and gender lines for a national goal.

In 1907 the secret Sinminhoe (New People's Association) was formed by An Chang-ho, Yang Gi-tak, Yi Dong-nyeong, and others. It aimed to cultivate a "new people" through education and enterprise, to build overseas bases, and to prepare for eventual independence, and it deliberately avoided open confrontation. Korean strategies were thus plural: constitutional reform, education, commerce, diplomacy, and, as the next sections show, armed resistance. Their very variety, and the divisions among their supporters, were reflected in later debates over whether a different strategy might have preserved independence. Historians generally stress that, given Japan's military strength and the diplomatic environment, no single Korean strategy could have decisively reversed the process by 1910.

Section 62.4

The Hague Mission, Gojong's Forced Abdication, and the Dissolution of the Korean Army

In 1907 Gojong attempted to appeal to the international community. He dispatched three envoys, Yi Jun, Yi Sang-seol, and Yi Wi-jong, to the Second Hague Peace Conference to protest that the 1905 agreement had been imposed by force. The delegates were not admitted by the conference, which treated Korea's foreign relations as under Japanese control. Yi Jun died in The Hague in July 1907, and Korean tradition has long described the death as a protest suicide, though accounts differ about its circumstances. The mission demonstrated both the monarch's continuing effort to resist and the limits of international sympathy.

Japanese officials treated the mission as a violation of the protectorate. Itō pressed the Korean cabinet, in which Yi Wan-yong was prime minister, to insist that Gojong take responsibility. Under heavy pressure, and after demonstrations in Seoul, Gojong abdicated in favor of his son, who became Emperor Sunjong (the transfer took place in July 1907). Korean accounts stress that the abdication was coerced and that the ceremony itself was irregular. The forced removal outraged many Koreans and heightened a sense that the dynasty was being dismantled.

On 24 July 1907 a further agreement, sometimes called the Third Japan–Korea Agreement or Jeongmi Treaty, required Korea to obtain the Resident-General's approval for laws and important administrative actions and to appoint Japanese officials, including Japanese vice-ministers, throughout the government. Secret clauses provided for the dissolution of the Korean army. In effect, it converted the protectorate into a system in which the Resident-General exercised real control over internal affairs.

The dissolution of the Korean armed forces followed on 1 August 1907. The Korean army, which numbered several thousand men and had been weakened and underfunded, was disbanded, with only a small guard unit retained for the palace. In Seoul, the commander Park Seung-hwan took his own life rather than accept the order, and units of the First Regiment rose against disbandment, fighting Japanese troops before being defeated. Many discharged soldiers carried their weapons and military training into the countryside. The Korean National Institute of History and other Korean research institutions treat this moment as the point at which resistance expanded from isolated local actions into a wider armed movement.

Section 62.5

The Righteous Armies and Armed Resistance to Japanese Control

The uibyeong, or "righteous armies," drew on a Korean tradition of locally organized resistance that had appeared during the Japanese invasions of the 1590s and in the 1890s. After 1905 a first wave was led largely by Confucian scholars and local notables. Min Jong-sik and Choe Ik-hyeon, an elderly scholar-official, raised forces in 1906, while Sin Dol-seok, a commoner who rose to command, led a force in the mountainous southeast. Choe was captured and exiled to Tsushima, where he died in 1907 after, in Korean accounts, refusing food prepared by his captors.

After the army's dissolution in 1907, a second and larger wave of resistance developed. Former soldiers brought organization and weapons, and the movement drew in peasants, hunters, miners, and some local elites. Leaders such as Yi In-yeong and Heo Wi sought to coordinate actions. In late 1907 a loose Thirteen Provinces Allied Righteous Army assembled and attempted to advance on Seoul, but it was defeated by Japanese forces before reaching the city. Fighting continued in many provinces, especially Gyeonggi, Gangwon, Chungcheong, and Jeolla.

Japanese forces responded with counterinsurgency. Japanese garrison troops, gendarmes, and police pursued guerrillas, burned villages that sheltered them, and conducted large sweeps, most famously the "Great Southern Punitive Operation" in southwestern Korea in the late summer and autumn of 1909. Japanese military records list well over ten thousand Korean insurgents killed between 1907 and 1910, but such totals derive from the army's own tallies, which may include civilians and cannot be independently verified. Whatever the exact numbers, accounts from Korean sources, missionaries, and Western observers describe destroyed villages and heavy civilian suffering.

By 1910 organized armed resistance inside Korea had been severely reduced, and many surviving commanders and fighters moved to northern border zones, Manchuria's Jiandao region, and Russia's Maritime Provinces, where Korean communities were already settled. These exile networks became the foundation for the armed independence movements of the 1910s and 1920s (Section 62.24). The righteous armies illustrate a point that recurs in the chapter: resistance emerged from several social groups at once, including scholars, soldiers, and peasants, and was continuous with later movements even though its leadership changed.

Section 62.6

An Jung-geun, Itō's Assassination, and Japan's Decision to Annex Korea

On 26 October 1909, An Jung-geun, a Korean Catholic who had been active in the exile resistance in the Russian Far East and Manchuria, shot Itō Hirobumi at the railway station in Harbin as Itō prepared to meet a Russian finance minister. Itō died shortly afterward. An was arrested at the scene. He declared that he acted as a lieutenant general of the Korean righteous army, and he presented his reasons in court and in writing, listing charges against Itō that included the forced 1905 agreement and the deposition of Gojong. He also began a work often rendered as On Peace in East Asia, which argued for cooperation among Asian states on the basis of equality and was left unfinished at his execution at Lüshun (Port Arthur) in March 1910.

The relation between the assassination and annexation is frequently misunderstood. Popular narratives sometimes suggest that Itō's death caused Japan to annex Korea. Historians point out that Japan's government had already moved toward annexation. In July 1909, months before the assassination, the Japanese cabinet had approved a policy of eventually annexing Korea, and Itō had resigned as Resident-General in June 1909 while remaining a leading figure. Itō's own position, though debated by historians, was that Korea could be controlled through a protectorate for the time being. The assassination was used by Japanese advocates of annexation, and by Korean collaborators, to argue that the protectorate was unstable, but the policy shift preceded it.

Responses to An's act varied. Many Koreans, in the peninsula and abroad, regarded him as a patriot; Japanese official and press reaction was one of outrage, while some Western and Chinese observers sympathized with his motives while criticizing the use of assassination. Within Korea, Japanese authorities used the incident to tighten surveillance and to make arrests. Later, An became a prominent figure in Korean national memory, and his commemoration remains politically sensitive in Japan–Korea relations.

After Itō's resignation, Sone Arasuke served briefly as Resident-General, and in 1910 Terauchi Masatake, the army minister, took the post with a mandate to complete annexation. Japan consulted the major powers diplomatically, and those powers, preoccupied with their own interests, did not intervene. In summer 1910 Terauchi prepared a treaty, pressed the Korean cabinet, and increased military and police preparations in Seoul. The annexation therefore did not arrive suddenly in 1910. It followed a sequence: diplomatic isolation, loss of foreign relations, control of administration and police, removal of the monarch's independent role, disbandment of the army, suppression of armed resistance, and a final treaty.

Section 62.7

The Annexation Treaty of 1910 and the End of the Korean Empire

In May 1910 Terauchi Masatake, a senior army officer who remained minister of the army, took over as Resident-General with instructions to complete annexation. He moved police functions fully into Japanese hands, banned political meetings, and positioned troops in Seoul. In August the Korean cabinet under Prime Minister Yi Wan-yong, who had long cooperated with Japanese officials, was presented with a treaty of annexation. The text was signed by Yi Wan-yong and Terauchi on 22 August 1910, but its existence was withheld from the public for a week. It was proclaimed on 29 August together with an imperial rescript in the name of Emperor Sunjong.

The treaty consisted of a short preamble and eight articles. In the Japanese presentation, the Korean emperor made a complete and permanent cession of all rights of sovereignty to the emperor of Japan, and the Japanese emperor accepted. Japan promised titles, honors, and financial support for the Korean imperial house and for certain Koreans who had performed meritorious service, and it undertook to protect the persons and property of Koreans who obeyed the new laws. The word "annexation" (heigō, "merger" or "union") was chosen by Japanese officials in preference to "conquest," a vocabulary that fit their claim that the action was a lawful union. The Korean Empire was renamed Chōsen. Sunjong was given the title of "King Yi" within the Japanese imperial family system, and Gojong that of "retired king." Seventy-six Koreans were ennobled, and some received cash grants.

The legitimacy of the treaty remains disputed along lines similar to those of 1905. Korean scholars argue that Sunjong did not give valid assent, that the imperial rescripts were irregular, that the pressure of military occupation tainted the proceedings, and that Gojong, retired but still a public figure, was not consulted. Japanese officials contended that Korean ministers had concluded the agreement through lawful processes and that the powers had acknowledged it. After 1945, the treaty's legal status became a diplomatic problem, as the Japan–Korea Basic Relations Treaty of 1965 and later statements show, with each side stressing different readings of "already null and void." Whatever the legal conclusions, the historical fact is that the Korean state lost its institutional existence and the peninsula came under direct Japanese rule.

The announcement was met in Korea by quiet. Open demonstrations were suppressed in advance, and some prominent figures chose suicide or exile. A number of former officials, scholars, and activists left for Manchuria, Russia, China, and the United States. Others adapted. These different responses already point toward a theme developed later: the annexation did not create a unified Korean response, but forced individuals and groups to choose how to live under a regime they had not chosen.

Section 62.8

The Government-General of Korea: Governors, Bureaucracy, and Military Police

The new colonial state was headed by a Governor-General (sōtoku) appointed by the Japanese emperor on the advice of the cabinet. The office combined wide executive power with the authority to issue ordinances having the force of law. For the entire period before 1945, every Governor-General was an army general or navy admiral: Terauchi Masatake (1910–1916), Hasegawa Yoshimichi (1916–1919), Saitō Makoto (1919–1927 and 1929–1931), Yamanashi Hanzō (1927–1929), Ugaki Kazushige (1931–1936), Minami Jirō (1936–1942), Koiso Kuniaki (1942–1944), and Abe Nobuyuki (1944–1945). A reform in 1919 nominally allowed a civilian to hold the office, but none was ever appointed. Below the Governor-General, a civilian-style administrative head (the Director-General of Political Affairs) and bureaus for general affairs, finance, police, education, agriculture, and other matters oversaw a bureaucracy in Seoul.

The country was divided into thirteen provinces, subdivided into counties, towns, and townships. In most senior positions Japanese officials predominated, and Koreans were hired as provincial governors in some provinces and as lower officials in many places, often under Japanese deputies and advisers. This arrangement gave the colonial state a Korean face at the local level while keeping decisions in Japanese hands. Salary scales and promotion rules favored Japanese employees, and Korean officials often saw their authority limited by Japanese supervisors.

The distinctive institution of the first decade was the gendarmerie-police system (kenpei keisatsu). The Japanese military police, the Kenpeitai, was merged with the civil police under a single commander, with the chief of the military police also serving as head of the police bureau of the Government-General. Gendarmes were stationed in small posts in towns and villages and exercised wide powers over security, public order, taxation enforcement, sanitation, and everyday behavior. Korean auxiliaries (hojo) helped staff the system. Police could impose short sentences summarily, without a court trial, under the Summary Judgment Ordinance, and a Flogging Ordinance of 1912 allowed corporal punishment to be applied to Koreans; it was abolished in 1920. Japanese schoolteachers and some civil officials wore uniforms and swords in a practice that Korean memory recalls as a symbol of military-style rule.

Historians have emphasized that colonial governance was most visible to ordinary Koreans at the local level. A village head, a police auxiliary, a tax collector, or a schoolmaster could be the person through whom the Government-General's rules reached a household. The evidence includes the Government-General's own Official Gazette, annual statistical reports, court and police records preserved in Korean archives, mission accounts, and later memoirs. Each shows the system from a different position, and none by itself shows how Koreans in different regions, classes, and age groups experienced it.

Historical Vocabulary
Governor-General (sōtoku)

The Governor-General of Chōsen was the highest official of the colonial government. He was appointed by the emperor, reported to the Japanese government, and held authority to issue ordinances (colonial ordinances, seirei) with the force of law in Korea. He also commanded Japanese military forces in Korea during most of the period, and held broad control over police and administration. The office was comparable to that of the Governor-General of Taiwan but was distinct from the Japanese cabinet system of ministers responsible to the Diet.

Related terms: a protectorate (1905–1910) left a state's formal existence in place while transferring control of key functions; annexation (1910) ended the state's existence and incorporated its territory into the empire; an imperial subject (shinmin) was a person under the emperor's authority, which did not guarantee equal rights. The English word "colony" is used by historians even though Japanese official terminology at the time avoided it, speaking instead of "Chōsen" as part of the "empire" or of "the inner and outer lands."

Section 62.9

Colonial Law, Courts, Policing, and Unequal Imperial Citizenship

After annexation, Koreans became Japanese nationals in the sense that they were subject to the Japanese emperor and held Japanese nationality. They did not, however, enter the Japanese legal order on equal terms. The Meiji Constitution's rights and the electoral system applied fully only to people registered in the "inner land" (naichi) family registers. Koreans were recorded in separate household registers, and movement of a person from one register to another was legally restricted, a boundary only partly relaxed by a 1918 law that allowed marriage and adoption across registers. This system kept "Japanese" and "Korean" as legal categories within a single nation-state structure.

The Japanese Diet did not represent Korea, and the colonial territory was governed largely by ordinance rather than by statute passed with Korean participation. Metropolitan laws could be extended to Korea by imperial decision, but did not apply automatically, and the Governor-General's ordinances filled the gap. As a result, a wide range of measures, including the Peace Preservation Law, which was extended to Korea in 1925, and rules on assembly, associations, and speech, could be crafted with less parliamentary scrutiny than in Japan. Koreans who lived in the Japanese metropole could vote or stand for election if they met property and residence qualifications, so a small number participated in Japanese politics, but this was an exception to a system that withheld representation in Korea itself.

Civil law offers an example of how legal categories shaped daily life. Colonial civil ordinances, notably those of 1912, applied Japanese civil law to Japanese residents and provided that Koreans' matters of kinship and inheritance would be governed by "Korean custom" as interpreted by colonial courts and officials. This arrangement has been interpreted in two ways. Defenders at the time said it respected local tradition. Scholars of gender and law have shown that colonial authorities and judges often froze a selective and patriarchal version of custom, particularly in family law, and that the legal category of custom changed through colonial interpretation (Section 62.23).

The court system included Japanese and Korean judges, but Japanese judges predominated at senior levels. Prosecutors and police had wide discretion, and political cases were prosecuted under security laws. Police could detain suspects for lengthy periods, and torture in custody was widely reported by Korean sources, missionaries, and defendants in political trials. The legal order thus supported two principles at once: the claim that Koreans were imperial subjects, and the practice of unequal treatment. The tension between the two was later widely exploited by Korean critics, who argued that Japan promised equality while institutionalizing inequality, and by officials who justified unequal treatment as a temporary stage until Koreans were "ready."

Section 62.10

The Land Survey of 1910–1918: Property, Taxation, and Rural Transformation

One of the Government-General's first major projects was a cadastral land survey, carried out between 1910 and 1918 by a Land Survey Bureau, with legal underpinning in survey ordinances of 1910 and 1912. Surveyors measured and mapped parcels, local committees assisted in determining boundaries, and landholders were required to report their holdings within set periods. The resulting registry fixed ownership by named individuals or institutions, produced maps, and provided the basis for a more reliable land tax assessed on a uniform basis. For the colonial government, the survey was a tool to secure revenue, to clarify title for investors and settlers, and to make the countryside legible to administrators.

The survey's social consequences are among the most debated questions in Korean colonial history. For decades, many Korean historians argued that it had been a vast instrument of dispossession, with a large share of Korean land seized by the state or transferred to Japanese companies and settlers. Some later researchers, including economic historians in Korea and Japan, challenged that picture, arguing that most owners who reported their land received secure title, that the survey modernized property rights, and that the transfer of land to Japanese owners occurred gradually through the market. More recent studies take a differentiated view. They find that the survey did strengthen formal ownership for many landowners, but also that categories of land with unclear individual claims, such as former crown and royal estates, public offices' lands, common village and lineage lands, and some temple lands, were frequently classified as state property or entered under individuals who held administrative power. Cultivators with customary or hereditary use rights, especially tenants, often saw those rights weakened when the law recognized only the owner's rights.

Mechanisms mattered. A farmer who did not understand the reporting procedure, was absent, or had an informal claim could lose it. Landlords and local elites who could navigate the documentation, or who had connections to officials, benefited. The land survey was accompanied by a forest survey and by ongoing policies in forestry that, as in the studies of David Fedman on colonial forestry, shifted control of resources toward the state. Large Japanese corporate landowners, especially the Oriental Development Company (founded in 1908), acquired land, partly from former state holdings and partly through purchase. Historians caution against unsupported sweeping percentages. The reliable conclusion is that the survey reorganized property relations, increased fiscal control, and favored those with documentation, capital, and institutional access, with outcomes varying by region.

Section 62.11

Railways, Ports, Finance, Settlers, and the Economic Foundations of Colonial Rule

Transportation was an early colonial priority. The Seoul–Busan railway (Gyeongbu line) had opened in 1905 and the Seoul–Sinuiju line (Gyeongui) in 1906, both built under Japanese military and commercial direction during the war. A bridge across the Yalu River, completed in 1911, linked the Korean railway with lines in Manchuria, and additional lines extended to the south-west and to the east coast. Port works at Busan, Incheon, and other harbors tied Korea to Japanese shipping. These networks facilitated the movement of rice, minerals, troops, officials, and settlers, and they integrated Korea into the economy of the empire, with railway planning shaped by military and commercial interests in Japan and Manchuria. Korean passengers and workers used the railways, and Korean laborers built them, often under harsh terms, but decisions about routes, rates, and priorities were made by Japanese authorities.

Finance supported this structure. The Bank of Korea created in 1909 became the Bank of Chōsen in 1911 and served as the colony's central bank, issuing currency tied to the Japanese yen. The Chōsen Industrial Bank, established in 1918, channeled credit into agriculture and industry. The Government-General's Company Ordinance of 1910 required official permission to establish a company in Korea, a rule that gave the government a means to regulate Korean entrepreneurs and favor Japanese ones. It remained in force until 1920. Land taxation, revenue from monopolies, and fees financed administration, so Koreans paid for a government they had no vote in.

Japanese settlers formed a distinct community. Their numbers rose from roughly 170,000 at annexation to more than 700,000 by the early 1940s, concentrated in cities such as Seoul, Busan, Incheon, and Pyongyang, and in commercial and administrative occupations. Settlers included officials, merchants, professionals, farmers, laborers, and people seeking opportunity unavailable at home. Their relations with Koreans varied by place and occupation, from intimate neighbor contact to strict social segregation. Jun Uchida's study of settler leaders shows that they acted as brokers between the colonial government and Japanese and Korean society, sometimes pressing the Government-General in directions it did not favor.

Historians stress the question of whose interests this infrastructure served. Railways and ports increased Korea's capacity for trade and production, and some Koreans gained employment or business opportunities. They were also designed to extract rice and minerals, move troops, and open markets for Japanese goods, and the rules of corporate registration, credit, and tariffs favored Japanese capital. The Korean economy became more commercialized and more closely linked to Japan's. This did not mean uniform harm or uniform benefit: outcomes depended on class, region, and access to capital and information.

Section 62.12

The First Decade of Colonial Repression: Education, Churches, Newspapers, and the 105-Person Incident

The colonial government moved quickly to restrict independent Korean organizations. Patriotic and political associations were dissolved, and the Korean-language press that had flourished before 1910 was largely closed. The main Korean-language newspaper permitted was the Maeil Sinbo, the Government-General's own organ, which had been formed from the older Daehan Maeil Sinbo. Publications were subject to censorship, and mail and meetings were monitored. These restrictions were far more severe than those applied in the Japanese metropole.

Education was reshaped by the Chōsen Education Ordinance of 1911 and the Private School Regulations of 1911, revised in 1915. The ordinances emphasized training "loyal and good subjects," instruction in the Japanese language, and practical skills. They set up four-year common schools for Koreans, in contrast to the longer elementary schools for Japanese children. Private schools, including those of Korean nationalists and mission societies, were required to follow a standard curriculum and to obtain official authorization, and many were closed or reorganized. Mission schools, especially Presbyterian and Methodist institutions, were an object of tension because Christian missions were foreign-run and, in places such as Pyongyang, closely associated with Korean patriotic activity. A 1915 regulation restricting religious education in recognized schools produced sharp debate between the Government-General and mission bodies.

In the first year of the new regime, the authorities also moved against organized nationalist networks. After an alleged plot to assassinate Governor-General Terauchi during a trip to northwestern Korea in late 1910, police made hundreds of arrests, mostly among Christians and members of the Sinminhoe. In 1912 a court convicted 105 people, which gave the case its name. Among them were prominent figures such as Yun Chi-ho and Yang Gi-tak. The prosecution's evidence, including confessions obtained after police interrogation that defendants said involved torture, was criticized by missionaries and foreign observers. On appeal in 1913, most convictions were overturned and only a handful were upheld. The case demonstrated that colonial officials were willing to use the legal system to weaken potential leadership and also, through the exposure of defendants' accounts, that the colonial legal order faced limits when foreign attention intervened.

Repression did not eliminate Korean cultural and political life. Local associations, religious networks, private tutoring, and secret societies persisted. Groups such as the Korean National Association in the United States and Hawaii, the Korea Restoration Society (Gwangbokhoe) formed in 1915 around Daegu, and small clandestine circles kept the independence idea alive. Cheondogyo, a native religion with a large rural membership, organized quietly. By 1918, the combination of wartime economic strain, news of the end of World War I and of self-determination, and the accumulated pressure of a decade of control created the conditions for the movement examined in the next division.

Division III

The March First Movement and the Transformation of Colonial Politics

Section 62.13

The Road to March 1919: Gojong's Death, World War I, and Self-Determination

By 1918 Korean society had endured eight years of gendarmerie-police rule, land survey, press restrictions, and a wartime economy that raised prices and rice exports. At the same time, the end of World War I changed international politics. The collapse of the Russian, German, and Austro-Hungarian empires, the Russian revolutions of 1917, and Woodrow Wilson's public support for national self-determination encouraged colonized peoples around the world to expect that the postwar settlement might reconsider imperial control. Wilson's principles were, in practice, applied mainly to Europe, but Korean activists, like nationalists in Egypt, India, and China, drew on the language of self-determination in framing their own claims.

Korean communities abroad moved first. In Shanghai, activists formed the New Korean Youth Party and sent Kim Gyu-sik to Paris in 1919 with a petition to the peace conference. In the United States, Syngman Rhee and others pursued appeals to American officials. Most influential for events within Korea was the Korean student community in Tokyo. On 8 February 1919, several hundred Korean students gathered at the YMCA in Tokyo and issued a declaration of independence, drafted principally by Yi Gwang-su, calling on Japan and the world to recognize Korean independence. Police arrested organizers, but the declaration circulated, and emissaries carried news to Korea.

Inside Korea, leaders of religious organizations began planning independently. Cheondogyo, a religion that grew out of the Donghak movement, had a large organization and funds, and its leader Son Byeong-hi was a central figure. Christian leaders, particularly Presbyterians and Methodists, such as Yi Seung-hun and Gil Seon-ju, also joined, and a Buddhist monk, Han Yong-un, took part. The organizers deliberately chose a nonviolent strategy: a public declaration followed by peaceful appeals, rather than armed revolt.

The death of Gojong on 21 January 1919 gave the plan a focus. Rumors spread that the former emperor had been poisoned, and that Japanese officials were responsible; historians note that these claims were widely believed in Korea, but that evidence for the rumor is not conclusive. Whatever the truth, the planned state funeral for 3 March would bring crowds to Seoul from across the country. The organizers chose 1 March, two days before the funeral, for the proclamation. Gojong's death thus provided both an emotional trigger and a logistical opportunity.

Section 62.14

March First, 1919: The Declaration of Independence and Nationwide Demonstrations

The Declaration of Independence was signed by thirty-three representatives, including about fifteen Cheondogyo leaders, sixteen Christians, and two Buddhists, among them Son Byeong-hi, Gil Seon-ju, Yi Seung-hun, and Han Yong-un. The text was drafted largely by the writer and publisher Choe Nam-seon, and Han Yong-un contributed a three-point pledge. On the afternoon of 1 March the signatories gathered at a restaurant called Taehwagwan in Seoul rather than at Pagoda Park, where a crowd of students and others had assembled. They read the declaration privately, telephoned the police, and were arrested. At Pagoda Park, a student, Jeong Jae-yong, read the declaration publicly, and the crowd marched into the streets shouting for Korean independence. It is important to distinguish these two events: the signing and reading by the thirty-three at Taehwagwan, and the public reading and march at Pagoda Park.

Printed copies of the declaration had been sent in advance to cities including Pyongyang, Wonsan, Uiju, and Haeju, and demonstrations took place on the same day in several northern towns. In the weeks that followed, protest spread through the countryside, with crowds gathering in market towns, often on market days, reading the declaration, raising Korean flags, and shouting slogans. The movement continued through March, April, and into May. Databases compiled by the National Institute of Korean History record well over a thousand separate demonstrations, in every province, though the number depends on the criteria used to define an event.

Participation extended beyond the elite signers. Students, including young women such as those of Ewha school and girls' high schools, carried the message; Yu Gwan-sun, a student of Ewha, organized a demonstration in the Aunae market near Cheonan in April and later died in prison in 1920. Farmers, merchants, laborers, Confucian scholars, Christians, Cheondogyo followers, Buddhists, and, in some places, kisaeng (female entertainers) joined local protests. The early phase was strongly nonviolent; in rural areas, as repression increased, some demonstrations turned to attacks on police stations, local offices, and sometimes Japanese or Korean officials considered collaborators.

The declaration's language and strategy reflected its authors' views. It did not call for revolt against Japanese people; it appealed to principles of justice and humanity and urged the Korean people to act in an orderly manner. Its three-point pledge emphasized that the action was for the sake of the whole nation, that people should express their will to the end, and that all actions should be orderly. Some Koreans, especially those who favored armed struggle, saw its approach as too moderate, while others saw the nonviolent discipline as a source of its moral authority. Historians generally treat the movement as a national event that crossed class, region, religion, and gender, while emphasizing that its participants had varied motives and local circumstances.

Section 62.15

Colonial Repression and the Human Cost of March First

The Government-General responded with force. Gendarmes and police, reinforced by regular army units from within Korea and troops sent from Japan, fired on crowds, arrested participants, and conducted house-to-house searches. Churches, schools, and houses were burned in places identified as centers of activity. Courts tried thousands of people, and many were sentenced to prison terms and some were beaten or tortured in custody. Women students and other detainees reported abuse.

The best-known incident of village-level violence occurred on 15 April 1919 at Jeam-ri, in Hwaseong county south of Seoul. Japanese soldiers entered the village after local demonstrations, gathered a number of men in a church, and fired on them, and burned the building; they also burned houses. A Canadian missionary, Frank Schofield, and American missionaries investigated and reported on the killings, and their accounts, together with press reports, brought international attention. Other places experienced similar episodes. Some Japanese observers within Japan, including the political thinker Yoshino Sakuzō and the folk-art theorist Yanagi Muneyoshi (later associated with the mingei movement discussed in Chapter 72), publicly criticized the harsh handling of Korea, though their criticism rarely extended to renouncing imperial control.

Numbers are disputed and should always be attributed. The nationalist historian Park Eun-sik, writing in 1920, estimated about 7,500 Koreans killed, nearly 16,000 wounded, and some 47,000 arrested, and he put total participation at roughly two million. Government-General figures were far lower: on the order of 550 killed, 1,400 wounded, and 12,500 arrested. Modern researchers using police, court, prison, mission, and diplomatic records have refined some of these figures but have not agreed on a single total. The differences result from the sources' purposes, from varying definitions of participation and of the period counted, and from uncertain record keeping in the countryside.

The consequences extended beyond casualties. The movement did not achieve independence, and in the short term it was suppressed. But it persuaded many Koreans that peaceful appeals to the postwar powers would not alone succeed, strengthened organizations in exile, and led many activists to shift toward armed or diplomatic strategies or toward long-term cultural and social movements. It also persuaded the Japanese government that rule solely through gendarmes was politically and diplomatically costly.

Cause and Effect

The March First Movement

Long-term causes

The loss of sovereignty between 1905 and 1910; eight years of gendarmerie rule, press restrictions, and land and tax changes; the survival of religious, educational, and patriotic networks.

Short-term causes

Wartime economic pressure, self-determination rhetoric after World War I, the Tokyo students' declaration of 8 February, and the death of Gojong and the planned funeral.

The event

A declaration of independence by thirty-three leaders and mass demonstrations lasting from March into May across the peninsula.

Immediate effects

Violent repression, mass arrests, village-level atrocities such as Jeam-ri, international criticism, and the creation of exile governments.

Long-term consequences: Reforms in colonial administration, expansion of Korean-language publishing, the Provisional Government and exile politics, a shift in strategies among nationalists, and a lasting place for March First in Korean national memory.

Historical complexity: The movement cannot be explained by the death of Gojong or by Wilsonian ideas alone. It depended on preexisting religious networks, student organization, anger over colonial rule, and local decisions to participate or refrain, and its meaning has been contested by later nationalist, socialist, and state narratives.

Section 62.16

The Korean Provisional Government and the International Independence Movement

In the spring of 1919, several exile governments emerged. A Korean National Assembly formed in Vladivostok in March among Koreans in the Russian Far East. In Shanghai, activists proclaimed a Provisional Government on 11 April and adopted a provisional charter declaring the Republic of Korea (Daehan Minguk) with sovereignty in the people, a break from monarchy. In Seoul, a document proclaiming another "Hanseong Government" circulated in April, with Syngman Rhee named as a leader though he was in the United States. In September, representatives of these groups agreed to consolidate in Shanghai under a unified provisional government, with Rhee as president, Yi Dong-hwi as prime minister, and Ahn Chang-ho as a key organizer.

The Provisional Government faced severe obstacles. It lacked territory, an army, and a secure revenue base, relied on contributions from Korean communities in China, the United States, and elsewhere, and operated under pressure from Japanese and French-concession police in Shanghai. Leaders disagreed about strategy. Rhee favored diplomacy and appeal to the Western powers; others, including Yi Dong-hwi, prioritized armed struggle and ties with the Soviet Union; others emphasized education and preparation. In 1925 the Provisional Government's legislative body impeached Rhee, in part over his absence from Shanghai and a dispute about his earlier petition to the League of Nations proposing mandate status for Korea, and the leadership changed hands several times.

Despite its weakness, the government served as a symbol of continuity of the Korean state and as an institutional center for exile networks. It maintained a newspaper, issued documents, sent representatives to international forums such as the Washington Conference of 1921–22, and later moved with the Chinese Nationalist government through several cities, eventually settling in Chongqing in 1940. Historians differ in their assessments: some emphasize its limited practical power, while others stress its role in creating a republican and constitutional tradition in Korean politics. Its documents, including provisional constitutions, have been used in later South Korean arguments about the legitimacy of the Republic of Korea, a modern political use that should be distinguished from the government's own historical record.

Section 62.17

The Shift to So-Called Cultural Rule: Reform, Surveillance, and Continuing Colonial Power

In August 1919 the government appointed Admiral Saitō Makoto as Governor-General under Prime Minister Hara Takashi, who favored an approach sometimes described as extending the homeland to the colony and assimilating Koreans over time. Saitō arrived in Seoul in September, and a Korean nationalist, Kang U-gyu, threw a bomb at his carriage at the station, an attack that failed to kill him. Under the new policy, the gendarmerie-police system was abolished and police functions were reassigned to a regular civil police force, officials ceased wearing swords, the flogging ordinance was repealed, and the government allowed Korean-language newspapers, including the Dong-A Ilbo and the Chosun Ilbo, to begin publication in 1920. Some associations and literary and cultural societies were permitted under licensing.

Historians treat "cultural rule" cautiously. The title was a Japanese official slogan, and many Korean scholars use "so-called cultural rule" to signal skepticism. The reforms were real and affected Korean public life: Korean-language publishing and civic associations expanded, and a more public, if tightly monitored, sphere emerged. But police numbers, stations, and budgets increased, censorship routinely suppressed articles and entire issues, political organizations were closely watched, and in 1925 the Peace Preservation Law was applied to Korea and used against independence and socialist movements. New advisory bodies at provincial and local levels were elected by a narrow, property-based electorate and had limited powers. The system did not provide Koreans with representation in either Korea or the Diet.

The policy also served diplomatic purposes. The Japanese government hoped to reduce international criticism after March First and to win moderate Korean leaders to cooperation, while dividing the nationalist movement between those who would work within the system, such as advocates of gradual economic and cultural development, and those who rejected any cooperation. Some Koreans took advantage of the new openings to promote education, enterprise, and publishing, a choice that later generated controversy over how to judge such activity (Section 62.28).

Division IV

Economy, Society, Education, and Everyday Life under Colonial Rule

Sections 62.18–62.23 · 1910s–1930s
Section 62.18

The Rice Production Increase Program and Agricultural Restructuring

Korea's economy remained overwhelmingly agricultural throughout the colonial period, and rice became the commodity linking Korean villages to the Japanese empire. After the Rice Riots of 1918 in Japan (Chapter 68) exposed the vulnerability of Japan's food supply, the Government-General launched the Rice Production Increase Program in 1920, intended to raise output through irrigation, improved seed, fertilizer, and better cultivation. A first plan ran in the early 1920s, and a second, larger plan began in 1926 and continued until it was suspended in the mid-1930s.

Output did rise. Irrigation associations built reservoirs and canals, commercial fertilizer use expanded, and new rice varieties spread. Yet the program's costs and benefits were unevenly distributed. Irrigation associations were financed by loans and fees that fell on landowners and, through rents, on tenants, and the program's credit mechanisms favored larger landholders and Japanese-owned enterprises. Many small farmers fell into debt. Meanwhile, the expanding rice trade directed a rising share of the harvest to Japan, at times approaching half of the crop. Per-capita rice consumption among Koreans, as measured by official statistics, declined, and cheaper grains such as millet imported from Manchuria filled part of the gap.

The interpretation of these data is a matter of method. Higher production and exports show that colonial agricultural policy did increase output; falling domestic consumption and rural indebtedness show that the benefits did not reach many cultivators. Historians also point out that official statistics were produced by the colonial government and that figures for production, consumption, and rice stocks can vary. The program's purpose was explicit: to supply the Japanese market. Its consequences for Korean farm households depended on whether they owned land, how much they rented, what rent and debts they owed, and whether they lived in rice-exporting regions.

Section 62.19

Landlords, Tenant Farmers, Rural Protest, and Migration

Colonial rural society was marked by a high rate of tenancy. By around 1930 a large majority of Korean farm households either rented all of their land or rented part of it in addition to owning some. Tenants typically paid a substantial share of the harvest, often around half, as rent, and in many cases bore additional charges such as taxes on land or irrigation fees by contract. Landlords included Korean notables, absentee owners living in towns, and Japanese individuals and companies. Registration of ownership after the land survey strengthened the owner's legal position, while customary rights of cultivators were generally not protected, so tenants could be evicted or have their rents raised with little recourse.

Tenants responded through petitions, collective negotiation, and organized disputes. Tenancy disputes increased sharply in the 1920s, culminating in prolonged conflicts such as that at Amtae Island in South Jeolla in 1923–1924, where tenants protested high rents. Peasant unions and, later, left-leaning organizations sought to coordinate action. The Government-General treated many such disputes as public-order issues and sometimes mediated, and in the 1930s it introduced tenancy regulations and rural relief measures, notably the Rural Revitalization Campaign launched under Governor-General Ugaki after the agricultural depression of the early 1930s. These policies sought stability and mobilization of rural society rather than a basic change in landholding.

Poverty and debt also drove migration. Some Korean farmers moved to Manchuria and Siberia, joining existing communities; others went to Japan, where Korean workers found jobs in factories, mines, and construction, and where the Korean population grew from a few tens of thousands around 1920 to hundreds of thousands by the 1930s and, with wartime mobilization, to about two million by 1945. These migrants faced low wages, discrimination in housing and employment, and, in the aftermath of the 1923 Kantō earthquake, deadly violence, a subject treated in Chapter 71. Migration thus tied the Korean countryside to Japan's cities and industries and created the foundations of the later Zainichi community.

Everyday Life and Environment

A Tenant Household in the 1920s

A typical tenant household in the southern plains consisted of an extended or stem family working a few small plots of rented paddy and dry fields. The year followed the rice cycle: seedbed preparation in spring, transplanting in early summer, weeding and water management through the hot months, and harvest in autumn, when the landlord's agent or the landlord himself would claim a fixed share. Households often supplemented rice with barley, beans, vegetables, and wild plants, and rural women worked in the fields as well as at spinning, weaving, food preparation, and child care. Children, especially boys, might attend a village school if fees and work obligations allowed; girls were less likely to attend.

Debt was a constant concern. Families borrowed grain or money in the spring before harvest, often from landlords, moneylenders, or, increasingly, credit associations, and repaid with interest. A poor harvest, a price decline, or illness could force a household to give up land, sell a daughter's labor, or send a family member to a city, a mine, Manchuria, or Japan. Taxes and compulsory labor service added further burdens. Yet households also developed strategies: mutual aid among neighbors and kin, cooperative labor groups, and, in the 1920s, tenant unions. Evidence for these lives comes from statistical surveys, Government-General reports, tenant petitions, social investigations, newspapers, folklore studies, and oral histories, all of which must be read with attention to who produced them.

Section 62.20

Industrialization, Railways, Hydroelectric Power, and Colonial Cities

Industrial development grew slowly until the 1920s and accelerated in the 1930s, when Japan's military and economic strategy shifted toward heavy industry and the northern parts of the peninsula became attractive for chemicals, mining, and power. After the Company Ordinance was abolished in 1920, Japanese firms invested in Korea more freely, and in the 1930s large corporate groups, including Noguchi Shitagau's enterprises, built hydroelectric plants on the Pujon (Bujeon) River and the Chosin (Jangjin) region and, linked to them, a large chemical-fertilizer complex at Hungnam. A giant dam on the Yalu River at Sup'ung (Suiho) was completed during the Second World War. Mining of gold, coal, iron ore, and other minerals expanded. By the late 1930s, measured manufacturing output had grown to rival and then exceed agricultural output in value, although the majority of Koreans still worked on the land.

Korean entrepreneurs also participated. The Gyeongseong Spinning Company, studied in Carter Eckert's Offspring of Empire, grew out of a Korean landowning family's investments and became one of the most prominent Korean-owned industrial enterprises, with strong ties to colonial banks and officials. Korean-owned shops, small factories, and trading firms flourished in Seoul and other cities. But Japanese capital dominated large industries, and Korean workers, including women in textile mills and men in mines and factories, typically received lower wages than Japanese workers and were concentrated in unskilled jobs and in lower-level supervisory roles.

Cities changed visibly. Seoul (Keijō) developed a Japanese commercial district in the south around Honmachi (today's Myeongdong), while many Koreans lived and ran businesses in the northern quarter around Jongno. Todd Henry's study of colonial Seoul shows how urban sanitation campaigns, public ceremonies, parks, and shrines were used to reshape the city. Pyongyang developed as a commercial and Christian center, Busan as the main link with Japan, and new industrial towns appeared in the north. Department stores, cafés, trams, cinemas, and bookstores introduced a modern urban culture in which Korean and Japanese worlds overlapped and remained unequal. Keijō Imperial University, opened in 1926, symbolized higher education but enrolled many Japanese students.

Historical Debate

Colonial Development, Inequality, and the Meaning of "Modernization"

The question: Colonial rule coincided with railways, factories, rising output, and new cities. How should historians understand this growth: as exploitation, as modernization, or as something else?

Interpretation A — Exploitation and distorted development: Many Korean historians, and scholars of dependency and extraction, argue that colonial policy served Japanese interests: rice exports, cheap labor, markets, and military strategy. Infrastructure and industry were built mainly for Japanese capital and defense, Korean ownership and skilled employment were restricted, and political rights were denied. Growth, on this view, occurred despite Korean needs and at their cost.
Interpretation B — Colonial modernization or growth: Some economic historians, in Korea, Japan, and elsewhere, using national-accounts estimates, stress that production, trade, literacy, and urbanization expanded substantially, that institutions of property, finance, and education established under Japanese rule persisted after 1945, and that dismissing these facts distorts the record.
Interpretation C — Colonial modernity: A third approach, associated with works such as those edited by Gi-Wook Shin and Michael Robinson, treats modern institutions and new forms of life as real but inseparable from colonial hierarchy, and focuses on how Koreans experienced, used, contested, and sometimes benefited from them.

Evidence: The debate depends on statistics produced by the colonial government, later reconstructions of national accounts, labor and wage records, landholding data, trade figures, and memoirs. Aggregate growth does not show who owned capital, who received income, or what choices Koreans had.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these arguments shapes interpretations of colonial responsibility, of the origins of post-1945 Korean economies, and of present-day political disputes over memory.

Section 62.21

Schools, Literacy, Korean Language, and Colonial Educational Policy

The 1911 Chōsen Education Ordinance created a separate system for Koreans, whose common schools were shorter than the elementary schools of Japanese children and stressed Japanese language and vocational skills. The educational ordinance revised in 1922 declared a unified system, with Japanese and Korean schools following parallel curricula and a common length of six years, and with the criterion for admission being, in principle, Japanese-language ability rather than nationality. In practice, schools remained largely separate: Japanese children attended elementary schools and Korean children mostly common schools, and higher schools accepted a small number of Koreans.

Access to schooling expanded but remained limited. Fees, distance, and household labor needs kept many children, especially girls and the rural poor, out of school. Only a minority of Korean children attended in the 1910s and 1920s, and by the early 1940s roughly half of school-age Korean children were enrolled in primary schooling, with girls well below boys. Demand for schooling was strong, and Koreans pressed the government and built private and community schools, night schools, and village reading groups. Korean-language literacy campaigns, such as a popular movement of university students in the early 1930s sponsored by newspapers, taught thousands of adults to read Hangul.

Language became the central issue. Colonial schooling taught Japanese as the language of instruction, but Korean was initially kept as a subject, and Korean scholars worked on standardizing Hangul orthography and compiling a dictionary through the Korean Language Society (Joseon Eohakhoe). In the late 1930s and early 1940s, Korean-language teaching was reduced and then eliminated from the school curriculum (Section 62.30). Higher education followed a similar pattern of limited access: private institutions such as Yonhi and Boseong colleges provided Korean-run higher education, but the elite state-run Keijō Imperial University, established in 1926, enrolled mainly Japanese students.

Section 62.22

Religion, Newspapers, Literature, and Korean Cultural Renewal

Religious institutions were important social spaces under colonial rule. Protestant Christianity, especially strong in the northwest, with Pyongyang sometimes called the "Jerusalem of the East," supplied schools, hospitals, and networks, and many Korean Christians participated in nationalist activity. Buddhism, regulated by the Temple Ordinance of 1911 that placed head temples under government oversight, produced reform voices, including the monk and poet Han Yong-un. Cheondogyo maintained a large rural following and a printing apparatus, and new movements such as Won Buddhism (founded in 1916) and Daejonggyo, which venerated the legendary founder Dangun, developed in the colonial period. Government policy sought to control religious institutions while promoting State Shinto, a conflict that sharpened in the 1930s (Section 62.29).

The press and publishing expanded after 1920 within the limits of censorship. The Dong-A Ilbo and Chosun Ilbo reported on social issues, promoted education and rural campaigns, and nurtured a Korean-language public sphere, while censors confiscated issues and ordered deletions. Magazines carried debates on socialism, women, literature, and national culture. Literature flourished in diverse forms: Yi Gwang-su's serialized novels, Yeom Sang-seop's urban realism, Kim Dong-in's short stories, Han Yong-un's poetry, and the verse of Kim Sowol and, later, Yun Dong-ju, who died in a Fukuoka prison in 1945. Proletarian writers formed the KAPF federation in 1925. Early Korean films, such as Arirang (1926), also appeared.

Scholarship on Korean history and language became a form of cultural preservation. Historians such as Sin Chae-ho, who wrote about Korea's ancient past and later died in a Japanese prison in Lüshun, and Park Eun-sik, as well as scholars of the Joseon studies (Joseonhak) movement of the 1930s, produced Korean-centered accounts. The Government-General, in turn, sponsored its own historical projects, including a history-compilation committee in the 1920s that promoted narratives emphasizing Korean dependence and stagnation, an interpretation Korean and later scholars have criticized as colonial historiography. Cultural renewal was not unified: nationalist, religious, liberal, and socialist writers debated what Korean identity should be, and many writers' choices changed with political pressure.

Section 62.23

Women, Families, Gender Reform, and Everyday Colonial Society

Women's lives under colonial rule were shaped by Korean patriarchal custom, colonial law, economic change, and new ideas of education and "the New Woman" (sin-yeoseong). Girls' schools, many founded by missionaries, such as Ewha, educated a small number of women who entered teaching, nursing, journalism, and the arts. Na Hye-sok, a painter and writer, published fiction and essays criticizing marriage and women's subordination and became a celebrated and controversial figure; Kim Il-yeop, Kim Hwal-lan, and others took varied positions on feminism, nationalism, and socialism. In 1927 a national women's organization, Geunuhoe, was formed in association with the Singanhoe movement (Section 62.25). Theodore Jun Yoo's study of gender in colonial Korea shows how debates over marriage, education, and sexuality became part of both nationalist and colonial projects.

Law and policy affected the family. Colonial courts applied selected "customs" in matters of marriage, inheritance, and household headship, strengthening the male household head's formal authority. Reformers argued against early marriage, concubinage, and restrictions on widows, and some Korean women's organizations pressed for change. Ideals of "wise mother, good wife" circulated in both Japanese and Korean contexts, while many women, especially working-class and rural, labored in fields, textile mills, rubber factories, and domestic service. In 1931 a rubber-factory worker, Kang Ju-ryong, climbed onto the roof of the historic Eulmildae Pavilion in Pyongyang in May 1931 to protest wage cuts at the Pyeongwon Rubber Factory, an episode remembered as a symbol of women's labor activism.

Urban life offered new experiences: department stores, cafés, cinemas, magazines, and fashions introduced consumer culture, and "modern girls" and "modern boys" (modeon geol, modeon boi) became subjects of fascination and criticism. At the same time, entertainers such as kisaeng worked within a regulated and sexualized economy shaped by both Korean tradition and colonial licensing.

Section 62.24

Armed Independence Movements in Manchuria and Siberia, 1919–1920

After March First, many activists concluded that independence required armed struggle. The base for this effort was the Korean exile population across the Tumen and Yalu rivers. Jiandao (Gando) in the Chinese borderland and the Maritime Province of Russia already contained hundreds of thousands of Korean farmers, along with schools, churches, and associations. Their territory lay beyond the Government-General's direct control, though within reach of Japanese diplomatic and military pressure. Armed groups, some of them continuous with the righteous armies, formed there in 1919–1920 under leaders such as Hong Beom-do, Kim Jwa-jin, Yi Beom-seok, and Ji Cheong-cheon, with support from Korean communities and funds from sympathizers abroad.

Two engagements in 1920 became central to Korean historical memory. In June, a force combining Hong Beom-do's units and others defeated a Japanese pursuing detachment at Bongodong, near the Tumen River. In October, units under Kim Jwa-jin and Hong Beom-do fought Japanese forces in the Cheongsanri area over several days; Korean accounts describe it as a major victory, while Japanese sources describe different casualty totals and circumstances. The exact numbers on both sides remain contested, and historians use Korean, Japanese, and Chinese sources with care.

Japanese retaliation fell heavily on civilians. During the autumn of 1920, Japanese troops, using the pretext of suppressing "bandits" in the Jiandao region, burned villages, executed people, and destroyed schools and churches in what Korean writers call the Gyeongsin Massacre. Estimates of Korean deaths range from a few thousand to considerably more, and the figure depends on whether sources count only confirmed killings or broader losses. Armed groups withdrew, regrouped, or moved north into Soviet territory, where in 1921 conflict and disarmament in the "Free City" (Alexeyevsk/Svobodny) incident further weakened them. In 1925 the Mitsuya Agreement between the Government-General's police chief and the Manchurian authorities of Zhang Zuolin increased pressure on Korean armed groups in China. Armed resistance continued on a smaller scale, but the large-unit campaigns of 1920 were not repeated.

Section 62.25

Domestic Mass Politics: Students, Workers, Farmers, and the Gwangju Movement

Inside Korea, the 1920s saw a widening range of public movements. Labor unions and peasant organizations formed, including federations coordinating workers and tenants, and strikes occurred in ports, mills, and mines. A large dock-worker strike at Wonsan in 1929 lasted for months and drew solidarity from other regions. Youth societies, student associations, and women's groups organized lectures, night schools, and campaigns. Socialist and nationalist ideas competed and sometimes combined.

The death of the deposed Emperor Sunjong in April 1926 offered another occasion for mass action. On the day of his funeral, 10 June 1926, students and others distributed leaflets and shouted independence slogans along the procession route in Seoul. The June 10 Movement was smaller than March First and quickly suppressed, with several hundred arrests, but it demonstrated the continuing role of students and links between nationalists and socialists. The following year, in February 1927, nationalists and socialists formed the Singanhoe (New Korea Society), a united-front organization that campaigned for political and economic rights, supported tenant and labor disputes, and cooperated with the women's association Geunuhoe. It was dissolved in 1931 after internal disagreement over cooperation and amid police pressure.

The Gwangju Student Movement of 1929 grew from a confrontation on a train between Korean and Japanese students in late October, in which Korean girls were reportedly harassed, and from conflict over the police response. Protests began on 3 November, the anniversary date of the Meiji Emperor's birthday celebrated as a holiday, and spread across the country over the following months, involving well over a hundred schools and tens of thousands of students. Students raised demands including release of arrested classmates, an end to discriminatory treatment, and, in many cases, independence. The movement was the largest student-led protest since 1919. These movements show that colonial politics in the 1920s was not confined to petitions or exile; it included organized labor, peasants, students, women, and mixed nationalist and socialist coalitions.

Section 62.26

Exile Politics, the Provisional Government, and International Independence Campaigns

By the late 1920s, the Provisional Government was weak and divided. Kim Gu, a former Donghak follower and veteran of the independence movement, took on a larger role, and in 1931 he organized the Korean Patriotic Organization (Hanin Aeguktan) in Shanghai, promoting targeted acts against Japanese officials. In January 1932, Yi Bong-chang threw a bomb at the Japanese emperor's procession in Tokyo; he failed to kill the emperor and was executed. On 29 April 1932, Yun Bong-gil, a member of the organization, threw a bomb at a Japanese celebration in Hongkou Park in Shanghai, killing General Shirakawa Yoshinori and wounding other Japanese officials. The action drew the attention of the Chinese Nationalist government, which increased its support for Korean exiles, though it also brought Japanese pressure and forced Korean leaders to move.

The Provisional Government relocated through several Chinese cities as the Sino-Japanese War spread, finally settling in Chongqing in 1940. Other Korean exile organizations operated alongside it. Kim Won-bong's Righteous Fraternity (Uiyeoldan), founded in 1919, advocated direct action, and in 1938 he formed the Korean Volunteer Corps in Hankou, which later partly merged into the Korean Liberation Army (Hanguk Gwangbokgun), established by the Provisional Government in September 1940. This force numbered a few hundred to a few thousand people and engaged mostly in intelligence, propaganda, and liaison work with Chinese and Allied forces rather than in large battles. In the United States, Syngman Rhee and others lobbied officials for recognition. The Cairo Declaration of December 1943 stated that Korea would "in due course" become free and independent, a phrasing that Koreans welcomed but also noted as conditional.

Exile politics illustrates both determination and fragmentation. Leaders differed about strategy, ideology, and the role of foreign powers. Many exile activists lived in poverty, and the movement depended on the fortunes of host governments. At the same time, it kept alive an organizational tradition and links with the Allied powers that mattered in 1945, though the Allies did not formally recognize the Provisional Government.

Section 62.27

Socialist, Communist, and Guerrilla Resistance Movements

Marxist ideas reached Korea through Japan, China, and the Soviet Union in the years after 1917, and attracted students, writers, laborers, and tenant organizers. The Korean Communist Party was founded secretly in Seoul in 1925. It was repeatedly broken up by police arrests and, in 1928, the Communist International withdrew recognition because of factionalism. Communists nevertheless remained active in labor unions, peasant associations, and student groups. In the 1930s, "red" peasant and labor unions emerged in parts of northern Korea such as Hamgyeong province, and police repression through the Peace Preservation Law led to thousands of arrests.

In Manchuria, many Korean communists joined the Chinese Communist Party rather than organizing separately, following Comintern policy. After Japan's occupation of Manchuria in 1931–32 (Chapter 75), anti-Japanese guerrilla forces formed, including units later grouped into the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army, with a large number of Korean participants. Korean fighters were also victims of internal suspicion: in the early 1930s, the Chinese Communist movement in the Jiandao region conducted purges of Koreans suspected of being Japanese spies in the so-called Minsaengdan affair. Japanese counterinsurgency combined military campaigns, special police and army detachments, pacification, and the forced relocation of villagers into guarded settlements. One Korean commander, Kim Il Sung, led a small unit that raided the border town of Pochonbo in 1937; North Korea later made this episode central to its national narrative, while historians evaluate its scale and significance using Japanese, Chinese, and Korean sources.

This history should be studied on its own terms rather than through later North–South Korean political narratives. Both North and South Korea developed selective memories of anti-colonial resistance, elevating some figures and organizations and downplaying others. Historians distinguish the Provisional Government, nationalist reformers, socialist and communist activists, guerrilla units, and diaspora communities, and note that their goals, social bases, and relations with each other varied, from cooperation to bitter conflict.

Section 62.28

Collaboration, Accommodation, Coercion, and the Limits of Individual Choice

Colonial rule depended on Korean participation. Korean officials staffed local government, Korean police and auxiliaries worked in the security apparatus, Korean teachers taught in colonial schools, Korean landlords and businesspeople worked with banks and officials, and Korean intellectuals wrote for colonial-licensed publications. In Korean public memory, those who cooperated with Japan are called chinilpa ("pro-Japanese"), a term of moral condemnation. For historians, the category is too broad to explain behavior without distinctions.

A useful analysis separates several kinds of cooperation. Some Koreans supported Japanese rule ideologically, believing in Japan's civilizational mission or in assimilation: the leaders of the Ilchinhoe and officials such as Yi Wan-yong are examples. Others cooperated for economic interest: entrepreneurs who needed licenses, credit, or contracts, such as the founders of Gyeongseong Spinning, or landlords whose property depended on colonial law. Others took accommodating positions in pursuit of gradual cultural or economic development, working within permitted institutions while hoping to strengthen Korean society for a future independence; critics called this approach collaboration, while defenders saw it as pragmatic. Still others, such as policemen, clerks, and village heads, were drawn into the system as a livelihood or as a condition of protecting their families. Some individuals shifted over time: Yi Gwang-su, author of the 1919 Tokyo declaration, later supported wartime assimilation and encouraged Korean students to volunteer for the military.

Distinguishing these cases requires evidence about circumstances, alternatives, and consequences. Historians such as Yun Hae-dong have used the term "gray zone" to describe the wide space between active resistance and enthusiastic collaboration where most Koreans lived. Using this term does not erase responsibility or deny that some Koreans committed or enabled harm, including police abuse or the recruitment of laborers. It asks that judgments about individuals be based on what they did and under what pressures, rather than on a binary label. After 1945, the question became politically charged in both Koreas, and in 2009 a South Korean government commission published a report identifying roughly a thousand individuals as pro-Japanese collaborators, a document whose criteria and conclusions remain debated.

Section 62.29

Imperial Ideology: Naisen Ittai, State Shinto, and the Imperial Subject

After Japan's occupation of Manchuria in 1931 and especially after the start of the full-scale war in China in 1937, colonial policy moved toward intensified assimilation. Under Governor-General Minami Jirō, who took office in 1936, the government promoted naisen ittai ("Japan and Korea as one body") and kōminka ("making imperial subjects"). The goal was to bind Koreans to the emperor and the war effort by promoting Japanese language, Shinto-style rituals, loyalty oaths, and cultural conformity. In 1937 the Government-General introduced an Oath of Imperial Subjects, recited in schools, offices, and gatherings, and required participation in rituals such as bowing toward the imperial palace.

Shrine policy was central. The Chōsen Shrine (Chōsen Jingū) was dedicated on Namsan in Seoul in 1925, enshrining the Sun Goddess Amaterasu and Emperor Meiji, and in the late 1930s the government encouraged or required the building of shrines in towns and villages and attendance at ceremonies. Government officials insisted that shrine worship was a civic and patriotic duty rather than a religion, a claim that Christians and others rejected. Many mission schools and churches faced a choice between participating and closing. In 1938 the Presbyterian General Assembly, under heavy pressure, accepted shrine attendance, while some pastors and believers refused and were imprisoned, and some mission institutions closed rather than comply.

The policy contained a contradiction that critics noticed then and historians have analyzed since. The slogan proclaimed unity, but legal and institutional inequalities persisted: separate family registers, no parliamentary representation in Korea, discriminatory wage and educational patterns, and limits on advancement. Some Koreans, particularly younger and better-educated people, took the slogan seriously and demanded equality as a condition of loyalty. Officials, by contrast, saw assimilation as a means of mobilization rather than as a commitment to equal status.

Historical Vocabulary
Naisen ittai, kōminka, and sōshi-kaimei

Naisen ittai (内鮮一体) means "the inner land (Japan) and Korea as one body." It was an official slogan of the late 1930s that asserted unity while leaving legal differences in place. Kōminka means "turning into imperial subjects" and refers to the program of ritual, language, and ideological assimilation, including the Oath of Imperial Subjects and shrine participation. Sōshi-kaimei (創氏改名) refers to the 1939–1940 policy in which Koreans were required to register a family name (uji) in the Japanese manner and could apply to change their personal given name.

These terms should not be read as equivalent to the voluntary adoption of a foreign culture or to political equality. They describe state policies backed by administrative pressure. The English word "assimilation" is used by historians, but they emphasize its coercive character and that Japanese officials simultaneously sustained legal and social hierarchy.

Section 62.30

Language Policy, Schools, and Sōshi-Kaimei: The Pressure to Become Japanese

Educational policy followed the ideological shift. The Third Chōsen Education Ordinance of 1938 renamed Korean schools to match Japanese ones and made Korean language an optional subject in practice. In 1941 elementary schools were reorganized as "national schools" (kokumin gakkō), and by 1943 a further revision removed Korean from the curriculum. Students were pressed to speak Japanese ("national language") at school, and some schools imposed penalties for speaking Korean. In wider society, officials and newspapers promoted Japanese-language use in offices, shops, and public places, though large numbers of Koreans continued to speak Korean in daily life, and many adults never learned Japanese well.

The name policy is among the best-known and most carefully studied. Ordinances issued in late 1939 and effective on 11 February 1940 required household heads to register a family name for the household, intended to reshape family structure along Japanese lines. A separate provision allowed people to apply to change given names. Officially the changes were presented as voluntary in the case of personal names and mandatory in the case of the family name; if a family did not register one within the set period, the surname would be recorded automatically as the existing clan name. Korean women traditionally kept their own lineage names after marriage, so the policy touched the Korean kinship system directly.

Research distinguishes these elements, while showing that formal categories did not capture the pressure involved. Government officials, police, and school and local authorities urged compliance, and families that did not register Japanese-style names could face difficulties for their children at school, in employment, or in access to rationed goods and services. By the registration deadline in August 1940, a large majority of households had registered new family names, though some people devised strategies to keep lineage identity, for example by choosing names that retained references to their clan origin, and some protested. Korean memoirs and later oral histories record a range of reactions from humiliation to calculated acceptance, and scholars warn against reading either simple victimhood or simple consent into the statistics of registration.

Section 62.31

Surveillance, Censorship, Political Policing, and Cultural Suppression

The 1930s and 1940s saw a tightening of police and ideological control. The Special Higher Police (tokkō) and thought police divisions monitored and prosecuted political dissent under the Peace Preservation Law, and a 1936 ordinance allowed "ideological offenders" who had been released to be kept under supervision and, in some cases, detained preventively. Mass arrests broke up communist and nationalist networks, and many activists were pressed to renounce their beliefs in "conversion" (tenkō) procedures, in Korea as in Japan.

Censorship also intensified. A well-known incident occurred in 1936 when the Dong-A Ilbo published a photograph of the Korean marathon runner Sohn Kee-chung (Son Gi-jeong), winner of the gold medal at the Berlin Olympics, with the Japanese flag on his uniform erased; the government suspended the paper for several months and several staff were punished. Sohn had competed under the Japanese name Son Kitei. The case shows how a sports victory became a flashpoint for national feeling. In August 1940 the authorities forced the Dong-A Ilbo and Chosun Ilbo to cease publication, leaving the Government-General's Korean-language newspaper as the main remaining Korean-language daily.

Language work was targeted directly. In October 1942, police arrested dozens of members of the Korean Language Society (Joseon Eohakhoe), which had been compiling a Korean dictionary, on charges of promoting nationalist aims through language work. Several members were imprisoned, and two died in custody. The incident is remembered as an attempt to extinguish Korean as a written and scholarly language. Church services and clergy were also monitored, and religious groups judged insufficiently loyal were repressed. The relationship between colonial security and wartime ideology changed during these years: policing that had focused on organized political opposition expanded to cover cultural symbols, speech, names, and private expression.

Section 62.32

Labor Mobilization, Industrial Production, and Military Recruitment

As the war in China widened and the Pacific War began, Korea became a source of labor, goods, and soldiers. The National General Mobilization Law, passed in Japan in 1938, was extended to Korea, and the Government-General expanded control over industry, materials, and the labor market. Factories, mines, and construction projects in Korea, Manchuria, and Japan needed workers as Japanese men entered the military.

Labor was recruited in stages. From 1939, companies in Japan sent recruiters to Korea under a system of "recruitment" (boshū), nominally voluntary but often involving pressure from local officials. From 1942, the Government-General organized "official mediation" (kan assen), in which officials assigned quotas to localities and assisted employers. In September 1944 the National Requisition Ordinance was applied to Korea, giving legal basis to conscripted labor. Estimates of the number of Koreans taken to Japan under these programs generally exceed 700,000, with many more mobilized within Korea and to other parts of the empire. Terminology is debated, with Korean scholars and many others using "forced mobilization" and some Japanese scholars and officials stressing legal distinctions among stages. Conditions in mines and factories were often harsh, with long hours, inadequate food, injuries, and deaths. The wider comparative treatment of forced labor belongs to Chapter 79.

Military recruitment followed a similar progression. In 1938 the Army Special Volunteer Soldier system began accepting Korean volunteers, with strong administrative pressure and a high ratio of applicants to accepted recruits; a naval program followed in 1943, and in 1943 Korean students in higher education were pressed to enlist. Japan announced in 1942 that conscription would be introduced in Korea, and army inductions began in 1944. Korean naval mobilization, begun through a special-volunteer system in August 1943, subsequently operated through both volunteer and conscription channels during the final war years; it should not be reduced to a single 1945 starting date. In total, hundreds of thousands of Koreans served in the Japanese military and as civilian military employees, including guards in prisoner-of-war camps, some of whom were later tried as war criminals. Their position was unequal: Koreans were required to fight for an empire in which they did not have equal rights, and many died or were wounded in combat or labor service.

Wartime Mobilization of Korean Labor and Soldiers

Note: these groups did not have equal power, and responsibility for coercion lay primarily with those who designed and enforced mobilization policy.

Korean workers: Some were recruited with promises of wages and later found conditions harsher than promised; others were selected by local officials or compelled. Recruited soldiers: Volunteers and conscripts included young men who enlisted from conviction, hoped for social advancement, faced local pressure, or were drafted. Their later reputations in Korea and Japan have been complex, as veterans, victims, and in some cases defendants.
Families: Families lost workers and sons, received little information, and in some cases never learned what happened to them. Mobilization also reshaped household labor, with women and elderly relatives taking on farm work.
Section 62.33

Wartime Everyday Life: Food, Families, Women, and the Human Costs of Empire

By the early 1940s, wartime controls reached into every household. A rice-delivery system required farmers to turn over fixed quantities of grain to the government, often at low prices, leaving some farm households short of food. Rationing applied to staples, cloth, and fuel; scrap metal, including household brass, was collected for war production; and neighborhood associations monitored compliance and organized drills and ceremonies. Schools reorganized students into labor service, and youth groups, women's associations, and neighborhood units were integrated into mobilization.

Women's lives were affected in several ways. Many took on additional agricultural work as men were recruited, and women and girls were mobilized for factory and service labor, including organized labor corps under an ordinance of 1944. Families faced separation, death notices, and shortages, while propaganda urged women to support the war as "mothers of soldiers." Some Korean women also worked as nurses, teachers, and officials in the wartime apparatus.

The most severe form of gendered violence was the military "comfort station" system, in which women were held and sexually exploited by Japanese military personnel across the empire's war zones. Korean women were a large part of the victims, alongside women from China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Indonesia, and other places, and Japanese women. Recruitment methods included deception, debt, coercion, and in some cases abduction, and the colonial system, with its police, recruiters, and economic pressures, enabled these practices. Scholars disagree on total numbers, with estimates ranging widely, and the documentary record is incomplete. The issue became public in 1991 when Kim Hak-sun came forward as a survivor, and in 1993 the Japanese government issued the Kōno Statement acknowledging military involvement and coercion in many cases. The issue remains a subject of diplomatic and political dispute, and its full treatment is assigned to Chapter 79.

Wartime colonial life thus combined material deprivation, ideological pressure, family disruption, and violence. For many Koreans, the war years were the most coercive period of colonial rule, and their memory shapes how Koreans and Japanese discuss the colonial past.

Section 62.34

Korea within Japan's Expanding Imperial System: Taiwan, Sakhalin, Manchuria, and the Pacific

Japan's empire was not a single legal or administrative unit. It combined territories incorporated as prefectures, such as Okinawa and Hokkaido, with colonies, leased territories, a League of Nations mandate, and, after 1932, a nominally independent client state. Korea's status was that of an annexed territory governed by a Government-General, with its population holding Japanese nationality but subject to separate registers and ordinance-based rule. With a population of roughly twenty million or more in the 1930s, it was by far the largest of Japan's colonies in population terms.

Taiwan, acquired in 1895 (Chapter 60), was also governed by a Governor-General, with powers to issue ordinances. Unlike Korea, Taiwan had a mixed population of Han Chinese settlers and Indigenous peoples, and from 1919 to 1936 its Governor-Generals were civilians, before military appointees returned. Southern Sakhalin (Karafuto), ceded by Russia in 1905, was administered through a separate agency, settled by Japanese migrants, and home to Indigenous Ainu, Uilta, and Nivkh people and to Korean workers; in 1943 it was brought closer to the metropolitan administrative system. The Kwantung Leased Territory around Port Arthur and Dalian, transferred from Russian to Japanese lease in 1905, was governed separately and became the base of the Kwantung Army, and Japan held railway rights and zones in southern Manchuria through the South Manchuria Railway Company. After the 1931 takeover of Manchuria, Japan created Manchukuo, formally independent but dominated by Japanese military and civilian officials (Chapter 75). In the Pacific, Japan administered former German Micronesian islands from the 1920s under a League of Nations Class C mandate, the South Seas Mandate, and continued to hold them after leaving the League.

These distinctions mattered. A leased territory implied time-limited rights; a mandate carried formal obligations to an international body; a client state avoided annexation's legal labels while serving Japanese interests; and Korea and Taiwan were treated as permanent parts of the empire. They also shaped movements of people: Koreans migrated to Manchuria, Japan, Sakhalin, and the Pacific, and Korean experiences were tied to these other territories. Even so, the empire's structures shared features: military influence, settler privileges, ordinance-based rule, and economic integration with Japan. On 1 April 1945, with defeat approaching, Japan promulgated legislation allocating 23 lower-house Diet seats to Korea and five to Taiwan, with a restricted, tax-qualified colonial electorate. The necessary implementing ordinance was never issued, and no elections took place before surrender.

Section 62.35

The Collapse of Japanese Colonial Rule in 1945 and the Legacies of Empire

Japan's surrender on 15 August 1945 ended colonial authority in Korea, though not in the manner of an orderly transfer. Governor-General Abe Nobuyuki and his officials, fearing violence against Japanese residents and the collapse of public order, negotiated with Korean leaders, notably Yeo Un-hyeong, who organized the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence. Local committees formed across the country, and on 6 September a self-proclaimed Korean People's Republic was announced. Soviet troops had entered northern Korea in August, and American forces arrived at Incheon on 8 September; the Japanese surrender in Korea was signed in Seoul on 9 September. Earlier, American officers had proposed the 38th parallel as a dividing line between zones of occupation. Korea's liberation thus came with foreign military administrations and a division that would become permanent (Chapters 81 and 90 address the broader surrender and postwar settlement).

Liberation was celebrated, and 15 August remains a national holiday in both Koreas. It also left unresolved problems: how to deal with colonial officials, police, and businesspeople; how to redistribute land and Japanese-owned property; how to remember the colonial period; and how to rebuild political authority. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans returned from Japan, Manchuria, and other places, while many Koreans remained in Japan, becoming the Zainichi community whose legal status remained contested. Japanese settlers were repatriated, a story treated in Chapter 81.

Historians debate how to interpret colonial rule's legacies. Institutions, infrastructure, industrial facilities, schooling patterns, bureaucratic practices, and police structures persisted in various ways into the postwar states, while deep social changes, such as changed landholding patterns and urbanization, also shaped later events. Relations between Japan and Korea after 1945 have been affected by differing views of the legality of annexation, claims for compensation, the wartime labor and sexual-slavery issues, and textbook and memorial controversies. The 1965 normalization treaty and its claims agreement did not end these disputes, and in 2018 South Korean Supreme Court rulings on wartime labor compensation revived them. The legacy is thus both historical and ongoing.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Assess Development, Coercion, Resistance, and Collaboration?

The question: Colonial Korea saw both coercion and social change, both resistance and cooperation. Should historians organize the period around a framework of national resistance and collaboration, or around the everyday experience of colonial society?

Interpretation A — The nationalist (resistance) framework: Many Korean historians have narrated the period as one of foreign domination and national struggle, emphasizing the illegitimacy of annexation, the violence of repression, and the continuity of the independence movement. This approach preserves memory of those who resisted and holds accountable those who aided harm.
Interpretation B — Colonial modernity and the "gray zone": Other scholars argue that this framework simplifies a society in which most people neither resisted openly nor collaborated enthusiastically, and that modern institutions, consumer culture, and education shaped Korean life in ways not captured by a binary. They emphasize agency within constraint, while critics reply that this emphasis risks minimizing coercion.

Evidence: Both draw on police and court records, Government-General statistics, newspapers, memoirs, oral histories, and literature, and on interpretations of what choices were available to people in particular circumstances.

Why it matters: The answer affects how scholars treat figures such as entrepreneurs, writers, and officials; how nations remember the colonial past; and how Korean and Japanese societies discuss responsibility.

Section 62.36

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced Korea's loss of sovereignty, the construction of the colonial state, the economic and social transformations of colonial society, Korean resistance and accommodation, assimilation and wartime mobilization, and the empire's collapse. Before proceeding, students should be able to distinguish the following categories in the historical record: political coercion (the treaties, police, and legal order), economic change (land, rice, industry, and trade), social experience (tenancy, schooling, family, and urban life), cultural policy (press, language, religion, and names), organized resistance (armed, diplomatic, mass, and socialist movements), imperial ideology (naisen ittai and kōminka), and historical interpretation (how scholars and later governments have understood these developments).

The core insights are these. The annexation of 1910 completed a sequence of measures that began in 1905 and whose legitimacy remains disputed. The colonial state combined military and police power with legal inequality, and it changed its methods after the March First Movement without ending its fundamental control. Economic development occurred alongside unequal ownership and political subordination, and historians continue to debate how to weigh these facts. Koreans responded in varied ways, from armed struggle and exile politics to cultural work, labor and tenant organizing, accommodation, and collaboration. The wartime period brought intensified assimilation, labor and military mobilization, and sexual violence enabled by colonial structures. After 1945, liberation coincided with division and unresolved questions that continue to shape Korea–Japan relations.

Students should also be able to explain which sources support each of these insights and whose perspectives each source represents: Government-General reports and ordinances; Korean newspapers and memoirs; mission and diplomatic accounts; court and police records; statistical series; and later testimony. The next chapters turn to Japan's metropolitan social and cultural history (Chapters 63–65) before returning to the empire in Japan's wartime expansion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined four decades in which Korea moved from nominal independence to colonial incorporation in the Japanese empire and then to liberation amid division. Its central argument is that Japanese colonial rule was a changing system of political, legal, economic, and cultural control, and that Koreans responded to it in diverse ways that cannot be reduced to a single story of victimhood, resistance, or modernization.

The destruction of Korean sovereignty was gradual. The Eulsa Agreement of 1905 removed Korea's foreign relations, the Residency-General under Itō Hirobumi built a system of advisers and control over finance, communications, and police, and in 1907 the Hague mission's failure, Gojong's forced abdication, and the dissolution of the army extended Japanese power over internal administration. The righteous armies resisted, and Japanese counterinsurgency suppressed them. An Jung-geun's assassination of Itō in 1909 did not originate annexation policy, which was already under way. The treaty of August 1910, whose legitimacy remains contested, ended the Korean Empire.

The colonial state that followed was led by a military Governor-General and relied in its first decade on gendarmerie police. Koreans were Japanese subjects without equal rights, governed by ordinances, separate registers, and a legal order that favored Japanese interests. The land survey, railways, banks, and company regulations reorganized property and economic life, with outcomes that varied by region and class and that historians continue to study with attention to sources and mechanisms.

The March First Movement of 1919 was the central political turning point. Organized by religious leaders and carried by students, women, farmers, and townspeople across the peninsula, it was met with severe repression. It led to the formation of the Provisional Government, a shift in strategy among nationalists, and Japan's adoption of "cultural rule," which permitted Korean-language newspapers and associations while enlarging policing and censorship and retaining military governors.

Colonial society changed in the 1920s and 1930s. The Rice Production Increase Program tied Korean agriculture to Japanese markets and increased tenancy pressure and debt; industrialization, particularly in the north, expanded in the 1930s; cities, schools, newspapers, literature, religion, and women's lives were transformed, within a system of unequal access and strict controls. Historians debate whether these developments are best described as exploitation, modernization, or colonial modernity, and this chapter has presented the evidence for each position.

Resistance took many forms: armed struggle in Manchuria and Siberia, exile politics, student and labor protest, tenant organizing, socialist and communist organizing, and cultural preservation. These movements differed in ideology and strategy and were often in tension. Collaboration and accommodation also took many forms, from ideological support to economic interest to constrained survival, and the "gray zone" concept helps historians avoid a simple binary while preserving the possibility of moral judgment.

After 1931, and especially after 1937, policy shifted toward assimilation and mobilization. Naisen ittai, shrine and oath requirements, restrictions on the Korean language, and the name policy of 1939–1940 pressed Koreans to adopt Japanese identity while institutional inequality persisted. Labor recruitment, volunteer military programs, and conscription extended the empire's demands, and the colonial system enabled forms of coercion and sexual violence whose wider treatment belongs to Chapter 79.

Korea's position in the empire differed from that of Taiwan, southern Sakhalin, the Kwantung Leased Territory, Manchukuo, and the South Seas Mandate, which had different legal statuses and populations. These differences matter because the empire was not a single uniform structure, even as common practices of military influence, settler privilege, and economic integration linked its parts.

Japan's surrender in August 1945 ended colonial authority, but not the consequences of colonial rule. Liberation was accompanied by foreign occupation, division along the 38th parallel, and unresolved questions about collaboration, property, and memory. Contemporary disputes over treaty legality, compensation, and historical education show that this history continues to shape the present.

For students of Japanese history, the chapter shows that the modern Japanese state's development cannot be understood without its empire. The constitutional government, industrial capitalism, and military victories described in this part of the book coexisted with, and depended on, the domination of Korea and other territories. Understanding colonial Korea is therefore part of understanding Japan itself.

This project asks you to study one place in colonial Korea (for example, Seoul, Pyongyang, Gwangju, Hungnam, Jiandao, or a rural county) and to connect local evidence to the chapter's larger arguments.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because Korea was central to Japan's transformation into a modern empire. The institutions, resources, and strategic position of the peninsula shaped Japanese policy toward Manchuria and China, and the colonial experience shaped Japanese ideas about administration, assimilation, and hierarchy. The constitutional order and industrial economy examined elsewhere in Part IX developed alongside an empire in which millions of people lived under rule they could not influence through representation.

The chapter also reveals how colonial rule produced and responded to social change within Korea. Land registration, rice exports, schools, newspapers, cities, factories, and family law changed Korean society, and Koreans shaped these changes through protest, organization, enterprise, writing, and accommodation. Their experiences were not uniform, and differences of class, region, gender, religion, and generation affected how colonial power was felt and answered.

Many later developments grew from this history. Wartime mobilization created the conditions for the large Korean population in Japan and for the forced labor and military sexual slavery issues addressed in Chapter 79. The collapse of the empire in 1945 led to occupation and division, and Korea–Japan relations since then have been shaped by competing memories of the colonial period. Understanding these disputes requires the evidence-conscious approach this chapter has tried to model: distinguishing documents, claims, interpretations, and memory.

Finally, the chapter extends the textbook's insistence that Japanese history is the history of many communities across and beyond the archipelago. Korean, Japanese settler, Taiwanese, Ainu, Ryukyuan, and other peoples were connected by empire in unequal ways. Students who understand colonial Korea are better prepared for the chapters that follow on mass society, militarism, war, and defeat, in which the empire continues to play a central role.
