Religion under Tokugawa Rule

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A townsperson in eighteenth-century Edo or Osaka might spend an evening at a kabuki theater, spend the next morning at a Buddhist temple arranging a memorial service for a deceased parent, visit a neighborhood shrine on the way home, and belong to a confraternity that saved money for a pilgrimage to Ise. Few people in that society would have seen these actions as belonging to competing religions.

Chapter 46 followed Tokugawa people into the commercial pleasure districts, theaters, and print markets of the cities. This chapter follows the same people into a different part of their lives: the temples, shrines, graveyards, mountain paths, household altars, and ethical teachings through which they confronted death, illness, hardship, obligation, and hope. Religion in Tokugawa Japan was not a separate department of life that people entered on special days. It was woven into the household, the village, the city ward, the calendar, the economy of travel, and the machinery of government.

The central argument of the chapter is that Tokugawa religion was neither a single state orthodoxy nor a collection of neatly separated religions called "Buddhism," "Shinto," and "Confucianism." Most people took part in overlapping religious worlds. Households relied on Buddhist temples for funerals, graves, and ancestral rites; worshiped kami at shrines; joined devotional associations and pilgrimages; consulted diviners and healers; and met Confucian ethical language in family teaching and political life. At the same time, the Tokugawa state made religion part of governance. It suppressed Christianity and, over the seventeenth century, came to require that households be affiliated with Buddhist temples. Religious pluralism and religious regulation therefore existed simultaneously, and understanding Tokugawa society requires holding both in view.

The chapter's organizing interpretive idea is plural religious practice inside administrative regulation. It is the religious counterpart of ideas developed earlier in this Part: the autonomy within subordination that characterized village life in Chapter 44, and the freedom within constraint that characterized urban culture in Chapter 46. Authorities generally policed institutional boundaries, prohibited what they defined as dangerous affiliations, and supervised temples and shrines, but they did not impose a single doctrine on the population. Within those boundaries, an extraordinarily varied religious life flourished.

The chapter also treats Christianity as a genuine religious community rather than only as a problem of state policy. Chapter 37 introduced the Portuguese traders, Jesuit missionaries, and Japanese converts of the sixteenth century. Here the story resumes after the unifiers had turned against the faith. Students will examine why Christianity came to be regarded as politically dangerous, how persecution and the trampling of images (fumi-e) worked, how some communities survived in concealment as kakure Kirishitan, and how the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638 should be understood: not simply as a "Christian revolt," but as an uprising rooted in heavy taxation, harvest failure, harsh local rule, and regional dislocation in which Christian networks and symbols played a powerful role.

The evidence for this subject is uneven, and students should learn to read it critically. Temple registers, household registers, shrine and temple regulations, bakufu and domain edicts, pilgrimage records, guidebooks, woodblock-printed amulets, tombstones, objects such as fumi-e plaques, Christian and European missionary accounts, and later memoirs all survive, but each reflects a particular position. Official documents show what authorities wished to control, not necessarily what people believed. Ordinary believers left few statements of faith; their religious lives must often be inferred from practice, from account books, and from material objects. Accounts of Christian suffering come from both Christian and anti-Christian sources, and each was written with purposes of its own.

The chapter has firm boundaries within the larger book. The commercial and diplomatic architecture of Nagasaki, the Dutch and Chinese trade, and the question of "isolation" belong to Chapter 48; the chapter discusses Nagasaki only as a site of Christian history and religious policing. Festival culture, food, and everyday leisure belong to Chapter 49. Intellectual history, including detailed schools of Confucian thought, publishing, and Dutch learning, belongs to Chapter 50. The Meiji separation of kami and buddhas and the construction of State Shinto belong to Chapter 55; this chapter's task is to show that what Chapter 55 transformed was a Tokugawa religious order in which Buddhist institutions and kami worship were deeply intertwined.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why describing Tokugawa Japan as having three separate religions, Buddhism, Shinto, and Confucianism, is misleading.
  2. Describe the institutional organization of Tokugawa Buddhism and its relationship to households, funerals, and ancestral rites.
  3. Trace the origins and development of temple certification (terauke) and the parishioner (danka) relationship.
  4. Analyze how an anti-Christian measure became an enduring instrument of social administration without making temples simple agents of the state.
  5. Distinguish Tokugawa kami worship and shrine life from the later construction of State Shinto, and explain shinbutsu shūgō.
  6. Assess the role of Confucian ethics in household, education, and political life, and the historical problem of classifying Confucianism as a religion.
  7. Interpret pilgrimage, devotional associations, mountain religion, and popular ritual as organized and meaningful forms of religious life.
  8. Trace the movement of Christianity from tolerated mission to prohibited religion, and explain the political and religious reasons for suppression.
  9. Evaluate competing explanations of the Shimabara Rebellion and its consequences, separating its causes from the later history of religious registration.
  10. Compare how a household, a priest, a pilgrim, a hidden Christian, and an official experienced Tokugawa religious policy differently.
  11. Connect religious regulation to the international concerns taken up in Chapter 48, and Tokugawa religion to the Meiji transformations examined in Chapter 55.

Historical Context

Tokugawa religious life inherited a long and layered past. Buddhism had entered the archipelago in the sixth century and, as Chapters 10 and 15 showed, had become closely bound to imperial authority and elite patronage. Over the medieval centuries, as Chapters 26 and 30 explained, Pure Land, Nichiren, and Zen movements broadened Buddhism's social reach, while large monastic centers such as Mount Hiei and Mount Kōya accumulated land, armed followers, and political power. Kami worship was carried on in shrines of every scale, from imperial and provincial centers to village tutelary sites, and by the medieval period shrines and temples had grown into overlapping complexes.

The sixteenth century disrupted these arrangements. The warfare of the Sengoku era (Chapters 35 and 36) pulled temples into military conflict, and Oda Nobunaga's campaigns against religious institutions, described in Chapter 39, broke the independent military power of major Buddhist centers. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, as Chapter 40 showed, confirmed the subordination of religious institutions to secular authority. At the same time Christianity, introduced in 1549 and discussed in Chapter 37, had acquired communities of believers, several daimyō patrons, and an institutional presence in Kyushu, especially around Nagasaki. When the Tokugawa established their regime after Sekigahara (Chapter 41), they inherited both a principle that armed religious institutions could not rival the state and a growing suspicion of Christianity.

The Tokugawa order described in Chapters 42 through 45 provided the setting for what followed: a political system in which the shogun and the domains shared governance, a society organized through status groups and households, villages with substantial self-management, and a commercial economy with extensive roads and large cities. Religious institutions had to be fitted into this order. The bakufu issued regulations for temples and later created offices to supervise temples and shrines, and domains supervised religious institutions within their own territories. Religion was thus never outside politics, even though politics did not dictate belief.

Students should keep in mind three cautions. First, the modern conception of Shinto as a clearly bounded religion institutionally separate from Buddhism is largely a later development; Tokugawa people nevertheless used terms and traditions associated with Shintō and kami worship in varied ways, as in the teachings of the Yoshida house, and shrine worship was ordinarily embedded in a wider religious environment. Second, institutional records, especially those of temples and the state, are far more abundant than records of individual belief. Third, regional variation was substantial: the religious life of a Kyushu coastal village, an Edo ward, a Tōhoku farming community, and a mountain pilgrimage center differed considerably, and the regulation of Christianity was concentrated in particular regions and times.

Section 47.1

From Pleasure to Belief: Religion in Everyday Tokugawa Life

The commercial culture of Chapter 46 and the religious culture of this chapter were not separate worlds. Theaters and temples often shared neighborhoods, and the great Edo and Osaka temples and shrines were themselves sites of fairs, entertainments, and exhibitions. A single person could attend a play, sponsor a Buddhist memorial service, pay respects at a shrine, join an Ise association, consult a diviner about a business venture, and recite the household's ethical maxims without sensing any contradiction. These were different kinds of action serving different needs, not rival declarations of identity.

This observation raises a methodological problem. Modern readers, especially those shaped by religious traditions in which membership is exclusive, tend to ask "which religion did this person belong to?" For most Tokugawa people the question had no single answer. The household was registered with a temple; the village worshiped at a tutelary shrine; an individual might join a pilgrimage association; and the household head might read Confucian moral primers. The better questions are what institutions touched a person's life, what each provided, and what obligations each created.

It is also important not to romanticize this pluralism. Participation was shaped by status, gender, region, and wealth. Women in many households managed ancestral offerings and the household altar, yet were barred from certain sacred mountains. Poorer families could afford fewer memorial services. Outcast groups, discussed in Chapter 43, faced discrimination in religious as in other settings, including exclusion from some institutions and separate burial arrangements. And the state's anti-Christian policy meant that for some people religious affiliation was a matter of survival. Religious freedom in the modern sense did not exist, even though doctrinal uniformity was not demanded.

Section 47.2

A Religious Landscape, Not Three Separate Religions

A familiar textbook formula states that Tokugawa Japan had three religions, Buddhism, Shinto, and Confucianism, while Christianity was banned. The formula is too neat. It treats these traditions as parallel denominations, each with its own membership, clergy, and doctrine, when in practice they operated at different levels and overlapped heavily. Buddhism was the most institutionalized, with temples, priests, hierarchies, and property. Kami worship centered on shrines, festivals, and local communities and had a much less developed doctrinal apparatus. Confucianism was primarily a body of ethical and political teaching carried by scholars and household instruction, with a few ritual elements. Beyond these lay pilgrimage, ritual specialists, healing, and divination that did not belong to any single institution.

For most of the Tokugawa period, temples and shrines frequently formed shared sacred institutions: shrine-temple complexes, priests who performed both Buddhist and kami-related rites, and widely accepted explanations linking kami and buddhas. This is the practice called shinbutsu shūgō, discussed in Section 47.8. The sharp institutional separation of kami and buddhas that students may take for granted was produced by Meiji government policy in the late 1860s and after, taken up in Chapter 55. Projecting that later distinction backward would misrepresent the Tokugawa world.

Historians therefore describe the Tokugawa religious landscape as an overlapping field: Buddhist institutions, kami worship, Confucian ethics, local cults, pilgrimage, household rites, and ritual specialists coexisting and often combining. Christianity was exceptional not because it was the only non-mainstream tradition, but because the state increasingly defined it as an impermissible affiliation. The same was true, in a narrower way, of a small number of Buddhist groups, such as the Nichiren branch known as Fuju fuse, which was prohibited in the 1660s over its refusal to accept offerings from non-believers, a reminder that state regulation could also reach Buddhist communities.

Section 47.3

Buddhism after the Wars: Temples, Sects, and Institutions

After the wars of the sixteenth century, Buddhism in Japan was institutionally formidable but politically subordinated. The major traditions inherited from earlier chapters continued: Tendai and Shingon, with their roots in the Heian period; Pure Land schools, including the Jōdo and Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land) traditions; Zen schools, principally Rinzai and Sōtō, joined in the seventeenth century by the Ōbaku school introduced from China by the monk Ingen (Yinyuan) in 1654; and the various Nichiren traditions. Jōdo Shinshū was among the largest in lay membership, and in 1602 the Tokugawa encouraged a division of its main temple, the Honganji, into western and eastern branches, weakening its capacity for unified action.

The Tokugawa governed Buddhism through hierarchy rather than doctrine. Early regulations issued to individual sects and temples in the first decades of the seventeenth century set expectations of discipline, scholarship, and obedience to secular authority. Sects were organized into head-and-branch temple systems (honmatsu seido), in which each local temple was tied to a head temple that answered for its conduct. This arrangement gave authorities a chain of responsibility. The bakufu's commissioners of temples and shrines, created in 1635, adjudicated disputes among and within religious institutions, and domains maintained their own oversight. Temples held lands and received support, but could not form armed bands or extend their jurisdiction beyond limits.

The practical reach of Buddhism was enormous. The number of temples ran to tens of thousands, many of them small village establishments served by a single priest. Beyond funerals, temples served as sites of education for commoners' children (the "temple schools," discussed further in Chapter 50), as meeting places, and as stages for fairs and public displays of sacred images. The Tokugawa family patronized Buddhist institutions of their own: the Jōdo temple Zōjōji in Edo as a family temple, and the Tendai temple Kan'eiji in Ueno, founded in 1625. The monk Tenkai, a Tendai figure close to Ieyasu and his successors, helped arrange the posthumous deification of Ieyasu as Tōshō Daigongen at Nikkō, an example of Buddhist and kami traditions working together in a state cult.

Tokugawa Buddhism has often been judged as intellectually stagnant, a "decline" from the dynamism of the medieval era. The charge deserves caution. Much scholarship has been directed at doctrinal creativity, and Tokugawa Buddhism did produce significant scholastic work and reform within sects, including the Zen revival associated with Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769) in the Rinzai tradition. But the principal historical significance of Buddhism in this period lay less in new doctrine than in institutional reach: its integration into the family, the community, and the administration of death.

Section 47.4

Death, Ancestors, and the Buddhist Household

The strongest tie between Buddhism and ordinary people was death. In earlier centuries, elaborate Buddhist funerary practice and permanent family graves had been largely the privilege of elites. During the seventeenth century this changed, as peasants and townspeople of modest means increasingly acquired family graves, household memorial tablets (ihai), and a cycle of memorial services performed by priests at intervals after death. The household altar (butsudan) became a common domestic fixture, linking the family to its ancestors in daily offerings. These practices, widely visible by the mid-Tokugawa period, are among the most durable features of Japanese religious life.

This development connects directly to the household (ie) analyzed in Chapter 43. The ie was conceived as a continuing entity that extended across generations, holding property, occupation, and name. Buddhist memorial rites gave that continuity religious form: the dead remained members of the household, and the living household had obligations to them. The grave and the tablet were evidence of lineage. For families with property or status to protect, the ability to maintain a respectable funeral and regular services was part of social standing.

Historians such as Nam-lin Hur, in his study of death and social order in Tokugawa Japan, have emphasized that Buddhism's role included an economy of death as well as an administrative one. Funerals, posthumous names (kaimyō), memorial services, and graveyard plots produced income for temples, and parishioners' payments sustained the temple. The relationship was therefore reciprocal: households needed temples for rites they considered essential, and temples needed households for support. This mutual dependence helps explain why the parishioner system lasted, and why it generated frictions, such as disputes over fees and complaints about priests' conduct.

Not everyone had equal access. Rural poor and itinerant populations might lack graves or lasting affiliation, and groups discriminated against as eta and hinin were often assigned separate temples or marked in registers and posthumous naming in ways that recorded and reinforced stigma. Historians have studied such temple records as evidence for the historical background of later Burakumin discrimination (Chapters 43 and 94). Religious institutions were thus both consolations and instruments of social hierarchy.

Section 47.5

Temple Registration: Terauke and the Danka System

The institution that most clearly joined religion and governance was temple certification, terauke, and the associated parishioner relationship, danka. Its starting point was anti-Christian policy. After the prohibitions of 1612–1614, authorities in various domains and in bakufu territory required people suspected of Christian connections, and later wider populations, to obtain a certificate from a Buddhist temple stating that they belonged to it and were not Christian. The practice was initially local and varied. After the Shimabara Rebellion, as Section 47.19 shows, the bakufu intensified investigation, creating an office for religious inquiry in 1640 and pressing domains to examine religious affiliation.

Over the middle decades of the seventeenth century the practice broadened into annual registration. In 1664 the bakufu directed domains to establish officials responsible for religious investigation, and registers of religious affiliation and household composition, called shūmon aratame-chō, became increasingly general from the mid-1660s. In 1671 the bakufu standardized the form and reporting procedures of what was then commonly called the shūmon ninbetsu aratame-chō, adding systematic household and population information. Institutionalization was thus a process, not the product of a single decree, and local timing differed. Each household was affiliated with a temple, whose priest certified its members. In effect, nearly every household belonged to a Buddhist temple, and the temple acted as an intermediary of administration.

Two simplifications should be avoided. The first is that temples became "police stations" of an oppressive state. Temples were religious institutions with their own interests; priests certified households partly because the relationship supported funeral income and parish stability, and local priests and households negotiated their dealings, sometimes conflictually. The second is that registration was merely a benign census. It was rooted in religious suppression, and the burden of proof fell on households to demonstrate that they were not Christian. People accused of belonging to prohibited groups could be investigated, and those who left their registered temple, or were expelled by it, could face difficulty. Terauke certification could also be needed for travel, marriage, adoption, and changes of residence, so that temple documents served wider purposes of identification.

Historical Vocabulary
Terauke and Danka

Terauke (temple certification) refers to the practice by which a Buddhist temple certified that a household or individual was affiliated with it and was not Christian. Danka (also dannaji-danka relationships) refers to the parishioner households attached to a temple. The related term danka seido is often rendered "parishioner system." Together they describe a relationship in which a household supported a temple and received funerary and memorial services and certification in return.

Modern equivalents such as "church membership" or "census registration" are misleading. Danka affiliation was not a matter of personal conversion, and registration was not purely a government census. It was a hybrid of religious service, household identity, and administrative record, which is why historians continue to debate how best to describe it (see the Historical Debate below).

Section 47.6

Religious Registration as Social Administration

By around 1700 the system of temple affiliation was functioning as a general mechanism of supervising the population as well as an anti-Christian device. Village officials compiled lists of households with members' names, ages, and relationships, and these were checked against temple certification. Registers were revised annually and, in many places, provided the basis of what scholars treat as a form of population records, although demographers using them must contend with underreporting, local conventions, and omissions of particular groups.

For ordinary people, registration meant a set of obligations and entanglements. Households needed to maintain their relationship with their temple, to notify it of births, deaths, marriages, and adoptions, and to seek certificates when moving or traveling. Temples, in turn, gained leverage: priests could refuse certification, and parishioners who sought to change temples needed the old temple's consent, which gave temples some power over households but also exposed them to complaint and litigation. Local records show disputes over burial fees, temple assessments, and the conduct of priests, and some households and villages challenged temples through petitions to authorities.

The interpretive point is that a measure created to police religious danger became part of routine social governance. Once embedded in village and household bookkeeping, it outlived the urgent anti-Christian purposes that created it. By the eighteenth century the system was less about hunting Christians than about maintaining administrative order, even as authorities continued to treat Christianity as prohibited. This transformation of meaning is one of the chapter's central lessons: institutions created for one purpose can acquire others, and the new functions may then sustain them.

Historical Debate

Was Tokugawa Temple Registration Primarily Religious Control or Social Administration?

The question: Did the terauke and danka system mainly serve to control religion, by suppressing Christianity and binding people to Buddhist institutions, or mainly to administer society, by providing a routine mechanism for registering households and supervising movement?

Interpretation A — Religious control: The system originated as anti-Christian surveillance. Households had to prove their non-Christian status, the burden of proof lay on them, and affiliation with a Buddhist temple was legally compulsory. On this view, registration was fundamentally a tool of religious policing that also gave Buddhist institutions a privileged, state-backed position.
Interpretation B — Social administration: Whatever its origins, the system functioned over the long run as population registration and as a framework for funerals, household continuity, and village organization. Emphasizing funerals, household succession, and local negotiation, this view stresses that most people experienced the system through ritual and community life, not as inquisition.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the registers themselves, on bakufu and domain orders, and on temple records of parish life. They differ in where they place weight: on the legal and political origins and compulsory character, or on the long-term social functions and local practice.

Why it matters: The question shapes how students understand the relationship between religion and the Tokugawa state, and how they interpret Buddhism's social role. The most defensible synthesis is that registration became both, and that its meaning changed over time and varied by place.

Student task: Identify one type of evidence that supports interpreting registration as religious control and one that supports interpreting it as social administration, and explain how a historian might use the two together.

Section 47.7

Kami, Shrines, and Local Sacred Landscapes

Alongside Buddhist temples, shrines were part of virtually every community. The kami, a broad category of sacred powers associated with places, natural forces, ancestors, and deified persons, were worshiped at shrines ranging from famous national centers to small village sites. Most villages and many urban wards had a tutelary shrine whose deity (ujigami or ubusuna, with differing connotations) protected the community. Shrine festivals, seasonal rites, and offerings organized communal time, and village shrines were often managed by local elites and parishioner groups (miyaza) that tied ritual roles to community standing.

Shrine personnel were not a uniform priesthood. Some shrines were served by hereditary priestly families; others had part-time or rotating roles filled by villagers; and many were attached to or served by Buddhist priests. In 1665 the bakufu issued regulations for shrine priests that confirmed the authority of the Yoshida house, a Kyoto family whose teachings combined ritual and doctrine, to license many priests, with the Shirakawa house holding a rival position. This was an effort to bring order to a fragmented body of practitioners rather than the creation of a "Shinto church."

Shrines also served as local centers of economic and social life. They held fairs, supported craftspeople, and sometimes lent money or hosted performances. Great shrines such as Ise, discussed in Section 47.12, drew nationwide followings, while others, such as Tōshō Daigongen at Nikkō, expressed the legitimacy of the regime. The deification of Ieyasu is a reminder that kami worship was a language of political authority as well as local belief.

Students should avoid two common errors. One is to describe Tokugawa shrines as "State Shinto," a term properly belonging to the Meiji period and later (Chapters 55 and 77). The other is to treat them as an untouched indigenous religion standing apart from Buddhism. Intellectual movements within the Tokugawa period, such as those that Chapter 50 will examine under the heading of National Learning, began to argue for a purified kami tradition, but these were scholarly currents and not the dominant practice of shrine life.

Section 47.8

Shinbutsu Shūgō: Kami and Buddhas Together

The relationship between kami and buddhas was among the most important features of Tokugawa religious life, and it deserves its own section because students otherwise carry a modern separation backward. Since the medieval period, many Japanese religious thinkers had explained kami as manifestations of buddhas and bodhisattvas, in a framework called honji suijaku (original ground and manifest traces). Shrines often had Buddhist temples attached, called jingūji or similar, and temples enshrined kami as protectors of their grounds. A visitor to a major sacred site might see Buddhist images, kami shrines, and priests of various kinds in the same precinct.

This mixture was not an accident or corruption of a pure original. It was the ordinary way in which religion was institutionally organized. Buddhist priests performed rites at shrines; shrines had Buddhist administrators; and household practices combined offerings to buddhas and kami. At Nikkō, the Tendai-linked doctrine behind Ieyasu's deification drew on a framework that merged Buddhist and kami thought. Popular deities, such as those of Konpira or Inari, could be understood and worshiped through both vocabularies.

Recognizing this overlap changes how students should read the Meiji period. The government's separation orders of 1868, examined in Chapter 55, forced shrines and temples to divide, led to the removal of Buddhist images and priests from many shrines, and in some places produced violent attacks on temples. These policies were a historical change that reorganized religious institutions, not the restoration of an ancient timeless norm. Understanding the Tokugawa arrangement is therefore necessary for understanding what Meiji reformers believed they were changing.

Historical Vocabulary
Shinbutsu Shūgō

Shinbutsu shūgō ("the fusion or combination of kami and buddhas") is a modern scholarly term for the practices and institutions through which kami worship and Buddhism were joined. It covers doctrinal explanations such as honji suijaku, institutional arrangements such as shrine-temple complexes, and ritual combinations in which priests of different backgrounds officiated together.

Calling it "syncretism" risks implying that two originally distinct religions were mixed together by choice. For much of Japanese history the two were developed together. The term matters here because it explains why Tokugawa people did not ordinarily treat kami and buddhas as alternatives, and why the Meiji separation was a major rupture.

Section 47.9

Confucianism: Ethics, Hierarchy, and the Ordered Household

Confucian teachings reached Japan centuries before the Tokugawa period, but in the seventeenth century they acquired new prominence. Scholars including Fujiwara Seika and his pupil Hayashi Razan, who entered Tokugawa service, promoted Zhu Xi learning, the Song-dynasty synthesis of Confucian ethics and cosmology, as a framework for understanding moral order and governance. Other scholars developed different Confucian approaches, such as Yamazaki Ansai, who combined Zhu Xi learning with kami-related teaching, and Nakae Tōju, associated with a more inward and practical moral emphasis. The detailed contest among these schools belongs to Chapter 50; here the concern is how Confucian ideas functioned as ethical and religious language in everyday and political life.

Confucian ethics emphasized filial piety, loyalty, proper conduct within graded relationships, and the cultivation of the self. These ideas fitted comfortably with a society organized by status and household. Moral primers, instructional texts for children, and household precepts circulated widely, often in simplified form and often mixed with Buddhist and kami-related teaching. Works associated with the scholar Kaibara Ekken, including texts on child-rearing and conduct, were influential, and a text titled Onna daigaku ("Greater Learning for Women"), traditionally linked to his name though its authorship is disputed, has been used by historians as evidence for normative expectations of female obedience, while its actual influence on women's lives is debated.

Confucianism's institutional position was modest. The Hayashi family served the bakufu as scholars and educators, and a Confucian shrine and academy was established in Edo, associated with the shogun Tsunayoshi in the 1690s. Later bakufu policy, notably in the 1790s, privileged Zhu Xi learning in the bakufu's own school, a development Chapter 50 will examine. But Confucianism never became a state church. Domains founded their own schools with varied curricula, and Confucian scholars were frequently critics of both bakufu policy and Buddhist institutions. Some domain lords experimented with Confucian-style funerals and reduced temples in their territories: in Okayama, the daimyō Ikeda Mitsumasa in the 1660s pursued a program of curbing temples and promoting alternatives, which shows both Confucian influence on rulers and the limits of any such program, since the domain later reverted toward the common pattern.

Scholars have warned against exaggerating Japan's "Sinification." Tokugawa Confucianism was not a simple import: it was adapted to a society whose political structure, with hereditary warrior rule and a court in Kyoto, differed from the examination-based bureaucracy of China, and Japanese thinkers sharply debated how Chinese concepts like Heaven's Mandate should apply. Confucian influence was deep without making Tokugawa Japan "a Confucian society" in any simple sense.

Section 47.10

Was Confucianism a Religion? Categories and Historical Problems

Students often ask whether Confucianism was a religion or a philosophy. The question is instructive because the categories themselves are historical. The modern Japanese use of shūkyō as the generic equivalent of the Western category "religion" took shape in the nineteenth century, especially during the diplomatic and institutional transformations of the Meiji era examined in Chapters 52 and 55. Tokugawa people used other terms, such as shūmon and michi ("way"), and drew different lines. Applying the modern binary of religion versus philosophy to them can mislead.

Confucianism as practiced in Japan included features that fit both labels. It offered ethical and political teaching, but also concepts of Heaven and cosmic order, ritual forms such as ceremonies for the sage Confucius and ancestral practice, and a program of self-cultivation with moral and almost devotional qualities. Some Confucian scholars sought funerary and ancestral rites distinct from Buddhist ones, and some linked Confucian ideas with kami worship. Others kept their scholarship separate from private religious practice, and many scholars were also affiliated with Buddhist temples.

The goal here is not to settle what "religion" means philosophically. It is to teach students that classifications such as "Buddhism," "Shinto," and "Confucianism" were not fixed boxes in the Tokugawa period and that historians should be cautious about the labels they use. When this chapter describes Confucianism as a language of ethics, ritual, and hierarchy, it is describing function and practice, not assigning it to a category that contemporaries would have recognized in the same way.

Section 47.11

Popular Religion: Protective Gods, Healing, Divination, and Everyday Need

Much Tokugawa religious life was practical, directed at the concrete needs of survival and prosperity. People sought help with illness, childbirth, harvests, fires, travel, business, fertility, and death. They did so through amulets and talismans issued by temples and shrines, vows made to particular deities, offerings, household shrines, roadside images, and consultation with specialists. Historians avoid the label "superstition," which treats such practices as irrational remnants; they were ordinary responses to real vulnerability in a society with limited medicine, frequent disease, and unpredictable harvests.

A rich world of protective and benefit-giving deities developed. Inari, associated with grain and later commerce, had shrines in villages and cities alike. Jizō, a bodhisattva, was linked with children and travelers and appeared in roadside images. Benzaiten, Ebisu, and other figures attracted merchant devotion, and Kannon was widely revered in pilgrimage networks. Deities of smallpox and other diseases were propitiated, and protective charms were pasted on doors and carried on journeys. Temples and shrines sold such items, and the markets of the cities supported their printing and distribution.

Ritual specialists operated alongside formal clergy. Diviners used calendars and almanacs with lucky and unlucky days; healers combined herbal medicine with prayer; mediums and wandering priests, women and men, performed rituals of spirit communication and exorcism; and itinerant performers carried religious stories. The line between religion, medicine, and entertainment was not sharply drawn. Authorities occasionally prohibited particular groups or practices they considered disorderly, but generally tolerated a wide range of them provided they did not threaten order.

Everyday Life and Environment

Death, Healing, and the Household Altar

In a typical commoner household, religion was lived through repeated small actions. A household altar might hold memorial tablets for deceased members and an image of a buddha or protective deity, with offerings of rice, tea, or flowers; a separate shelf might hold charms from a shrine or from Ise. Memorial services marked anniversaries of deaths, and visits to the family grave at seasonal occasions maintained ties with ancestors. Women often had primary responsibility for daily offerings, though priests and male household heads performed formal roles.

Illness prompted a mix of responses: medicine where available, prayer to deities known for healing, vows promising offerings in return for recovery, and consultation with specialists. Childbirth, a period of high risk, drew visits to shrines and the use of protective charms. A journey called for a travel amulet, and a new business might seek the blessing of a deity of commerce. None of these practices required an exclusive affiliation. What they shared was a view that human life depended on relationships with sacred powers, living and dead, that needed regular care.

Section 47.12

Pilgrimage, Religious Associations, and Sacred Mobility

Chapter 45 explained how highways, post stations, and inns enabled movement. This section asks why sacred travel mattered. Pilgrimage was among the few reasons ordinary commoners could obtain permission to travel far from home, and it combined devotion with sightseeing, commerce, and the experience of the road. The great destinations included Ise, with its Inner and Outer Shrines; Kumano; Zenkōji in Shinano; Konpira in Sanuki, on Shikoku; the thirty-three-site Kannon pilgrimage circuits of western Japan; and the circuit of eighty-eight temples on Shikoku associated with the monk Kūkai, which attracted both ascetics and lay pilgrims. Sacred mountains, including Fuji, were also destinations.

Devotional associations (kō) made pilgrimage possible for many who could not afford it individually. Members paid regular dues into a common fund, and lots or rotation selected representatives who traveled on behalf of the group and returned with charms. Ise confraternities (Ise-kō) were common in villages. Guides called oshi (or onshi) based at Ise and elsewhere arranged lodging, rites, and offerings for pilgrims, distributing charms and calendars across the country. These networks created connections between local communities and distant sacred centers and were embedded in commercial arrangements.

Periodically, enormous numbers of people set out for Ise in movements known as okage mairi ("thank-you pilgrimages"), notably in 1705, 1771, and 1830. Contemporary estimates for 1830 run to several million people within a few months, though the numbers are uncertain. Participants included many who left without formal permission, including apprentices and children, and local communities provided food and assistance to passing pilgrims. These movements show the capacity of religious networks to mobilize popular participation beyond official control, and they are often treated as evidence of both devotional energy and social tension in the later Tokugawa period.

Pilgrimage also had gendered and social dimensions. Women took part in many pilgrimages and associations, though some sacred mountains excluded them, and travel was constrained by regulations on women's movement discussed earlier in this Part. Pilgrims from outcast groups faced barriers. Regional pilgrimage also connected the northern and southern margins of the archipelago to the center only partially; for example, Ezo and Ryukyu had their own religious traditions, and Chapter 48 returns to those regions.

Section 47.13

Mountain Religion, Shugendō, and Religious Specialists

Mountains were sacred in many Japanese traditions, and in the Tokugawa period the practice of mountain asceticism, called Shugendō, had a recognized institutional form. Its practitioners, yamabushi, undertook austerities in the mountains, performed rites of healing and protection, and served as guides for pilgrims. In the early seventeenth century the bakufu regulated Shugendō by placing most practitioners under two principal institutional lines, associated with the Tendai and Shingon traditions, and requiring them to register with a head institution. Yamabushi settled in villages and towns, where they conducted healing prayers, divination, and rituals for ordinary people, and at major mountain centers they ran lodging and guide services for pilgrims.

Mountain religion also generated lay movements. The cult of Mount Fuji, organized through confraternities (Fuji-kō), drew on the teachings of figures such as Hasegawa Kakugyō (traditionally dated 1541–1646) and the later Jikigyō Miroku (1671–1733), who is associated with the revival and spread of the cult in the eighteenth century. Edo-area Fuji associations built miniature replicas of the mountain in city precincts, allowing climbing rites to be performed symbolically. Other mountains, such as Ōyama and Ontake, had similar networks.

The yamabushi illustrate why the categories "Buddhist priest," "Shinto priest," and "layperson" are often too tidy. Many specialists combined roles, were affiliated with institutions yet lived among commoners, and performed rites drawn from several traditions. They also reflect the porous boundary between institutional and popular religion: although regulated and registered, they operated in markets of ritual services where clients chose among practitioners.

Section 47.14

Christianity after the Unifiers: From Mission to Prohibition

Chapter 37 described the Christian mission of the sixteenth century. By the turn of the seventeenth century, scholars estimate that Christians numbered in the hundreds of thousands, concentrated in Kyushu, with other communities in the Kyoto region and elsewhere, though precise figures are uncertain and depend on missionary reports that may have exaggerated. The community included converts from many social groups, a number of daimyō (some sincere, some motivated partly by trade), and organizations of lay believers who maintained devotional life when priests were scarce.

Hostility toward the faith developed in stages. In 1587, shortly after conquering Kyushu, Hideyoshi issued an order that missionaries leave Japan; the order was not rigorously enforced, but signaled concern. Contemporary explanations point to several anxieties: that Christian daimyō might hold loyalties conflicting with obedience to the ruler, that Nagasaki had been ceded to the Jesuits for a period and so offered a foreign-controlled base, that missionaries were linked to Portuguese commercial and possibly military ambitions, and, in some accounts, that Christians had destroyed Buddhist temples and shrines in some domains. In 1597 Hideyoshi ordered twenty-six Christians, including foreign missionaries and Japanese believers, executed at Nagasaki.

The Tokugawa at first tolerated Christianity in practice, partly for the sake of trade, but moved toward prohibition. A ban in bakufu lands came in 1612, and a nationwide prohibition and expulsion of missionaries in 1614 followed, after which prominent Christians such as the daimyō-turned-exile Takayama Ukon were sent out of the country. Recent scholarship stresses that these measures grew from a combination of religious competition and political concern about a transregional institution, the Catholic Church and its religious orders, whose authority lay outside the Tokugawa order. Anti-Christian policy was thus not merely hostility toward foreign ideas; it reflected a developing view that political legitimacy required control over religious allegiance.

Christianity was also changing in this period. Japanese Christians organized confraternities and lay leadership, practices that would matter later in concealment. The mission's institutional presence was gradually destroyed as missionaries were expelled or executed or went into hiding, and the community was left to sustain itself without regular access to priests.

Section 47.15

Persecution, Apostasy, and the Fumi-e

During the 1610s and 1620s, authorities sought out Christians, demanded that they renounce their faith (korobu, "to fall," is the term often used for apostasy), and punished those who refused. Methods varied by time and region. Executions included burning and beheading, as in the mass execution of 1622 at Nagasaki in which dozens of Christians died, and some officials used prolonged torture designed to produce renunciation rather than death. Informers were encouraged by rewards and by collective responsibility within neighborhood groups. Missionaries who entered secretly were hunted, and records of their arrest and execution survive in both Japanese and European sources. Both kinds of evidence require caution: European accounts often emphasized martyrdom and could exaggerate, while official records stressed order and obedience.

A particular technique of investigation was the fumi-e, "stepping picture": people suspected of Christian belief were required to step on an image of Christ or the Virgin Mary, and refusal was taken as evidence of faith. The practice is associated with Nagasaki from the 1620s and 1630s and was later conducted annually in some regions. Early images were printed on paper; later, metal plaques were produced for repeated use. Many Christians were probably willing to tread on the images as a formal act, while others refused or suffered inner conflict.

It would be wrong to portray persecuted Christians simply as passive victims. Believers made varied choices: some died rather than recant; some apostatized formally while maintaining private belief; some fled to other regions or abroad; some concealed their faith within households and villages while participating outwardly in Buddhist life. The result was a mixture of martyrdom, compromise, and survival, and also of abandonment: many Christians did recant, and their numbers fell steeply.

Historical Vocabulary
Fumi-e

Fumi-e ("treading picture") were images of Christ or the Virgin Mary that suspected Christians were made to step on to demonstrate that they were not believers. They are important both as instruments of religious policing and as historical artifacts; several survive in museum collections, including the Tokyo National Museum.

The term should not be taken to mean that every Tokugawa person was routinely tested: fumi-e were used in particular places and periods, most intensively in Kyushu, and later became a formalized annual practice in some localities. Students should distinguish the practice from the broader system of temple registration, with which it was related but not identical.

Section 47.16

Kakure Kirishitan: Christianity in Concealment

Not all Christians were eliminated. Communities in Kyushu, notably around Nagasaki, on the Gotō and other islands, and in parts of Amakusa, preserved Christian-derived practice in secret for some two centuries, usually called kakure Kirishitan ("hidden Christians"). They maintained their outward registration as Buddhist parishioners, attending temple rites and taking part in fumi-e, while privately preserving prayers, baptismal practice, and sacred objects. Lay leaders, such as those who kept a ritual calendar and administered baptism, took on roles that priests had once held.

Over generations without priests or contact with institutional Catholicism, these practices changed. Prayers passed down orally (orasho) preserved fragments of Latin, Portuguese, and Japanese, and sacred images were sometimes disguised, as in figures of Kannon that served as images of Mary. Practices drew on Buddhist and kami-related forms, and many communities came to venerate ancestors and local martyrs, so that their religion was a historical development and not unchanged Catholicism preserved underground. Scholars debate how far the descendant practices should be called Christian and how far they became a distinct religion.

The communities were not undetected. Periodic investigations, such as those at Urakami near Nagasaki in 1790 and again later, uncovered groups and led to arrests, though punishments were often less severe than in the early seventeenth century. After Japan's treaty ports opened (Chapters 51 and 52), a French priest in Nagasaki was approached in 1865 by local people who revealed their faith, an event often called the discovery of the hidden Christians. Persecution continued into the early Meiji years, and the public notice boards prohibiting Christianity were removed only in 1873 (Chapter 55). Some communities rejoined the Catholic Church and others continued their own tradition. In the twenty-first century, sites associated with hidden Christians in the Nagasaki and Amakusa regions have been recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage property.

Section 47.17

Shimabara and Amakusa before the Rebellion

To understand the rebellion of 1637, one must begin with the places where it occurred. The Shimabara Peninsula and the Amakusa islands, in western Kyushu, had been among the strongest centers of Japanese Christianity. The Arima family, Christian lords of the Shimabara area, had been patrons of the mission, and Amakusa also had large Christian communities. After the Tokugawa victory, these regions were reassigned: Shimabara to the Matsukura house, and the Amakusa islands to Terazawa Katataka of the Karatsu domain. Local warrior families displaced by these changes became part of a pool of former retainers, some of whom had lost status and income.

The new rulers imposed heavy burdens. The Matsukura built a large castle at Shimabara and sought to use the domain for military and ceremonial service to the bakufu, which required high taxation; contemporary and later accounts describe harsh collection, including severe penalties for arrears and coercion of tax-delinquent peasants. Amakusa under Terazawa was also heavily assessed. Poor harvests in the 1630s, with reports of crop failure in the years before the rising, increased the strain, and the regions' persecution of Christians continued. Descriptions of the cruelty of Matsukura officials come from sources that sometimes served political purposes, for example by blaming the lord, so students should treat specific accounts of torture carefully, while recognizing the broad pattern of heavy exactions and distress that scholarship supports.

Christianity existed within this larger crisis, not apart from it. Former Christian communities, some of whom had formally apostatized and some of whom had maintained secret belief, shared grievances with other peasants and with dispossessed rōnin. Scholarship on the rising stresses its multiple origins: oppressive rule, taxation, harvest failure, regional dislocation, and the persistence of Christian networks and symbols that provided organization and meaning to people in distress.

Section 47.18

The Shimabara Rebellion, 1637–1638

In the late autumn of 1637 (the tenth month of the lunar year), disturbances broke out in both Shimabara and Amakusa, reportedly provoked by an incident of official coercion, and spread rapidly. Villagers attacked officials and in some places Buddhist and shrine institutions; as the uprising grew, participants renounced their prior apostasy and displayed Christian symbols. A young figure, Amakusa Shirō (Masuda Shirō Tokisada), then in his teens, became the rising's symbolic leader, supported by older leaders including former retainers. Contemporary and later sources attribute to him charismatic and quasi-prophetic qualities, but much of what is said about him comes from accounts written afterward, and historians treat his personal role cautiously.

After initial attacks, including an unsuccessful assault on a castle in Amakusa, the insurgents and their families, men, women, and children, withdrew to Hara Castle on the Shimabara Peninsula, an abandoned fortress of the former Arima lords, and fortified it. Estimates of the numbers within vary widely, commonly in the tens of thousands including noncombatants. Banners and symbols with Christian imagery were displayed, and the defenders held out through the winter.

The bakufu dispatched forces from multiple domains. The first commander, Itakura Shigemasa, was killed during an assault in the new year of 1638, after which Matsudaira Nobutsuna took command and pursued a siege that cut off supplies. The besiegers, drawn from Kyushu and other domains and numbering well over a hundred thousand by common estimates, finally overran the castle at the end of the second lunar month of 1638 (in late February or early March by the Western calendar). The great majority of those inside were killed, including many noncombatants, and the heads of many were displayed. Numbers of dead are uncertain. Authorities also made use of a Dutch ship's cannon at one stage, an episode that Chapter 48 considers in the context of Dutch relations with the bakufu.

The key formulation is that Shimabara was a rebellion with powerful Christian dimensions, not simply a "Christian revolt caused by Christianity." The insurgents' religious language and symbols were real and significant, but so were their grievances over taxes and hunger. Contemporary authorities emphasized Christianity, in part because it allowed them to treat the rising as an extension of a prohibited creed rather than as a response to misrule. Later historians have emphasized one or the other dimension depending on their interests, and the best accounts hold both together. The lord of Shimabara, Matsukura Katsuie, was held responsible for misgovernment and was executed, and Terazawa lost his Amakusa holdings, a sign that bakufu authorities recognized failures of local rule.

Section 47.19

After Shimabara: Anti-Christianism and the Consolidation of Religious Control

The rebellion mattered politically whatever its causes. The bakufu read it in part through the lens of Christian danger, and in its aftermath intensified anti-Christian institutions. Investigations of religious affiliation increased, the bakufu created its office for religious inquiry in 1640, and domains were pressed to adopt systems for detecting prohibited belief. Rewards for informers, neighborhood responsibility, and religious affiliation certificates all developed or were strengthened. These measures reinforced the registration system analyzed in Sections 47.5 and 47.6 and contributed to its spread across domains over the following decades.

The consequences extended into foreign relations: the bakufu already regulated Portuguese contact and in 1639 expelled the Portuguese, in part on anti-Christian grounds. These developments, and the broader regulation of foreign trade and movement, belong to Chapter 48 and are not narrated here. What matters for this chapter is that Shimabara became a powerful reference point for officials: it was cited as proof that Christianity could generate mass organized resistance. The memory of the rising helped justify decades of vigilance in Kyushu.

It would nonetheless be mistaken to see Shimabara as the single cause of the registration system. Anti-Christian certification had begun before 1637, and the system's later development depended on bakufu and domain choices, parish and household interests, and long-term administrative convenience. The rebellion accelerated and intensified a process already under way. Keeping the two histories apart, the history of registration and the history of the rising, prevents the common error of explaining one by the other.

Long-term causes

Strong prior Christian communities; displacement of local warrior families; reassignment of domains.

Short-term causes

Immediate effects

Mass mobilization, the siege of Hara Castle, and the destruction of the insurgents.

Intensified anti-Christian enforcement; punishment of the local lords; reinforcement of registration and of regional vigilance.

Section 47.20

Belief, Practice, and Rule: Religion in a Stable but Dynamic Society

Returning to the chapter's thesis, Tokugawa people were not simply forced Buddhists living under Confucian ideology, nor were they choosing among discrete religions like consumers in a modern denominational marketplace. They practiced religion through households, temples, shrines, pilgrimages, rituals, ethical traditions, sacred specialists, and local communities. The state prohibited Christianity and made Buddhist registration administratively compulsory, while permitting a remarkable range of other practices as long as they did not challenge order. Pluralism and regulation coexisted, and each shaped the other.

The system also changed over time. In the seventeenth century, the emphasis fell on suppression and institution-building. In the eighteenth, the main features were routine administration, an expanding market of pilgrimage and devotional groups, and the commercialization of religious services. In the early nineteenth, mass pilgrimage, new popular movements, and intellectual challenges to Buddhist predominance, topics for later chapters, prepared the ground for the upheavals of the Bakumatsu and Meiji years. The stability of the regime concealed continuing religious creativity.

Religious control cannot be fully understood without examining the international connections that made Christianity appear dangerous and the tightly regulated foreign relationships Tokugawa Japan continued to maintain. Chapter 48 takes up that subject, examining Nagasaki's role in Dutch and Chinese trade, relations with Korea, Ryukyu, and the Ainu, and the limits of describing Tokugawa Japan as isolated.

A village household registered at a Buddhist temple: Registration was an annual obligation tied to funerals, graves, and household standing. A Buddhist priest issuing certification: Certification was an administrative duty and a source of leverage and income, but also a responsibility for which the priest answered to the head temple and to authorities. Priests had interests of their own, including parish stability, and varied widely in wealth and learning.
A shrine priest or ritual specialist: Shrine priests were organized and licensed by houses such as the Yoshida, and yamabushi by Shugendō institutions. Their livelihood depended on festivals, charms, and clients, and many worked at the boundary between institutional and popular religion.
A woman in a pilgrimage association: Associations offered a rare route to travel and to participation in sacred centers, and women managed household offerings. But women faced barriers, including exclusion from some sacred mountains and constraints on travel, so their religious opportunities were both real and limited.
A hidden Christian household in Kyushu: Outwardly registered Buddhists, such households risked punishment if discovered. They preserved prayers and practices at great cost and adapted them over generations without priests, living in constant tension between outward conformity and private faith.
A Tokugawa or domain official responsible for detecting prohibited belief: Officials saw Christianity as a threat to order and obedience, a view reinforced by Shimabara. They relied on registers, informers, and examinations, and balanced vigilance against the practical limits of enforcement.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Life from the Unifiers to the Early Nineteenth Century

What Changed

  • Christianity moved from a tolerated mission to a prohibited, hidden tradition
  • Buddhist temples gained a near-universal parish base and an administrative role
  • Ordinary people acquired graves and memorial rites that were once largely elite
  • Pilgrimage and devotional associations expanded in scale and commercial organization

What Stayed the Same

  • The overlap of Buddhist, kami-related, and ethical traditions in practice
  • Community-based shrine worship and seasonal ritual
  • Subordination of religious institutions to secular authority
  • Household continuity as a central religious value

Who experienced change most directly? Christians experienced the most drastic change, from a growing community to persecution or concealment. Households newly bound to temples and graves also experienced significant change. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers whose shrine festivals and local rites continued largely unchanged. Why the pattern matters: it shows that Tokugawa stability coexisted with deep institutional transformation, and sets the baseline against which Meiji religious policy would operate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Tokugawa religion was neither a single state orthodoxy nor a set of three neatly separated religions. It was an overlapping field of Buddhist institutions, kami worship, Confucian ethics, local cults, pilgrimage, household ritual, and religious specialists, within which most people moved easily. The familiar formula of Buddhism, Shinto, and Confucianism as three established religions, with Christianity banned, obscures this complexity and projects modern categories backward.

Buddhism was the most institutionalized tradition. Governed through hierarchy and regulation rather than doctrinal control, temples were organized into head-and-branch systems and supervised by bakufu and domain officials. Their social importance lay chiefly in the administration of death: funerals, graves, memorial tablets, and memorial services, which gave households a religious means of sustaining the continuity of the ie. The relationship was reciprocal, and it also reproduced social hierarchy, as shown by the treatment of outcast groups in temple records.

Temple certification originated in anti-Christian policy but grew, over the seventeenth century, into annual registration of households and a general instrument of population supervision. Historians debate whether it is best described as religious control or social administration; the best-supported view is that it became both and that its meaning changed over time and place. Temples were intermediaries with their own interests, not simple arms of the state.

Kami worship was carried on in shrines from village sites to national centers, and in most places shrine and temple were linked through shinbutsu shūgō. The shrine priesthood was organized and licensed by houses such as the Yoshida, but there was no "Shinto church," and the later separation of kami and buddhas in the Meiji period was a rupture, not a restoration.

Confucianism supplied a language of ethics, hierarchy, and household duty, and Zhu Xi learning gained prominence in bakufu service, yet it never became a state church and was practiced alongside other traditions. The question whether it was a religion shows that the categories themselves are historical. Popular religion, pilgrimage, devotional associations, and mountain religion provided practical help with illness, travel, and livelihood, and mass movements such as okage mairi showed the vitality of religious networks beyond institutional control.

Christianity, once a growing community in Kyushu, was suppressed in stages, through the edicts of Hideyoshi and the Tokugawa prohibitions of 1612–1614, executions, and the use of fumi-e. Believers responded in varied ways, from martyrdom to apostasy to concealment. The hidden Christians preserved Christian-derived practice for two centuries, changing it over time, until they became visible again in the nineteenth century.

The Shimabara–Amakusa rising of 1637–1638 grew from oppressive rule, heavy taxation, harvest failure, regional dislocation, and surviving Christian networks and symbols. It was a rebellion with powerful Christian dimensions, not simply a Christian revolt, though the bakufu interpreted it largely through the lens of Christian danger and intensified anti-Christian enforcement afterward. The histories of registration and of the rising are related but separate.

Overall, the chapter shows religion under the Tokugawa as plural practice within administrative regulation. Authorities prohibited what they defined as dangerous and supervised institutions, but did not impose doctrinal uniformity. The next chapter turns to the international connections that made Christianity appear threatening and to the controlled foreign relations of Tokugawa Japan.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regime that prized order governed belief without governing doctrine. Religious institutions were folded into administration, yet religious life escaped administrative categories in pilgrimage, healing, household ritual, and ethical teaching. The pairing of regulation and pluralism is a pattern that recurs in other aspects of Tokugawa society.

The chapter also reveals how institutions change function. Temple registration began as a response to a perceived threat and became part of the ordinary fabric of households and villages. The funerary and ancestral practices it helped entrench, from family graves to memorial rites, remained central to Japanese religious life long after the Tokugawa period, a continuity that reaches well into later chapters.

Finally, the chapter connects regions and peoples. Christians in Kyushu, pilgrims from Edo and Osaka, mountain ascetics, village shrine communities, and Confucian scholars shared a society but experienced its religious regulation differently. Their histories lead forward to Chapter 48, where the foreign connections that made Christianity appear dangerous are examined, and to Chapter 55, where the Tokugawa religious order was dismantled and reconstituted in the service of a new state.
