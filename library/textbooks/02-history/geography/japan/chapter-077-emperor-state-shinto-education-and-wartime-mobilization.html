Emperor, State Shinto, Education, and Wartime Mobilization

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On October 21, 1943, in a cold rain, tens of thousands of university and college students marched into the Meiji Jingū Outer Garden Stadium in Tokyo. They were about to leave their classrooms for military service, and the ceremony around them had been planned as carefully as any in the capital's calendar: ranks of uniformed students, officials on a reviewing platform, speeches, and radio coverage that carried the occasion to a national audience.

The send-off of 1943 is a useful entry into this chapter because it displays, in a single afternoon, nearly every instrument by which the Japanese state tried to make a whole society serve a war. A law, issued in the name of the emperor, had ended the draft deferment that had protected most students in the humanities and social sciences. A ministry organized the ceremony. A broadcasting system and a newspaper press, both under official guidance, presented it as a solemn national event. Schools had prepared the students for it through years of drill and instruction in loyalty. Behind the marching ranks stood families, neighborhood associations, and employers who had also been drawn into the apparatus of war. Yet the rain, the individual students whose later diaries and memoirs record fear and doubt, and the classmates who were excused as science majors or medically unfit reveal what the ceremony could not fully control: the varied circumstances and private reactions of the people being mobilized.

Chapter 76 explained how Japan's war in China became a prolonged conflict that military conquest could not resolve. This chapter turns from the battlefield to the society that sustained it. Its subject is the internal organization of wartime Japan between 1937 and 1945: the emperor system and the ideology of national polity (kokutai); State Shinto and the shrine system; the schools; the machinery of propaganda and censorship; the legal and administrative foundations of total mobilization; and the associations that reached into factories, neighborhoods, households, and classrooms. The chapter's central question is how the government transformed established institutions into a system of mass wartime mobilization, and how far that system succeeded in controlling what people believed, said, and did.

The chapter argues that wartime mobilization was neither the work of an army imposing commands on a passive population nor the expression of a single national mind. It combined emperor-centered ideology, existing religious and educational institutions, state propaganda, legal coercion, economic controls, and local organizations, and these elements reinforced one another. Participation reflected patriotism, occupational incentive, family obligation, community solidarity, habit, fear, and legal compulsion in proportions that varied from person to person. Equally important, the system never became seamless. Army and navy competed, ministries quarreled, businesses protected their interests, and ordinary people found ways to accommodate, evade, and privately criticize what they were asked to do.

The institutions examined here did not appear suddenly in 1937. The emperor's constitutional position dates from the Meiji Constitution of 1889; the Imperial Rescript on Education was issued in 1890; the shrine hierarchy was built in the Meiji decades; the special political police and the Peace Preservation Law belong to the years examined in Chapters 67–69 and 74. Wartime governments intensified, redirected, and combined these inheritances. The chapter therefore pays close attention to continuity, and it resists the temptation to treat the late 1930s as the sudden invention of a wholly new system, as well as the opposite temptation to treat the war years as mere continuation of the 1920s.

The people affected were not a uniform "Japanese public." Government officials, schoolteachers, priests and monks, newspaper editors, filmmakers, factory managers, industrial workers, farmers, housewives, schoolchildren, and university students experienced mobilization from different positions and with different degrees of choice. Residents of Korea, Taiwan, and occupied territories were subject to related but not identical demands, and often to harsher ones; their experience belongs primarily to Chapters 78 and 79, but this chapter notes where metropolitan policies extended to them. Buddhist priests, Christian ministers, and members of new religions faced a particular combination of invitation and pressure. Women were urged to embody an ideal of motherhood and household service while also being drawn, unevenly, into labor and organized volunteer work.

The evidence for this subject is rich but demands careful reading. It includes statutes and imperial ordinances published in the Official Gazette (Kanpō); Ministry of Education and Home Ministry records; the ideological texts the state itself issued; newspapers, magazines, films, and broadcasts; police and military surveillance reports; the publications of patriotic organizations; and private diaries, letters, and memoirs. Official sources show what the authorities intended people to hear and do; they do not by themselves show what people believed. Throughout the chapter, students should keep asking who produced a source, for whom, and what it leaves out. The chapter's boundaries are also deliberate. It examines the institutions that organized society for war; the material consequences of that war for civilians, including shortages, bombing, and the Battle of Okinawa, belong to Chapter 80, and the forced labor and violence experienced by colonial subjects belong to Chapter 79.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the emperor as a constitutional institution, Hirohito as an individual participant in government, and the emperor's image in official propaganda.
  2. Explain the concept of kokutai and analyze how the Ministry of Education's Kokutai no Hongi (1937) redefined loyalty, sacrifice, and the individual's place in the nation.
  3. Evaluate how historians assess Hirohito's participation in wartime decision-making, avoiding both the image of a dictator and that of a powerless figurehead.
  4. Describe how the shrine system, imperial rites, and Yasukuni Shrine functioned as instruments of national ritual, and why the state's classification of shrine rites as nonreligious was contested.
  5. Compare the wartime experiences of Buddhist, Christian, and new-religion communities under the Religious Organizations Law and related pressures.
  6. Trace how schools, from national schools to universities, were reshaped to cultivate disciplined imperial subjects, and how educators responded.
  7. Analyze the machinery of propaganda and censorship across press, radio, film, literature, and music, and distinguish what authorities intended from what audiences believed.
  8. Explain the provisions, purposes, and limits of the National Mobilization Law of 1938 and the wartime command economy.
  9. Assess the Imperial Rule Assistance Association and the 1942 election as examples of authoritarian political mobilization that did not abolish constitutional institutions.
  10. Analyze how neighborhood associations and women's organizations could serve both mutual assistance and surveillance, and how women's prescribed and actual roles differed.
  11. Interpret the intensified student and youth mobilization of 1943–1945 and its effects on education and labor allocation.
  12. Evaluate competing explanations of what sustained wartime mobilization and read official sources critically for their purposes and gaps.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the legal and ideological structure of the Meiji emperor system make it possible to present wartime policy as an expression of imperial will, even when decisions emerged from competing institutions?
  2. What did officials mean by kokutai in the 1930s, and why did they believe that liberal and individualist ideas threatened it?
  3. How should historians weigh Hirohito's documented participation in policy against the collective character of wartime decision-making?
  4. In what sense was State Shinto "religious," and why did the legal answer matter to Buddhists, Christians, and members of new religions?
  5. How did schools and universities transmit ideology, and where did educators and students adapt, resist, or find private meanings?
  6. How did the state's propaganda and censorship system operate, and what limits does the evidence place on claims about what ordinary people believed?
  7. Which instruments of mobilization depended mainly on law, which on organization, and which on social pressure?
  8. Why did the Imperial Rule Assistance Association not produce a single-party state of the kind seen in Nazi Germany, and what does this reveal about the Japanese political structure?
  9. What limitations emerged as military demands outran the capacity of the mobilization system?

Historical Context

The ideological and institutional materials of wartime mobilization were assembled over the preceding half century. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 declared the emperor sacred and inviolable, described his reign as continuing in an unbroken line, vested him with sovereignty and with supreme command of the army and navy, and also created an elected House of Representatives and a cabinet whose members were responsible to the emperor rather than to the Diet (Chapter 59). The Imperial Rescript on Education of 1890 gave moral instruction an emperor-centered form and was displayed, read aloud, and revered in schools across the country. The Meiji state also built a hierarchy of Shinto shrines under government administration, created Yasukuni Shrine to honor those who died for the emperor, and developed a system of compulsory schooling that reached nearly all children by the early twentieth century (Chapter 55).

Politics during the 1920s and early 1930s added further elements. Party cabinets governed for much of the period from 1918 to 1932, and universal male suffrage was enacted in 1925 (Chapter 69), but the Peace Preservation Law of the same year gave police tools against radical organization, and the Special Higher Police monitored dissent. The Manchurian Incident of 1931, the assassinations of political leaders, and the military's growing autonomy ended party government in 1932 (Chapters 74 and 75). In 1935 an attack on the constitutional scholar Minobe Tatsukichi's "organ theory," which described the emperor as an organ of the state, led the government to issue statements affirming the national polity and to ban his books, a controversy that directly prepared the ground for the ideological campaign of 1937. The February 1936 mutiny of young officers, and the reinstatement of the rule that army and navy ministers must be serving officers, further strengthened the military's position within the cabinet.

The outbreak of full-scale war with China in July 1937 (Chapter 76) transformed what had been an ideological and political trend into an urgent administrative project. The Konoe Fumimaro cabinet, formed in June 1937, launched the National Spiritual Mobilization movement in the autumn; the Cabinet Planning Board began coordinating resources; and by 1938 the Diet had enacted the National Mobilization Law. Japan was not alone in turning to state direction of economy and society in the 1930s, and historians have compared its measures with those of other industrial states preparing for or fighting total war. But the Japanese system grew from specific inheritances: an emperor-centered constitutional order without a single supreme executive, strong ministerial and military bureaucracies, a dense network of local associations, and a school and shrine system that already reached into every community.

These foundations also help explain what the system could not do. The constitutional structure divided authority among the cabinet, the Diet, the Privy Council, the House of Peers, and the army and navy general staffs, and no wartime reform replaced this fragmentation with unified command. Mobilization therefore proceeded through accretion and competition among agencies. The population, finally, was neither an undifferentiated mass nor a blank slate. Decades of schooling, commercial mass media, urbanization, and organized social movements (Chapters 65 through 70) had produced a society with high literacy, a vigorous press, a rich popular culture, and experienced civic and labor organizations. Wartime authorities used these resources and also feared them.

Section 77.1 · Division I — The Emperor System, National Polity, and the Ideology of Wartime Japan

The Emperor System and the Political Foundations of Wartime Authority

The legal foundation of wartime authority was the Meiji Constitution, promulgated in 1889 as a gift from the emperor to his subjects. Its opening articles described Japan as governed by a line of emperors unbroken for ages eternal, declared the emperor sacred and inviolable, and made him the holder of sovereign rights who exercised legislative power with the consent of the Diet. Other articles gave him command of the army and navy, the power to declare war and make peace, the authority to issue emergency ordinances, and the right to appoint and dismiss officials. Read alone, such provisions could suggest personal rule. Read with the rest of the document and with its practice, they describe something more complicated: a sovereign in whose name government was conducted, but who acted on the advice of ministers, the military chiefs, and a circle of senior statesmen and court officials.

Three features of this system mattered especially in wartime. First, the cabinet had no constitutional status as a unified executive: each minister was responsible individually to the emperor, and the prime minister was formally first among equals. Prime ministers could rarely compel their colleagues, and a cabinet could be destroyed if the army or navy withdrew its minister or refused to supply a replacement, a power the services regained in 1936 when ministers were again required to be serving officers of high rank. Second, the "right of supreme command" (tōsuiken), which was customarily interpreted to place military operations and organization under the army and navy general staffs rather than the cabinet, meant that civilian leaders could be excluded from decisions about operations. Third, the emperor's person gave legitimacy to policy. Whatever the real balance of forces behind a decision, it was announced as an imperial decision, accompanied by a rescript or an imperial conference, and criticism of it could be construed as disloyalty to the throne.

This last feature made the emperor system an unusually potent instrument of mobilization. Policies could be presented not as the preferences of a party, a faction, or a general, but as the will of a sovereign above politics. Officials who wished to silence opponents could describe criticism as an offense against the national polity. Wartime campaigns routinely invoked the "imperial army" and the "imperial subjects," and the declaration of war in December 1941 was issued as an imperial rescript. At the same time, the system diffused responsibility. Because the emperor did not initiate most policies and ministers acted in his name, no single office carried the full burden of decision, and the ambiguity became a long-term problem for any attempt to determine responsibility for the war (Chapter 83).

Historians therefore distinguish three things that popular accounts often blur. The emperor as a constitutional institution possessed formal powers and symbolic authority that shaped what others could do. Hirohito as an individual was a participant in government who read reports, asked questions, and sometimes influenced events, as Section 77.3 explains. And the emperor as represented in propaganda was an object of devotion whose image was carefully managed: portraits were enshrined in schools, his name was not to be spoken casually, and references to him in print were governed by strict conventions. The last of these was the one most directly useful for mobilizing the population, and it did not depend on how much power Hirohito actually exercised.

Students should be cautious about assuming that the emperor system created a uniformly obedient population. The constitutional structure produced conflicts as well as unity: between the army and navy, between the services and civilian ministries, between the cabinet and the court, and between factions within each. Wartime ideology insisted on unity under the throne precisely because that unity could not be taken for granted. The evidence that officials continued to worry about public morale, rumors, and "dangerous thought" throughout the war, in surveillance reports examined later in this chapter, shows how much effort the state devoted to producing the consensus it claimed already existed.

Section 77.2 · Division I

Kokutai, the Imperial Way, and the Official Ideology of National Unity

Kokutai, usually translated "national polity" or "national essence," was the central concept of official ideology. In its broadest sense it referred to the distinctive character of the Japanese state: the rule of an unbroken imperial line descended, in the mythological tradition, from the sun goddess Amaterasu. The term had circulated since the nineteenth century, and its meaning was never fixed. In the 1930s, however, the government set out to define it authoritatively. After the 1935 controversy over Minobe Tatsukichi's constitutional theory, the cabinet issued statements declaring that the emperor was the ruler of the state rather than an organ of it, and the Ministry of Education was charged with turning these declarations into a comprehensive statement of principle. The result, published in March 1937 by the ministry's Bureau of Thought Control (renamed the Bureau of Education and Learning that year), was the Kokutai no Hongi ("Fundamental Principles of the National Polity").

The text was distributed to schools, universities, government offices, and other institutions in large numbers, and later editions were issued in simplified forms. The original March 1937 printing numbered approximately 300,000 copies; more than two million copies were ultimately distributed in Japan and its empire, according to the publication history summarized by Columbia University. The initial print run and the later cumulative distribution are different measures and should not be conflated. Its audience was elite and professional as much as popular: teachers were expected to study it, and it served as a reference for school ceremonies and curricula. Its argument rested on a reading of the mythological chronicles, the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, as sources of historical truth about the origins of the state, a reading that scholars outside the ideological establishment did not share (Chapter 8). Critical historical study of the chronicles, which had flourished in the 1920s, was consequently placed under pressure. The jurist and historian Tsuda Sōkichi, whose analyses of the early chronicles treated their accounts of the imperial ancestors as later constructions, was prosecuted for insulting the dignity of the throne in 1940, and his books were banned.

Several ideas in the text deserve attention. First, it described the relationship of emperor and people through the metaphor of the family-state: the nation as a single household with the emperor as its head, in which loyalty to the sovereign and filial devotion to parents were treated as one moral principle. This metaphor made the demands of the state continuous with the obligations of daily life and gave ordinary family virtues a political meaning. Second, it presented sacrifice as the fulfillment of the self rather than its denial: serving the emperor was described as the realization of one's true nature as a Japanese subject. Third, it argued that modern Western thought, especially liberalism, individualism, socialism, and communism, was founded on assumptions incompatible with the national polity and had produced social disorder in Japan. These ideas were to be replaced, or rather absorbed, through a return to what the text called the Japanese way.

The Imperial Way (kōdō) was a related slogan used by the army and by ultranationalist writers in the early 1930s, and it had been associated with the "Imperial Way faction" of officers purged after the February 1936 mutiny (Chapter 75). By 1937 the term had been absorbed into a wider official vocabulary. The Ministry of Education followed Kokutai no Hongi in 1941 with Shinmin no Michi ("The Way of Subjects"), a companion text addressing the conduct required of the people in wartime. The two books were not identical in tone or emphasis, and some scholars see differences between the more abstract arguments of the first and the practical injunctions of the second, but together they defined the ideology the schools and the patriotic associations were to transmit.

Official ideology did not go unchallenged even within the educated elite. Some intellectuals regarded the book as an embarrassing mixture of mythology and polemic; others privately dismissed it while publicly conforming; and still others, including academics with Christian, Marxist, or liberal commitments, struggled over how much to say. At the same time, many people found elements of the message compelling: the sense of national mission in Asia, the glorification of sacrifice, or the appeal of a unified moral order in a society that had experienced rapid change and economic crisis. The ideology's strength came less from its logical coherence than from its institutional reach, since it was embedded in textbooks, ceremonies, and legal categories.

Section 77.3 · Division I

Emperor Hirohito, Imperial Conferences, and the Exercise of Wartime Power

Hirohito, born in 1901, had been emperor since December 1926. He was educated to regard himself as a constitutional monarch, and in the years before the war he developed habits of political engagement that varied with circumstances. In the Zhang Zuolin affair of 1928, he reportedly reprimanded Prime Minister Tanaka Giichi so sharply that the cabinet fell; in the February 1936 mutiny he insisted that the rebels be suppressed rather than accommodated; and in the same years he generally avoided overruling a cabinet's unified recommendation, in keeping with a constitutional convention he considered binding. These episodes illustrate the dilemma at the center of historians' debates: the emperor could act decisively when he chose, but the conventions of the system and his own temperament led him to confine most interventions to questions, warnings, and requests for reconsideration.

After the outbreak of war in 1937, a set of institutions linked Hirohito to policy. Imperial General Headquarters was established in November 1937 as the supreme command, with the emperor nominally at its head, and a Liaison Conference between the government and the military chiefs began meeting to coordinate strategy and policy. Major decisions, such as the commitment to war with the United States and Britain in 1941, were formalized at imperial conferences (gozen kaigi) held in the emperor's presence. At these meetings the participants had usually settled their positions beforehand, and the emperor customarily did not speak. The conference of September 6, 1941, which set a deadline for diplomacy before preparations for war, is remembered because Hirohito broke custom by reciting a poem of the Meiji emperor, which his listeners understood as an expression of preference for peace. The conference of December 1, 1941, confirmed the decision for war, and the rescript declaring war was issued a week later.

Between conferences, the emperor received regular audiences from ministers, the chiefs of staff, and senior statesmen, and he read or heard operational briefings. Military orders required his approval, and records of these audiences, such as the later-published diary of his grand chamberlain and the memoranda kept by court officials, show that he asked pointed questions about operations, expressed dissatisfaction with military performance, and sometimes asked for changes in plans. After the fall of Saipan in July 1944, he reportedly pressed for a decisive victory, and court insiders and former prime ministers helped bring down the Tōjō cabinet. In February 1945 the former prime minister Konoe Fumimaro presented a memorial warning of the danger of communist revolution after defeat, and Hirohito reportedly responded that another military success would be necessary before peace could be considered. The sources, though subject to debate about how they were compiled, establish a ruler who was informed about the war and who sometimes shaped it.

The central historiographical question is how much influence these interventions represented. One body of scholarship, associated with historians such as Herbert Bix, emphasizes Hirohito's active role in strategic and political decisions and argues that the "powerless figurehead" account was constructed after 1945 to protect the throne and to serve American occupation goals (Chapter 83). Another body, associated with scholars such as Stephen Large and, in different ways, others, stresses the constraints of the constitutional system and the limits of the emperor's capacity to veto the unanimous advice of ministers and chiefs, while acknowledging that he exercised influence. A third line of work focuses on the structure of the "emperor system" as a whole, in which responsibility was diffused across institutions. The evidence does not support either extreme: Hirohito was neither the omnipotent dictator of wartime Allied propaganda nor a ceremonial spectator. He participated, at times influentially, within a system designed to prevent any single actor, including the emperor, from governing alone.

The distinction between the emperor's role and the emperor's image remains important. Whatever the emperor's private role, the state's public presentation of him as the object of devotion and the source of national policy was a key resource of mobilization. Soldiers swore loyalty to him; subjects were told that service to the state was service to him; and a rescript issued in his name could reframe a controversial policy as a sacred obligation. The question of how far Hirohito's personal decisions contributed to the policies that his image legitimized is a question of historical responsibility, which this textbook takes up directly in Chapter 83.

Section 77.4 · Division I

National Spiritual Mobilization, Patriotic Ritual, and the Cultivation of Sacrifice

In September 1937, within weeks of the escalation of fighting around Shanghai, the Konoe cabinet announced a National Spiritual Mobilization movement (Kokumin seishin sōdōin undō). Its slogans, repeated on posters and in speeches, called for national unity, service to the country with utmost loyalty, and perseverance through hardship. In October a central league of existing organizations was formed to coordinate the effort, with participation by ministries, the army and navy, industrial and business associations, religious bodies, youth and women's groups, and local governments. The movement was not a mass party but an umbrella that used existing channels, and its character was therefore partly top-down, partly dependent on the initiative of local leaders and private organizations.

Its methods were persuasion, ritual, and example. Officials organized flag-waving send-off ceremonies for departing soldiers, lantern parades to celebrate victories, collective visits to local shrines to pray for military success, and moments of silent prayer at fixed times. Neighborhood groups collected comfort bags and handmade "thousand-stitch belts" (senninbari), which women were asked to sew with a stitch from each of a thousand passersby. From 1939, the first day of each month was designated a day of service to the revitalization of Asia (Kōa hōkokubi), on which people were asked to raise the flag, visit a shrine, and eat a simple lunch of rice with a pickled plum in the center, resembling the national flag. After the beginning of the Pacific War, the eighth day of each month commemorated the imperial declaration of war. Campaigns promoted frugality and discouraged luxuries, and the slogan "Luxury is the enemy," which appeared in 1940, became one of the most remembered of the period. The 2,600th anniversary of the legendary founding of the imperial line, celebrated with ceremonies throughout the country in November 1940, gave the state an occasion for national ritual on an unprecedented scale.

The central message of these campaigns was sacrifice: of consumption, of personal comfort, of time, and ultimately of life. In January 1941 the army minister, Tōjō Hideki, issued a Field Service Code (Senjinkun) to soldiers that emphasized loyalty to the emperor, discipline, and the shame of surrender, a text later cited in discussions of the treatment of prisoners and of the reluctance of soldiers to surrender. Funerals for the war dead, shrine ceremonies for departed soldiers, and the conferral of honors on bereaved families presented death in battle as the highest form of service. The state encouraged the bereaved to express pride rather than grief, and families that did otherwise could face criticism from neighbors.

The effect of these campaigns on belief is difficult to measure. They certainly produced public participation: crowds at send-offs, donations of money and metal, and an enormous number of local meetings. Some people took part with sincere enthusiasm, especially in the early years when victories in China seemed to promise a quick end to the conflict. Others participated because refusal was socially dangerous, because employers or neighbors expected it, or because ritual participation had become a habit. Diaries and later memoirs describe the monotony and obligation of such events as well as their emotional pull, and police reports record rumors, grumbling, and ridicule of campaign slogans. As the war dragged on and shortages grew, the spiritual campaigns increasingly had to be reinforced by material controls and legal sanctions, a sequence examined in Division IV.

The spiritual mobilization of 1937 illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout this chapter: the state used existing associations and ceremonial forms, adding new slogans and demands, rather than creating an entirely new apparatus. Its limits were also apparent early. The central league could coordinate but not compel, and it was absorbed in 1940 into the larger Imperial Rule Assistance structure (Section 77.16). The movement's lasting significance lay less in its organization than in the habits it inculcated and in the vocabulary of sacrifice and service that later mobilization measures would invoke.

Section 77.5 · Division II — State Shinto, Religious Institutions, and Wartime Education

State Shinto, Shrines, Imperial Worship, and Patriotic Ritual

The term "State Shinto" (kokka Shintō) is a convenient label for a system of state-administered shrine rites, imperial ceremonies, and associated ideology that took shape after the Meiji Restoration. It is not a term the wartime state used officially, and scholars debate its precise scope. In practice it referred to the shrines under the supervision of the government rather than all Shinto belief or practice, which included local festivals, household worship, and the organized "sect Shinto" (kyōha Shintō) groups such as Tenrikyō's predecessors and Kurozumikyō (Chapter 55). The Meiji government had ranked thousands of shrines in a hierarchy, with the Ise Grand Shrine, dedicated to the imperial ancestress Amaterasu, at the top, followed by shrines receiving official offerings and then by prefectural, district, and village shrines. By the late 1930s well over one hundred thousand shrines of various sizes stood in the archipelago, many tied to local communities whose members had little interest in national doctrine.

Shrine administration was handled by a Bureau of Shrines in the Home Ministry, separate from the Bureau of Religions in the Ministry of Education that supervised Buddhist, Christian, and sect Shinto organizations. This division reflected the official doctrine that shrine rites were not "religion" but national observances that every subject could perform, a doctrine reconciled with the freedom of belief guaranteed by the constitution, subject to law and the duties of subjects. In November 1940 the government elevated shrine administration to an independent Shrine Board (Jingiin), and wartime policy increasingly emphasized the shrines' role in national life. Shrine priests were encouraged to lead patriotic ceremonies, hold services for military success, and conduct rites for departing soldiers.

Yasukuni Shrine, founded in 1869 in Tokyo as a shrine for those who died fighting for the imperial cause, had by the 1930s become the central site for commemorating the war dead. It was unusual in being administered jointly by the army and navy ministries and in enshrining the spirits of the dead as kami. The souls of soldiers killed in battle were ritually enshrined in periodic ceremonies, sometimes attended by the emperor, and bereaved families were sometimes invited to Tokyo for the occasion. In 1939 the prefectural shrines for war dead were renamed "protect the nation" shrines (gokoku jinja), integrating them into a nationwide network. The significance of this system for mobilization is clear: it told soldiers and their families that death in battle would be honored by the emperor and the nation, and it linked local grief to national purpose. Its significance for later controversy about Yasukuni, which resumed after the war with the enshrinement of convicted war criminals, is discussed in later chapters.

Schools, public offices, and local communities were drawn into shrine ritual. Students were taken on group visits to local shrines, particularly on days of national observance; school ceremonies took place before the imperial portrait; and officials presided at shrine festivals as representatives of the state. Households were encouraged to keep a small shrine shelf (kamidana) with a talisman from Ise. Beyond Japan proper, the state built shrines in Taiwan, Korea, Karafuto, Manchuria, and the Pacific mandates, and from the 1930s pressed colonial subjects, including students and Christian communities, to take part in rites. In Korea, resistance to compulsory shrine attendance, especially among Christians, led to school closures and arrests; the full history of these policies belongs to Chapters 78 and 79.

The official claim that shrine rites were secular was historically consequential and contested. Defenders argued that bowing at a shrine expressed civic loyalty rather than religious belief; critics, including many Christians and Buddhists, argued that rites addressed to kami, with priests, offerings, and prayers, were religious in any ordinary sense. The Catholic Church's Vatican office ruled in 1936 that Catholics could take part in such ceremonies as patriotic acts, a decision that eased tension for Japanese Catholics; Protestant churches negotiated their own accommodations, often under heavy pressure. The classification did not remove the religious dimension of shrine worship for believers, nor did it settle the question of who defined what counted as religion. After 1945 the Occupation abolished the system (Chapter 82).

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Kokutai, Imperial Authority, and State Shinto

Kokutai ("national polity") referred to the supposed essential character of the Japanese state as an emperor-centered community. It was not a technical legal category but a political concept whose boundaries the state could stretch; its use in the Peace Preservation Law made it a basis for criminal prosecution. Imperial authority in this chapter refers to three distinct things: the emperor's constitutional powers, the personal participation of Hirohito in government, and the symbolic use of the emperor in propaganda. State Shinto is a modern scholarly label for state-administered shrine rites and associated doctrine, used with caution because officials distinguished shrine rites from "religion" and because most Shinto practice was local, household, or sectarian.

These terms matter because they were instruments as well as descriptions. To say that a policy served the kokutai was to claim immunity from criticism; to say that shrine rites were "not religion" was to claim that they could be required of everyone. English words such as "dictatorship," "theocracy," or "state religion" misleadingly suggest that these categories were clear and uncontested.

Section 77.6 · Division II

Buddhism, Christianity, New Religions, and Religious Mobilization

Religious institutions outside the shrine system were governed by a patchwork of regulations until the Religious Organizations Law (Shūkyō dantai hō) of April 1939, which took effect in April 1940. The earlier practice had been to regulate religious bodies through the Ministry of Education's administrative guidance and the general police powers of the state, since Buddhism, Christianity, and the sect Shinto groups had no unified statutory framework. The new law placed Buddhist denominations, Shinto sects, and Christian churches under a system of government recognition: organizations had to obtain approval, submit to inspection, and meet standards for their doctrine and leadership, and the minister could order the dissolution of those regarded as harmful to public order or the duties of subjects. Its stated purposes included protecting religious bodies and promoting their development, and many religious leaders had long sought legal recognition. Its effect, however, was to give the state a tool for compelling consolidation and conformity.

Buddhist denominations, which had been subject to state pressure since the persecution of Buddhism early in the Meiji period (Chapter 55), had a strong record of institutional cooperation. Major denominations, including the Jōdo Shinshū branches, Sōtō and Rinzai Zen, Nichiren groups, and Shingon, issued statements supporting the war, sent chaplains and fundraising appeals to soldiers and families, held memorial services for war dead, and reinterpreted doctrine to support national service. The Shin Buddhist tradition used older distinctions between religious truth and worldly obligation to justify conformity. After 1945 several denominations publicly acknowledged and apologized for wartime cooperation, though the dates and terms of those statements varied, and the issue has been revisited by scholars and by Buddhist communities themselves. At the same time, individual priests and lay groups dissented: the Buddhist Youth League for Renovation, a radical group, was suppressed in the mid-1930s, and its leader, Seno'o Girō, was prosecuted. Dissent was rare but not absent, and cooperation did not necessarily imply private conviction.

Christians, a small minority, were in a particularly exposed position because of the association of Christianity with the West and with foreign missionary organizations. Under the Religious Organizations Law, the Protestant denominations were pressed to unite, and in June 1941 more than thirty groups merged to form the United Church of Christ in Japan (Nihon Kirisuto Kyōdan). Some church leaders favored union on theological grounds; others accepted it as the price of survival. Foreign missionaries left the country in large numbers as relations with the United States and Britain deteriorated. The Catholic Church, whose Japanese leadership grew during the 1930s, also accommodated, with the first Japanese archbishop of Tokyo appointed in 1937. Members of some smaller groups, including the Holiness Church, were arrested in 1942 on charges connected with their teachings about the second coming of Christ, which the authorities treated as incompatible with the emperor's position. Christian pacifism and universalism found little public expression, though some individuals and congregations quietly maintained private convictions.

New religions presented the state with particular problems because they often offered teachings with their own cosmologies and charismatic leadership. Ōmoto, a movement with a strong millenarian message, had been suppressed in 1921 and again in December 1935, when its leaders were arrested and its headquarters destroyed. Hito no Michi, a precursor of what became PL Kyōdan, was suppressed in 1937. Tenrikyō, which had a much larger membership, adjusted its teachings and activities to demonstrate loyalty. The Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai, a lay Nichiren movement founded by the educator Makiguchi Tsunesaburō, was targeted for refusing to accept shrine talismans; Makiguchi and his associate Toda Jōsei were arrested in 1943, and Makiguchi died in custody in November 1944. The pattern shows that official policy rewarded accommodation and punished movements that challenged imperial authority or shrine rites, but it also shows that the targets were selected by the police and the Ministry of Education rather than by a stable doctrinal standard.

Historians therefore distinguish between voluntary cooperation and administrative coercion, though the boundary was often blurred. Religious leaders who supported the war may have done so from conviction, a desire to protect their institutions, or a calculation about what was necessary; ordinary believers followed in various ways. The law made nonconformity costly, and the example of arrests encouraged caution. The record of religious mobilization is also a record of religious diversity: communities absorbed, reinterpreted, and sometimes quietly resisted state demands, and the war years left a legacy of postwar reckoning within religious bodies about responsibility and complicity.

Section 77.7 · Division II

The Imperial Rescript on Education, National Schools, and the Formation of Imperial Subjects

By the late 1930s, the Japanese school system was one of the most extensive institutions of the state. Nearly all children attended compulsory primary school, and many teachers had been trained in government normal schools with a strong emphasis on loyalty and discipline. The ideological foundation of schooling was the Imperial Rescript on Education, issued in the emperor's name in October 1890, which set out a moral vision of subjects devoted to filial piety, harmony, and loyalty and ready to offer themselves courageously to the state in emergencies. Copies of the rescript and portraits of the emperor and empress were distributed to schools, and in many schools they were kept in special fireproof repositories, brought out on national holidays for ceremonial reading. Teachers and principals were held responsible for their safe-keeping, and there are documented cases of principals resigning or taking their own lives after fires or other accidents damaged the portraits.

School ceremonies on national holidays such as New Year's Day, the legendary founding day of the empire in February, the emperor's birthday, and the Meiji emperor's birthday followed a fixed pattern: students bowed toward the portrait, the principal read the rescript, and the assembly sang the national anthem. Textbooks, which had been compiled or approved by the government since the early twentieth century, included "ethics" (shūshin) lessons in which stories of loyal subjects and filial children illustrated the principles of the rescript, and national history lessons that began with the mythological age. Physical training, including group exercises, marching, and martial arts, was strengthened. Many students also encountered the world through reading and singing, in which patriotic songs and war stories became increasingly prominent after 1937.

In December 1937, the Konoe government created an Education Deliberative Council (Kyōiku Shingikai) to propose a comprehensive reorganization of schooling, and its recommendations led to a series of reforms. The most visible was the National School Order (Kokumin gakkō rei), issued March 1, 1941, and put into effect on April 1, which renamed elementary schools "national schools" (kokumin gakkō) and defined their purpose as providing the basic training of imperial subjects in accordance with the Imperial Way. The curriculum was reorganized into four broad areas, national studies (including ethics, language, history, and geography), science and mathematics, physical training, and the arts and crafts, in an effort to integrate subjects around the cultivation of the subject. Plans to extend compulsory education from six to eight years were announced but repeatedly postponed as the war made implementation impossible.

The new textbooks of 1941 introduced simplified reading materials with heavy patriotic content, and the emphasis on drill, group training, and physical discipline increased. Teachers were instructed in the ideals of the new system through the study of official documents including Kokutai no Hongi. Classroom practice, however, varied. Some teachers embraced the ideals with enthusiasm; others followed the prescribed forms while keeping their distance; and some used the system's own language to protect students or to emphasize humane values. Evidence from teachers' memoirs, student diaries, and school records suggests that the quality and intensity of ideological instruction depended heavily on local circumstances and on individual teachers. Rural children in small schools and urban children in large ones had different experiences, as did boys and girls, who were taught distinct gender roles.

Beyond the metropolis, similar models were applied in Korea and Taiwan, where schools were used as instruments of the assimilation policy known as "imperialization" (kōminka). In Korea, a revised education ordinance in 1938 reduced and later eliminated instruction in the Korean language, and students were required to recite an oath of imperial subjecthood; in Taiwan, language and name policies followed a similar pattern. Colonial education differed from metropolitan education in structure and purpose, and the forms of coercion and the responses of colonial communities are treated in Chapter 79.

Section 77.8 · Division II

Secondary Schools, Universities, Youth Training, and the Militarization of Learning

Beyond the primary schools, the state extended its influence through secondary and higher education and through institutions for youth who left school early. Military drill, taught by active or reserve army officers assigned to schools since 1925, was a regular part of the middle-school and higher-school curriculum; by the late 1930s its content and importance had expanded, and school performance in drill could affect advancement. Youth schools (seinen gakkō), established in 1935 by merging youth training centers and vocational continuation schools, gave part-time education to young men and women who had entered the workforce; from 1939, attendance for boys became compulsory. These schools combined technical training, moral education, and military preparation and brought many working youth into regular contact with state-sponsored training for the first time.

Patriotic student organizations grew rapidly. School patriotic corps (gakkō hōkokudan), organized from 1941 and brought under a national federation in 1942, replaced older student clubs and organized students for drill, labor service, and group activities. Participation in the corps carried the presumption of loyalty and made nonparticipation conspicuous. Teachers were mobilized as well: in 1943 the government raised the level of normal schools to that of specialized colleges, partly in order to attract more candidates, and teachers were pressed to serve as leaders of youth and community groups. Curricula changed: in higher schools and universities, science and engineering received priority, humanities and social science faculties were reduced or reorganized, and the length of study was shortened by several months to a year.

Universities were the most contested institutions. They had developed traditions of academic autonomy in the Taishō years, and the state's efforts to discipline them predated the war. The Kyoto Imperial University law professor Takikawa Yukitoki was forced from his post in 1933 after a dispute with the Ministry of Education over his writings; Minobe Tatsukichi was driven from public life in 1935; and in December 1937 the economist Yanaihara Tadao resigned from Tokyo Imperial University under pressure after publishing an essay critical of the government's China policy and expressing his Christian pacifist convictions. In 1939 the economist Kawai Eijirō, a liberal critic of both fascism and Marxism, was suspended from Tokyo Imperial University and his books were banned, and Tsuda Sōkichi's prosecution followed in 1940. Each case involved different circumstances and different official justifications, but together they taught scholars the boundaries of acceptable speech.

Responses within universities ranged widely. Some professors enthusiastically supported the new ideology, producing scholarship on the national polity, the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, or the cultural mission of Japan; others reworked their research programs to meet official priorities, particularly in the sciences, where military funding offered resources and security; still others focused on narrowly technical or classical subjects that avoided political controversy. A few resisted openly and paid for it. Students likewise varied: some joined patriotic corps with conviction, others went through the forms, and a minority engaged in quiet study of banned authors. The "Kyoto School" of philosophy, to give one example, produced thinkers whose wartime writings combined philosophical sophistication with support for the war, and their legacy remains controversial.

This section concerns the preparations rather than the climax of educational mobilization. Through 1942, the state expanded drill, shortened courses, and shifted resources toward science and technology, but most students continued to attend classes. The large-scale mobilization of students for labor and military service, which transformed education after 1943, is examined in Section 77.19. The earlier measures prepared the ground by habituating students and teachers to the idea that schooling existed to serve national purposes and by weakening the institutional independence that might have resisted the later and more drastic demands.

Section 77.9 · Division III — Censorship, Propaganda, Media, and Wartime Culture

The Cabinet Information Bureau and the Machinery of Wartime Propaganda

Before the late 1930s, control of information was divided among several agencies. The Home Ministry's censorship section examined publications and could ban or require deletions under the Publication Law and the Newspaper Law; the Ministry of Communications supervised broadcasting; the Foreign Ministry handled information directed at other countries; and the army and navy maintained press offices that issued communiqués and pressed newspapers to follow their line. This division produced competition and inconsistency, and as war with China widened, officials concluded that a central coordinating body was needed. A Cabinet Information Committee was created in 1936, and in September 1937 it was expanded into a Cabinet Information Department (Naikaku Jōhōbu), which was tasked with coordinating propaganda and information policy and with managing relations with the press.

In December 1940, under the second Konoe cabinet, the department was reorganized as the Cabinet Information Bureau (Naikaku Jōhōkyoku), established on December 6. The bureau absorbed functions previously held by other ministries, including the supervision of news, magazines, and books, the guidance of broadcasting, and the coordination of films and cultural propaganda, and its staff included civilian officials as well as military officers. It issued guidelines to newspapers and publishers, organized campaigns, sponsored exhibitions and public lectures, and published illustrated weeklies, notably the Shashin Shūhō (Photographic Weekly), begun in 1938, which combined news photographs with exhortation. Its authority nonetheless had limits. The Home Ministry retained important censorship powers, the army and navy continued to insist on control over military information, and the Ministry of Communications and other agencies defended their prerogatives. The bureau was a coordinating agency, not a single ministry of propaganda on the German model.

The legal framework for control expanded in parallel. The Military Secrets Protection Law was tightened in 1937; the National Mobilization Law included provisions allowing the government to restrict newspapers and other publications; the National Defense Security Law of 1941 broadened the definition of protected secrets; and a Wartime Special Law on speech, publication, assembly, and association, enacted in December 1941, required licenses for political meetings and organizations and increased penalties for statements deemed harmful. These laws were enforced through a combination of prior review, after-the-fact punishment, and informal pressure on editors and publishers, many of whom knew that a single disfavored article could lead to suspension or prosecution.

In practice, the bureau's most effective method was "guidance" (shidō): regular meetings in which officials told editors what topics to emphasize, what to avoid, and how to frame events. Editors might be told that an issue was a "dangerous topic," such as the economic condition of workers, the scale of military losses, or the activities of foreign governments. Daily directives and informal rules created a sense of what could and could not be said, and self-censorship often did the work that formal prohibitions could not. Foreign news was heavily filtered through the official news agency Dōmei Tsūshin, formed in 1936 by merging earlier agencies, which became the primary source of news from abroad.

The aims of this system were broader than suppression. Authorities sought to create a shared picture of the war: its causes, its justification as a campaign to liberate Asia from Western domination, the heroism of Japanese soldiers, and the unity of the nation behind the emperor. Officials also tried to manage morale by controlling bad news, a practice that became increasingly consequential as the war turned against Japan in 1942 and after. The communiqués released by Imperial General Headquarters about the Battle of Midway in June 1942, for instance, greatly understated Japanese losses, and the public was not told the scale of the defeat. The effect of such management on public belief, however, depended on whether people had alternative sources of information, a question taken up in the next two sections.

Section 77.10 · Division III

Newspapers, Radio Broadcasting, and the Management of Public Opinion

Japan entered the war with a highly developed commercial press. The large dailies, notably the Asahi Shimbun and the Mainichi group, had circulations in the millions, supported extensive reporting staffs, and relied on advertising and subscriptions. The press had often been critical of the government in the 1920s, but after the Manchurian Incident of 1931 most major papers adopted a nationalistic line, in part because war coverage sold copies and in part because of pressure from the army and from right-wing groups. This history is important: it means that the wartime press was not simply a victim of control but a participant whose commercial interests and editorial choices contributed to the climate of support for the war.

From 1937 the state intervened more directly. Official communiqués from the army, navy, and Imperial General Headquarters became the principal source for news about military operations, and journalists attached to the forces were subject to military censorship. Press clubs attached to ministries gave officials a means of managing reporters. In December 1941 a Newspaper Business Ordinance authorized the government to consolidate newspapers, and under the policy of "one prefecture, one paper" the number of daily newspapers fell from roughly 1,200 in 1937 to approximately 55 in 1943, according to a historical study of newspaper consolidation; these are counts of daily newspapers and should not be confused with the much larger number of weekly and occasional publications. Consolidation was justified by the need to conserve paper and manpower, and it also gave the government fewer outlets to supervise and made it easier to appoint sympathetic managers. A national newspaper association under official guidance coordinated policy. Magazines and books were subject to parallel controls: publishers were merged into large associations, paper allocations were controlled, and publishing houses that displeased the authorities could find their paper supplies cut. In July 1944 the magazines Chūō Kōron and Kaizō, two of the most prominent general-interest journals, were ordered to cease publication.

Radio was the most direct instrument for reaching the population quickly. The Japan Broadcasting Corporation (NHK), created in 1926 from earlier local stations and operating as a single national system under government supervision, had expanded its audience rapidly through the 1930s; by the early 1940s licensed receivers numbered in the millions, roughly six million or more by around 1942, though not every household had one. NHK broadcast official news, speeches by political and military leaders, and programs of patriotic music, and radio calisthenics, broadcast daily since 1928, had become a routine in schools and neighborhoods. On December 8, 1941, the announcement of the attack on Pearl Harbor and the reading of the declaration-of-war rescript reached the public by radio. Listening to foreign broadcasts, particularly after the outbreak of the Pacific War, was prohibited and could be punished, although some people did listen secretly. Broadcasting in the colonies and occupied territories was conducted in local languages as well as Japanese, and it served as an instrument of control as well as information.

How should historians understand the interaction of commercial interest, patriotism, professional cooperation, and coercion? The evidence suggests that all operated together. Some editors and broadcasters were convinced supporters; others calculated that cooperation would protect their organizations; others complied under direct threat. A small number of journalists resisted or tried to evade restrictions, and some, such as Kiyosawa Kiyoshi, whose private wartime diary records sharp criticism of the war, wrote carefully within the limits of what could be published while recording their own views in private. The result was a press that was not uniformly propagandistic in tone but that operated within narrow boundaries and shared the official framing of most events.

The effect on readers and listeners was correspondingly mixed. Official reports undoubtedly shaped what many people knew, especially in the early years of victories. But people also used other sources: letters from soldiers at the front, conversations with returning veterans, rumors, and their own observations of shortages and casualties. Police reports on rumors, which recorded stories about military setbacks and official corruption, show that unofficial information circulated widely and that officials treated its spread as a threat. The state's control of formal channels was therefore substantial but not total, and the gap between official reporting and lived experience grew as the war continued.

Section 77.11 · Division III

Film, Newsreels, Photography, and the Visual Representation of War

By the 1930s cinema was a major form of mass entertainment, with a large domestic industry, a network of theaters in cities and towns, and an audience used to both Japanese and foreign films (Chapter 70). It was also a medium the state saw as both a risk and an opportunity. The Film Law (Eiga hō) of 1939, in force from October of that year, was modeled partly on German precedents and gave the government authority over the film industry. It required producers and directors to be licensed, subjected scripts to review before production, mandated the screening of approved "cultural films" and newsreels, and gave officials power to prohibit films that were considered harmful. The law did not abolish commercial filmmaking; it redirected it, and film companies, which depended on the government for raw film stock and permits, adapted accordingly.

Consolidation followed. The newsreel companies were merged into a single organization, Nippon News, from 1940, which produced standard newsreels shown before feature films. In 1942 the feature-film industry was reorganized into three companies, Shōchiku, Tōhō, and Daiei, the last formed by merging several smaller firms. Resource allocation, including film stock, was directed by the government, and the pressure to produce films aligned with national policy grew steadily. Cultural films, usually documentaries on topics such as agriculture, science, or the military, were shown in programs of compulsory length, and audiences were exposed to patriotic subjects whether or not they sought them.

Films about the war took several forms. Some, such as Mud and Soldiers (Tsuchi to heitai, 1939), directed by Tasaka Tomotaka, presented the experience of ordinary infantry in China with a realism that moved audiences and impressed critics, though it also reinforced the image of the Japanese soldier as disciplined and enduring. After the attack on Pearl Harbor, films such as The War at Sea from Hawaii to Malaya (1942) used elaborate special effects, developed by Tsuburaya Eiji, to dramatize the early naval victories; they were popular and technically impressive, and they presented the war as a triumphant progress. Other films treated the home front, emphasizing the heroism of ordinary workers, the sacrifices of mothers, or the training of youth. Animation also served the state: Momotaro's Sea Eagles (1943) and Momotaro: Sacred Sailors (1945), the latter funded by the navy, presented the war in the idiom of a children's folktale.

Not every wartime film was straightforward propaganda. Directors and writers worked within constraints, but some managed to make films with ambiguous or humane emphases, and censors sometimes objected to films they considered insufficiently martial or too sentimental. Kurosawa Akira's first film, Sugata Sanshirō (1943), a martial-arts story, and his The Most Beautiful (1944), about women workers in a factory making optical equipment, were produced under wartime conditions and have been read both as accommodations to official demands and as films with independent artistic interests. Scholars debate how much space directors had; the evidence suggests that the range was narrow but not zero, and that the meaning of films depended in part on how audiences interpreted them.

Photography and visual media served the same ends. Photographic magazines and the government weekly Shashin Shūhō published images of soldiers, mothers, and children, with captions that guided their interpretation. Official photographers, some of them distinguished professionals, were attached to the military and produced images that emphasized discipline, victory, and friendly relations with local populations in occupied territories. Photographs of the emperor, who appeared in public on horseback in military uniform, were treated with veneration, and the display of portraits was governed by rules. The visual representation of war was thus selective: it showed sacrifice and triumph but not the scale of destruction inflicted on others, and later in the war it increasingly avoided showing Japanese losses. Historians using such sources must account for what was left out as well as what was shown.

Section 77.12 · Division III

Literature, Theater, Music, and the Cultural Mobilization of Society

Writers, artists, and performers were pressed to serve national policy through a mixture of incentives and threats. The Home Ministry's censors could ban books; publishers needed paper that the government allocated; and writers who criticized the war risked losing their livelihood. In March 1938 the novelist Ishikawa Tatsuzō was prosecuted after the magazine Chūō Kōron published his Living Soldiers, a fictionalized account of Japanese troops in the campaign for Nanjing that dwelt on atrocities and the psychology of soldiers; the issue was banned and Ishikawa received a suspended sentence. The episode warned others about the limits of what could be written about the war, and the most popular war literature of the period took a different line. Hino Ashihei's Wheat and Soldiers (Mugi to heitai, 1938), based on his experience as a soldier in China, was a bestseller that presented the soldiers' endurance and camaraderie with restraint and avoided the issues that Ishikawa had raised.

The state also recruited writers directly. In 1938 a group of prominent writers was sent to the front in China as an official "pen corps," and their accounts were published as patriotic reportage. In May 1942 the Japan Literature Patriotic Association (Nippon Bungaku Hōkokukai) was formed as a consolidated body of writers under the guidance of the Information Bureau, and a Greater East Asia Writers' Congress was held later that year, bringing together writers from Japan and Japanese-controlled areas. Membership brought benefits, such as access to paper and publication, and exclusion brought risk. After the war, writers and critics disputed how far wartime cooperation had been voluntary, and some prominent figures were criticized for their wartime writings. Others turned to historical fiction, classical themes, or private forms such as the diary and the poem, and some of the most enduring wartime writing, such as Nagai Kafū's private diary and certain novels written but not published during the war, was produced outside the official system.

The performing arts experienced similar pressures. Leftist and modernist theater was suppressed: in August 1940 the major shingeki companies, including the New Cooperative Theater and the New Tsukiji Theater, were disbanded and many members arrested. Kabuki and Noh continued, drawing on historical and heroic themes that could be presented as consistent with national values, and new patriotic plays were staged. Popular entertainment was subject to scrutiny: in October 1941 the storytellers' association symbolically buried scripts of fifty-three rakugo stories judged inappropriate for wartime in a "grave" at a temple in Asakusa, a gesture of self-censorship that illustrates how performers anticipated official expectations. Dance halls were closed in Tokyo and other cities in October 1940, and by 1943 the use of English terms was discouraged in public life, including, in a well-known example, in baseball.

Music was a more direct instrument of mobilization. The government and NHK promoted patriotic songs, and public competitions solicited new works. "Umi yukaba," a 1937 setting of a poem from the eighth-century Man'yōshū that evoked loyalty unto death, became one of the most widely performed pieces and was often played at announcements of military defeats later in the war. Marching songs, school songs, and songs for soldiers and their families were circulated through radio, records, and school instruction. Western popular music, especially jazz, was discouraged and eventually banned as enemy music, although musicians continued to perform and listeners found ways to hear it. Composers and performers adapted in varied ways: some produced works for official purposes, some limited themselves to classical repertory, and some, including a number of jazz musicians, found other outlets.

The cultural landscape of wartime Japan thus included mobilization, accommodation, and continuing private life. Mass entertainment continued, including popular fiction, sports, and amusements, and the state sometimes tolerated or even encouraged leisure as a support for morale. New cultural organizations, such as associations of cartoonists, painters, and musicians, were formed under official guidance, and artists who joined could obtain materials and exhibitions. The existence of private cultural life, however limited, matters for how historians read the period: it cautions against assuming that propaganda occupied the whole of people's mental world, and it reminds students that some of the most important evidence of what people thought was recorded in private, not public, forms.

Section 77.13 · Division III

Thought Control, Political Surveillance, and the Limits of Dissent

The Special Higher Police (Tokubetsu Kōtō Keisatsu, abbreviated Tokkō) had been created in 1911 after the High Treason Incident and had expanded through the 1920s and 1930s, with units in Tokyo, Osaka, and other major cities and officers placed in every prefectural police headquarters (Chapter 69). Their targets were communists, anarchists, and others considered subversive, and they developed extensive networks of informants, files, and methods of interrogation, which in many cases included beatings and torture. The writer Kobayashi Takiji, author of the proletarian novel The Crab Cannery Ship, died in police custody in 1933 after a Tokkō interrogation. The Military Police (Kenpeitai) conducted parallel surveillance, especially of military personnel and in occupied areas, and the two agencies sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed.

The principal legal instrument was the Peace Preservation Law, enacted in 1925 and amended in 1928 to include the death penalty for leaders of organizations aiming to alter the national polity (Chapters 69 and 74). In March 1941 the law was thoroughly revised: its provisions were extended to cover preparatory acts and groups whose purposes were judged to be contrary to the national polity, including "pseudo-religious" organizations, and it introduced preventive detention, allowing officials to hold individuals considered likely to reoffend even after they had served sentences. Research on enforcement from the law’s enactment in 1925 to its repeal in October 1945 estimates more than 70,000 arrests in metropolitan Japan under the Peace Preservation Law, with tens of thousands more in the colonial territories, especially Korea. Arrests, prosecutions, convictions, and the number of individuals affected are different measures, and published totals vary with coverage and definition. The numbers show the scale of repression without capturing its effects on the much larger number of people who avoided risk.

Official policy toward arrested dissidents emphasized "conversion" (tenkō): persuading prisoners to renounce their beliefs and declare loyalty to the national polity. The practice became prominent after 1933, when the imprisoned communist leaders Sano Manabu and Nabeyama Sadachika publicly announced their conversion and were followed by large numbers of others. A system of probation introduced in 1936 supervised those released. Conversion could take many forms, from sincere intellectual change to tactical declaration to collapse under pressure, and the writers and thinkers who passed through it produced a significant literature about the experience. For the state, conversion was valuable because it demonstrated that dissent could be reversed and because it discouraged organized opposition.

Repression was selective and often arbitrary. Intellectuals associated with Marxism, liberal scholars, religious dissenters, and labor organizers were targeted, and publishing houses and journals could be pressured to dismiss staff. The "Yokohama Incident" of 1942–1945, in which more than sixty editors and writers associated with leading journals were arrested on charges of conspiring to reconstitute the Communist Party, produced several deaths in custody and was later recognized as based on fabricated charges. Politicians were also affected: the Diet member Saitō Takao, who delivered a speech in February 1940 criticizing the government's handling of the war in China, was expelled from the Diet, and in 1943 the politician Nakano Seigō, who had opposed Tōjō, was arrested and died by suicide after release. Such cases, which received attention in the press only in sanitized form, taught the educated public about the risks of opposition.

Yet the surviving evidence also shows widespread skepticism, private criticism, and selective accommodation. Police reports recorded rumor, graffiti, jokes about officials, and complaints about shortages. Diaries by writers and journalists, such as those of Kiyosawa Kiyoshi and Nagai Kafū, record sharp private views of the war and the government. Wartime letters from soldiers and civilians contain expressions of doubt, fear, and fatigue. Prosecutions for "unpatriotic speech" and for spreading rumors, which multiplied as the war went badly, themselves show that expressions of discontent were common enough to concern the authorities. Historians therefore warn against reading silence in the public record as enthusiastic agreement: people had many reasons not to speak, and the sources that survive were generated under conditions that discouraged candor.

Section 77.14 · Division IV — Total Mobilization, Political Organizations, and Wartime Social Discipline

The National Mobilization Law of 1938 and the Legal Foundations of Total War

Japanese planners had studied the First World War's lessons about industrial war since the 1910s, and army officers argued through the 1920s and 1930s that a future conflict would require state direction of the whole economy. The Cabinet Planning Board (Kikakuin), created in October 1937 by merging earlier research and resource agencies, was the staff body for this effort. It drafted the Materials Mobilization Plan, begun in 1938, which allocated scarce imported materials among military and civilian uses, and it helped draft the legal framework that would permit such allocation. That framework was the National Mobilization Law (Kokka sōdōin hō), submitted to the Diet in early 1938 and promulgated on April 1, 1938, with effect from May.

The law was a framework statute. It defined national general mobilization as the organization of all human and material resources of the nation in wartime so as to make the most effective use of the state's total strength for national defense, and it authorized the government to carry out this mobilization by imperial ordinance rather than by further legislation. Its articles gave the government power to requisition the labor of subjects, to control wages and working conditions, to regulate the production, distribution, and consumption of materials, to direct finance and investment, to oblige firms to cooperate, and to impose limits on newspapers and other publications. Because the details were left to ordinances issued by the cabinet, the law amounted to a broad delegation of lawmaking authority from the Diet to the executive, and its use expanded as the war continued. The law was extended, with variations, to Korea, Taiwan, and Karafuto.

The Diet's debate on the bill is revealing. Some members raised constitutional objections, arguing that the delegation infringed on the Diet's legislative role. In the committee hearings in March 1938, the army's representative, Lieutenant Colonel Satō Kenryō, shouted "Shut up!" at a member who interrupted his explanation, an incident that caused controversy but did not stop the bill. The socialist politician Nishio Suehiro, formerly a leader of the labor movement examined in Chapter 69, was expelled from the Diet after a speech that appeared to compare Konoe's policies with those of European dictators. The government gave assurances that the law would be applied with restraint and that the most sweeping powers would be used only if necessary, and the bill passed with large majorities. The episode shows both the continuing capacity of the Diet to voice objection and its readiness to yield.

The law's implementation proceeded gradually. Among the first major ordinances were the National Requisition Ordinance of 1939, which established a system of registration for skilled workers and permitted their assignment to designated industries; wage-control ordinances, and a general price freeze of September 1939. Later ordinances controlled the rationing of key goods, the consolidation of businesses, and the allocation of capital. The 1941 Important Industries Control Association Ordinance created control associations for major industries, in which industry leaders worked under government supervision to allocate materials and production. Each of these steps was justified by the law, but each also required administrative capacity, and the gap between legal authority and effective control became a continuing problem.

For historians, the law is significant for what it did and did not do. It gave the executive the authority to restructure the economy and to regulate daily life without returning to the Diet, and it contributed to the shift of power toward ministries and the military. It did not abolish the Diet or the constitution, and it did not create a unified command structure; implementation depended on competing agencies. Nor did it automatically produce effective control: ordinances were often issued piecemeal, enforcement was uneven, and private interests found ways to resist or adapt. Distinguishing the legal authorization of controls from their actual effectiveness is essential to understanding wartime Japan.

Section 77.15 · Division IV

Industry, Labor, Production, and the Wartime Command Economy

The Japanese economy of the late 1930s was a mixture of large conglomerates, medium and small firms, and a large agricultural sector. The great zaibatsu houses (Chapter 56) had expanded into heavy industry and munitions, and the government's priority was to direct their capacity and that of newer firms toward military production. Industrial policy combined planning agencies, control associations, and cooperation with business leaders. A group of "reform bureaucrats" and military officers, including Kishi Nobusuke, who had worked in Manchukuo (Chapter 75), advocated a planned economy in which public interest would take precedence over private profit. Business leaders resisted proposals that would have reduced shareholder rights or transferred management to the state, and the "New Economic Order" of 1940–1941 produced compromise: firms remained privately owned, but their activities were increasingly directed by control associations and ministry guidance.

Labor was reorganized more drastically. The independent unions that had survived the 1930s, including the Sōdōmei examined in Chapter 69, dissolved themselves in July 1940 as the political parties dissolved, and workplace associations modeled on the idea of cooperation between labor and management took their place. The Industrial Patriotic Association (Sangyō Hōkokukai), established nationally in November 1940, organized workers by factory, with managers and employees in the same body, under the slogan of service to the nation through industry. It had no independent bargaining function. Wages were controlled by ordinance, workers were restricted in changing jobs, and labor registration allowed the state to direct people into war industries. The number of factory workers grew substantially, and employment in heavy industry rose faster than in consumer goods, which were curtailed.

The wartime labor force was assembled by drawing on several sources. Men of working age were increasingly conscripted into the military, and their places were taken by women, students, middle-aged men transferred from civilian trades, and, from the early 1940s, large numbers of Korean and Chinese workers, brought by recruitment, pressure, or force (Chapter 79). Small businesses were consolidated or closed so that their workers could be redirected to munitions production. Farm families, which supplied both soldiers and workers, were stretched, and agricultural labor shortages grew. The government also introduced national labor service: an ordinance of November 1941 required men and unmarried women in specified age groups to perform up to thirty days of labor service a year, and later measures made it more demanding.

As the war turned against Japan, the military's role in economic decisions grew. After the early victories of 1941–1942 had secured access to resources of Southeast Asia, shipping losses from Allied submarine and air attack in 1943 made it increasingly difficult to bring materials to Japan (Chapter 78). In response, the Tōjō government reorganized industrial administration. In November 1943, the Ministry of Munitions (Gunjushō) was created, absorbing the Planning Board and parts of the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, with Tōjō himself as minister and with authority to designate key firms as "munitions companies" whose managers were made responsible to the state for production. The goal was to concentrate authority over aircraft production, which had become a priority, and to override bureaucratic divisions. In practice, rivalry between the army and navy over allocation of materials, aircraft, and engineers continued, and the system never achieved unified direction.

The wartime economy thus combined real expansion with structural strain. Production of military goods grew rapidly, and in some categories, notably aircraft, output continued to rise until 1944. At the same time, the disruption of shipping, the diversion of labor, the exhaustion of stockpiles, and the weakening of civilian industries produced shortages, declining quality, and bottlenecks. Controls reduced the freedom of workers and firms but could not conjure materials that did not exist. The material consequences for households, including rationing, shortages of food and fuel, and the effects of bombing, are the subject of Chapter 80; the point here is that administrative controls interacted with industrial capacity and international circumstances, and that the system's effectiveness depended on resources the state could not command.

Section 77.16 · Division IV

The Imperial Rule Assistance Association and the Reorganization of Political Participation

The idea of a "new political order" (shintaisei) gathered force in 1939 and 1940 among military officers, bureaucrats, intellectuals, and politicians who regarded the party system as divisive and unable to meet the demands of total war. Konoe Fumimaro, who had resigned in 1939 and returned as prime minister in July 1940, was widely seen as the figure who could lead such a transformation. Some of its advocates, especially on the left of the political spectrum, imagined a mass movement that would place workers and farmers at the center of national life; others, including many conservative politicians and officials, hoped for a unifying national organization that would end partisan competition. Conservative critics, led by figures such as Hiranuma Kiichirō, warned that a single political organization would contradict the constitutional principle of direct imperial rule by implying a power between the sovereign and the people.

The parties dissolved themselves in the summer of 1940, in anticipation of the new structure: the Social Mass Party in July, the main branch of the Seiyūkai in late July, and the Minseitō in August. On October 12, 1940, the Imperial Rule Assistance Association (Taisei Yokusankai) was inaugurated, with Konoe as its president. The movement's name expressed an intention to assist the emperor's rule rather than to wield power independently. Its founding declaration spoke in general terms of national unity and service, and its organization drew on local governments, with prefectural governors serving as heads of branches, and on existing associations. In its early months the movement was criticized from several directions. Right-wing critics charged that it resembled a communist or totalitarian party; business interests feared radical economic planning; and officials in the Home Ministry worried that it would rival their authority. In response, in early 1941 the government redefined the association as a public-service body for assisting national policy, not a political organization, and removed or constrained the reformist elements. The association's character thus changed from an aspirational political movement to a vehicle for administrative mobilization.

It operated through a hierarchy that extended from the central headquarters to prefectures, cities, and the neighborhood associations examined in Section 77.17, and it incorporated or coordinated a range of affiliated bodies, including the Industrial Patriotic Association, the Great Japan Youth Corps (formed in January 1941), and later the women's association. Its tasks included spreading official campaigns, organizing local ceremonies, collecting funds and materials, and encouraging participation in savings drives and civil defense. It provided a structure through which national messages could reach communities, and through which communities could be mobilized, but it did not set policy and did not control the cabinet.

The 1942 general election, held on April 30, illustrated both the reach and the limits of the system. The House of Representatives' term, which would have expired in 1941, had been extended by a year by special law. A council organized under the association's auspices recommended a slate of candidates, and the government and local officials supported them, while candidates not on the slate faced obstacles including police interference, restrictions on campaign materials, and limited access to funds. The recommended candidates won roughly four-fifths of the 466 seats, a decisive majority. Yet more than eighty non-recommended candidates were elected, among them veteran politicians such as Ozaki Yukio and Hatoyama Ichirō, and voter turnout was high, about eighty percent by standard accounts. After the election the winners were organized in a Diet association, the Imperial Rule Assistance Political Association, which gave the government a reliable majority. The election thus combined official management with limited electoral competition.

It is misleading to equate this system with the structure of Nazi Germany or Fascist Italy. Japan did not develop a single ruling party with its own leader, ideology, and paramilitary organization: the association was a state-sponsored umbrella with no independent authority, and power remained dispersed among the army, navy, ministries, the court, and the Diet. Tōjō, who became prime minister in October 1941, concentrated several offices, serving at times as army minister, home minister, and, from February 1944, chief of the army general staff, but he governed within the constitutional structure and was forced from office in July 1944 by a coalition of senior statesmen, court figures, and military officers. Historians have debated whether "fascism" is an appropriate label for wartime Japan, with some stressing differences in mass movement and leadership, and others emphasizing shared features such as militarism, ideological mobilization, and suppression of dissent. The more important point for this chapter is that the system combined authoritarian control with the survival of constitutional institutions, and that its limits were produced by that survival.

Section 77.17 · Division IV

Neighborhood Associations, Tonarigumi, and the Administration of Everyday Mobilization

The lowest level of the mobilization system was the neighborhood. Japanese villages and urban districts had long possessed forms of local association, from village councils to groups of households that shared responsibility for tasks such as fire watching and tax collection, and earlier governments had periodically drawn on them, including the five-household groups of the Tokugawa period (Chapter 44). In the 1930s such associations, known variously as chōnaikai in towns and burakukai in villages, were widespread but varied in form and authority. In September 1940 the Home Ministry issued guidelines directing the standardization and strengthening of these groups and the creation, within them, of "neighborhood groups" (tonarigumi) of about five to ten households. In 1943 a revision of the local-government system gave the neighborhood associations formal legal recognition as the base of the administrative hierarchy.

Their functions multiplied as the war continued. They distributed official notices, circulated bulletins, and relayed government campaigns; they collected savings and war bonds; they organized collection drives for metal, which in 1941 included the requisition of temple bells, railings, and household metal goods; they distributed rationed goods, beginning with matches and sugar in 1940 and including rice, which was rationed through a passbook system in major cities from April 1941; they took part in civil-defense preparations under the Air Defense Law, organizing fire-fighting drills, blackout enforcement, and bucket-relay practice; and they helped register residents and track those who moved. They also arranged send-offs for departing soldiers, helped families of the war dead and wounded, and organized collective labor. The preparations for air defense, rather than the experience of raids themselves, are the subject here; what happened when cities were bombed belongs to Chapter 80.

The associations performed a double function. On one hand, they provided mutual assistance and the practical coordination that wartime conditions required: neighbors helped one another with childcare, shared scarce goods, and supported families whose members were away. Such cooperation was often genuine and was remembered by many people with warmth. On the other hand, the associations were instruments of surveillance and conformity. Because every household belonged and officers knew their neighbors' habits, the group could identify people who failed to take part in drills, lacked a flag on national holidays, grumbled about shortages, or kept foreign contacts. Reports on such people could reach the police, and social pressure operated even where no formal sanction was applied. Historians have emphasized that solidarity and surveillance operated through the same organizations and could not always be distinguished by those involved.

Women played central roles, because neighborhood groups were typically represented in daily affairs by housewives. A woman who attended drills, collected scrap, and distributed ration tickets was performing duties on behalf of her household that had a public character. This deepened the state's reach into domestic life while also giving some women informal authority within local communities. The associations also reflected local hierarchies: leaders were often established male household heads, landlords, or shopkeepers, and local elites could use the system to reinforce their position.

The associations' effectiveness varied. In well-organized urban neighborhoods and cooperative villages, they could distribute goods efficiently and carry out drills with discipline. In others they were poorly led, riven by personal quarrels, or ignored. In rural areas the association was often continuous with existing village institutions, and its wartime role added to long-standing obligations. In cities, where populations were more mobile, the tonarigumi was a newer imposition. As shortages grew, the associations' role in distributing scarce goods made them both essential and resented, and accusations of favoritism or hoarding were common.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Mobilization, Censorship, and Local Organization

Mobilization (dōin) in this period meant the organization of people, resources, and institutions for war. The state spoke of "spiritual" mobilization (belief and morale), "material" mobilization (goods and industry), and "total" mobilization (all of them together). Censorship (ken'etsu) covered both prior review of publications and films and punishment after publication; it was reinforced by "guidance," informal instructions that encouraged self-censorship. Tonarigumi were neighborhood groups of about five to ten households, and chōnaikai and burakukai were the town and village associations above them; together they constituted the local base of wartime administration.

These terms matter because they cover practices that could be voluntary or compulsory, supportive or coercive. Modern English words such as "volunteer," "club," or "censor" can mislead: a neighborhood association was not optional, "voluntary" contributions were often expected, and censorship worked through an expectation of self-restraint as much as through prohibitions.

Everyday Life and Environment

A group of eight households on a city lane might meet monthly in the home of its group leader, usually an established shopkeeper or older man. The meeting would read out notices from the ward office: new ration arrangements, instructions for air-defense drills, requests for donations of metal or cloth, and announcements of ceremonies. In the morning, residents might gather for group calisthenics broadcast on the radio; on the first of the month, they would raise the flag and perhaps visit the local shrine.

Such a group could be a source of real help. A family whose breadwinner had been drafted might receive assistance with household tasks; neighbors might share vegetables or care for children. It could also be a source of pressure. A household that skipped the drill, kept a foreign radio, or complained openly about the war might be noticed, and the group leader was expected to report unsuitable behavior. The same women who organized mutual aid might also be responsible for monitoring conformity. Memories of such groups vary: some people recall warmth and cooperation, others recall intrusion and fear. The evidence supports both.

Section 77.18 · Division IV

Women's Associations, Gender Ideology, and National Service

Women had been drawn into patriotic organization since the early twentieth century. The Patriotic Women's Association (Aikoku Fujinkai), founded in 1901, supported soldiers and their families; the Great Japan National Defense Women's Association (Kokubō Fujinkai), founded in Osaka in 1932, adopted a distinctive white apron and sash as a uniform and recruited members among housewives; and a federation of women's groups coordinated other organizations. These bodies were large but divided, and the state came to regard their rivalry as inefficient. In February 1942 the three principal organizations were consolidated into a single Great Japan Women's Association (Dai Nippon Fujinkai), placed under the guidance of the Ministry of Health and Welfare and integrated into the Imperial Rule Assistance structure. Membership was extended to all adult women under a principle of automatic enrollment, and its membership reached approximately nineteen million within about a year of the February 1942 consolidation, as recorded in the National Diet Library’s institutional history. Enrollment totals demonstrate the scale of organization, not the extent of voluntary ideological commitment.

The association's activities reflected the state's expectations of women. Members sewed and collected comfort bags and clothing, organized farewell parties for departing soldiers, provided assistance to families of the war dead and wounded, ran savings campaigns, took part in civil-defense drills, and instructed households in thrift and conservation. The ideological message emphasized the roles of mother and household manager: women were to raise sons willing to die for the emperor, to sustain the family under hardship, and to subordinate their personal wishes to national need. This ideology was reinforced by population policy. A January 1941 policy statement set a goal of a national population of one hundred million by 1960, urged earlier marriage, and encouraged large families, and a National Eugenic Law of 1940 sought to limit the births of people judged "unfit." Slogans such as "bear children and multiply" encapsulated the pronatalist aims. The ideal of "military mothers" and of mothers of the war dead who bore loss with pride was celebrated in public ceremony and propaganda.

Yet women's actual lives diverged from the prescribed ideal. Rural women, who already carried much of the burden of farm labor, took over additional work as men were drafted. Urban women entered factories, offices, and transport work in increasing numbers as men left, and unmarried women were subject to labor service requirements. The state was reluctant to conscript married women into industry, partly because of its own household ideology, and Japan relied less on formal female labor conscription than some other belligerents, but pressure rose as labor shortages deepened: from 1944, a Women's Volunteer Labor Corps (Joshi teishintai) organized unmarried women, including schoolgirls, for factory work. Women also sustained households under rationing, queuing for goods and managing scarce supplies, and many experienced the war primarily through the work of keeping their families alive.

Women's responses to mobilization varied. Some embraced the opportunity for public roles and recognition; for women who had campaigned for suffrage and social reform in the 1920s (Chapter 69), wartime organizations offered a platform and a way to press for practical improvements in welfare and daily life, and some prominent suffrage leaders, including Ichikawa Fusae, took part in wartime advisory bodies, for which they were later criticized and, under Occupation purge rules, temporarily barred from public office. Others participated reluctantly or with private criticism, and some wrote of grief and anger at the loss of husbands and sons. Because women could not vote or hold office, their influence on policy was limited, and their presence in organizations was often that of implementers rather than decision makers.

The evidence available to historians is uneven. The records of the associations preserve their programs and slogans, and government surveys record labor patterns, but women's own accounts come mainly from diaries, letters, and memoirs, many written or collected after the war and shaped by later reflection. Historians accordingly distinguish the state's prescribed ideal of femininity from what women actually did and believed, and they stress that women were both objects of mobilization and agents who interpreted and sometimes reshaped its demands.

Section 77.19 · Division IV

Youth, Student Labor, and the Intensification of Mobilization, 1943–1945

Until 1943, most Japanese students, particularly those in universities and higher schools, retained a deferment from conscription until graduation. As the war's demands grew, especially after the heavy losses of 1942–1943, the government moved to draw students into service. In June 1943 a cabinet decision on student wartime mobilization called for the organization of students for labor and for strengthening their military training. In October 1943 an imperial ordinance ended the conscription deferment for students in the humanities, social sciences, and related fields, while students in science and engineering, deemed essential to war production, kept their deferments. The first group of departing students was honored at the ceremony in Tokyo on October 21, 1943, described at the opening of this chapter, and similar ceremonies took place in other cities.

The consequences for students were immediate. A large number of young men, many of them from the educated elite, left school for the army and navy, and a program of special training for student officers drew many into the services. The experiences of these "student soldiers" are among the best-documented of the war, in part because many were writers and diarists, and their letters and memoirs, including those collected after the war, record a range of responses: patriotic resolve, resignation, anger, and anguish. Some thought of their sacrifice as meaningful, some as a tragedy imposed by others, and many experienced both at once. Conscription fell unevenly: students who stayed in school, women, and younger children experienced different pressures.

The mobilization of students for labor expanded in parallel. A cabinet decision of January 1944 ordered a broad extension of work service, and an August 1944 Student Labor Order gave the system a legal basis. Middle-school, girls' school, and college students were organized into labor units assigned to factories, mines, farms, and construction sites, often for most of the school year. By February 1945 the number mobilized for labor was on the order of three million; one scholarly reconstruction reports approximately 3.11 million students engaged in industry by March 1945. Such counts require attention to reporting date, school level, and whether agricultural work and military conscription were included. Girls were organized in the Women's Volunteer Labor Corps. Students did work in munitions factories, produced aircraft parts, harvested crops, and dug defensive works, and their labor helped sustain aircraft output in 1944. School buildings were used for storage or as factories, and teachers accompanied their students or were themselves reassigned.

Education itself was progressively subordinated. In March 1945, a government decision on emergency measures for decisive-battle education suspended classes for a year in almost all schools above the primary level, except in the first six years of national schools, and organized students into corps for labor and defense. In May 1945 a Wartime Education Order formalized the arrangement by organizing students into "student corps" that could be mobilized for local defense as well as work. In practice, the education of most older students ceased by 1945, and younger children were evacuated from major cities, a process whose human consequences belong to Chapter 80.

The mobilization of youth reveals the system's strengths and its strains. It demonstrated the state's capacity to redirect millions of young people quickly through the school system and to use educational institutions as administrative channels. It also demonstrated the costs: the neglect of education, the exposure of young people to hazardous work, and the unequal burdens that fell on students from different fields, classes, and regions. It shaped the generation that would lead postwar Japan and left a legacy of memory, grief, and political commitments, some oriented toward pacifism and others toward preserving the memory of wartime sacrifice. The student send-offs of 1943 presented the mobilization as a ceremony of national unity; the record of the students themselves shows a more varied and more painful reality.

Cause and Effect

How Prolonged Warfare Drove the Expansion of State Controls

Long-term causes

The emperor-centered constitutional order with divided authority; ideological systems built in schools and shrines since the Meiji period; a bureaucracy with experience in social management (Chapter 69); military influence over government after 1931; an economy dependent on imported materials; and interwar planning debates.

Short-term causes

The escalation of war in China in 1937 into a prolonged conflict; resource shortages and trade tensions with the United States and Britain; the entry into the Pacific War in 1941; and, from 1942–1943, mounting losses of ships, aircraft, and territory.

Event or transformation

The delegation of authority by the National Mobilization Law, the consolidation of parties, unions, press, and associations into state-sponsored bodies, and the use of schools, shrines, and neighborhoods as channels of mobilization.

Immediate effects

Expanded control of labor, materials, information, and association; a managed political system; rising participation in organized activities; and increasing pressure on women, students, and workers as the war turned.

Long-term consequences and historical complexity

Controls expanded in response to failure as well as success: each shortage or defeat prompted new measures, which strained the same institutions that were supposed to remedy it. The expansion was not simply the execution of a plan; it resulted from improvisation, institutional rivalry, and crisis. Some of the structures, including administrative guidance and local associations, would reappear in altered forms after 1945, while others were dismantled under the Occupation. The case cannot be reduced to a single cause, such as military ambition, ideology, or economic necessity.
Section 77.20 · Division V — Historical Synthesis and Chapter Review

The Emperor System, Wartime Ideology, and the Mobilized State: Historical Synthesis and Review

The preceding sections have examined wartime Japan from several vantage points: the emperor system and its ideology; the shrines and religious bodies; the schools; the press, film, and culture; the law of total mobilization; the reorganization of politics; and the associations that reached into neighborhoods, households, and classrooms. This synthesis addresses three questions. How did preexisting institutions facilitate wartime mobilization? What combination of persuasion, participation, and coercion sustained it? And what limitations emerged as military demands became increasingly difficult to fulfill?

On the first question, the answer is that the wartime state built mainly on what it already had. The emperor system supplied a source of legitimacy that allowed policies to be presented as the sovereign's will, even though authority was dispersed among competing institutions. The Imperial Rescript on Education and the shrine hierarchy provided ceremonial and moral forms that reached nearly every community; the school system reached nearly every child; the Home Ministry's police and local administration reached every locality; and the commercial press, radio, and film industry offered channels of communication that the state could direct. Neighborhood associations had antecedents centuries old. The wartime state intensified these inheritances, redefined their purposes, and linked them through new laws and organizations, but it did not create a new society from scratch. For that reason the period has been read both as a rupture and as a continuation of earlier trends, and historians continue to weigh the balance.

On the second question, evidence suggests that persuasion, participation, and coercion operated together and that the mixture changed over time. Persuasion worked through ideology, ritual, schooling, and media, and it was probably most effective when it connected with existing beliefs and interests: pride in national achievement, concern for family members at the front, community solidarity, or occupational incentives. Participation was encouraged by the incentives of membership, such as access to rations, paper, or employment, and by the cost of nonparticipation. Coercion, in the form of police surveillance, legal penalties, preventive detention, and the threat of social ostracism, defined the limits of dissent and made some forms of expression dangerous. Because these elements overlapped, membership in an organization or attendance at a ceremony is weak evidence of belief. The evidence of diaries, letters, memoirs, and police reports reveals people who supported the war, people who doubted it, and many who did both depending on circumstances and time.

On the third question, the system's limits were evident from the start and grew with the war's course. Divided authority meant that controls were applied unevenly and that competing agencies, above all the army and the navy, were never fully coordinated. Legal authority could not create resources that did not exist, and after 1943 shipping losses, industrial strain, and manpower shortages undermined the economic basis of mobilization. Propaganda faced the problem of credibility: official reports increasingly diverged from what people saw and heard, and surveillance records show growing concern about rumors, war weariness, and declining morale. The reliance on students, women, and colonial labor revealed the exhaustion of the ordinary labor supply. These limits did not produce open revolt; the structures of control, the habit of obedience, and the absence of organized alternatives constrained expression of discontent. But they help explain why the system could neither win the war nor, until the end, take the decision to end it.

Historical Debate

What Most Effectively Sustained Wartime Mobilization: Conviction, Institutional Cooperation, Social Pressure, or Coercion?

The question: Historians disagree about how to explain the high level of organized participation in wartime Japan. Was mobilization sustained primarily by belief in the national cause, by the institutional interests of organizations that cooperated with the state, by pressure within communities, or by the threat of force?

Interpretation A — A state-directed, administered system: Scholars such as Gregory Kasza have emphasized the role of administrative structures in organizing the population from above, arguing that mass associations were created and run by the state to channel participation and prevent independent mobilization. On this view, institutional design and legal compulsion explain participation more than ideological conviction does.
Interpretation B — Participation grounded in society and in earlier reform: Others, including Sheldon Garon in his work on the state and everyday life, stress the continuity between wartime campaigns and earlier moral-suasion and social-management efforts, in which civic groups and private organizations took an active part. Studies of wartime experience, such as those by Thomas Havens, and collections of oral testimony, emphasize the range of motives and the persistence of private life. On this view, voluntary action, community norms, and the interests of organizations helped sustain mobilization alongside state direction.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on statutes, organizational records, police reports, newspapers, and personal testimony. They differ in the weight they give to top-down design versus social participation, and in how they interpret the same evidence: a high rate of organizational membership may indicate compulsion or consent. Work associated with the "total war and modernization" thesis adds a further perspective, treating wartime institutions as part of long-term administrative and social transformation. Many scholars now regard the explanations as complementary rather than exclusive.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians assess responsibility and continuity: whether wartime mobilization was an imposition on society, an outgrowth of it, or both, and how far its institutions and habits shaped postwar Japan.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political and Social Institutions Before and After the Mobilization Measures of 1937–1943

What Changed

  • Parties and independent unions were dissolved and replaced by state-sponsored associations.
  • The executive gained broad authority by ordinance over labor, materials, and publications.
  • Propaganda and press control were centralized, and newspapers and film companies were consolidated.
  • Primary schools were redefined as national schools, and students and women were drawn into labor and service on a large scale.

  • The emperor's symbolic role, the Imperial Rescript on Education, and the shrine hierarchy

Students, young men subject to conscription, women drawn into labor and associations, religious minorities subject to licensing, and journalists and writers working under guidance. Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan between 1937 and 1945 attempted to organize its society for prolonged war. Its central argument is that mobilization combined emperor-centered ideology, existing religious and educational institutions, state propaganda, legal coercion, economic controls, and local organizations, and that participation reflected patriotism, institutional interest, social pressure, and fear in varying proportions. The system produced an increasingly militarized society, but it never became seamless, and it never fully controlled what people believed.

The emperor system supplied the foundation of legitimacy. The Meiji Constitution made the emperor sacred and sovereign, but it also dispersed practical authority among a cabinet without a unified executive, the army and navy with their independent right of supreme command, the Diet, and a circle of court officials and elder statesmen. Policy could be presented as the sovereign's will without any single institution bearing full responsibility. Historians distinguish the emperor as a constitutional institution, Hirohito as an individual who received reports, asked questions, and sometimes influenced events, and the emperor as an image in propaganda. Neither the image of a dictator nor that of a powerless figurehead fits the evidence.

Official ideology was codified in the Kokutai no Hongi of 1937, which presented the nation as a family-state under an unbroken imperial line, equated loyalty with filial piety, defined sacrifice as fulfillment, and condemned Western individualism and related ideas. The National Spiritual Mobilization movement and its campaigns of ritual, frugality, and send-off ceremonies tried to translate that ideology into practice. The prosecutions of scholars such as Tsuda Sōkichi and the removal of Minobe, Yanaihara, and Kawai showed the limits of permitted discussion, and the evidence of private doubt shows that the ideology's reach was never complete.

State Shinto and education were the principal channels of ritual and indoctrination. The shrine system, headed by Ise and including Yasukuni and the network of gokoku shrines, commemorated the war dead and linked local communities to the throne, while the state classified shrine rites as nonreligious, a claim contested by Christians, Buddhists, and others. The Religious Organizations Law of 1939–1940 brought Buddhist, Christian, and sect and new-religion bodies under licensing and encouraged consolidation, producing cooperation, accommodation, and a small number of prosecutions, including those of the Holiness Church and the Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai. Schools, guided by the Imperial Rescript on Education and reorganized as national schools in 1941, taught loyalty through ceremony, textbooks, and drill, and universities lost autonomy, although teachers and students responded in diverse ways.

The propaganda system tried to organize information and culture as well as to suppress dissent. The Cabinet Information Bureau of 1940 coordinated press, radio, film, and cultural policy, backed by laws on military secrets, publication, and association. Newspapers were consolidated, broadcasting was directed, and the Film Law of 1939 gave the state authority over cinema; writers, theater companies, and musicians were drawn into official organizations. Yet audiences interpreted messages according to their own experience, private cultural life persisted, and police reports and diaries show circulating rumor and criticism. Political surveillance by the Special Higher Police, the revised Peace Preservation Law, and the practice of ideological conversion defined the boundaries of dissent without eliminating it.

The legal foundation of total mobilization was the National Mobilization Law of April 1938, a framework statute that delegated broad authority to the executive. Its implementation produced the wartime command economy: planning agencies, control associations, wage and labor controls, the replacement of independent unions by the Industrial Patriotic Association, and in 1943 the Ministry of Munitions. Output of munitions, especially aircraft, rose until 1944, but the effectiveness of controls was limited by shortages, shipping losses, institutional rivalry, and the reliance on students, women, and colonial laborers.

Political participation was reorganized rather than abolished. The parties dissolved in 1940 and the Imperial Rule Assistance Association was created as a state-sponsored umbrella, but it did not become a single ruling party on the German or Italian model; the Diet, constitution, and bureaucracy survived, and the 1942 election, though managed to favor recommended candidates, returned a number of independents. Neighborhood associations, formalized in 1940 and legally recognized in 1943, became the base of everyday mobilization, serving as channels of mutual aid and of surveillance. The Great Japan Women's Association of 1942 consolidated women's organizations, and women's actual lives, in farms, factories, and households, diverged from the state's ideal of motherhood.

From 1943, the mobilization of youth intensified. The end of most student deferments in October 1943, the student labor measures of 1944, and the suspension of most schooling in March 1945 subordinated education to labor and defense. This intensification showed the system's reach and its strain: it could redirect millions of young people, but only by exhausting the ordinary means of supplying labor and by exposing young people to dangerous work. The chapter's closing argument is that wartime Japan was neither an army imposing orders on a passive nation nor a society united by a single shared belief. It was a complex mobilized state in which political authority, patriotic belief, bureaucratic organization, social participation, and coercion reinforced one another while never eliminating institutional tension or private difference. Chapter 78 turns to the expansion of the war in the Pacific, and Chapter 80 to the material consequences of the war for the people living within this system.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a modern state tried to turn an entire society into an instrument of war without abolishing the constitutional forms it already possessed. The mobilization of 1937–1945 drew on a throne, a school system, a shrine network, a press, local associations, and a bureaucracy that had been built for other purposes, and it combined them through new laws and organizations. Understanding that process requires attention to institutions and to people: to statutes and ordinances, and also to the teachers, priests, editors, workers, women, and students who carried them out, adapted them, or privately doubted them.

The chapter also shows the importance of reading evidence carefully. Official sources, from the Kokutai no Hongi to the picture-stories of the Imperial Rule Assistance Association, reveal what the state wished people to think and do. Diaries, police reports, memoirs, and the records of prosecuted dissenters reveal a more varied society. The gap between the two is itself historical evidence: the size of the apparatus of persuasion and surveillance suggests how much effort was needed to produce unity, and the persistence of private criticism shows that it was never complete. Students who carry this habit of reading into later chapters will be better equipped to handle the contested memory of the war.

The institutions and experiences examined here shaped later history in several ways. The Occupation dismantled the State Shinto system, the Religious Organizations Law, the Peace Preservation Law, the Information Bureau, and the ideology of the national polity, and rewrote the constitutional position of the emperor (Chapters 82–84). Yet administrative habits, local associations, and the memory of mobilization did not vanish, and postwar Japan's debates over Yasukuni Shrine, textbooks, the emperor's responsibility, and pacifism all reach back to the questions discussed here. The generation of students who passed through the mobilization of 1943–1945 would help shape postwar education, journalism, and politics.

Finally, the chapter connects the archipelago to its empire and to the wider war. The shrines, schools, and laws of the metropole were extended to colonies and occupied territories under different conditions, and the demands of mobilization drew on the labor and resources of those societies. Chapter 78 follows the war into the Pacific; Chapter 79 examines the experience of colonial subjects and the violence of the wartime empire; and Chapter 80 turns to what happened to the people of Japan as the system described here confronted bombing, shortage, and defeat. Together these chapters ask how a society organized for war lived, endured, and ultimately collapsed within that organization.
