Part IV — The Rise of the Samurai and Kamakura Japan
23

The Genpei War

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of 1180 a man whose first battle had just ended in defeat boarded a small boat on the Sagami coast and crossed the bay to the Bōsō Peninsula. He had almost no followers, no court office, and no obvious future. Less than five years later, in the currents of the Kanmon Strait, the family that had driven him into exile lost its fleet, most of its leaders, and a child emperor of about six years old. Between those two moments lay a civil war that no one involved had planned.

The conflict of 1180–1185 is most often called the Genpei War, a name assembled from the Sino-Japanese readings of the characters for Minamoto (Gen) and Taira (Hei). The name is convenient and deeply embedded in Japanese culture, but it can mislead. It suggests two unified clans facing one another across a single front. What happened was more tangled. The Taira regime whose formation Chapter 22 traced was challenged by a prince, by aging court warriors, by an exile in Izu, by a highland warrior in Shinano, by Kantō families who were themselves largely of Taira descent, by armed temples, by provincial strongmen in Shikoku and Kyūshū, and by a famine that fell hardest on the regions the Taira depended on. Historians therefore often prefer the more precise label Jishō–Juei War, after two era names associated with the conflict’s central phase, and treat it as a multi-sided civil war.

This chapter asks how a rebellion against Taira dominance became a prolonged war, why the Taira lost, and how later literature transformed a complicated conflict into the remembered “Genpei War.” Its answers stress contingency. In 1180 the Taira controlled the capital, the child emperor Antoku, dozens of provincial governorships, and the Inland Sea. Their fall required a chain of events: failed Taira expeditions, a famine, a rival Minamoto leader who seized Kyoto and then alienated it, a retired emperor who repeatedly changed sides, and a maritime campaign in which local seafarers decided the outcome. Minamoto no Yoritomo emerged as the strongest survivor. That result was not inevitable.

The war was fought over legitimacy, land, and patronage as much as over lineage. Both sides claimed to act in the name of the imperial house. The Taira held the reigning emperor and the regalia, and for a time the court’s authority. The rebels held a prince’s call to arms, the resentment of provincial warriors, and eventually the support of the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa. Eastern warriors chose sides according to their own interests: who would confirm their landholdings, who would protect them from rival neighbors, and who seemed likely to win. This is why the war cannot be described as an ancient clan feud, though later writers often described it that way.

Ordinary people experienced the war very differently from the warrior elite. In the Yōwa famine of 1181–1182, harvest failure, requisitioning, and disrupted transport filled the roads and the Kamo River bed in Kyoto with the starving. The great temples of Nara were damaged by fire during a Taira attack. Boatmen, fishing communities, and coastal lords on the Inland Sea and the Kanmon Strait were drawn into naval campaigns. Court ladies, attendants, and children shared the fate of the Taira household at Dan-no-ura. A chapter that followed only the famous commanders would leave most of this history out.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually treacherous. Court aristocrats such as Kujō Kanezane kept diaries that record news as it reached Kyoto, often late and partial. The Azuma kagami, compiled by the Kamakura regime long after the events, presents the war from a Yoritomo-centered eastern perspective. The Heike monogatari and related war tales (gunki monogatari) developed over generations as oral and written performance and preserve some of the most famous stories in Japanese literature, together with layers of invention. Numbers of troops, heroic speeches, single combats, supernatural omens, and picturesque battlefield episodes must all be tested rather than inherited.

The chapter also stays inside its boundaries. Chapter 22 explained how warriors entered central politics and how Taira no Kiyomori dominated the court; this chapter carries forward only what is needed to understand the crisis of 1180. Chapter 24 will explain how Yoritomo converted military supremacy into durable institutions, including shugo, jitō, vassalage, and the Kamakura bakufu. Here the story ends with military victory and its immediate political consequences. The war answered the question of who possessed the strongest military coalition. It did not yet answer how that victory would become a system of government.

Finally, this chapter is about memory. Within a few generations the war had become a Buddhist parable of impermanence, a repertoire of tragic and heroic episodes, and a source of theater, painting, fiction, and film that continues into the twenty-first century. Students should learn to distinguish the war that can be reconstructed from documents from the war that Japanese culture chose to remember, and to ask why the second proved so much more durable than the first.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why “Genpei War” is an incomplete description of the conflict and why historians also use “Jishō–Juei War.”
  2. Trace the sequence of campaigns from Prince Mochihito’s call in 1180 to Dan-no-ura in 1185.
  3. Analyze how Yoritomo assembled an eastern coalition, and why Kantō warriors made contingent rather than automatic choices.
  4. Assess the effects of the Yōwa famine and wartime requisitioning on campaigning, on the Taira, and on ordinary people.
  5. Compare the roles and aims of Yoritomo, Kiso Yoshinaka, Minamoto no Yukiie, Noriyori, and Yoshitsune, and explain why Minamoto unity did not follow from anti-Taira success.
  6. Interpret the significance of the Taira flight from Kyoto with Emperor Antoku and the regalia, and the enthronement of Go-Toba.
  7. Evaluate the geography of the war, including the Kantō, the Hokuriku passes, the capital region, the Inland Sea, and the Kanmon Strait.
  8. Distinguish documentary evidence from literary tradition in accounts of Fuji River, Kurikara, Ichi-no-Tani, Yashima, and Dan-no-ura.
  9. Explain what the victory settled and what it left unresolved, especially concerning the imperial court and the government of the new military leadership.
  10. Connect the Heike monogatari tradition to Buddhist ideas of impermanence, performance culture, and later Japanese cultural memory.

Historical Context

By 1179 the political order Chapter 22 described had reached a delicate balance. The imperial court still conferred rank, office, and legitimacy, and retired emperors such as Go-Shirakawa still governed through cloistered rule (insei). But military households had entered court politics. After the Hōgen and Heiji disturbances of 1156 and 1159–1160, Taira no Kiyomori had become the dominant figure in Kyoto by combining court office, marriage ties to the imperial line, control of numerous provincial governorships, and a base on the Inland Sea, where his family cultivated ports and trade with Song China. The Minamoto, by contrast, had been broken by defeat in the Heiji conflict. Yoritomo, a boy at the time, was spared and sent into exile in Izu.

Taira power rested on court office and on personal retainers, not on institutions capable of ruling the provinces on their own. That was a strength in the capital and a weakness elsewhere. In the Kantō and other eastern regions, warrior families held lands and local offices in a landscape of overlapping claims among provincial governors, estate proprietors, and neighboring lords. Chapter 21 described their kinship networks, horse pastures, and mounted archery. Such families were often bound by ties of service to court-appointed governors, but they were also competitors, and many would respond to any leader who could confirm their holdings. Notably, many prominent Kantō families, including the Miura, Chiba, and Hōjō, traced descent from the Taira line of the ninth-century Emperor Kanmu, not from Kiyomori’s Ise Taira house. Genealogical labels were not reliable predictors of loyalty.

In November 1179 Kiyomori had marched troops into Kyoto, dismissed many officials, and confined Go-Shirakawa. In early 1180 the retired emperor’s son Takakura abdicated in favor of Kiyomori’s infant grandson, who became Emperor Antoku. This concentration of power provoked resentment among aristocrats, temples that had lost influence, and princes excluded from the succession. The environmental background was also unstable. Weather and harvests were poor in the years after 1179, a fact that would deeply shape the war. Meanwhile Tōhoku remained under the semi-autonomous Northern Fujiwara at Hiraizumi, rich in gold and horses and rooted in a frontier society that included Emishi heritage, while Kyūshū and Shikoku contained local lords whose loyalty to the Taira was often conditional. In the far south, Ryukyu was still a world of scattered communities and regional lords, outside this conflict and taken up in Chapter 32; the war would nevertheless later enter Ryukyuan and Ainu memory in unexpected ways, as Section 23.15 shows.

Section 23.1

What Was the “Genpei War”?

The word “Genpei” combines Gen, an alternative reading of the character for Minamoto, with Hei, a reading of the character for Taira. Contemporaries did not fight a “Genpei War.” Court diarists wrote of rebels, of punitive expeditions against them, and later of the “pursuit and destruction” of the Taira. The war tales spoke of Genji and Heishi (or Heike), meaning the Minamoto and Taira houses. The compound Genpei is attested soon after the conflict, but the standardized label “Genpei War” is later. It is a convenient shorthand, useful for teaching and for remembering, but it carries an assumption of two sides that this chapter will repeatedly test.

The more precise scholarly label, the Jishō–Juei War, ties the conflict to the era names (nengō) associated with its central phase, though the final campaigns fell in the following Genryaku era. Jishō (1177–1181) was the era of the first risings, and Juei (1182–1184) the era of the Kurikara campaign, Yoshinaka’s seizure of Kyoto, and his destruction. Era names themselves were part of the struggle. Because the court in Kyoto proclaimed them, adopting or ignoring a new era name could imply acceptance or rejection of the authority that issued it. Some eastern records for a time continued to use Jishō, a small but revealing sign that the rebels treated the Taira-dominated court’s decisions as illegitimate.

Neither “side” was united. The Taira in the strict sense were Kiyomori’s Ise Taira household and its close kin, who held court rank, governorships, and western bases. Yet the eastern warriors who eventually served Yoritomo included many Taira-descended families. Some Minamoto had served the Taira; others fought each other. Yoritomo, Yoshinaka, Yukiie, and the Kai Genji shared a broad Minamoto identity, but they commanded separate forces with separate goals, and their relationships ended in war. The conflict is better understood as a series of overlapping struggles: over the court and its legitimacy, over provincial authority and land, over the loyalty of warriors in different regions, and over the control of sea routes.

It also did not begin as a plan to create a shogunate. Prince Mochihito’s call was a call to remove Taira domination, not to found a new government. Yoritomo’s aims in 1180 probably included survival, vengeance, and security for the men who joined him; the shape of his later regime emerged from the war rather than preceding it. Reading later Kamakura institutions back into 1180 would make the war seem like an early stage of a foreordained outcome. It was not.

Historical Vocabulary
Genpei / Jishō–Juei War

“Genpei” is a compound of the first characters of Minamoto (源, Gen) and Taira (平, Hei), and refers in modern usage to the war between these houses. “Jishō–Juei War” (sometimes “Jishō–Juei civil war”) names the conflict by two era names associated with its central phase, and avoids implying a clean two-clan structure. The compound Genpei is attested soon after the conflict, but the standardized label “Genpei War” is later, and “Jishō–Juei” is a modern scholarly label.

The term matters because it embodies an interpretation. “Genpei” stresses lineage and later cultural memory. “Jishō–Juei” stresses political contingency and regional complexity. This textbook uses “Genpei War” as the recognized chapter title and shorthand, and “Jishō–Juei War” when the fragmented character of the conflict is at issue.

Any account of this war depends on choosing among sources with very different purposes. Court diaries such as Gyokuyō (by Kujō Kanezane) and Meigetsuki (by the poet Fujiwara no Teika) are contemporary, but their authors lived in Kyoto, learned of events at a distance, and wrote about rank, ritual, and the price of rice. The Azuma kagami was compiled by the Kamakura regime around the end of the thirteenth century from earlier records, and organizes the past around Yoritomo and the Hōjō. The Heike monogatari reached its best-known form in the fourteenth century, and the still larger Genpei jōsuiki incorporates additional layers. Temple records, letters, land documents, and archaeological evidence contribute less dramatic but often more reliable information. Section 23.15 returns to the war tales as historical evidence of a different kind.

Section 23.2

The Crisis of 1180: Antoku, Prince Mochihito, and the Call to Arms

Chapter 22 explained how Kiyomori’s coup of November 1179 removed his court opponents and confined Go-Shirakawa. Kiyomori’s grandson was enthroned as Emperor Antoku in the spring of 1180, when the boy was about two years old. For Kiyomori this was the fulfillment of a long marriage strategy. For many others it was an alarming concentration of imperial and military power in one household, achieved by force. Retired emperors, aristocrats, monasteries, and excluded princes each had a grievance.

Prince Mochihito, an older son of Go-Shirakawa who had been passed over for the throne, became the focus of the resistance. Later tradition attributes to him a call to arms directed to the Minamoto and to warriors and temples generally, usually described as a prince’s order (ryōji). Versions of its text survive in later sources, and their wording is not certain, but the essential act is well attested: a member of the imperial family authorized resistance to the Taira in language that treated the Taira as usurpers. This provided rebels with a legitimizing script. They could present themselves not as insurgents against the court but as defenders of the imperial house against a dominant family.

The most important warrior in the plot was Minamoto no Yorimasa, an elderly veteran of court politics who had served alongside the Taira in earlier crises and was also a distinguished waka poet. War tales explain his rebellion through a personal insult to his son Nakatsuna by Kiyomori’s son Munemori, an anecdote involving a horse that reflects narrative convention more than documented motive. A better explanation is that Yorimasa, like the prince, had lost status under Taira dominance. Once the plot was discovered, the prince fled to the monastery Mii-dera, one of several large temples with their own armed retainers, then attempted to reach Nara, where the other great temples were expected to help.

At Uji, south of Kyoto, the retreating party made a stand at the river crossing near the Byōdō-in. Sources agree that bridge planks were removed to slow pursuit, that the Taira army crossed the river in force, and that Yorimasa was killed, according to tradition by his own hand, while the prince was overtaken and killed soon afterward. War tales dwell on individual crossings and a famous farewell verse composed by Yorimasa. The historical core is simpler and more sobering: the first attempt to unseat Taira power failed within weeks. Contemporaries could not have known that it would matter.

It mattered because its message spread. Yukiie, Yoritomo’s uncle, carried the prince’s call to Minamoto and allied warriors in the east, including Yoritomo in Izu. Taira retaliation made matters worse: the Taira moved against the temples that had sheltered the rebels, and Kiyomori briefly relocated the capital to Fukuhara on the Inland Sea, partly, it seems, to escape the pressures of Kyoto’s powerful monasteries. The move was reversed within months. It illustrates a point that recurs in this chapter: the Taira regime had powerful resources but limited capacity to control a wide and fragmented archipelago, and it responded to each crisis at the point of maximum threat rather than through durable institutions.

Section 23.3

Yoritomo’s Revolt in the East

Yoritomo was born in 1147, the third son of Minamoto no Yoshitomo. After Yoshitomo’s defeat in the Heiji disturbance he was exiled to Izu, where local families with Taira ties watched him. He lived there for about twenty years, married Hōjō Masako, daughter of the local warrior Hōjō Tokimasa, and developed relations with regional families. Later chronicles, especially the Azuma kagami, emphasize his devotion to shrines and his patient preparation. Such accounts should be read with care. They were composed by a regime that needed its founder to appear destined for command.

In the eighth lunar month of 1180 (September), Yoritomo and a small band killed the local deputy of the Taira-aligned governor and attacked his residence. The act was an open rebellion against authority delegated by the Taira-controlled court. Yoritomo’s force was small, and his first serious battle, at Ishibashiyama in Sagami, was a defeat. Taira-aligned Kantō warriors, including Ōba Kagechika, overwhelmed his party. Some potential allies from the Miura family arrived too late. Yoritomo and a few companions hid in the mountains near Hakone before escaping by boat from the Sagami coast to Awa on the far side of the bay. Tradition credits an enemy commander, Kajiwara Kagetoki, with sparing him in a cave; this story, like many later legends, should be treated as tradition rather than fact.

The historically significant point is what happened next. A defeated exile was able to rebuild a force within weeks, and did so because of the conditions of Kantō society, not merely his own qualities. Warriors there held land under complicated claims and competed with governors’ deputies, neighboring lords, and estate managers. Some had grievances against Taira-aligned officials. Others were rivals of families that had joined the Taira side. In Awa, Yoritomo gained support from the Miura and from Chiba Tsunetane, and Kazusa Hirotsune, whose commitment was hesitant, eventually joined with a large following. The decision of each family depended on the calculations of land, patronage, feud, and opportunity described in Chapter 21.

Yoritomo’s appeal was practical. He could offer recognition of existing holdings and the hope of rewards from defeated enemies, and he could claim to act under the prince’s order. Taira-aligned families on the other side could make similar offers but were farther away and less immediately able to protect eastern interests. The mechanism was not vassalage in its mature form, which Chapter 24 examines. It was a series of contingent decisions by warriors who wanted their holdings protected and who remained free, for a time, to change their minds. The Azuma kagami reports hesitations, delayed arrivals, and later punishments, all of which suggest that Yoritomo’s command had to be negotiated.

Court diaries in Kyoto followed these events from a distance. Their authors called Yoritomo a rebel and recorded rumors, exaggerated troop numbers, and shifting reports of where the rebels were. This is a reminder that the war’s early stages were seen very differently in the capital than in the Kantō. For Kyoto, Yoritomo was one of several distant disturbances. For the eastern warriors who joined him, he was the leader who could give a political form to their local conflicts.

Section 23.4

Kamakura and the Eastern Coalition

In the tenth lunar month of 1180 (October), Yoritomo entered Kamakura in Sagami Province. The choice of base was practical. Kamakura lies on Sagami Bay, surrounded on three sides by steep hills and accessible by a few narrow passes, making it defensible and easy to control. It had earlier Minamoto associations. Yoritomo’s ancestors had held property nearby, and he relocated the Hachiman shrine now known as Tsurugaoka Hachimangū, dedicated to a war-related deity associated with the Minamoto. This act illustrates the religious dimension of wartime leadership. Warrior leaders sought divine support through shrine patronage, and Yoritomo also cultivated the shrines and temples of Izu and Hakone. This chapter treats Kamakura as a wartime headquarters, not yet the seat of a governing institution.

The Kantō supplied several strategic resources. It was a major horse-breeding region, with pastures in Musashi, Kōzuke, and neighboring provinces, and its warrior households were trained in mounted archery. Its agricultural lands supported large numbers of retainers. Its distance from Kyoto gave Yoritomo the room to consolidate before facing a Taira response. Kantō warriors also knew the region’s rivers, roads, and marshes, all of which mattered for campaigns in a landscape without a single central authority.

Yet it would be a mistake to describe the eastern warriors as simply obeying “the Minamoto.” The region was politically divided. Hatakeyama Shigetada and the Edo and Kawagoe families were initially aligned with the Taira but later submitted to Yoritomo as his army passed through Musashi. In the north, the Satake of Hitachi resisted until Yoritomo attacked them. Kazusa Hirotsune’s later fate, execution on suspicion of disloyalty according to the Azuma kagami, shows how carefully Yoritomo watched the loyalty of large-scale eastern lords. In Kai and Shinano, Takeda Nobuyoshi and other Minamoto-descended warriors acted on their own account. Some eastern warriors joined for land, some to escape retaliation, and some because their neighbors had done so.

Each decision was contingent. Warriors asked whether Yoritomo could protect them, whether he could defeat the Taira if they came east, and what his rivals might offer. The coalition that formed by the end of 1180 was therefore powerful but not automatic. It represented a temporary alignment among leaders who had to be rewarded, restrained, or removed, a problem that would shape Yoritomo’s conduct for the rest of his career. Chapter 24 explains how he turned these ad hoc relationships into a system. Here it is enough to notice that wartime Kamakura depended on continuing negotiation.

Section 23.5

Fuji River and the Failure of an Immediate Taira Victory

The Taira response was to send a court army east under Kiyomori’s grandson Taira no Koremori, with Tadanori and other commanders. It set out later than intended and moved slowly along the Tōkaidō. Recruitment along the route was uneven and desertion was heavy, the western provinces from which the Taira drew resources were already strained by poor harvests, and the army entered a region where local lords had begun to side with the rebels. Meanwhile Kai Genji forces under Takeda Nobuyoshi had moved into Suruga, and Yoritomo advanced from Kamakura to meet them.

The armies faced each other across the Fuji River in the tenth lunar month (late autumn 1180). Rather than fight a decisive battle, the Taira army withdrew during the night. The most famous account, in the Heike monogatari, says that waterfowl rose in the dark, and the noise convinced the Taira that they were under attack. The story is memorable and has been repeated for centuries. It is probably better understood as a literary expression of a real situation than as literal fact: an army that was hungry, undermanned, and uncertain of local support decided to retreat. Kyoto’s diarists and the Azuma kagami, despite their different perspectives, both report an army in disarray with desertions.

Why did the Taira fail? They had assembled their force hurriedly, at the wrong season, in poor conditions, and against an opponent whose numbers were growing. They lacked local bases in the east. Their army had been recruited from men whose commitment was to court authority or to particular commanders, not to a sustained campaign far from home. In such circumstances a retreat was rational.

The political importance of Fuji River exceeded its military significance. No one was annihilated. But the Taira, who claimed to be the court’s army suppressing rebels, failed to suppress them, and their failure encouraged others. Risings appeared in Shinano, Ōmi, Kii, Iyo, and Kyūshū. Yoritomo, on the advice of leading eastern allies, did not pursue the Taira toward Kyoto. He first turned to consolidate the Kantō, notably by defeating the Satake, and to secure allegiances. It was a slow, careful strategy that reflected the conditional nature of his coalition. According to tradition, at about this time he met his younger half-brother Yoshitsune, who had traveled from Hiraizumi in Mutsu, where the Northern Fujiwara had sheltered him, to join the cause.

Primary Source Window

The Fuji River Night in Three Kinds of Source

Creators: the anonymous, evolving tradition of the Heike monogatari (best-known recitation text, 1371); Kujō Kanezane, court statesman and diarist (Gyokuyō, written at the time); the compilers of the Azuma kagami (Kamakura, about a century later) · Type: war tale, contemporary court diary, and later regime chronicle

Context: The withdrawal of the Taira army from the Fuji River in 1180 is reported in all three types of source, which offer very different kinds of information. The following are paraphrases of their general content, not quotations.

Paraphrase of the war-tale account: The two armies camp on opposite banks. In the night a warrior’s movement startles a great flock of waterfowl, whose wingbeats sound like an immense host. The panicked Taira flee without order, leaving even their belongings behind. The story is told with vivid detail, humor, and moral judgment.
Paraphrase of the contemporary diary and Kamakura chronicle: News reaches Kyoto in pieces, with the impression of a failed expedition, desertions, shortages, and an army that turned back without the great battle expected. The Azuma kagami similarly stresses the Taira army’s loss of men and its withdrawal, presenting it as evidence of Yoritomo’s growing strength.

Analysis: The three accounts agree that the Taira retreated without decisive combat. They differ in what they explain. The diary records what a Kyoto aristocrat was told and worried about; it is close in time but distant in space. The Azuma kagami serves the Kamakura regime’s own story. The war tale converts a situation of weakness into a scene of ridiculous fear, and it fits a broader moral pattern in which pride and luxury make the Taira unfit to fight. Historians therefore treat the waterfowl as a story about the meaning of the retreat, not as its cause, and look to logistics, desertion, and politics for the causes.

Section 23.6

War, Famine, and a Fragmented Archipelago, 1181–1182

By the end of 1180 the archipelago was politically fragmented. The Taira held Kyoto, the child emperor, and much of the west. Yoritomo controlled most of the Kantō. Kai and Shinano Genji operated independently, and Yoshinaka was building his own force in the highlands. The Northern Fujiwara at Hiraizumi remained neutral and were courted by both sides. In Shikoku and Kyūshū, local lords rebelled or shifted allegiance, and the great monasteries of Ōmi and Nara maintained forces of their own. The war had no single front.

Nara brought a religious catastrophe. In the twelfth lunar month (January 1181) a Taira force under Shigehira, punishing Nara temples that had supported the rebels, attacked Nara. Fires spread through the city, and Tōdaiji and Kōfuku-ji, two of the most important centers of state Buddhism (see Chapter 15), were badly damaged; the Great Buddha hall was destroyed and the Buddha itself severely harmed. Contemporaries, and later tradition, read the destruction as deliberate sacrilege and as an offense against Buddhism itself and as proof of Taira arrogance. The reconstruction was undertaken by the monk Chōgen, who raised funds and drew on Song-Chinese craftsmanship, and it became a major religious and political project supported by the Kamakura leadership.

Kiyomori died in the second lunar month of 1181 (March), of illness. The Heike monogatari describes a fevered death, hot enough to make water boil, and attributes it to divine retribution; medical detail and moral judgment in such an account are literary. What can be said is that the Taira lost their most experienced leader at a bad moment. Munemori inherited headship but lacked Kiyomori’s authority, and Go-Shirakawa resumed cloistered government following the death of Takakura. Court decision-making became more complicated and the retired emperor’s ability to bargain with all sides increased.

The Yōwa famine then dominated events. A poor harvest in 1180 was followed by disastrous conditions in 1181, with severe effects in western Japan. Kamo no Chōmei, in the Hōjōki written years later, described the famine and epidemics in Kyoto. Farmers abandoned their fields and streamed into the capital; people died in the streets and along the Kamo River; and a priest reportedly counted corpses in the tens of thousands over a period of weeks. Chōmei’s figures are an impression, not a census, but the scale of suffering is corroborated by the interruption of campaigns and by the diary evidence of shortage and disorder.

Famine affected the war in several ways. Armies required grain, and both sides requisitioned it, adding to the burden on cultivators. The Taira were especially exposed because much of their support base and the tax rice that fed Kyoto came from the west; conflicts along the Tōkaidō and in Hokuriku also cut or slowed supply routes to the capital. Campaigns in 1181, such as Taira Tomomori’s defeat of Yukiie at the Yahagi River and efforts to counter Yoshinaka in Echigo, produced little decisive result. The court’s attempt to put Fujiwara no Hidehira in office as a check on Yoritomo also had limited effect. By 1182 the fighting largely stalled. This pause was not peace. It was a period of exhaustion and consolidation in which Yoritomo strengthened the east and Yoshinaka expanded in the northwest.

Everyday Life and Environment

Living Through the War and the Famine

For cultivators, war meant requisition of grain, horses, and labor; the burning of settlements in the path of armies; and lost planting seasons. Hunger drove families toward cities, temples, and the estates of powerful lords. Porters and boatmen bore the burden of supplying armies, and the recruitment of men for campaign left villages short of workers. Temples and shrines could be refuges, employers, or targets: The fires during the Taira attack on Nara show how sacred institutions were drawn into the war.

Women appear in the sources mostly as members of elite households, such as the ladies-in-waiting who traveled with the Taira, but women in ordinary communities carried on cultivation and household work under wartime strain. Disease accompanied famine, and the sources rarely describe its effects on children and the old. Historians should note the silence of the evidence, which comes largely from aristocrats, chroniclers, and storytellers, not from the people who suffered most. The famine also reminds students that a war of “heroes” was fought within an environment of scarcity that constrained every commander.

Section 23.7

Kiso Yoshinaka and the Problem of Multiple Minamoto Leadership

Minamoto no Yoshinaka destroys any picture of a single Minamoto army. Born about 1154, he was Yoritomo’s cousin. His father, Yoshikata, had been killed in an intra-Minamoto conflict in 1155 by Yoritomo’s elder brother Yoshihira, and the infant Yoshinaka was raised in the mountainous Kiso district of Shinano by the warrior Nakahara Kanetō. He therefore grew up among highland warriors, with no connection to Yoritomo’s eastern networks and a family memory that included violence between Minamoto branches.

Yoshinaka rose in Shinano in 1180, at almost the same time as Yoritomo, and expanded northward into Echigo and the Hokuriku provinces. The Taira court tried to suppress him through the Jō family of Echigo, but the effort failed. His army combined Shinano warriors, Kōzuke families, and local lords of Hokuriku. Its resources included the Hokuriku sea route, along which tax rice reached the capital; controlling the region gave him leverage over Kyoto’s food supply at a moment of famine. He also sheltered a son of the late Prince Mochihito, a person who could claim imperial legitimacy connected to the original call to arms. That gave Yoshinaka a source of authority that did not depend on Yoritomo.

Relations between the two Minamoto leaders were tense. In 1183 Yoritomo prepared to attack, and Yoshinaka avoided conflict by sending his young son Yoshitaka to Kamakura as a hostage. Other figures complicated matters. Minamoto no Yukiie, who had carried Mochihito’s call, quarrelled with Yoritomo and moved into Yoshinaka’s camp. Yoritomo’s uncle Shida Yoshihiro rose in Hitachi and also joined Yoshinaka’s side. The result was not a single Minamoto movement but several coalitions competing for the same prize.

The evidence on Yoshinaka is heavily colored by his defeat. Kamakura sources are hostile, and the Heike monogatari depicts him as a rustic and ill-mannered outsider, though it also gives him poignant scenes of loyalty and death. Kyoto diarists recorded the misconduct of his troops. Losers rarely find advocates in the sources that survive, and modern historians therefore have to reconstruct him with caution. His later reputation partly recovered: the poet Matsuo Bashō chose to be buried beside him at Gichū-ji in Ōmi, a sign of the affection that Yoshinaka’s tragic story attracted.

Section 23.8

Kurikara and the Taira Withdrawal from Kyoto

In the spring of 1183 the Taira sent a large army north to eliminate Yoshinaka. The war tales give figures as high as 100,000, which is not credible; the true size is unknown but plainly substantial. Leading commanders included Koremori, Michimori, and Tadanori. After early Taira gains, including the fall of a fortress at Hiuchi in Echizen (the tale of a traitor who released the water defending it is literary tradition), the armies met in Etchū and Kaga near the Kurikara and Tonami passes. Yoshinaka’s forces, using their knowledge of the terrain, defeated the Taira in early summer, and a further victory at Shinohara followed. The famous scene of cattle with torches on their horns, driven into the Taira camp at night, closely resembles an anecdote from ancient Chinese warfare and is best treated as a literary borrowing rather than a documented tactic.

What the campaign demonstrates is more important than its legends. The Taira had lost a major army, and Yoshinaka, with allies including monastic forces around Mount Hiei, could advance on Kyoto from the northeast, while Yukiie approached from another direction. The Taira lacked the strength to hold the capital against both. In the seventh lunar month (August 1183), they set fire to their residences and abandoned Kyoto. The decision was momentous. They did not merely retreat; they took with them Emperor Antoku, his mother Kenreimon’in, many court ladies, and the imperial regalia, and sailed west along the Inland Sea with the intention of establishing a base in Kyūshū or Shikoku.

Their calculations were political as well as military. If the Taira held the emperor and the regalia, they could claim to be the legitimate court, whatever happened in Kyoto. They had also intended to take Go-Shirakawa with them. But the retired emperor, forewarned, had escaped to Mount Hiei, and he returned to Kyoto in the company of the advancing Minamoto. It was a decisive reversal. Go-Shirakawa, who had been the Taira’s prisoner in 1179, now stood at the head of a court that ordered the pursuit and destruction of the Taira. The Taira had lost the capital and, with it, the exclusive claim to speak in the emperor’s name.

Section 23.9

Two Courts, Two Emperors, and Competing Legitimacy

The flight of the Taira created an extraordinary constitutional problem. The reigning emperor, Antoku, was in the hands of the Taira, and so were the three sacred treasures. Yet Kyoto needed an emperor, and Go-Shirakawa was determined to keep control of the succession. Within weeks he had installed a new emperor: Go-Toba, a young son of Takakura and thus a younger half-brother of Antoku. Yoshinaka reportedly favored a son of Prince Mochihito, whom he had sheltered in Hokuriku, but the retired emperor did not accept his candidate. In effect, Go-Shirakawa used his own authority as the senior member of the imperial house to override both the Taira and the strongest Minamoto commander in the capital.

For a time, therefore, there were two emperors and two centers of imperial authority: Antoku with the Taira in the west, and Go-Toba in Kyoto. The enthronement of Go-Toba took place without the regalia, an anomaly that court officials and later writers found troubling. The Taira never formally abdicated Antoku’s claim. This is why possession of the regalia mattered so much in 1184 and 1185: recovering them would make Go-Toba’s enthronement complete and end the uncertainty. The situation would appear again in the fourteenth century with the Northern and Southern Courts (Chapter 28), and comparing the two episodes shows how the imperial institution could be divided by political circumstances.

Legitimacy also worked in other ways. Yoritomo’s authority was first based on Mochihito’s call. In the tenth lunar month of 1183, the court issued the decree later known as the Juei 2 Tenth-Month Decree. Its original text does not survive intact, but summaries in Hyakurenshō and Gyokuyō describe an order to restore shōen and public-land revenues in the eastern circuits and to entrust Yoritomo with enforcing the settlement. Historians have consequently treated it as an important recognition of his eastern authority. His restoration to court status is reported separately. This recognition did not create the institutions of Chapter 24, but it did give his eastern position a court-approved dimension. The Taira, previously the court’s army, became rebels in the eyes of the capital. Antoku’s partisans had a symbolic emperor but no capital. Yoshinaka had force but no lasting legitimacy. Go-Shirakawa, who held the capital and the power to grant titles, was the winner of this phase of the struggle for authority, even though he had no army of his own.

Historical Vocabulary
Sanshu no jingi (the Three Sacred Treasures)

The three sacred treasures, the mirror, the sword, and the curved jewel, were the imperial regalia, objects tied in court tradition and in the early chronicles to the legitimacy of the imperial line. In 1183 the sword and jewel accompanied Antoku, and the mirror was also taken west with the Taira party. Their custody made the child emperor’s claim more than symbolic.

They matter to this chapter because Go-Toba’s enthronement without them was unprecedented, and because the treasures’ fates at Dan-no-ura differed: the mirror and jewel were recovered, while the sword of the court regalia was lost in the sea. Later traditions disagree over whether it was Kusanagi itself or a replica. The court later obtained a substitute. Historians treat the regalia as evidence of how much later tradition invested legitimacy in objects; in 1183 the objects were an essential political resource.

Section 23.10

Yoshinaka in Kyoto and Yoritomo’s Intervention

Yoshinaka entered Kyoto as a liberator, and within months he was hated. His army, made up of highland and northern warriors, arrived in a capital that was already suffering from famine, and it lived by foraging. Kyoto diarists complained of soldiers seizing goods, harvesting green rice from fields, and behaving with disrespect toward the court. Some of the criticism reflects aristocratic contempt for provincial warriors, but the underlying problem was real. A large army in a starving city could not be fed without requisition, and the court had little means to supply it.

Yoshinaka received court appointments and reportedly received a generalissimo title in early 1184 (sources differ over the precise title: he is commonly said to have received seii taishōgun, while Gyokuyō preserves a report of seitō taishōgun), but he was in an impossible position. The court wanted the Taira defeated and the regalia recovered. He led an expedition west in the autumn of 1183, but the Taira, having reorganized their fleet in the Inland Sea, defeated his forces at Mizushima in Bitchū, and Yukiie was beaten at Muroyama. The Taira regained ground. Meanwhile the court had opened direct negotiations with Yoritomo, and Yoshinaka returned to find that the retired emperor’s attention had turned east.

In the eleventh lunar month (December 1183) Yoshinaka attacked Go-Shirakawa’s palace, took control of the retired emperor, and replaced court officials. This was a coup in the manner Kiyomori had used in 1179, and it severely damaged his standing with Go-Shirakawa and the Kyoto court. Yoritomo, who had waited, now sent an army under his half-brothers Noriyori and Yoshitsune. In the first lunar month of 1184 (late February), they approached Kyoto by the Seta and Uji crossings. Yoshinaka’s defenses, thinly spread, collapsed. The war tales give the crossing at Uji as a race between two Kantō warriors, Sasaki Takatsuna and Kajiwara Kagesue, to be the first across the river; the story illustrates warrior competition and reward-seeking, but its details are literary. Yoshinaka fled and was killed at Awazu in Ōmi. Tradition places his foster brother Imai Kanehira at his side, and the war tales add the woman warrior Tomoe Gozen, whose historical reality cannot be confirmed.

The episode shows that anti-Taira victory did not create Minamoto unity. Yoshinaka’s fall gave Yoritomo’s forces control of the capital region, and Yukiie was later hunted down and killed. It also showed how far Yoritomo was willing to use his own family members as commanders while remaining in Kamakura. Yoritomo did not fight the war personally after 1180; he directed it from the east, delegated command, and used the reports and rewards that moved between Kamakura and the armies to maintain control. That pattern, delegated command combined with distant oversight, would become one of the features of his regime.

Historical Debate

Does “Genpei War” Obscure the Fragmented Nature of the Conflict?

The question: Should historians continue to use “Genpei War,” or does the name hide the fact that the conflict was a complex civil war rather than a struggle between two clans?

Interpretation A — “Genpei” captures something real: Genji and Heishi identities did matter. Kiyomori’s Taira household dominated the court, and the leaders of the resistance invoked Minamoto lineage and Minamoto memory of earlier defeat. Later participants, chroniclers, and audiences experienced the war in these terms, and the distinction between red and white banners in later tradition shows how quickly the simpler story took hold. The label has explanatory value if it is understood as a shorthand for two poles of political identity, not as a complete description.
Interpretation B — The name misleads: Many participants did not fit the categories. Taira-descended warriors led the eastern coalition, Minamoto fought Minamoto, and temples, princes, court factions, and local lords pursued their own aims. Yoshinaka and Yoritomo were rivals before they were allies. A name that suggests two clans encourages a narrative of ancestral enmity and obscures the role of land, famine, legitimacy, and regional politics. “Jishō–Juei War” or “civil war” is a more accurate description.

Evidence: The two positions draw on the same sources. A relies on the self-presentation of leaders, the language of the war tales, and the later symbolic use of banner colors. B relies on genealogies of the eastern families, the conflict between Yoritomo and Yoshinaka, and the diaries’ use of terms such as rebel and punitive expedition.

Why it matters: The choice of name affects what students expect to find. “Genpei” invites a story of two families. “Jishō–Juei” invites a study of coalitions, contingency, and legitimacy. Most modern historians combine the two, using the familiar name while explaining its limits.

Student task: Choose one Kantō family, one Minamoto leader, and one court figure discussed so far. For each, decide whether “Genpei” or “Jishō–Juei” better describes their role in the war, and justify your choice.

Section 23.11

Ichi-no-Tani: Driving the Taira West

After leaving Kyoto, the Taira had reorganized in the west. By early 1184 they had strengthened their base on the Inland Sea and fortified a position at Ichi-no-Tani, on the Suma coast near modern Kōbe, an eastern gateway to their maritime domain. The site combined a coastline and mountain slopes behind it, protected by palisades and supported by ships. The Taira could retreat by sea if needed. The court in Kyoto meanwhile was negotiating with them over the regalia and, some sources suggest, a possible truce. The exact nature of those contacts is debated, and it is unclear whether the Minamoto attacked in the middle of negotiations or simply ignored them.

Yoritomo’s brothers advanced in two forces. Noriyori attacked from the east along the coast. Yoshitsune moved through the hills to the north and west, and struck the fortifications from the rear. In the second lunar month of 1184 (March), the Taira line collapsed. The war tales tell of Yoshitsune’s descent of a precipitous slope on horseback (hiyodori-goe), a famous story that is difficult to verify and is probably exaggerated. The essential facts are more prosaic: a two-pronged attack, superior mobility, and confusion within the Taira defenses. Taira casualties included leading figures such as Tadanori, Michimori, and Tsunemasa. Shigehira, who had commanded the Taira attack on Nara, was captured; he was later handed over to Nara authorities and executed.

Among the dead was the young Taira no Atsumori, killed according to the war tales by the Kantō warrior Kumagai Naozane, who was said to have been shaken to discover that his victim was little older than his own son. That episode became one of the most beloved in Japanese literature and theater (see Section 23.15), but it comes from narrative sources. What is clearer is that Taira heads were paraded in Kyoto, over the court’s objections, and that the display drew on the anxieties of a capital that had been terrorized by the war.

Ichi-no-Tani did not end the war, because the Taira escaped by sea. This is the key strategic fact. The Taira could not hold a land position against Minamoto cavalry, but they still controlled ships, ports, and many coastal communities. They withdrew to Yashima in Sanuki on Shikoku and to other Inland Sea positions, and the Minamoto lacked the fleet needed to pursue them. Noriyori’s campaign in the far west during late 1184 stalled for lack of ships and provisions. Meanwhile tensions grew between Yoritomo and Yoshitsune, who accepted court appointments from Go-Shirakawa without Yoritomo’s approval. That conflict belongs mainly to the next chapter, but it already shows that military success brought a new problem: how to control commanders who were becoming important figures in their own right.

Section 23.12

Yashima and the Maritime War

The Inland Sea, a chain of islands, narrow straits, and strong tidal currents, had been the artery of the Taira’s power. Chapter 22 described their investment in ports and their trade with Song China. Their strength depended on seafaring families, coastal lords, and the ships and sailors these communities could provide. In 1184 and 1185 this strength became militarily decisive. The war was no longer determined by cavalry in river valleys, but by shipping, ports, provisions, and the loyalties of local maritime communities.

Yoshitsune assembled ships at Watanabe in Settsu, drawing on vessels and men from coastal families that had come over to the Minamoto. According to the war tales and the Azuma kagami, he sailed in stormy weather in the second lunar month of 1185 (March) and crossed to Awa on Shikoku in a few hours with a small force. Tradition emphasizes the boldness of the crossing and a dispute with Kajiwara Kagetoki over naval tactics, a story that may reflect later hostility toward Kagetoki. Local allies in Shikoku, such as the Kondō, provided guides and men. Yoshitsune then attacked the Taira position at Yashima from the landward side and burned nearby houses, so that the Taira, believing a large army approached, took to their ships.

The war tales’ most celebrated episode at Yashima has the Taira displaying a fan on a boat and challenging the Minamoto to shoot it. Nasu no Yoichi, a Kantō archer, rode into the surf and hit the target, and a Taira warrior who danced in admiration was shot as well. The story is a masterpiece of martial literature and is preserved with images in later painting, but it cannot be used to reconstruct the campaign. So too with the anecdote that Yoshitsune risked his life to retrieve a dropped bow so that the enemy would not see how weak it was. Such stories reveal warrior values and literary conventions. The historical outcome was that Yashima was lost and the Taira withdrew farther west.

Elsewhere the Minamoto cut off the Taira’s land options. Noriyori, with ships supplied by local Kyūshū warriors, crossed to Bungo in early 1185, breaking the Taira’s hope of falling back on Kyūshū and depriving them of provisions. Local families in Kyūshū, Shikoku, and Iyo made choices as they had in the Kantō. Some, such as the Kōno of Iyo, and the Kumano sea forces associated with the monk Tanzō, are reported to have provided ships or men for the Minamoto. The Taira were left with a shrinking maritime position and a dwindling supply of food and allies in a western Japan still scarred by famine. In the third lunar month, they took position near the Kanmon Strait.

Section 23.13

Dan-no-ura and the Destruction of the Taira Regime

The final battle took place on 25 April 1185 (the twenty-fourth day of the third lunar month) in the Kanmon Strait, the narrow channel between Honshu and Kyūshū at Dan-no-ura. The Taira, under Tomomori, had assembled a fleet, with Antoku, Kenreimon’in, Nii-dono, and many attendants aboard, and the Minamoto fleet under Yoshitsune, with Noriyori guarding the land side, confronted them. The Azuma kagami gives roughly 840 Minamoto ships against 500 Taira ships, while versions of the Heike give substantially larger totals. None of these figures can be treated as secure. Later sources generally portray the Minamoto coalition as having achieved a numerical advantage by the final battle, thanks to allied ships from many coastal groups.

Tidal currents have long figured in explanations of Dan-no-ura, but their role is disputed. The Heike monogatari describes the Taira initially fighting with a favorable current, while the famous theory that a reversal of the tide decided the battle was developed by modern historians. Later studies have questioned whether currents in the likely battle area were strong enough to determine the outcome. The shifting allegiance of maritime forces, the Taira’s shrinking supply base, fleet organization, and battlefield defections therefore deserve at least as much weight. In the accounts, the Taira formation eventually lost its coherence and Minamoto boats closed in. Defections, notably that of the Awa lord Taguchi Shigeyoshi, whom tradition credits with revealing which ship held the emperor, are reported but hard to verify. The war tales also describe Minamoto archers targeting rowers and helmsmen, an action that violated warrior etiquette; this may be a moral comment on victory rather than a certain fact. What is certain is that the Taira fleet was destroyed.

Many members of the Taira household died. Nii-dono, according to the Heike monogatari, took the child emperor in her arms, and with the sword and jewel, entered the sea. Antoku was about six years old by Western reckoning. The account of her words to him, that a capital lay beneath the waves, is among the best-known lines in the tale, and shows the way later writers created consolation from catastrophe. Tomomori and other leaders drowned. Kiyomori’s son Munemori and his son Kiyomune were taken prisoner. Kenreimon’in was rescued from the water and later became a nun in Ōhara; Go-Shirakawa’s visit to her is the subject of a celebrated episode. The mirror and jewel were recovered, but the sword of the court regalia was lost. Later traditions disagreed over whether the lost object was Kusanagi itself or a substitute or replica; Atsuta Shrine tradition maintained that Kusanagi remained at Atsuta.

Noncombatants were central to the event. Court ladies, children, attendants, and boat crews were aboard the Taira ships. Many drowned, some were captured, and some survived to live in seclusion. Popular tradition later told of Taira survivors who fled to remote mountain villages, while local tradition at Akama Jingū in Shimonoseki holds that surviving Taira female attendants continued annual observances for Antoku; in later generations local courtesans carried on the memorial procession. Such traditions are evidence of local memory, not proof of the women’s postwar lives. Munemori and Kiyomune were executed later in 1185. The direct line of Kiyomori would be pursued into the next decade, and some Taira retainers were hunted down.

Dan-no-ura ended the Taira as a political force. It did not end the presence of Taira-descended families in Japanese society, and the Hōjō, who would soon govern in Kamakura, were themselves of Taira descent. This is one of the strongest reasons for viewing the war as a struggle over power and legitimacy rather than lineage. The loss of the sword, meanwhile, remained an unresolved symbolic wound in the imperial tradition.

Dan-no-ura

Taira leaders and the imperial household: For the Taira commanders, the battle was the last chance to preserve a regime that had lost the capital and its allies. For the child emperor, his mother, and the women of the household, it meant the end of a way of life at court and, for many, death. For the warriors of the Kantō, it was the end of a war in which service could earn recognition and land. Sailors, shipowners, and coastal communities: Boat crews and coastal lords supplied the fleets and their decisions helped determine the outcome. They faced danger on the water, uncertain compensation, and the aftermath of a war that also affected their home ports. The written sources say little about them.
The court in Kyoto: For Go-Shirakawa and the aristocracy, victory meant the return of most of the regalia and the possibility of ending the dual-emperor problem. It also meant dependence on a new military leader in the east. The court’s celebration was mixed with anxiety about what victory would mean.
Section 23.14

What Did Victory Actually Settle?

Militarily, the war settled a great deal. The leadership of the Taira regime was destroyed. Yoshinaka and, soon afterward, Yukiie were eliminated. Yoritomo commanded the largest and best-organized military coalition in Japan, strengthened by the victories won by his brothers and by lands that had belonged to the Taira, which now became available for reward. He could act across provinces in a way no earlier warrior leader had. Contemporaries understood that the balance of power had shifted from Kyoto’s court and Kiyomori’s household to the leader in Kamakura.

Yet the victory left the court in place, and the court still mattered. Go-Shirakawa remained the central figure of imperial politics, and only the emperor and retired emperor could confer rank, title, and legitimacy. Yoritomo needed the court to endorse his authority and to give legal form to actions such as the appointment of officials in the provinces. Later in 1185 he obtained new military-administrative arrangements from the court, whose character and consequences are examined in Chapter 24. The essential point here is that the arrangements were the result of negotiation, and that the court was not a passive party.

The victory also opened a new conflict within the Minamoto. Yoshitsune’s independent acceptance of court honors and his prominence in the war’s final battles led Yoritomo to regard him as a threat. Yoshitsune fled and eventually sought refuge with the Northern Fujiwara at Hiraizumi, where he died in 1189. Yoritomo’s subsequent campaign against Hiraizumi belongs to Chapter 24. The pattern was the same as in the case of Yoshinaka: military success created rivals within the victorious camp.

Finally, the war’s costs were widely distributed. Nara’s temples had been badly damaged by fire during the Taira attack, the famine had killed many, and western Japan had been devastated by requisitions and fighting. Seafaring communities had seen their vessels and men drawn into naval campaigns. The court had lost a generation of power-holders. The war answered who possessed the strongest military coalition. It did not yet answer how that victory would be converted into a system of government, or how the warrior leadership and the imperial court would divide authority. That was the work of the following years, and of Chapter 24.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Imperial Legitimacy Before and After the War

What Changed

  • Warrior leaders had demonstrated that they could remove and install emperors’ allies, and that military coalitions could stand outside the court’s control.
  • The regalia were no longer secure symbols: the sword was lost and a substitute was needed.
  • Court officials learned that they had to bargain with distant military leaders, especially Yoritomo.
  • A new understanding of the roles of retired emperors, child emperors, and military commanders began to emerge.

What Stayed the Same

  • All sides sought imperial authorization; no participant tried to abolish the imperial institution.
  • Court rank and title remained the main currency of legitimacy.
  • Go-Shirakawa continued to govern as a retired emperor.

Who experienced change most directly? Court aristocrats and the imperial family faced the greatest change in their political position. For many rural communities, continuity was greater: the war brought hardship but did not immediately alter local structures. Why the pattern matters: The war changed who could threaten the court but did not change the belief that legitimacy flowed through it, which shaped the compromise developed in the following decades.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of the Taira Regime

Long-term causes

A regime built on court office and personal retainers, not on lasting provincial institutions; grievances of excluded princes, temples, and provincial warriors; the concentration of power in Kiyomori’s household.

Short-term causes

Kiyomori’s coup of 1179, Antoku’s enthronement, Mochihito’s call, Yoritomo’s and Yoshinaka’s risings, the failed expeditions of 1180 and 1183, and the death of Kiyomori.

Event or transformation

The abandonment of Kyoto in 1183, the loss of the capital and the retired emperor’s allegiance, and the defeat at sea at Dan-no-ura in 1185.

Immediate effects

The end of the Taira regime; the death of Antoku; the recovery of the mirror and jewel and loss of the sword; Yoritomo’s military ascendancy.

Long-term consequences

Creation of conditions for warrior government (Chapter 24); the elaboration of a warrior–court relationship; a durable cultural memory of the war.

Historical complexity

The Taira did not lose because they were “decadent,” as later tradition claims. Famine, geography, the shifting loyalty of Go-Shirakawa, Minamoto rivalries, and the choices of eastern and maritime warriors all shaped the outcome.
Section 23.15

From War to Memory: The Heike Tradition

The Heike monogatari (Tale of the Heike) is the central literary transformation of the war. It is not an eyewitness chronicle. It grew over generations from oral and written sources. A fourteenth-century essay tradition connects its origins to a court figure and a blind performer, but the story of a single author is uncertain. Several versions survive, including reading texts such as the Engyō-bon and, most famously, the Kakuichi-bon of 1371, a recitation text associated with the blind performer Akashi Kakuichi. The larger Genpei jōsuiki incorporates additional material. These works differ in wording, emphasis, and even in what they tell, which is why historians speak of a tradition rather than a single text.

The tale is framed by Buddhist ideas, above all mujō, the impermanence of all things. Its famous opening, about the sound of the bell of the Gion monastery and the flowers of the sala trees, turns the fall of the proud into a lesson about the transience of power. Kiyomori’s arrogance, the destruction of Nara’s temples, and the later downfall of his family are read as the workings of karma. At the same time, the tale expresses compassion for the defeated. The Taira are not simply villains: Atsumori’s death, Tadanori’s poetry, Antoku’s drowning, and Kenreimon’in’s life as a nun evoke sorrow. Performances also had a ritual function, pacifying the spirits of the dead. The story of the blind musician Hōichi, who plays for ghosts at Dan-no-ura, retold in English by Lafcadio Hearn, reflects this belief in the lingering presence of the fallen.

Historical Vocabulary
Biwa hōshi and Heikyoku

Biwa hōshi (“lute priests”) were itinerant, often blind performers who accompanied recitation on the biwa, a plucked lute. Heikyoku is the recitation of the Heike monogatari. Performers were organized in associations and, by the later medieval period, enjoyed patronage from warrior and court elites.

This matters because the Heike tale reached most audiences through performance, not reading. Performers adapted, abbreviated, and elaborated episodes, which helps explain why the tradition is so varied. The term “priest” is a description of appearance and role, not necessarily of formal ordination.

Certain episodes became cultural institutions. Atsumori and Kumagai Naozane, whose remorse leads him to become a monk, inspired Noh plays and the kōwaka dance-narrative. Noh drama, which flourished in the Muromachi period (Chapter 30), returned repeatedly to Heike figures, including Tadanori, Tsunemasa, Sanemori, and Yashima itself, often presenting the ghost of a warrior who cannot find rest. Yoshitsune, who had become a symbol of the talented young man betrayed by his family, was celebrated in the fifteenth-century Gikeiki, in plays including Noh and later Kabuki and Bunraku, and in the sympathy for the doomed hero known as hōgan-biiki. Yashima’s fan, Tomoe Gozen, and the drowning of Antoku became subjects for painting and prints throughout the Tokugawa period.

Later communities also fitted the war into their own histories. Edo-period writers claimed that Yoshitsune had escaped to Ezo, and some Wajin sources identified him with the Ainu culture hero Okikurumi. This identification originated largely in Wajin efforts to place Ainu traditions inside a Japanese narrative, while Ainu oral tradition concerning Okikurumi has its own independent history (see Chapter 48). A parallel legend appears in the Ryukyuan chronicle Chūzan seikan (1650), which says that Minamoto no Tametomo, Yoritomo’s uncle, reached the Ryukyu Islands and fathered a founding ruler; historians treat this tradition as later political myth linking Ryukyu and Japan (Chapters 32 and 48). The war’s memory thus traveled far beyond the events it recounted.

In the modern period, the tradition has continued through novels, especially Yoshikawa Eiji’s Shin Heike monogatari, NHK historical dramas about Kiyomori and Yoshitsune, television anime, and popular imagery. The association of red with the Taira and white with the Minamoto is often cited as one origin of the red-and-white team convention in Japanese contests, though such claims about long-term cultural transmission should be treated as plausible rather than proved. Why did these stories outlast the documentary record? They condensed political catastrophe into human scenes, offered a religious explanation of loss, gave performers and audiences characters to mourn and admire, and could be reshaped by every era. The war of documents and the war of memory are both historical evidence, but they answer different questions.

Chapter Summary

The conflict of 1180–1185, remembered as the Genpei War and studied by historians as the Jishō–Juei War, began as resistance to Taira dominance and became a multi-sided civil war. It did not begin as a plan to create a shogunate, and it was never simply a fight between two unified clans. The Taira regime in Kyoto controlled the emperor, the regalia, many governorships, and the Inland Sea. Its opponents included princes, court warriors, provincial lords, armed temples, and rival Minamoto leaders.

Prince Mochihito’s call to arms in 1180, and the failed rising at Uji, transformed discontent into open warfare and gave later rebels a legitimizing script. Yoritomo, an exile in Izu, revolted in the autumn, suffered defeat at Ishibashiyama, and then rebuilt a force in the Kantō. His success reflected the conditions of eastern society, where warriors chose leaders according to their claims to land, patronage, and protection. Kamakura became his wartime base, but the eastern coalition remained contingent and needed continuous management.

The Taira expedition of 1180 collapsed at the Fuji River without a decisive battle. The war-tale story of waterfowl illustrates how later literature gave meaning to a real failure caused by logistics, desertion, and lack of local support. The failure encouraged risings elsewhere and left the war without a single front. The fires during the Taira attack on Nara in 1181 and the death of Kiyomori further damaged the Taira, and the Yōwa famine then constrained every army and devastated civilians, especially in the west on which the Taira depended.

Kiso Yoshinaka’s rise in Shinano and Hokuriku demonstrated that the Minamoto were not a single camp. His victories at Kurikara and Shinohara opened the way to Kyoto in 1183 and forced the Taira to abandon the capital, carrying Antoku and the regalia westward. Go-Shirakawa, who escaped the Taira, installed Go-Toba, creating a period of two imperial centers. The struggle became a contest over legitimacy as well as territory.

Yoshinaka’s army, hungry and undisciplined, alienated Kyoto, and his coup against Go-Shirakawa gave Yoritomo the opportunity to intervene. Yoshitsune and Noriyori defeated him in early 1184. Anti-Taira victory thus produced Minamoto conflict rather than unity, a pattern that would recur in Yoritomo’s quarrel with Yoshitsune.

The campaigns of Ichi-no-Tani, Yashima, and Dan-no-ura moved the war to the sea. The Taira survived defeat on land because they retained ships and ports, but they lost the maritime war as coastal and Kyūshū warriors changed sides, provisions dwindled, and the Minamoto assembled a larger fleet. At Dan-no-ura in 1185, Antoku, most Taira leaders, and many noncombatants died, and the sword of the regalia was lost.

Yoshitsune emerges in these campaigns as a capable and daring commander, but not as a solitary genius. Noriyori, local allies, shipowners, guides, and the coalition’s logistics were essential, and the stories that make him a legend belong largely to later tradition.

The victory settled the immediate question of military power. Yoritomo commanded the strongest coalition, and lands formerly held by the Taira became available for rewards. But the court remained the source of legitimacy, and the imperial institution was neither abolished nor challenged directly. The war left unresolved how military success would become a system of government, the question taken up in Chapter 24.

The war’s costs fell heavily on ordinary people, and this chapter has tried to keep them visible: cultivators facing requisition and famine, temple communities, boatmen, women and children, and the noncombatants at Dan-no-ura. The sources are dominated by aristocrats, chroniclers, and storytellers, which is a reason for caution as well as a limit on what can be known.

The Heike monogatari and related traditions converted this conflict into a Buddhist meditation on impermanence and a repertoire of tragic scenes, and they inspired Noh, Kabuki, prints, novels, and film. Their durability owes as much to performance, religion, and emotional power as to historical accuracy. Historians therefore read the war tales as evidence of how the war was remembered, while relying on diaries, chronicles, and documents to reconstruct what happened.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can collapse through the interaction of many pressures rather than the will of a single opponent. The Taira regime fell through failures of legitimacy, logistics, and alliance, under the strain of a famine that no leader controlled. The story the war tales tell, of proud men punished by fate, is powerful but is not the whole history.

The war also reveals how power worked in medieval Japan. Provincial warriors bargained for protection and land. The imperial court retained a unique ability to confer legitimacy even when it lacked armies. Seafaring communities and temples were political actors. The Kantō, the Hokuriku, the Inland Sea, and Kyūshū each shaped the outcome. The Northern Fujiwara stood outside the war but would soon be drawn into its consequences, and the war’s memory would eventually reach the Ainu and Ryukyuan worlds in ways that show how stories can be used to bind or claim peripheries.

What continued after 1185 is as important as what changed. The imperial institution remained central. Taira-descended families such as the Hōjō would rise in the very regime founded by the victor. Court and warriors would negotiate their relationship for centuries. The tension between military power and legitimate authority, unresolved in 1185, is a thread that runs through Kamakura, Muromachi, and Tokugawa history.

For students, the chapter also offers a lesson in reading the past. The documents of the war are fragments and partisan records; its most famous scenes were created by later performers and writers. To understand why the war matters, one has to keep both in view. Chapter 24 turns from the war to the institutions that followed it, and asks how Minamoto no Yoritomo converted a coalition of contingent loyalties into the first durable warrior government.
