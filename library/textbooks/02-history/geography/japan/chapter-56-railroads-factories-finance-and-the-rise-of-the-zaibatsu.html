Part VIII — Bakumatsu and the Meiji Revolution
56

Railroads, Factories, Finance, and the Rise of the Zaibatsu

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 14 October 1872 (by the Gregorian calendar; Japan did not adopt that calendar until the following year), a steam train ran for the first time along the full length of the line between Shimbashi in Tokyo and Yokohama, a distance of roughly 29 kilometers. Within a few weeks of that opening, a French-designed silk-reeling mill in the upland town of Tomioka, in present-day Gunma Prefecture, began operating with several hundred young women at its basins. The railway and the mill were very different enterprises, but both were instruments of the same government project.

The railway was an engineering feat directed by foreign engineers and paid for in large part with a loan raised in London. The mill, by contrast, was a government-owned factory that sought to teach Japanese silk producers the techniques of mechanical reeling. Between them they point to the problem this chapter takes up: how did a society that, in the 1850s, had no steam railways, no mechanized factories, no unified currency, and no central bank come, within about three decades, to possess all of them? And how did that transformation shape the lives of the people who farmed, wove, mined, reeled, financed, and traveled in the new economy?

The chapter's argument is that Japanese industrial capitalism was neither a sudden import from the West nor a creation of government alone. The Meiji government (Chapter 53) built railways, subsidized shipping, imported machinery and foreign engineers, reformed the currency, and for a time operated factories and mines of its own. But it did so on top of an economy that was already commercialized and in some regions already proto-industrial. Merchant houses with more than a century of experience in finance and trade, villages that combined farming with spinning and reeling, craftsmen with real metalworking skills, and domain governments that had already experimented with furnaces and shipyards all contributed. Foreign technology and foreign markets were indispensable, but they were adopted selectively and adapted to Japanese conditions, including the abundant labor of rural households and the shortage of capital.

Growth had costs, and a serious account of the period must show them. Government spending on industry and the military was financed overwhelmingly by the land tax (Chapter 54), which fell on farming households. When the government stabilized its finances in the early 1880s, the resulting deflation drove many farmers into debt, and some into the loss of their land. Factory work offered wages to young women and a few men, but under conditions of long hours, dormitory discipline, and illness that reformers and officials would later document. Mines drew on convict labor as well as free workers, and their wastes began to damage farmland and waterways. Meanwhile, the sale of state enterprises to a small number of well-connected merchants in the 1880s helped lay the foundations for the family-controlled business groups later called zaibatsu.

Historians reconstruct this history from an unusually varied evidentiary record: government statistics and ministry reports, the account books and company histories of merchant houses, banking regulations and currency laws, newspaper coverage, foreign consular reports, engineering drawings, surviving buildings and rails, village records, and the recollections of workers. Each source has limits. Official figures tend to measure what the state wished to count; company histories were often written to celebrate the firm; and workers, especially young women, left few written accounts of their own. This chapter therefore pays attention to who produced each kind of evidence and to whose experience is missing from it.

The chapter belongs within a division of labor with its neighbors. Chapter 54 explained the abolition of the domains, the land-tax reform, and the end of samurai stipends; Chapter 55 examined schooling and the new social order. Here those developments appear only as context. Chapter 57 will follow the political movements that grew in part from rural distress, and Chapter 63 will take up strikes, unions, and the Ashio pollution conflict. Imperial expansion, which transformed Japanese industry after 1895, belongs to Chapters 60 to 62. This chapter covers principally the years from 1868 to about 1900, ending where Japan's first modern integrated steelworks began operation in 1901.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Tokugawa commercial institutions, rural manufacturing, and technical skills shaped the starting point of Meiji industrialization.
  2. Analyze the goals and limits of the slogans fukoku kyōhei and shokusan kōgyō as guides to government policy.
  3. Trace how land-tax revenue, borrowing, and fiscal priorities financed railways, model enterprises, and the military.
  4. Assess the contribution of foreign engineers and imported machinery, and the ways Japanese officials and entrepreneurs adapted them.
  5. Describe how railways, steamships, telegraphs, and postal services changed the movement of goods, information, and people, and why their effects were uneven across regions.
  6. Compare state-sponsored enterprise at Tomioka with private mechanized cotton spinning in Osaka.
  7. Interpret the experiences of rural households and factory workers, especially women, using social-historical evidence and recognizing its limits.
  8. Explain how currency reform, national banks, and the Bank of Japan created a monetary system, and how that system operated.
  9. Evaluate the causes and social consequences of the inflation of the 1870s and the Matsukata deflation of the 1880s.
  10. Distinguish the differing origins of Mitsui, Sumitomo, Mitsubishi, and Yasuda, and explain how privatization shaped their growth.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether Japanese industrialization was state-led or driven by private enterprise.
  12. Connect early industrial development to Hokkaido, Okinawa, and Japan's wider commercial relationships.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is it misleading to describe Meiji industrialization as beginning "from nothing" in 1868, and what did the Tokugawa economy contribute?
  2. What problems was the government trying to solve with state-sponsored industry, and why did it later sell most of its factories?
  3. How did the way the government raised revenue shape who bore the costs of industrial development?
  4. In what ways did railways, shipping, telegraphs, and postal services integrate markets, and which regions and groups benefited least?
  5. How did the rural household economy and the factory economy depend on one another, especially in silk and cotton?
  6. How did a monetary and banking system function, and why did inflation and deflation have such different effects on different social groups?
  7. How did the zaibatsu differ from one another in origin, and why should their later conglomerate form not be projected back onto the 1870s?
  8. What does the history of this period reveal about the relationship between government policy and private initiative, and about the limits of the evidence on which historians base that judgment?

Historical Context

The new government of 1868 inherited a country that had been opened to trade only a decade earlier. Under the treaties of the 1850s and the tariff convention of 1866, Japan could levy only low import duties, foreign residents in the treaty ports lived under their own consuls' jurisdiction, and much of the early overseas trade passed through the hands of foreign merchants at Yokohama, Nagasaki, and later Kobe. Raw silk and tea became the principal exports; cotton cloth and yarn, woolens, and munitions were prominent imports. The government could not shelter new industries behind high tariffs, and its leaders worried that cheap imports and the outflow of gold and silver would erode both economic independence and military security. Those anxieties are the background to the policies examined in this chapter.

Chapters 53 to 55 described the political revolution that brought the Meiji leaders to power: the Boshin War, the abolition of the domains in 1871, the fiscal reform that replaced domain taxes with a national land tax paid in money, and the dissolution of the legal distinctions among samurai, farmers, artisans, and merchants. These changes matter for this chapter in practical ways. A national government could now tax, spend, issue money, and build infrastructure across the whole country; barriers that domains had maintained against the movement of goods and people were removed; and former samurai, deprived of their stipends, looked for new occupations. Yet the new state was short of money, short of technical expertise, and uncertain of its own survival, especially in the years before the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877.

Students should keep in mind that "Japan" in this chapter is a state in the process of consolidating control over unequal regions. The core of the commercial economy lay along the Pacific coast corridor from Tokyo and Yokohama through the Nagoya area to Osaka, Kobe, and the Inland Sea. Hokkaido, which the government had begun to organize as a colonial development zone in 1869, and the Ryukyu Islands, which were incorporated as Okinawa Prefecture in 1879, had different histories and entered the national economy on different terms. The Ainu of Hokkaido, whose fishing and hunting economy had long been linked to Wajin merchants, saw their resources reorganized for settlers and for Japanese commerce. Okinawa's sugar economy remained oriented toward the Japanese market while its older land and tax arrangements were left in place for years. Much of the history of Meiji industrial capitalism is the history of how these differently situated places were drawn into a single national economy.

Section 56.1 · Division I — Foundations of Meiji Industrial Capitalism

The Tokugawa Economic Inheritance and the Challenge of Industrialization

Meiji industrialization did not start from a blank slate. Over the preceding two and a half centuries, Tokugawa Japan (Chapters 42 to 50) had developed one of the most commercialized pre-industrial economies in Asia. Osaka served as the nation's warehouse and credit center, where domains sold their tax rice and specialized wholesalers (ton'ya) handled goods for the Edo market. The Dōjima rice exchange traded in rice certificates and, in effect, futures contracts. Coastal shipping, notably the kitamaebune route linking Ezo (Hokkaido) and the Japan Sea coast to Osaka, carried herring fertilizer, kelp, and marine products produced under Matsumae-controlled fisheries that depended on Ainu labor and knowledge. Satsuma's control of Ryukyu allowed it to sell Okinawan sugar and to channel goods, including kelp, toward China. Merchant houses such as Mitsui and Sumitomo had accumulated experience in drapery, money exchange, copper refining, and government finance.

The countryside was equally important. Farm households in the Kinai region, the Kantō plain, and the upland districts of central Honshu had increasingly grown cash crops, including cotton, rapeseed, indigo, mulberry, and tobacco, and combined farming with by-employment in spinning, weaving, sericulture, sake brewing, and soy-sauce production. Under the putting-out system, merchants supplied raw materials to rural households and collected the finished products. Dried-sardine and oil-cake fertilizers purchased through commercial networks raised yields. Regional specialization, rising literacy, and the habit of keeping accounts gave many villages a commercial sophistication that Meiji entrepreneurs could draw upon.

Technical and organizational skills also existed. Japanese miners had worked copper, silver, and gold at Besshi, Sado, and Iwami for centuries; ironworkers practiced the traditional tatara process; and in the final decades of the shogunate, the bakufu and powerful domains such as Saga, Satsuma, Mito, and Chōshū experimented with reverberatory furnaces, cannon casting, and Western-style ships. The bakufu's Nagasaki ironworks (begun with Dutch assistance in the late 1850s) and the Yokosuka shipyard (begun in 1865 with French engineers) became direct predecessors of Meiji state enterprises. The Kamaishi blast furnace in northeastern Honshu, begun in the 1850s, shows both the ambition and the difficulty of such efforts.

The limits of this inheritance were equally real. Japan lacked steam-powered machinery, factories organized around mechanized production, a national currency, a banking system based on deposits and note issue, and a transportation network capable of moving bulk goods quickly over land. Domains had issued hundreds of kinds of paper notes, barriers and tolls hindered overland trade, and merchant guilds restricted competition. When the ports opened in 1859, foreign trade quickly demonstrated both Japanese capacity and Japanese vulnerability: silk exports boomed, partly because silkworm disease had damaged European production, while imported cotton yarn and cloth pressed on domestic spinners and weavers. The task of the Meiji era was therefore not to invent commerce but to reorganize it around new institutions, technologies, and a national government.

Historians reconstruct the Tokugawa economic base from merchant account books, domain tax records, village registers, price series, and the archives of long-lived houses. These sources are rich but uneven, because large firms and well-organized villages preserved records far more often than small shops and poor households. For this reason, the "inheritance" described here probably overstates the experience of the typical village and understates regional variation, a point students should remember when generalizing about the commercial economy of Hokkaido, Tōhoku, or Okinawa.

Section 56.2

Fukoku Kyōhei and Shokusan Kōgyō: The Government's Industrial Ambitions

The slogan fukoku kyōhei, "enrich the country, strengthen the military," had been used by late-Tokugawa thinkers worried about foreign pressure, and the Meiji leaders adopted it as shorthand for national survival. The reasoning was straightforward: military strength depended on weapons, ships, and the revenue to pay for them; revenue depended on a productive economy; and an economy tied to unequal treaties and low tariffs needed to substitute domestic production for imports and to earn foreign exchange through exports. The Iwakura Mission of 1871–1873 (Chapter 54), which sent a large part of the government's leadership to the United States and Europe, reinforced these convictions. Its members saw factories, railways, and shipyards in Britain, Germany, and elsewhere and concluded that wealth and power rested on industry.

The companion slogan shokusan kōgyō, "promote production and encourage industry," described the means. It did not mean that the state would own the economy. Rather, the government would introduce technology, train workers, build infrastructure, and in some cases operate pioneer enterprises, with the expectation that private business would eventually take over. Ōkubo Toshimichi, who led the new Home Ministry from 1873, was a major advocate; his 1874 proposals on industrial promotion called for the state to foster industries it considered essential, especially those that could replace imports or earn export income. The Home Ministry and the Ministry of Public Works (Section 56.4) became the principal agencies, supplemented by the Hokkaido Development Commission (Kaitakushi), which pursued its own program of mining, farming, and road building in the north. The government also organized the first National Industrial Exposition in Ueno in 1877 to display and encourage domestic products.

The government's ambitions were constrained by the realities of its resources. Revenue came overwhelmingly from the land tax, and the stipends owed to former samurai absorbed a large share of the budget until their commutation (Chapter 54). Foreign borrowing was kept small: leaders were aware of the experience of other Asian states that had lost control of their finances to creditors, and they limited themselves to two loans raised in London in the early 1870s. The treaty system also restricted tariff protection. As a result, the government had to choose carefully, and its choices reflected strategic priorities. Railways, telegraphs, shipping, arsenals, and mines received attention because they served both military and commercial needs, while many small manufacturing activities were left largely to private initiative.

Historical Vocabulary
Shokusan kōgyō (殖産興業)

Shokusan kōgyō, usually translated "promote production and industry" or "increase production and encourage industry," was the Meiji government's term for its policy of fostering new industries through state action: importing machinery, hiring foreign experts, opening model factories, sponsoring exhibitions, and building infrastructure. It was not a single program but a set of policies pursued by several ministries over two decades, with changing emphasis. In the 1870s the government directly operated many enterprises; after about 1880 it largely shifted toward selling them and encouraging private capital.

The term should not be confused with modern "industrial policy" in the sense of a coordinated plan with fixed targets, nor with socialism or state ownership as an end in itself. Many officials regarded government enterprise as temporary, and a number of the businessmen who later took over state factories had worked closely with these ministries. The concept matters because it frames the central question of this chapter: what did government encouragement contribute to industrial growth, and what did private entrepreneurs and the existing economy contribute?

Section 56.3

Financing Industrial Development: Taxes, Public Revenue, and State Investment

The central fact of Meiji public finance was dependence on the land tax. Chapter 54 explained the reform of 1873, which assessed land value and set the tax in money at a fixed rate, replacing a variety of domain-level payments in kind. In the 1870s the land tax supplied roughly 70 to 80 percent of ordinary government revenue; as other taxes grew, its share declined, falling well below half by the 1890s. The fixed money rate gave the government predictable income and gave farmers a clear obligation, but it also meant that the real burden on farming households rose when prices fell and eased when prices rose, a feature of decisive importance for the inflation and deflation described in Sections 56.17 and 56.18.

Out of this revenue the government had to pay for the military, the civil administration, the stipends and bonds issued to former samurai, and its investment programs. Industrial and infrastructure spending was substantial but never the largest item. The Ministry of Public Works spent heavily on railways, telegraphs, lighthouses, mines, and shipyards during the 1870s. The Hokkaido Development Commission was allotted a ten-year budget of about ten million yen beginning in 1872, supporting surveys, roads, mining, and the recruitment of settlers and American advisers. Such spending shifted over time: after the costs of the Satsuma Rebellion in 1877, and increasingly after the early 1880s, military expansion claimed a growing share of public resources.

Two foreign loans supplemented domestic revenue. A loan of one million pounds sterling, raised in London in 1870 at nine percent interest, financed the first railway and associated projects; a second, larger loan in 1873 helped support the conversion of samurai stipends into bonds. The government also borrowed domestically, issued bonds, and, as discussed below, printed paper money. The decision to rely mainly on taxes and domestic credit rather than extensive foreign debt preserved fiscal autonomy but shifted the burden to Japanese farmers. Students should not conclude that taxation "caused" industrialization in a simple way. The same revenue also paid for the military and for the political settlement with former samurai, and spending decisions reflected choices among competing goals.

The development of Hokkaido illustrates what state investment could mean for people outside the commercial core. Government-supported roads, coal mines, and farm settlements were built in Ainu homelands, often with the labor of convicts and of migrants from Honshu. Regulations on hunting and fishing issued in the 1870s and after restricted traditional Ainu livelihoods, and the resources of the north were redirected toward settlers and Japanese enterprises. Hokkaido thus shows that "national development" could mean displacement and loss for some communities, a theme that continues in later chapters on Ainu history.

Section 56.4

The Ministry of Public Works, Foreign Advisers, and Model Industries

The Ministry of Public Works (Kōbushō), established in 1870 and abolished in 1885, was the central agency of early state-sponsored technology transfer. Led in its early years by officials such as Itō Hirobumi and Yamao Yōzō, both of whom had studied in Britain, it supervised railways, telegraphs, lighthouses, mines, and shipbuilding, and ran a technical school. Yamao in particular advocated training Japanese engineers to replace foreigners. In 1873 the ministry opened the Imperial College of Engineering (Kōbu Daigakkō), organized under the Scottish engineer Henry Dyer, which provided instruction in civil, mechanical, mining, and electrical subjects and graduated Japanese engineers who by the 1880s were taking over senior technical posts.

The ministry and other agencies employed oyatoi gaikokujin, "hired foreigners," in large numbers: engineers, surveyors, teachers, doctors, naval and military instructors, and technicians from Britain, France, the United States, Germany, and other countries. Several hundred were on government payrolls at the peak in the mid-1870s, and over the whole period well over two thousand served. They were expensive: salaries were high by Japanese standards and absorbed a significant portion of the ministry's budget. The relationship was one of contract and subordination, with foreign experts working under Japanese officials who set the terms of employment and, in many cases, deliberately arranged for Japanese counterparts to learn their methods. The British engineer Richard Henry Brunton built lighthouses; Edmund Morel directed early railway planning before his death in 1871; the Frenchman Paul Brunat organized the Tomioka mill; and American advisers such as Horace Capron worked for the Hokkaido Development Commission.

Foreign private merchants and engineers also participated outside the state sector. The Scottish merchant Thomas Glover supported domain and bakufu modernization in Nagasaki and was involved in the early Takashima coal mine; British and other foreign firms supplied equipment and credit through the treaty ports. Japanese officials, however, generally sought to prevent foreigners from owning mines, railways, and other strategic assets, and treaty provisions limited foreign residence to the open ports, so that foreign influence worked largely through contracts, sales, and advice rather than ownership.

The government also ran or took over "model" enterprises meant to demonstrate new methods: machine works, cement and glass factories, a brewery in Sapporo, mines, shipyards, and the Tomioka mill. These were not uniformly successful. Some lost money, some failed to spread their technologies, and some served the military more than the civilian economy. Nonetheless, they trained Japanese managers and technicians and provided the equipment and know-how that private entrepreneurs would later acquire, often at favorable prices. Their significance lay as much in the people who passed through them as in their balance sheets, a point taken up in the Historical Debate in Section 56.19.

Section 56.5 · Division II — Railroads, Transportation, and Economic Integration

The Railway Revolution: From Shimbashi to Yokohama and Beyond

Japan's first public railway ran between Shimbashi in Tokyo and Yokohama, about 29 kilometers along the shore of Tokyo Bay. After surveys and construction directed by British engineers, with Japanese labor and Japanese-supplied materials where available, a section between Shinagawa and Yokohama opened in June 1872, and full service to Shimbashi began that October with a ceremony attended by Emperor Meiji. The line used the narrow 3-foot-6-inch (1,067 mm) gauge, a choice that lowered construction costs on difficult terrain but later limited speed and capacity. The journey took under an hour, a dramatic contrast with the day-long walk or the slower coastal boat. A British loan, secured against customs revenue, supplied much of the financing.

Expansion was gradual. A line connecting the treaty port of Kobe with Osaka opened in 1874, extended to Kyoto in 1877, and to Ōtsu on Lake Biwa in 1880. In Hokkaido, the Horonai line, built under American engineer Joseph Crawford to carry coal from the mines at Horonai to the port of Otaru, opened in 1880. Private capital entered with the Nippon Railway Company, founded in 1881 with aristocratic and merchant investors and the government's support; it opened its first line, from Ueno northward, in 1883 and reached Aomori in 1891. The Tōkaidō trunk line between Tokyo and Kobe was completed in 1889. Private railway companies multiplied in the late 1880s, and by the 1890s private lines carried a large share of the network. Total track grew from a few tens of kilometers at the start of the 1870s to more than 2,000 kilometers by about 1890 and well over 5,000 by 1900, approximate figures that show the scale of expansion.

The economic effects were real but uneven. Railways reduced transport costs and time for passengers, mail, and some freight, connected inland production zones to ports, and allowed businesses to coordinate across distances. Coal, silk, rice, and cotton moved increasingly by rail, and in the 1890s military mobilization also depended on the rail network. At the same time, much freight continued to travel by coastal and river boats in the 1870s and 1880s, which remained cheaper for bulk goods, and railways bypassed many mountain and northern districts. Railways also changed experience: scheduled departures, ticket classes, and standardized time altered habits of travel and gave people a new sense of distance. Pilgrimage, regional tourism, and urban commuting all grew as rail travel became cheaper.

For the state, railways posed difficult trade-offs. Construction was expensive and repayment slow, and officials debated whether to prioritize trunk lines for strategic reasons or branch lines for local economic development. Private companies filled part of the gap but required government guarantees and land concessions. The relationship between public and private railway ownership would remain a major issue in later decades.

Section 56.6

Steamships, Maritime Commerce, and the Competition for Shipping Routes

For an archipelago, shipping mattered at least as much as railways. In the early Meiji years, foreign steamship companies, notably the American Pacific Mail Steamship Company and the British Peninsular and Oriental Company, dominated Japan's international routes and carried much of its coastal trade as well, while Japanese sailing vessels and a small number of steamers served domestic routes. The government regarded this dependence as both a financial drain and a strategic risk. In 1872 it supported the creation of a postal steamship company, and the need to carry troops during the 1874 Taiwan expedition (Chapter 60) exposed the weakness of its own fleet. The government purchased a number of steamers and entrusted them to the firm that most impressed its leaders, Mitsubishi.

Mitsubishi began as an Osaka shipping business run by Iwasaki Yatarō, a former low-ranking Tosa samurai who had managed the Tosa domain's trading operations. The firm took the name Mitsubishi in 1873. Following the Taiwan expedition, the government turned over thirteen ships to Mitsubishi in 1875 and paid it an annual subsidy of 250,000 yen in return for maintaining regular routes. With this backing Mitsubishi entered the Yokohama–Shanghai route, competing directly with Pacific Mail; the government arranged to purchase Pacific Mail's Japanese assets and the American firm withdrew from the route. Mitsubishi also faced British competition and prevailed through rate cutting and state support. It carried troops and supplies during the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, further strengthening its relationship with the government and its finances.

Mitsubishi's dominance provoked opposition. Critics, including officials and businessmen who resented its privileges and Iwasaki's close ties to Ōkuma Shigenobu, supported rival companies. After Ōkuma's fall from office in 1881, a government-backed rival, Kyōdō Unyu Kaisha, was formed in 1882 with participation from Mitsui interests and Shibusawa Eiichi. A fierce rate war followed, damaging both companies. In 1885, shortly after Iwasaki Yatarō's death, the two merged to form Nippon Yūsen Kaisha (NYK), which became Japan's largest shipping line, while a second major firm, Osaka Shōsen Kaisha, served mainly western Japanese routes. NYK expanded to long-distance lines, including a Bombay route opened in 1893 that carried raw cotton for the spinning industry, and later to Europe. Mitsubishi, no longer a shipping company, redirected its energies elsewhere, a story told in Section 56.21.

The growth of Japanese shipping helped retain freight and insurance earnings within the country and reduced dependence on foreign carriers. It also reshaped ports: Yokohama and Kobe grew as international gateways, Nagasaki as a coaling and shipbuilding center, and smaller ports as terminals for coastal lines. Subsidies, however, meant that the interests of the state and of particular firms were closely intertwined, a pattern repeated in other industries.

Section 56.7

Telegraphs, Postal Networks, Roads, and Commercial Communication

Information moved more rapidly in the new economy. A telegraph line between Tokyo and Yokohama opened in 1869; the network was extended to Osaka, Kyoto, and Nagasaki in the early 1870s and to much of the country over the next decade, with government departments the principal users at first. Submarine cable connections to the Asian mainland, laid by foreign companies, linked Nagasaki to Shanghai and Vladivostok in the early 1870s, joining Japan to the international telegraph system. Telegraph rates were high, so early users were officials, newspapers, and merchants who needed price information, and the effect was to narrow price differences between major markets.

The modern postal service was begun in 1871 under Maejima Hisoka, a Tokugawa-era official who drew on British models, and quickly replaced the older system of private couriers and post stations. A uniform national rate was introduced in 1873, and Japan joined the Universal Postal Union in 1877. The postal system carried letters and, increasingly, newspapers and parcels; from 1875 the government also offered postal savings accounts, which collected small deposits from households that did not use commercial banks. Telephone service began in the 1890s, first between Tokyo and Yokohama. These services depended on literacy and on the willingness of local communities to host offices, so access spread unevenly.

Roads and local transport changed more slowly. Prefectures and local communities built and maintained roads, and the carts, rickshaws (which appeared around 1870), and horse-drawn vehicles of the early Meiji years supplemented rather than replaced porters, pack animals, and river boats. The former post-station and courier networks of the Tokugawa period were reorganized into transport companies, and many station towns lost trade when railways bypassed them. The combined effect of these changes was to increase the speed and reliability of commercial communication without eliminating regional differences in cost and access.

Section 56.8

Economic Geography: Connecting Cities, Ports, and Rural Production

The new infrastructure reinforced an existing economic geography. Two great urban-port systems dominated: Tokyo and Yokohama in the Kantō, where the government, the army and navy, and the silk export trade were concentrated; and Osaka and Kobe in the Kansai, where commercial finance, textile manufacturing, and shipping were centered. Yokohama, 30 kilometers from Tokyo and the site of the largest foreign settlement, handled most raw silk exports. Kobe, opened in 1868, grew rapidly as a rival gateway for the west. Nagasaki, once Japan's only window to Europe, remained important for coal and shipbuilding but lost relative standing. Between them, the Tōkaidō corridor became the spine of the industrial economy.

Beyond these cores lay specialized production zones. The upland districts of Nagano, Gunma, Yamanashi, and Fukushima produced silk cocoons and, increasingly, reeled silk. Osaka and its hinterland concentrated cotton spinning. Coal came from northern Kyushu, including the Chikuhō fields and Miike, and from Hokkaido; copper came from Besshi in Shikoku, Ashio in Tochigi, and Kosaka in Akita. The northeast (Tōhoku) supplied rice, labor, and raw materials but attracted little manufacturing. Hokkaido supplied coal, fish, and timber to Honshu markets, while Okinawa supplied sugar. These patterns were not new, but railways, shipping, and telegraphs sharpened specialization and widened the gap between regions that gained manufacturing and those that supplied raw materials and migrants.

Market integration reduced price differences among regions and made local economies more sensitive to national and international conditions. A fall in the price of raw silk in New York or Lyon, or a financial contraction in Tokyo, could now reach upland farm households within weeks. This sensitivity was an opportunity for those who could exploit it and a vulnerability for those who could not, especially debt-burdened tenant and small-owner farmers. Regional inequality was thus not an accidental by-product of growth but one of its features.

Section 56.9 · Division III — Factories, Industries, and the Transformation of Work

Tomioka Silk Mill and the State-Sponsored Model Factory

The Tomioka Silk Mill, in Gunma Prefecture, was the Meiji government's flagship demonstration of mechanical silk reeling. Raw silk was Japan's most valuable export, but foreign buyers complained about the uneven quality of hand-reeled thread, and the government hoped to raise standards and exports by introducing European methods. It hired the French engineer Paul Brunat, who had worked in the silk trade at Yokohama, to plan and direct the mill. Construction began in 1871 in a region with abundant cocoons, water, and coal. The main buildings combined European-style brick walls, made by local brickmakers following French instruction, with a timber frame of Japanese construction, a hybrid of imported design and local craft. The mill opened in 1872 with several hundred reeling basins, steam power, and French-built machinery. In the early twenty-first century, the complex was inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage site for its role in the transfer of silk-reeling technology.

Recruiting workers proved difficult at first. Rumors that foreigners drank the workers' blood, a misunderstanding probably arising from the French staff's wine, discouraged local families from sending daughters. The government turned to former samurai households, whose daughters were expected to learn the new methods and return home to teach them. Among the best-known early workers was Wada Ei of the former Matsushiro domain in Nagano, whose later recollections remain one of the few surviving first-hand accounts by a mill worker. Her account, like most such recollections, was written years after the fact and presents the mill from the perspective of an educated young woman of samurai background, so it cannot stand for the experience of all workers.

The mill's immediate financial results were disappointing; it was costly to run and did not become a profitable model. Its larger effect was in training. Women who learned at Tomioka returned to their home districts, particularly in Nagano, to organize filatures and teach others, and the mill served as a reference point for quality. Hours and conditions at the mill in its early years are often described as more regulated than those later found in private mills, though historians caution that surviving evidence is limited and that the contrast should not be exaggerated. The mill was sold to Mitsui in 1893 and subsequently passed through other private owners, continuing production until 1987.

Tomioka illustrates the logic of state-sponsored enterprise: the government used its capital, foreign expertise, and authority to establish a technology that private actors would then adopt and adapt. It also illustrates the limits: the mill's own technology was not simply copied. Small Japanese filatures, as the next section shows, soon developed cheaper, locally built equipment better suited to their capital and labor conditions.

Section 56.10

Silk, Sericulture, Rural Households, and Export Markets

Raw silk was the commodity on which early Meiji industrialization depended. In most years of the 1870s and 1880s it accounted for something like a third to a half of Japan's export earnings, paying for imported machinery, raw cotton, and military equipment. Its importance rested on international demand: European silk production had been struck by silkworm disease in the 1860s, and growing American and European markets sought Japanese thread. The treaty ports channeled this trade. At first foreign merchants in Yokohama controlled much of it, buying from Japanese dealers and arranging shipment; in the following decades, Japanese trading firms, with the help of banks such as the Yokohama Specie Bank (founded 1880) and trading companies such as Mitsui Bussan (1876), increasingly handled exports directly.

The silk economy rested on rural households. Farm families, usually under the direction of women, raised silkworms on mulberry leaves in spring and sometimes in autumn, producing cocoons alongside rice and other crops. Cocoons were sold to reelers, who drew the thread by hand (zaguri) at home, in small workshops, or increasingly in filatures with machine-assisted reeling. In districts such as Suwa in Nagano, entrepreneurs built inexpensive filatures using wooden frames and local steam or water power, employing girls and young women from nearby villages and from poorer mountain districts. The "Suwa style" of small, low-capital filature spread widely and produced most Japanese silk exports, in contrast to the state's capital-intensive model at Tomioka. In this way, Japan's most important industrial export depended on cheap female labor and on household farming, rather than on large-scale factory industrialization of the European type.

The links between agriculture and industry cut both ways. Silk income allowed many farm families to pay taxes in cash, buy fertilizer, and sometimes expand their land. But sericulture also exposed households to price swings in distant markets, to cocoon quality problems, and to credit dependence on merchants and moneylenders. The sharp fall in cocoon and silk prices during the deflation of the 1880s (Section 56.17) pushed many sericulture families into debt. Silk reeling also relied on a labor supply recruited from poorer districts: women from the mountains of Hida, in Gifu, crossed passes to work in filatures in Suwa and Okaya, a migration later remembered in literature and oral testimony.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Silk Household

In sericulture districts, the annual rhythm of the household was organized around the silkworm. During the weeks of the rearing season, women and children gathered mulberry leaves several times a day, kept the rearing rooms at the right temperature, and watched constantly for disease, sometimes moving out of their own sleeping space to give the worms room. The household's cash income depended on a few weeks of intense labor and on the price the cocoons fetched. Work did not stop with the sale of cocoons: some households reeled thread themselves, and daughters of the family often went to nearby filatures for several months of the year.

This household economy shaped family decisions. A daughter's wage could pay a debt or tax installment, and parents sometimes received advance payments from filature owners or brokers. Sources describing these arrangements come largely from officials, employers, and later reformers, and rarely from the households themselves, so historians must read them critically. What emerges is a picture in which household survival, the labor of women and girls, and the demands of an export industry were tightly bound together.

Section 56.11

Cotton Spinning, Mechanization, and the Emergence of Modern Factories

While silk was an export industry, cotton spinning developed as an import-substitution industry. After the opening of the ports, cheap machine-spun yarn from Britain and India displaced hand-spun thread, damaging rural spinners while supplying yarn to Japanese handloom weavers, whose cloth production in fact continued and, in many districts, grew on the basis of imported yarn. The question for policymakers and entrepreneurs was whether Japan could produce yarn itself. Early attempts included a Satsuma mill at Kagoshima using British machinery (1867) and government-sponsored mills in the 1870s. In the late 1870s the government ordered ten small mills of about 2,000 spindles from a British manufacturer and distributed them to private owners on easy credit. These mills were too small and too poorly located to be profitable.

The breakthrough came with the Osaka Spinning Company, planned in 1882 under the leadership of Shibusawa Eiichi, with capital from aristocratic investors and merchants, and a technical manager, Yamanobe Takeo, who had studied cotton manufacturing in Britain. Its mill began production in 1883 with about 10,500 spindles, far larger than earlier mills, powered by steam and sited in Osaka, with good access to ports, coal, and labor. It was profitable and soon adopted round-the-clock operation with two shifts of workers. Its success stimulated imitators; by the end of the 1880s, a number of large mills were operating around Osaka and elsewhere, and domestic machine-spun yarn began to exceed imports around 1890. Mechanical spindles increased from under 100,000 around 1880 to more than a million by 1900, rough figures that show the scale of expansion. Yarn exports, mostly to China and Korea, began in the 1890s and exceeded imports in 1897. Raw cotton, however, was imported from India, China, and the United States, tying the industry to the shipping and trading networks of Section 56.6.

Mechanized spinning did not replace the rest of the textile sector. Weaving remained largely in small workshops and rural households into the 1890s, using imported yarn and cheap devices adapted from Western designs; the first successful Japanese power loom, from Toyoda Sakichi, appeared late in the decade. Machinery thus reinforced rather than abolished dualism between large modern factories and small traditional-style producers, a feature of the Japanese economy that scholars have long emphasized.

Factory spinning also created a new labor system. Mills depended on young women, recruited from rural districts, who worked long hours in shifts and lived in company dormitories, a system described in Section 56.14. This was part of what made yarn production profitable: low wages for a plentiful supply of rural female labor, combined with imported technology and the capital mobilized by joint-stock companies.

Section 56.12

Coal, Copper, Mining, and the Extraction of Industrial Resources

Coal powered factories, locomotives, and steamships, and it was also an export commodity, shipped through Nagasaki and other ports to Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Singapore. Production rose from perhaps a million tons around 1880 to several million by the 1890s and roughly seven million by 1900 (approximate figures). The principal fields were in northern Kyushu: Takashima, an island mine off Nagasaki first developed with Saga domain and foreign participation; Miike, which the government took over and developed with the help of Western engineers; and the Chikuhō fields. Hokkaido's Horonai coal supplied the Horonai railway and the northern market.

Government ownership of the major mines was temporary. Takashima was transferred to Gotō Shōjirō and then to Mitsubishi in 1881; Miike was sold at auction to Mitsui in 1888, for a price of about 4.5 million yen. The Sado gold and silver mine and the Ikuno silver mine, originally government-run, passed to the Imperial Household in 1889 and to Mitsubishi in 1896. Copper, important for exports and for electrical goods, was mined at Besshi (Sumitomo), Ashio (Furukawa Ichibei from 1877, with sharply expanded output from the mid-1880s), and Kosaka (Fujita group, from 1884). Japan became a leading copper exporter.

Mining relied on varied labor systems. Free miners worked underground alongside laborers hired by labor bosses, in conditions of danger from collapse, gas, and flooding. Convicts also supplied labor. At Miike, prisoners worked in the mines for decades; in Hokkaido, the government established prisons from 1881 whose inmates built roads and worked in mines, with high death rates. In these years, convict labor was understood by officials as economical and as a means of opening Hokkaido, and the labor system became a subject of later criticism. Chapter 63 returns to workers, unions, and state repression.

The environmental consequences also began in this period. Copper smelting at Ashio sent sulfurous fumes over nearby forests and wastes into the Watarase River, damaging fisheries and farmland downstream; protests by affected villagers began in the 1880s and became a national controversy in the 1890s. The Ashio conflict, with its leader Tanaka Shōzō, belongs to Chapter 63, but students should recognize here that industrial growth imposed costs on farming and fishing communities that had no say in decisions about mining.

Section 56.13

Shipyards, Military Arsenals, Machinery, and Heavy Industry

Heavy industry was the area in which government involvement remained strongest, because its principal customers were the army and navy. The Yokosuka shipyard, begun by the bakufu with French help, passed to the navy and became the Yokosuka Naval Arsenal; the navy added Kure in 1889. The army operated arsenals in Tokyo and Osaka for weapons and ammunition. These institutions imported machinery and foreign expertise and trained a generation of Japanese mechanics and engineers, many of whom later worked in private industry. Military demand also sustained production of steel and machine tools that private markets alone could not yet support.

Private shipbuilding and machinery firms grew from state and domain foundations. The Nagasaki shipyard, derived from the bakufu ironworks, was leased to Mitsubishi in 1884 and sold to it in 1887; the Hyōgo shipyard went to Kawasaki Shōzō in 1886, forming the base of Kawasaki's later company; Ishikawajima, begun by the Mito domain, became private in 1876. Smaller machine firms included the Tanaka Works, founded in 1875 by Tanaka Hisashige, a celebrated maker of mechanical dolls and clocks, which became part of the Mitsui-controlled Shibaura Engineering Works in the 1890s. Their origins show how traditional craft skill could be redirected to modern engineering.

Despite this progress, Japan remained heavily dependent on imports for iron, steel, rails, and machinery through the 1890s. Domestic iron production was small, and domestic steel smaller. The government therefore planned an integrated steelworks at Yawata in northern Kyushu, financed in part by the indemnity from the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 and built with German technology; it began operation in 1901 after early technical difficulties. Yawata marks the end of the period examined here and the beginning of a new phase in which war, empire, and heavy industry reinforced each other, a subject for Chapters 60 to 62 and beyond.

Section 56.14

Factory Workers, Women, Families, and the New Industrial Workplace

By about 1900, factories employed several hundred thousand people, and the majority were women, concentrated in silk reeling and cotton spinning. Most were young, often between their early teens and early twenties, and came from farming households; many left after a few years to marry. They were recruited through brokers, through relatives and neighbors, and by company agents who traveled to villages. Contracts, often negotiated with fathers rather than daughters, specified a term of service and sometimes included advances paid to the family, which tied workers to employers. Wages were low, and women's wages were substantially lower than men's. Women's earnings were an important component of rural household income, and in some districts contributed to tax and debt payments.

Workplace conditions were demanding. Silk filatures commonly required twelve to fourteen hours of work a day, sometimes more during peak periods; cotton mills ran in two shifts, including night work. Employers housed many workers in dormitories on factory grounds, with strict discipline, limited mobility, and communal meals. Fines for lateness or damage, supervision by overseers, and restrictions on leaving the grounds were common. Workers suffered from respiratory disease, notably tuberculosis, which spread through crowded dormitories and was carried back to villages. Child labor was widespread. Male workers, concentrated in shipyards, arsenals, mines, and machine shops, often worked through labor bosses (oyakata) who recruited, supervised, and paid gangs of workers and sometimes took a share of their wages, a system with roots in Tokugawa craft practice. Skilled male workers moved readily among employers, while factory managers sought to build more stable work forces.

Evidence for these conditions comes largely from government inquiries, journalistic investigations, and reformers' accounts, notably the government's later survey of factory conditions (1903) and Yokoyama Gennosuke's 1899 study of urban poverty and factory and mine labor, Nihon no kasō shakai. These sources were written by observers with their own agendas, and they emphasize hardship. Workers' own accounts are scarce, though some women later described their experience, and historians have used oral histories and local records to recover their perspectives. Not all workers suffered equally or reacted in the same way: some used factory work to gain skill, wages, and independence from the household; some resisted by leaving, bargaining with brokers, or protesting; and others had little choice. Chapter 63 considers organized labor action and state repression.

Everyday Life and Environment

The Factory Day and the Dormitory

A young woman entering a filature or spinning mill left the rhythm of the farm for the rhythm of the factory. Work began at a fixed signal and followed the pace of machines, whistles, and supervisors, not of the seasons. Meals were provided by the company, often simple fare of rice, barley, and pickles, and sleeping space was shared in crowded rooms. Time off was limited to a few days a month and festivals. Letters home, made possible by the new postal system, and visits during holidays maintained ties with families.

Women also formed friendships, learned reading and sometimes sewing or other skills, and in some cases, took pride in their craft and earnings. Recruiters and employers emphasized the moral guidance and training that dormitories offered, a claim that critics considered a justification for control. The evidence is contested, and no single account captures the range of experiences from Tomioka's early workers to the thousands entering mills in the 1890s.

Section 56.15 · Division IV — Money, Banks, and the Financing of Industrial Capitalism

The Yen, Currency Reform, and the Creation of a National Monetary System

Tokugawa Japan had operated under a complex monetary order. The bakufu issued gold coins (centered in the Edo region), silver by weight (centered in Osaka), and copper coins for small transactions, with fluctuating exchange rates among them. Domains issued paper notes (hansatsu) that circulated within their territories, in very large variety. The opening of the ports in 1859 exposed the system to foreign merchants and speculators, who exploited the difference between the Japanese and international gold–silver ratios and triggered a large outflow of gold, accompanied by debasement of coinage and inflation in the 1860s.

The new government's first response was to issue its own paper notes, beginning in 1868 with Dajōkan satsu, inconvertible notes backed by nothing but government credit. They circulated at a discount and added to confusion. After the abolition of the domains in 1871, the government assumed and redeemed domain notes at set rates, a step essential to national monetary unification. The New Currency Act of 1871 created the yen, divided decimally into 100 sen and 1,000 rin, and defined it in terms of gold, roughly equivalent to the U.S. dollar. Itō Hirobumi, who had studied American monetary institutions, helped shape the design. In practice, however, silver dominated trade in East Asia, and foreign "trade dollars" circulated in the ports, so the new system functioned in effect as bimetallic or silver-based for much of the following period; a silver yen was authorized for trade in 1878.

Monetary standardization mattered for taxation (the land tax was now paid in yen), for trade across domain lines, for banking, and for business accounting. It also reflected a broader aim: to establish a national economy in which a single money circulated under a single authority. But a unified currency did not by itself ensure stable money: the government soon issued additional inconvertible notes, and the monetary history of the next decade was dominated by inflation and its reversal.

Section 56.16

The National Banking System and the Expansion of Commercial Credit

Modern banks were new to Japan, though merchant houses had long operated as moneylenders and exchange dealers. The National Bank Act of 1872, modeled on the American system, authorized privately owned "national banks" (kokuritsu ginkō; "national" in the sense of chartered under national law) to issue notes convertible into gold, backed by government bonds. Shibusawa Eiichi, who had resigned from the Finance Ministry in 1873, organized the First National Bank (Dai-ichi Kokuritsu Ginkō) that year with capital from the Mitsui and Ono houses, and went on to promote hundreds of other enterprises. Few banks formed at first, because convertibility into gold was burdensome, and the government revised the law in 1876 to allow notes convertible into government paper, which was easier to meet. The number of national banks then grew rapidly, reaching 153 by 1879, at which point the legal limit was reached.

Alongside the national banks, private banks multiplied. Mitsui opened a private bank in 1876; Yasuda Zenjirō, a money changer who became a leading financier, established the Yasuda Bank in 1880; the Yokohama Specie Bank, founded in 1880 with government encouragement, specialized in foreign exchange and trade finance. Postal savings, begun in 1875, gathered small deposits. Banks collected deposits, made loans to merchants and landlords, discounted commercial bills, and bought government bonds, some of which had been issued to former samurai as compensation for stipends (Chapter 54), many of whom invested their bonds in banks. Banks thus linked the fortunes of the former samurai class with commercial credit.

The banking system had weaknesses. Banks lent heavily to their own directors and shareholders; many were thinly capitalized; and the proliferation of inconvertible bank notes added to inflation after 1877. Industrial financing remained limited: banks preferred short-term commercial lending, and manufacturers relied on retained earnings, merchant credit, and share capital raised from wealthy individuals. Shibusawa and others promoted the joint-stock company as a means of drawing capital from many investors, and the successes of the Osaka Spinning Company and Nippon Railway showed what the form could do. Even so, ownership was concentrated among a small group of wealthy individuals, families, and former daimyō and court nobles (who had received substantial compensation), and ordinary households had little access to the capital market.

Section 56.17

Inflation, the Matsukata Deflation, and the Costs of Financial Stabilization

The Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 (Chapter 54) cost the government far more than it could raise in taxes, and it financed the war by issuing large amounts of inconvertible paper money. The national banks, now allowed to issue notes not convertible into specie, added to the supply. Prices rose sharply: the price of rice approximately doubled between 1877 and 1880–1881. The value of paper money fell against silver, and by 1881 a silver yen traded at a large premium over the paper yen (about 1.7 paper yen to one silver yen at the peak, an approximate figure). For many farmers, inflation had a paradoxical effect: because the land tax was fixed in money, higher crop prices reduced its real burden, and many landowning farmers prospered. Merchants and speculators profited as well. Salaried officials, former samurai living on bonds, and urban consumers were hurt.

Inflation also worried the government. It raised the cost of imports and the army, destabilized revenue, and encouraged speculation. In 1881, after a political crisis that removed Ōkuma Shigenobu from government, Matsukata Masayoshi became Finance Minister and undertook a deflationary program. He ran budget surpluses by cutting spending (except on the military, which was expanding), raised taxes on sake, tobacco, and other goods, withdrew and burned paper notes, accumulated specie reserves, and prepared to establish a central bank. The program restored confidence and made convertibility possible, but contracted the money supply sharply.

Historical Vocabulary
Matsukata deflation (松方デフレ)

The Matsukata deflation refers to the sharp fall in prices and the contraction of money and credit that followed Finance Minister Matsukata Masayoshi's fiscal and monetary program from 1881 to about 1885. Its measures included budget surpluses, withdrawal of paper notes, new consumption taxes, and the creation of a central bank. Prices of rice, cocoons, and other farm products fell by roughly half in a few years.

The term is sometimes loosely applied to the whole program of "Matsukata finance," including measures that supported later growth. It should be understood as a policy-driven contraction with winners and losers: it stabilized government finance and the currency, but it raised the real burden of the fixed money land tax and of debts, and caused hardship in rural areas. Historians continue to debate how large its long-term benefits were relative to its social costs.

The consequences for rural society were severe. With crop prices falling by about half but land taxes and debts fixed in money, farm households found their obligations suddenly heavier. Many borrowed from moneylenders or landlords at high interest; those who could not repay lost land by foreclosure or sale. Tenancy increased, as did the concentration of land in the hands of landlords, who often also acted as moneylenders and sometimes as silk or rice merchants. Estimates of the number of households that lost land during the early 1880s vary and are uncertain, but contemporaries and historians agree that the number was large, in the hundreds of thousands. Sericulture families were especially hard hit by falling cocoon prices. Rural distress fed unrest, including the Chichibu Incident of 1884, a debtors' uprising in Saitama Prefecture, and contributed to the political radicalism examined in Chapter 57.

Not everyone lost. Deflation benefited holders of cash and government bonds, banks, and larger landowners who could buy land cheaply. After the contraction came a boom: from about 1886 interest rates fell, and capital flowed into railways, spinning, and other enterprises, an upsurge sometimes called the enterprise boom of the late 1880s. The "Matsukata finance" thus helped create financial conditions for private industry, but it also exposed the distributional cost of building a stable monetary order on the shoulders of rural debtors. A financial panic in 1890 showed that stability remained fragile.

Cause and Effect

Monetary Instability, Matsukata Finance, and Rural Hardship

Long-term causes

A fiscal system dependent on a fixed-money land tax; a government with large obligations (stipends, military, industry) and little revenue flexibility; a currency system not yet unified or backed by a central bank; and exposure to international trade and silver prices.

Short-term causes

The costs of the Satsuma Rebellion, the issue of inconvertible government and bank notes in 1877 and after, rising prices, loss of confidence in paper money, and political conflict in 1881 over fiscal policy.

Event or transformation

From 1881, Matsukata ran surpluses, raised consumption taxes, withdrew notes, built reserves, and in 1882 founded the Bank of Japan, shifting from inflation to deflation.

Immediate effects

Prices fell sharply; the real burden of land taxes and debts rose; many farmers lost land; tenancy grew; sericulture suffered; rural protest increased; and the paper currency regained value.

Long-term consequences

The yen was stabilized and a central bank established; capital became cheaper and flowed to private industry after 1886; landlordism and tenancy expanded; and rural discontent became a factor in political movements.

Historical complexity: Matsukata's policy was not the sole cause of rural distress, and rural distress was not the sole cause of political unrest. Falling international silver prices, harvests, local credit conditions, and the specific local mix of crops all mattered, and some regions and households prospered. The deflation was a policy choice, not a natural event, and its costs were distributed unequally.

Section 56.18

The Bank of Japan, Central Banking, and Modern Financial Institutions

The Bank of Japan was established in 1882 under Matsukata's direction, modeled in part on European central banks, notably the National Bank of Belgium. It opened in October 1882 and began issuing convertible banknotes in 1885, redeemable initially in silver. Its notes gradually displaced government and national-bank notes, but the older note issues continued to circulate until the end of 1899. The creation of the Bank of Japan therefore centralized the modern note-issuing system before the older currencies were fully retired. The Bank also served as the government's banker, lender of last resort to commercial banks, and holder of reserves. These changes gave Japan a convertible currency and the beginnings of a financial system with a central institution at its core.

The final step was the move to the gold standard. After the Sino-Japanese War, China paid a large indemnity under the Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895), largely in pounds sterling held in London. Using these funds as reserves, the government enacted a currency law in 1897 establishing the gold standard, with the yen defined at half its 1871 gold content. The change linked Japan to international gold-based finance, aided foreign borrowing and trade, and signaled to other powers that Japan was a "modern" economy. It also bound the economy to the discipline of international finance, a matter that would have large consequences in the twentieth century. Details of the war and indemnity belong to Chapter 60.

By the 1890s the financial system included the Bank of Japan, ordinary commercial banks (the national banks were reorganized as ordinary banks by about 1899), special banks such as the Yokohama Specie Bank and the Nippon Kangyō Bank, savings banks, and postal savings. The Nippon Kangyō Bank, established in 1897, was intended to provide longer-term financing for agriculture and industry. Zaibatsu houses such as Sumitomo (whose bank dates from 1895) and Mitsubishi (which opened a banking division in 1895) also expanded their banking activities. The system remained concentrated in large cities and, in practice, favored well-connected borrowers.

Section 56.19 · Division V — Privatization, the Zaibatsu, and Industrial Transformation

From State Enterprises to Private Ownership

By the late 1870s, the government's industrial enterprises were a financial burden. Many lost money, the Ministry of Public Works consumed large sums, and the fiscal strains of the 1877 rebellion and inflation called for retrenchment. In November 1880 the government issued regulations for disposing of its factories (kōjō haraisage gaisoku), and the policy was applied in the early 1880s and again in the following decade. The principal exceptions were enterprises considered essential for the military or national infrastructure: arsenals, naval yards, telegraphs and posts, and most trunk railways, though even railways saw large private participation. Yawata Steel, opened in 1901, was a new state enterprise.

Buyers were typically established merchants and entrepreneurs with political connections and capital. Mitsubishi acquired the Nagasaki shipyard (1887) and Takashima coal mine (1881), and later Sado and Ikuno (1896); Mitsui acquired Miike (1888) and Tomioka (1893); Kawasaki Shōzō bought the Hyōgo shipyard (1886); Asano Sōichirō took over the Fukagawa cement works (1884); Tanaka Chōbei took over the Kamaishi ironworks (1887). Prices were often below construction cost and payment could be spread over years, making the terms attractive. The 1881 scandal over the proposed sale of Hokkaido Development Commission assets to a company led by former Satsuma officials at very low prices provoked public protest, helped trigger the political crisis of 1881, and led the government to cancel the sale. Contemporaries called politically connected merchants who benefited from such arrangements seishō ("political merchants").

Privatization shifted the financial burden from the state, transferred technology and managers to private hands, and consolidated the position of a few large firms. Whether it was sound policy has been debated: critics emphasize favoritism and the concentration of economic power; defenders note the fiscal pressures and the benefits of transferring enterprises to experienced owners. The next debate box addresses the broader question of how to weigh the role of government in Japanese industrialization.

Historical Debate

Was Japanese Industrialization Primarily State-Led or Driven by Private Enterprise?

The question: Japan industrialized rapidly within a few decades. How much of this was due to government policy and state-owned enterprises, and how much to private entrepreneurs, merchants, and the existing commercial economy?

Interpretation A — The state as initiator: Some scholars emphasize the government's role in building infrastructure, importing technology, training workers and engineers, subsidizing shipping, and creating a financial system. On this view, the state absorbed the risk and cost of introducing industries that private investors would not have attempted, and then transferred the enterprises to private firms. Thomas C. Smith's classic study of government enterprise, for example, stressed the lasting legacy of state-sponsored experimentation even where the enterprises themselves were unprofitable.
Interpretation B — Private enterprise and the existing economy: Others stress the contribution of private entrepreneurs such as Shibusawa Eiichi, merchant houses, and rural producers, and the commercial and technical foundations of the Tokugawa economy. In silk reeling, cotton spinning, and weaving, private and rural producers adapted technology to local conditions, often more successfully than government models. Historians of this orientation emphasize that much industrial growth took place in small enterprises and households, not in state factories.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same records: budgets showing the scale of state spending, enterprise records showing the performance of state and private firms, biographies of entrepreneurs, and regional studies of silk and cotton. Both can point to evidence: the state built the railways and arsenals and trained many engineers, while the Suwa filatures and Osaka Spinning were private. Many scholars therefore speak of a mixed or interactive pattern in which state and private action shaped each other.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors shapes broader arguments about "late development," about whether Japan's experience offers a model for other countries, and about the relationship between government and business that persisted in later decades. It also affects how we assess who benefited: a state-centered account may emphasize national achievement, while an account of rural producers highlights the contributions and costs borne by households and workers.

Section 56.20

Mitsui and Sumitomo: Merchant Houses Become Industrial and Financial Groups

Mitsui and Sumitomo were both family enterprises with deep Tokugawa roots. The Mitsui house began as a drapery business in Edo and Kyoto in the late seventeenth century under Mitsui Takatoshi and became a money-exchange agent for the bakufu. In 1710 the family set up a coordinating body, the Ōmotokata, to manage its interests and bind the branches together under family rules. In the turbulent final years of the shogunate, Mitsui supported the new government with loans. After 1868 it opened a bank (1872 in an earlier form, and as the Mitsui Bank in 1876) and founded Mitsui Bussan in 1876, a trading company led by Masuda Takashi with links to Inoue Kaoru, which handled coal, rice, silk, cotton, and machinery. Mitsui acquired Miike in 1888 and Tomioka in 1893, took over the Shibaura Engineering Works, and expanded into textiles, with Nakamigawa Hikojirō, head of the Mitsui Bank from 1891, playing a central role in diversification into manufacturing. The Mitsui family, however, moved cautiously, and the large holding company structure came only later.

Sumitomo's origins lay in copper refining and mining in the Kyoto–Osaka area. It operated the Besshi copper mine in Shikoku from 1691, and the Izumiya business in Osaka exchanged and refined copper. At the Restoration the new government threatened to take over Besshi, but the mine's general manager, Hirose Saihei, negotiated its retention and then modernized it, engaging a French engineer, Louis Larroque, in 1873. Sumitomo gradually diversified into coal, copper rolling, and banking, founding the Sumitomo Bank in 1895. Its head, Sumitomo Kichizaemon Tomoito, born into the court noble Tokudaiji family and brother of the politician Saionji Kinmochi, linked the house to the elite, while Hirose and later managers ran operations.

Both houses benefited from existing capital, credit, and networks, but they also adapted to a new environment. They moved from ties with the bakufu and domains to ties with the new government and its officials. They learned to use joint-stock and partnership forms and to hire university-trained managers. At the same time, as the following box emphasizes, they should not be mistaken for unchanged survivals of the Tokugawa past.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Tokugawa Merchant Houses and Modern Corporate Capitalism

What changed

  • The principal customer shifted from the bakufu and domains to the national government and, increasingly, to the market
  • Firms entered banking, mining, manufacturing, and international trade, using steam power and imported technology
  • Legal forms evolved, including joint-stock companies and partnerships, and professional salaried managers became central
  • Scale and diversification increased, laying groundwork for later conglomerates

What remained continuous

  • Family ownership and control, and family rules governing succession and conduct
  • Close relations with political authorities as a source of contracts and protection
  • Commercial and financial skills, networks, and reputations accumulated over generations
  • The importance of trusted managers (bantō in Tokugawa practice, salaried executives later)

Who experienced change most directly: Managers and employees experienced the most visible shift, from apprentice-style service in household firms to salaried work in large organizations, while workers in mines and mills faced new industrial discipline. Who experienced greater continuity: The owning families themselves retained control and status, and many small merchants and artisans continued familiar ways of business.

Why the pattern matters: The history of these firms shows that industrial capitalism in Japan was built on pre-existing institutions, but also that the later conglomerate form was a Meiji and post-Meiji creation, not a Tokugawa inheritance. Many Tokugawa merchant houses, including the Ono and Shimada houses, failed in the early Meiji years, so continuity was the exception and not the rule.

Section 56.21

Mitsubishi, Yasuda, and the Emergence of the Zaibatsu

Mitsubishi had a different origin. Iwasaki Yatarō, from a low-ranking Tosa family, used his role in managing the Tosa domain's trading operations to found a shipping business that became Mitsubishi in 1873. With government ships and subsidies (Section 56.6), it dominated Japanese shipping in the 1870s, and Iwasaki's ties with Ōkuma Shigenobu helped him. After the political and commercial reversals of 1881 to 1885 and Iwasaki's death in 1885, the shipping business merged into NYK, and Mitsubishi, led by Yatarō's brother Iwasaki Yanosuke, redirected its capital toward mining, shipbuilding, and real estate. It owned Takashima, bought the Nagasaki shipyard in 1887, purchased the Marunouchi district of Tokyo in 1890 (later redeveloped as a business district), acquired Sado and Ikuno in 1896, and established a banking department in 1895. In 1893 it reorganized as a family partnership under Yatarō's son Iwasaki Hisaya and Yanosuke.

Yasuda Zenjirō, from Toyama, began as a money changer in Edo in the 1860s and built a fortune in the early Meiji period by dealing in government notes and bonds. He founded the Yasuda Bank in 1880 and became a major financier, with relatively little involvement in manufacturing. Other prominent business families emerged from the same environment: Furukawa in copper, Asano in cement, Kawasaki in shipbuilding, Fujita and Ōkura in mining and trading, many with government connections.

The term "zaibatsu" came into common use around the early twentieth century, and historians apply it, often retrospectively, to large, family-controlled groups with diverse holdings in finance, trade, and industry. The great zaibatsu were not identical. Mitsui was a trading and financial group with Tokugawa roots and a conservative family structure. Sumitomo centered on copper and, later, banking. Mitsubishi was a Meiji-era creation built on shipping and state favor, then on mining, shipbuilding, and land. Yasuda was primarily a financial group. By 1900 none had reached the form of a fully integrated holding company with a pyramid of subsidiaries, which developed in the early twentieth century and is described in later chapters.

Historical Vocabulary
Zaibatsu (財閥)

Zaibatsu, literally "financial clique" or "wealth group," refers to large family-controlled business groups with interests in banking, trade, mining, manufacturing, and shipping. The term became widespread in the early twentieth century; in the early Meiji period contemporaries more often spoke of seishō, "political merchants," to describe firms that thrived on government connections.

Historians use "zaibatsu" most precisely for the integrated holding-company groups of the 1910s to 1940s, particularly Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, and Yasuda. Applying the term to the 1870s and 1880s risks anachronism, because the firms' structures were then simpler and more varied. The term also carries political connotations of concentration of economic power, which later reformers, including occupation authorities in the 1940s, sought to dismantle (Chapter 89).

Taken together, the rise of these groups was neither inevitable nor simply a product of state favoritism. Their growth depended on political connections, access to capital and credit, the acquisition of state-built assets, family control that allowed long-term planning, and the ability to hire able managers. It also depended on the context: the disposal of state enterprises, the stabilization of the currency, the pro-business environment of the late 1880s boom, and later, war and empire. The benefits of growth were uneven. Concentration of ownership and control produced great wealth for a few families while farmers and workers bore much of the cost, and the relationship among business, politics, and the state remained a source of criticism throughout the following decades.

Section 56.22

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the creation of Japanese industrial capitalism from 1868 to about 1900. Before moving on, students should be able to explain the following core insights.

Industrial development rested on prior Tokugawa foundations, including commercial networks, merchant houses, rural manufacturing, and technical skills, and was built in a context of unequal treaties, limited tariff protection, and military insecurity. The government pursued fukoku kyōhei and shokusan kōgyō by building railways, telegraphs, and postal services; subsidizing shipping; hiring foreign experts; and operating model enterprises, financed principally by the land tax and limited foreign borrowing. Silk, produced by rural households and filatures and sold abroad, paid for imports; cotton spinning, led by the Osaka Spinning Company, replaced imported yarn; mining and heavy industry expanded with state and private participation. Factory workers, most of them young women, faced long hours, dormitory discipline, and health risks, while miners included convicts, and mining pollution began to damage farms. Currency reform, national banks, and the Bank of Japan created a monetary system; inflation in the 1870s and the Matsukata deflation of the 1880s redistributed costs and gains and caused rural hardship. The sale of state enterprises, mostly in the 1880s and 1890s, helped build the groups later called zaibatsu, whose origins differed and whose fully developed forms came later.

Students should now be able to explain why this history cannot be reduced to government planning or to private initiative alone, why growth and hardship were linked, and why regions such as Hokkaido, Okinawa, and the silk districts experienced the new economy differently. They should also be able to identify the questions that later chapters will take up: the political mobilization of rural and urban discontent (Chapter 57), the cultural responses to modernization (Chapter 58), the constitutional order (Chapter 59), the war and empire that transformed industry (Chapters 60 to 62), and the organized labor and social conflict of the industrial age (Chapter 63).

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Japan's industrial transformation between 1868 and about 1900 was the product of interaction among state investment, inherited Tokugawa commercial institutions, private entrepreneurship, foreign technology, rural household labor, and financial restructuring. It was neither a sudden Western import nor solely a government achievement, and it carried real costs for farmers, workers, and communities whose resources were drawn into the new economy.

The starting point was a commercialized Tokugawa economy with great merchant houses, regional specialization, rural by-employment, and skilled craftsmen, but without mechanized industry, a unified currency, or modern banks. The Meiji government, concerned about military security, the treaty system, and the drain of specie, adopted the goals of fukoku kyōhei and shokusan kōgyō. Its means were limited by dependence on the land tax and by a deliberate decision to avoid heavy foreign debt.

The Ministry of Public Works, foreign employees, and model enterprises introduced technology and trained Japanese engineers and managers. The Shimbashi–Yokohama railway of 1872 began a transport revolution that, with steamships, telegraphs, and a national postal service, integrated markets and sharpened regional specialization. Mitsubishi's rise through government ships and subsidies, and the later formation of NYK, showed how state and private interests could become entwined.

In manufacturing, Tomioka demonstrated state-sponsored technology transfer but was not itself a commercial model; small private filatures in districts like Suwa, relying on household sericulture and cheap female labor, produced most silk exports. Cotton spinning, led by the Osaka Spinning Company, became the first large-scale private factory industry. Mining and heavy industry grew with state and private participation, using free and convict labor and imposing environmental costs, while military demand sustained shipyards, arsenals, and eventually the Yawata Steel Works.

Factory work was shaped by recruitment through families and brokers, dormitories, long hours, and low wages, especially for women. Evidence comes mainly from officials, employers, and reformers, and workers' own voices are scarce. Households benefited from wages but also bore risks, and factory conditions would become the subject of the organized labor conflicts examined in Chapter 63.

Monetary and financial institutions developed through the yen of 1871, national banks of 1872, the Bank of Japan of 1882, and the gold standard of 1897. Inflation after 1877 eased the real burden of the land tax, but the Matsukata deflation of the 1880s reversed that effect, stabilized the currency, and imposed heavy hardship on rural debtors, contributing to tenancy growth and unrest while helping create conditions for the enterprise boom after 1886.

The sale of state enterprises concentrated assets in a few politically connected firms. Mitsui and Sumitomo, with Tokugawa origins, and Mitsubishi and Yasuda, products of the Meiji era, followed different paths and should not be treated as identical or as already fully developed conglomerates in the 1870s. The term zaibatsu is best understood as a later and partly retrospective label.

Regional perspectives complicate the story. Hokkaido's development displaced Ainu communities and used convict labor; Okinawa was drawn into the Japanese market as a sugar supplier; upland silk districts prospered and suffered with export prices; and the Tōhoku and other regions supplied labor and raw materials with little manufacturing. Wider connections, including British, French, American, and German engineers and capital, markets in the United States and Europe, and trade with China and Korea, shaped Japan's industrial path in many directions.

The historical significance of these developments is twofold. They established the institutions, firms, and patterns of state–business relations that shaped Japan's economy through the era of empire and war, and they created social tensions, among them tenancy, urban labor, regional inequality, and environmental damage, that fueled political and social conflict in the following decades. Understanding this early industrial capitalism is essential for understanding the Japan of the twentieth century.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political revolution became an economic one. The Meiji leaders had abolished the domains and redefined social status; in the decades that followed, railways, factories, banks, and business groups gave the new state material substance. The institutions created in these years, including the yen, the Bank of Japan, the railway network, and the large firms, shaped Japanese economic life long after the leaders who built them were gone.

The chapter also reveals how development worked: unevenly, through interaction among state, market, and household, and at differing costs to different people. The silk filatures of Suwa, the dormitories of Osaka's mills, the mines of Miike, the farms of debt-ridden villages, and the fisheries and forests of Hokkaido all belonged to the same process but were experienced in radically different ways. Treating any single story as representative would distort the history.

Many of the problems introduced here carried forward. Rural distress and tenancy fed the political movements of Chapter 57; the concentration of economic power and the closeness of business and government shaped the politics of the constitutional era; labor conditions and pollution led to the conflicts of Chapter 63; and the combination of heavy industry, military demand, and imperial ambition links this chapter to the wars and colonial expansion that followed. Japan's economic ties to Korea, China, Taiwan, and the wider world also began to deepen in these years.

Students who understand early Japanese industrial capitalism as the outcome of choices made by officials, entrepreneurs, farmers, and workers within constraints they did not fully control are better prepared for the central questions of the chapters ahead: how a state built on these foundations came to govern a constitutional order, expand an empire, and confront the consequences of both.
