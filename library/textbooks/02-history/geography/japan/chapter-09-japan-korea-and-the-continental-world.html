Part II — Yamato and the Formation of Ancient Japan
9

Japan, Korea, and the Continental World

Japanese History: From Jōmon Japan to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A ship crosses the strait between the Korean Peninsula and northern Kyushu carrying more than cargo. Aboard are people: a scribe trained to write in the classical Chinese used across the region's courts, a smith who knows how to work iron into blades and fittings no local workshop can yet reproduce, perhaps a diplomatic envoy bearing a message from a Baekje king to a Yamato ruler, perhaps a family fleeing the collapse of a border fortress after a Goguryeo campaign. None of these people thinks of themselves as "foreign advisers" bringing "civilization" to a passive archipelago. They are participants in a world that already spans the sea.

Chapter 7 traced the emergence of powerful regional chiefs visible in the archaeology of monumental keyhole tombs. Chapter 8 showed how a network of alliances, ritual authority, and household organization around the Yamato rulers of the Kinai region gradually took the shape of a recognizable political order — a polity, not yet a fully centralized state, held together by kinship, prestige goods, and negotiated loyalty among regional elites. This chapter asks a question that the previous two chapters have already implied at every turn: where did the prestige goods, the specialized craftsmen, the horses, the iron, and eventually the writing itself that underwrote Yamato political power actually come from, and through what relationships did they travel?

The answer, this chapter argues, is that the Yamato polity did not develop in isolation and then later "open" to continental contact. It developed from the start as one node within an interconnected East Asian world of diplomacy, warfare, migration, and exchange that linked the Japanese archipelago to the Korean Peninsula and, through the peninsula and by direct sea routes, to China. The peninsula in this period was not a single country called "Korea" in any modern sense. It was a shifting arrangement of distinct and often rival kingdoms and confederacies — Goguryeo in the north, Baekje in the southwest, Silla in the southeast, and the smaller Gaya polities clustered along the southern coast nearest the archipelago — each pursuing its own strategic interests, each maintaining its own relationships with Chinese dynasties to the west, and each maintaining its own, frequently shifting relationship with the polities of the Japanese islands that contemporary continental sources called Wa.

This chapter also confronts, directly and carefully, one of the most historiographically fraught questions in the study of early Japanese-Korean relations: the old claim, rooted in the eighth-century Japanese chronicle the Nihon Shoki and later amplified by modern nationalist and colonial-era scholarship, that Yamato maintained direct territorial rule over a colonial administrative outpost in southern Korea. This claim centers on Mimana, the Japanese reading (Imna in Korean) of a historical geographical and political designation, 任那, whose precise referent and relationship to particular Gaya polities remain debated among historians, and, more specifically, on a supposed institution the Nihon Shoki associates with the name, later scholarship's "Mimana Nihon-fu" (Imna Ilbon-bu) colonial-administration theory. This claim was used, particularly in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, to help justify Japan's colonization of Korea by suggesting ancient precedent. Modern historians — Japanese, Korean, and international alike — have subjected this claim to intense scrutiny using archaeology, contemporary Korean and Chinese sources, and careful reading of the *Nihon Shoki* itself, and the great majority no longer accept it in its older form. This does not mean that Wa forces never intervened militarily on the peninsula; the evidence suggests they very much did. It means historians must distinguish military intervention, alliance, and influence from permanent territorial colonization, a distinction this chapter takes as one of its central historical-methods lessons.

Migration is the human center of this chapter. People moved across the Korea Strait in both directions throughout this period, but the archaeological, genealogical, and textual record makes clear that a substantial and consequential movement of people came from the peninsula into the archipelago — not as a single event, but as a recurring pattern shaped by warfare, political collapse, and opportunity on the peninsula, and by the demand for skilled specialists at the Yamato court and among regional elites. Modern historians commonly use the term toraijin ("people who came across") as a broad historiographical label for these migrants and their descendants; whatever term is used, they did not remain foreign guests. Many became farmers, artisans, scribes, ritual specialists, military officers, and, in some documented cases, ancestors of prominent court lineages. Their knowledge — of ironworking, ceramics, weaving, construction, horse breeding and equipment, and, above all, writing — became part of the archipelago's own developing political and material culture.

Writing deserves particular attention here, because it is often misunderstood. Chinese-written objects and character-bearing artifacts had already reached the archipelago well before this period, so this chapter's subject is not the first appearance of Chinese characters but rather the fifth and sixth centuries' sustained, court-centered use of writing, carried by literate migrants and by diplomatic and administrative necessity, becoming substantially more visible and politically consequential than before. This did not mean the immediate development of "Japanese literature" or even widespread literacy. For a long transitional period, Chinese characters were used mainly to write in Chinese, the shared diplomatic and administrative language of the region, for purposes such as royal titles, genealogies, diplomatic correspondence, and inscriptions — including the very sword inscriptions discussed in Chapter 8, though even these Sinitic-convention inscriptions already used characters to render some local names and titles. Only gradually, over a much longer span of time extending well beyond this chapter's chronological scope, did fuller systems develop for using Chinese characters to record the Japanese language itself, a process in which later phonographic conventions such as *man'yōgana* eventually contributed to the emergence of kana. Understanding this distinction matters for avoiding an anachronistic picture of early Yamato literacy.

Finally, this chapter is about legitimacy as much as it is about technology or migration. Yamato rulers and the regional elites who supported or rivaled them did not seek continental titles, imported goods, literate administrators, and diplomatic recognition from Chinese dynasties merely out of curiosity or admiration. These things carried real political weight at home. A ruler who could claim recognition from a Chinese emperor, who could field cavalry equipped with continental-style horse gear, who could commission a written record of ancestry and achievement, or who could host and reward a respected foreign specialist gained real advantages in the ongoing competition for authority among Kinai-region elites described in Chapter 8. Continental exchange, in other words, was not something that happened "abroad" and separately from Yamato's internal politics. It was one of the primary arenas in which that internal politics was conducted.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the fifth and sixth centuries not as a story of Japan gradually "catching up" with a more advanced continental civilization, but as a story of an emerging archipelagic polity actively operating within, shaping, and being shaped by a genuinely international East Asian world — one whose most consequential development, the arrival of Buddhism through these same networks, will open the next chapter and set the stage for a major domestic political and religious crisis at the Yamato court.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how sea routes across the Korea Strait connected the Japanese archipelago to the Korean Peninsula and, indirectly, to China.
  2. Distinguish Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and the Gaya polities as separate and often rival political powers on a peninsula that was not unified in this period.
  3. Trace the shifting pattern of alliance, rivalry, and diplomacy connecting Wa/Yamato to these peninsular kingdoms.
  4. Evaluate the older "Mimana" colonial model of Yamato rule in southern Korea against archaeological, Korean, Chinese, and Japanese textual evidence.
  5. Describe the migration of peninsular peoples into the archipelago and their social roles as farmers, craftspeople, scribes, and elites rather than as anonymous "advisers."
  6. Analyze the transfer of specialist technologies — ironworking, equestrian equipment, Sue ware ceramics, weaving, and construction techniques — as processes carried by skilled people.
  7. Explain how sustained, court-centered use of Chinese writing became more visible in this period and clarify the distinction between writing Chinese and later systems for writing Japanese.
  8. Connect literacy and record-keeping to the growing organizational capacity of the Yamato court introduced in Chapter 8.
  9. Assess why continental titles, goods, and alliances mattered for domestic political legitimacy among Yamato elites.
  10. Avoid projecting modern national boundaries, ethnic categories, or later imperial claims backward onto the fluid political geography of the fifth and sixth centuries.
  11. Critically read a contested primary source, such as the Gwanggaeto Stele, whose claims cannot be taken at face value.
  12. Anticipate how continental networks introduced in this chapter will carry Buddhism into the archipelago in Chapter 10.

Historical Context

Chapter 7 established, through the archaeology of keyhole tombs, grave goods, and mounted-warfare equipment, that powerful regional chiefs had emerged across much of the archipelago by the fourth and fifth centuries, competing and cooperating within widening networks of alliance. Chapter 8 traced how one cluster of elites based in the Kinai region — the emerging Yamato rulers — built a political order out of these networks through kinship ties, ritual authority, negotiated loyalty from regional chieftains, and the organization of specialized labor groups known as *be* and dependent households known broadly by terms including *hito*. That chapter deliberately left open a question it could not fully answer on its own: where did the imported prestige goods, the horses and equestrian gear, the advanced ironworking, and the earliest inscribed objects discussed there actually come from, and through what human relationships did they arrive?

This chapter answers that question by turning outward, to the Korean Peninsula and, through it, to China. Readers should resist two opposite temptations. The first is to imagine the archipelago as an isolated, self-contained world that only later "opened up" to continental influence — a picture this and earlier chapters have already shown to be false, given the evidence of continental contact reaching back through the Yayoi period discussed in Chapter 5. The second is to imagine the archipelago as a passive recipient of a single, one-directional flow of "civilization" from a culturally superior continental source, with Korea functioning merely as a conduit for Chinese culture. Both pictures collapse a genuinely complex, multidirectional, and politically contested set of relationships into an oversimplified story. Baekje, Silla, Gaya, and Goguryeo were not simply transmission belts for Chinese civilization; they were independent political actors with their own strategic interests, their own relationships with Chinese dynasties, and their own reasons for cultivating or resisting relationships with Wa.

Students should also understand the limits of the available evidence. This period is illuminated by several kinds of sources that must be read against one another rather than trusted individually: Korean chronicles compiled centuries later, most notably the twelfth-century Samguk sagi; Chinese dynastic histories, which recorded relations with Wa and with the peninsular kingdoms from a Chinese court's perspective and interests; a small number of contemporary inscriptions, most importantly the Gwanggaeto Stele examined later in this chapter; and the eighth-century Japanese chronicle the Nihon Shoki, compiled long after the events it describes and shaped by the political interests of the Yamato court at the time of its compilation. Archaeology — tomb goods, ceramics, ironwork, and settlement patterns on both sides of the strait — provides an independent check on all of these textual traditions, though archaeological similarity does not by itself prove migration, conquest, or political control, and must be interpreted cautiously.

A kingdom in the southwestern Korean Peninsula, generally Yamato's closest and most consistent diplomatic and cultural partner in this period, though the relationship shifted with circumstances and was never simply one-directional.

Section 9.1

The Archipelago in an East Asian World

It has become almost a reflex in popular writing to describe premodern Japan as an isolated island nation that periodically "opened" to the outside world — at the introduction of Buddhism, at the arrival of European traders in the sixteenth century, or at the end of Tokugawa seclusion in the nineteenth. This chapter should permanently dispel that reflex for the ancient period. The Japanese archipelago's most politically consequential region, the Kinai basin where Yamato authority was consolidating, lay at the end of a maritime corridor that had connected the islands to the Asian continent for millennia, as Chapters 1 and 5 have already shown for the Jōmon and Yayoi periods. In the fourth through sixth centuries, that corridor was busier, more politically charged, and more consequential for the internal development of Yamato power than at almost any earlier point in the archipelago's history.

The geography of this corridor deserves careful attention. The Korea Strait separating the southern Korean Peninsula from northern Kyushu is broken by the islands of Tsushima and Iki, which functioned as stepping stones reducing the longest single open-water crossing to a manageable distance for the watercraft available in this period. From northern Kyushu, the Seto Inland Sea provided a relatively sheltered corridor of onward travel toward the Kinai region, allowing goods, people, and diplomatic missions to move between the peninsula and the emerging center of Yamato power without extended open-ocean voyages. This route explains why northern Kyushu ports and the polities that controlled them held such strategic importance throughout this period, and why regional elites along this corridor — not only the Kinai-based Yamato rulers themselves — played active roles in continental relations.

It is essential to understand that this maritime corridor carried traffic in multiple directions and for multiple purposes simultaneously. Diplomatic envoys carried messages, gifts, and requests for military assistance between Wa rulers and peninsular kings. Merchants and craft specialists moved in search of markets, patronage, and opportunity. Migrants moved to escape warfare, political upheaval, or simply to seek better circumstances, sometimes as individuals or families and sometimes as larger groups following political or military disruption. Military forces moved when Wa rulers or regional Kyushu elites intervened, invited or otherwise, in peninsular conflicts. No single explanatory model — trade, diplomacy, migration, or warfare — captures the full character of this traffic; all four operated together, often through the very same relationships and even the very same individuals.

Understanding the archipelago's position within this East Asian world requires abandoning modern national frameworks. There was no "Japan" and no "Korea" in the sense of modern nation-states with fixed borders, unified governments, and singular national identities. There was a shifting, competitive, multipolar landscape of kingdoms, confederacies, and regional elites on both sides of the strait, linked by relationships of alliance, rivalry, trade, and kinship that cut across what would later become national boundaries. This chapter's task is to describe that landscape honestly, without importing later political categories that did not yet exist.

Section 9.2

The Korean Peninsula in the Fourth–Sixth Centuries

Students need a clear political map of the peninsula before they can make sense of Wa's relationships with it. By the fourth century, three major kingdoms had emerged from the peninsula's earlier landscape of smaller polities and confederacies: Goguryeo in the north, extending well into what is now Manchuria; Baekje in the southwest, centered initially near the Han River and later, after military defeat, farther south; and Silla in the southeast. Along the southern coast nearest the Japanese archipelago, a cluster of smaller, politically independent polities collectively known as Gaya (or Kaya) had not consolidated into a single kingdom and remained comparatively decentralized throughout most of this period, making them simultaneously attractive trading and diplomatic partners for Wa and vulnerable to pressure from their larger neighbors.

These four names — Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya — should not be treated as interchangeable labels for a generic "ancient Korea." Each had its own royal lineage, its own relationship with Chinese dynasties, its own military capacity, and its own foreign policy, including its own, often quite different, relationship with Wa. Goguryeo, the most powerful of the three kingdoms militarily, generally viewed Baekje as its principal peninsular rival and repeatedly campaigned against it, with major consequences (discussed below) for Baekje's relationship with Wa. Baekje, seeking counterweight against Goguryeo's power, cultivated close and generally durable ties with Wa, including exchanges of envoys, gifts, and eventually scholars and religious specialists. Silla's relationship with Wa fluctuated between cooperation and conflict, and Silla's own expansion at Gaya's expense eventually became one of the developments that ended Wa's most direct sphere of activity on the peninsula. Gaya, lacking the unified political structure of its neighbors, depended on relationships with multiple outside powers, including Wa, for both trade and security, a dependency that made it central to this chapter's most contested historical question, examined in Section 9.4.

All of these kingdoms, in turn, maintained their own relationships with Chinese dynasties, seeking recognition, trade, and, at times, military alignment against peninsular rivals. Chinese dynastic courts, for their part, viewed the peninsular kingdoms and Wa alike as tributary or semi-tributary polities whose rulers might be granted titles and diplomatic recognition in exchange for acknowledging, at least formally, the superior status of the Chinese emperor. This system of titles and tribute, examined further in Section 9.9, gave every polity in the region — Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, Gaya's leading families, and Wa rulers alike — an additional external audience whose recognition carried real domestic political value.

Section 9.3

Wa, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya: Diplomacy and Alliance

Rather than picturing fixed alliance blocs, students should picture a shifting diplomatic landscape in which relationships responded to immediate strategic circumstances. Baekje generally emerges from the evidence, including both the Nihon Shoki and later Korean chronicles, as Wa's most consistent and cultivated continental partner across this period. An actual surviving artifact, the Seven-Branched Sword (Chiljido in Korean, Nanatsusaya no Tachi in Japanese) held today at Isonokami Shrine, provides physical evidence of the kind of prestige-object diplomacy that cemented such relationships: its own damaged inscription bears a date commonly read as Eastern Jin Taihe 4, corresponding to 369 CE. The Nihon Shoki separately records the presentation of a seven-branched sword under the reign of Jingū, in a chronicle year nominally corresponding to 252. Historians have often connected the surviving artifact with the chronicle's account, but because the inscriptional date and the chronicle's nominal date do not match, this identification should be treated cautiously rather than as self-proving; what the sword itself independently establishes is a genuine relationship of gift exchange between Baekje and Wa in roughly the fourth century.

Relations with Silla present a more mixed picture. Wa forces and Silla forces are recorded in conflict at various points in this period, including campaigns referenced on the Gwanggaeto Stele examined later in this chapter. At other points, exchange and even cooperation are documented. Students should resist collapsing this relationship into a simple story of permanent hostility; like Baekje's relationship with Wa, Silla's relationship shifted according to the immediate calculations of rulers on both sides, including their assessments of the Goguryeo threat that loomed over both Baekje and Silla throughout this period.

Gaya-Wa connections were especially dense and sustained, though they took a different form from the formal royal-court diplomacy that characterized the Baekje relationship. Archaeological evidence — similarities in tomb construction, grave goods, ironwork, and horse equipment across the strait — indicates dense, ongoing material exchange between Gaya polities and communities in northern Kyushu and beyond. Gaya's access to iron resources made it a particularly valuable trading partner for Wa elites seeking the raw material and finished ironwork that underwrote military and agricultural power at home, a point developed further in Section 9.6. This relationship, and Wa's degree of political involvement in it, forms the subject of the chapter's central historiographical debate, examined next.

Section 9.4

War, Military Claims, and the Problem of Mimana

Mimana is the Japanese reading, and Imna the Korean reading, of the name 任那, a historical geographical and political designation whose precise referent — whether one particular Gaya polity, such as Geumgwan Gaya in the Gimhae region, or a broader Wa-oriented term for the Gaya region generally — remains debated among historians and is not the chapter's central concern. What matters more directly here is that the Nihon Shoki, compiled in 720, describes persons and offices connected with Mimana in language that later scholars developed into the theory of "Mimana Nihon-fu" (Imna Ilbon-bu), a supposed Yamato administrative outpost or colonial territory in southern Korea maintained for an extended period, complete with appointed officials and formal territorial control. This theory was seized upon by Japanese scholars and government officials in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, particularly during the period of Japanese colonization of Korea (1910–1945) examined in Chapter 62, as historical precedent legitimating renewed Japanese rule over the peninsula. The claim that Yamato had "always" controlled part of Korea in antiquity served an explicitly modern political purpose, and this history of political use is itself an essential part of understanding why the claim demands especially careful scrutiny.

Modern historians, working from archaeology, contemporary inscriptions, and independent Korean and Chinese textual traditions, have subjected the Mimana claim to sustained critical re-examination and, in its older, strong form — direct, continuous Yamato territorial administration comparable to a colonial government — the great majority no longer accept it. Several considerations drive this reassessment. First, the Nihon Shoki's account was compiled nearly two centuries after the events it describes, by a court with strong political interests in portraying Yamato's ancient authority as expansive and its ancestors as continentally dominant; this alone requires that its claims be checked against independent evidence rather than accepted at face value. Second, Korean sources, including the Samguk sagi, while themselves compiled centuries later and not free of their own political interests, do not corroborate a picture of sustained Wa territorial administration over Gaya. Third, archaeology has not produced evidence of the kind of administrative apparatus — fortifications, garrison settlements, or administrative infrastructure comparable to what is known from genuine colonial or garrison contexts elsewhere — that would be expected from a long-term territorial colony, as opposed to the trade goods, craft-related material, and occasional military equipment consistent with alliance, exchange, and intervention rather than annexation.

None of this means Wa forces never fought on the peninsula, and it would be an overcorrection to deny military intervention altogether simply because the older colonial framework has been discredited. The Gwanggaeto Stele, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, describes Wa forces operating in the southern peninsula in alliance with or in opposition to various peninsular powers during the reign of the Goguryeo king it commemorates, and Baekje and Gaya sources referenced in the Nihon Shoki, even when their details cannot be fully verified, are consistent with a broader pattern of Wa military cooperation with Baekje and Gaya against Silla and Goguryeo at various points. Current scholarship, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, has broadly moved away from the strong territorial-administration model toward a range of alternative interpretations — including alliance and military-cooperation models, diplomatic-or-administrative-agent models, and network/coalition models emphasizing shifting relationships among multiple autonomous Gaya communities — that treat Wa's relationship with the Gaya region as something considerably short of formal territorial rule. Students should understand the rejection of the old colonial-administration theory as the genuine scholarly consensus, while recognizing that historians continue to disagree over the precise political role of Wa personnel, the meaning of individual Nihon Shoki notices, the geographic scope of Imna/Mimana itself, and the degree of Wa political authority at particular moments — none of which is yet settled in the way the rejection of the older colonial model is.

Section 9.5

Migration into the Archipelago

Migration is the human center of this chapter, and it deserves treatment as a major historical process in its own right rather than as background color to a story about "technology transfer." People moved from the Korean Peninsula into the Japanese archipelago throughout this period for a range of overlapping reasons: displacement associated with warfare and political upheaval on the peninsula; the search for patronage and opportunity among specialists whose skills were valued at the Yamato court and among regional elites; existing kinship and trade relationships that made resettlement a viable option; and, in some cases, deliberate invitation or recruitment by Yamato rulers seeking specific expertise. Goguryeo's capture of the Baekje capital at Hanseong in 475 destabilized Baekje and contributed to wider population displacement on the peninsula; this event forms part of the broader late-fifth-century context in which movement toward the archipelago appears to have increased, though it should be treated as contributing context rather than as a single, directly documented trigger for a specific migration event.

Modern historians commonly use the term toraijin ("people who came across") as a broad historiographical label for these migrants and their descendants, examined in the Historical Vocabulary feature below. It is essential that students not picture these people as a homogeneous category of "foreign advisers" who arrived, delivered their expertise, and remained apart from archipelagic society. The evidence — including later genealogical records, such as the Heian-period *Shinsen Shōjiroku*, which catalogued the ancestry claims of aristocratic lineages including many tracing descent from continental migrants — indicates that migrant families and their descendants became farmers working land granted or settled under Yamato-affiliated authority, artisans operating within the specialized *be* labor groups described in Chapter 8, scribes and record-keepers serving the court directly, military specialists including cavalry officers and possibly siege or fortification experts, ritual practitioners, and, in a number of well-documented cases, ancestors of prominent aristocratic lineages whose members held significant court offices for generations afterward.

Two family lineages frequently discussed in later sources illustrate this pattern, though students should treat traditional accounts of their origins with the same source-critical caution urged throughout this chapter. The Hata lineage, later prominent in the Kyoto region, and the Aya (or Yamato-no-Aya) lineage both traced their ancestry, according to later genealogical tradition, to migrants from the continent — the Hata associated with traditions of descent from the Chinese state of Qin (transmitted via the peninsula) and the Aya associated with descent from Later Han Chinese lineages that had settled in Korea before moving to the archipelago. Whatever the precise historical accuracy of these specific genealogical claims — which, like much genealogical tradition from this period, cannot be independently verified and may reflect later prestige-seeking as much as literal ancestry — the existence and social prominence of lineages that identified themselves this way demonstrates that continental origin was, for many families, a source of status rather than stigma within the developing Yamato political order, closely tied to the valuable specialist knowledge such lineages were understood to carry or to have introduced.

Migration should also be understood as bidirectional and embedded in ongoing relationships rather than a single one-way flow. Wa envoys, hostages exchanged as part of diplomatic arrangements, and military personnel moved from the archipelago toward the peninsula as well, and some individuals and families likely moved back and forth across the strait more than once as circumstances changed. The picture that best fits the evidence is one of a genuinely transregional zone of movement and settlement around the Korea Strait, rather than a simple story of peninsular people "coming to Japan."

Section 9.6

Craft Specialists and New Technologies

The technologies most closely associated with this period of continental exchange did not simply "arrive" in the archipelago as abstract innovations. They arrived because specific people — smiths, potters, weavers, and builders trained in particular continental traditions — settled in the archipelago and either practiced their crafts directly or trained local apprentices, often organized, as Chapter 8 described, into specialized *be* labor groups under elite or court patronage.

Advanced ironworking is among the most politically consequential of these technologies, though its history in this period must be stated carefully. Iron objects, tools, and weapons had already circulated in the archipelago since the Yayoi period, described in Chapter 5, and archipelagic communities had already developed local blacksmithing skill, relying heavily on imported continental iron stock rather than local ore. What the fourth and fifth centuries brought was an intensification of access to iron materials, closely tied to Gaya's iron-producing regions, together with the introduction or expansion of more specialized forging techniques — including weapon- and armor-manufacture practices associated with migrant smiths who brought this expertise with them. Securely attested local primary iron-smelting furnaces, by contrast, do not become archaeologically visible until considerably later, generally placed around the mid-sixth century in the late Kofun period; students should not imagine fourth- and fifth-century Yamato as smelting its own ore from scratch. Control over iron material, forging, and distribution, discussed already in Chapter 8 as a foundation of elite power, depended directly on this continental technical knowledge and continued reliance on imported and Gaya-linked iron, making the smiths and their patrons central figures in the political economy of the period.

Equestrian culture underwent a similarly consequential transformation. Horses themselves, along with the equipment needed to ride and fight from horseback — saddles, stirrups, bits, and elaborate harness fittings, many recovered as grave goods in Kofun-period tombs — spread into the archipelago through the same continental networks, associated with specialists skilled in horse breeding, care, and equestrian equipment manufacture. Mounted warfare and its associated prestige, already visible in the tomb goods discussed in Chapter 7, depended on this ongoing technical and, in many cases, literal biological importation, since horses themselves had to be bred and maintained by people with the relevant expertise.

Ceramic technology tells a related story with its own particular significance, examined in the Historical Vocabulary feature below on Sue ware. Weaving technology, including techniques for producing finer and more varied textiles than earlier archipelagic traditions, is similarly associated with migrant craft specialists and their descendant lineages, some of whom, as noted in Section 9.5, became prominent aristocratic families closely associated in later tradition with textile production and court ritual. Construction and engineering techniques — including methods for building larger and more sophisticated structures, and skills that would soon prove directly relevant to the temple-building examined in Chapter 10 — likewise moved into the archipelago through migrant specialists whose knowledge had been developed in continental contexts.

Across all of these technologies, the consistent lesson is the same: political and economic power at the Yamato court and among regional elites depended not on abstract "influence" from a more advanced continent, but on the concrete recruitment, patronage, and organization of specific skilled people who carried this knowledge with them and who could be rewarded, settled, and organized into the labor structures already discussed in Chapter 8.

Historical Vocabulary
Sue Ware

Sue ware (Sueki) is a gray, high-fired stoneware ceramic tradition that spread into the archipelago beginning in the fifth century, produced using a specialized enclosed kiln technology and a fast-spinning potter's wheel, both previously unavailable to earlier archipelagic potters working in the softer, low-fired Haji-ware tradition. Sue ware production is closely associated with migrant potters, particularly those connected to Gaya, whose technical knowledge of kiln construction and firing temperature control could not be replicated without direct training.

Sue ware's significance extends beyond its practical uses for storage, ritual, and daily life. Its production required organized workshops, often under elite or court patronage as part of the specialized *be* labor structure described in Chapter 8, making the spread of Sue ware technology a useful archaeological indicator of the networks of migration, patronage, and elite organization this chapter describes. The gray stoneware's durability has also made it disproportionately visible in the archaeological record compared to some other technologies of this period whose physical traces survive less well.

Historical Vocabulary
Toraijin

Toraijin (渡来人), literally "people who came across [the sea]," is a modern historiographical label used by historians for migrants and migrant-descended populations who came to the archipelago primarily from the Korean Peninsula, and more indirectly from China, during the Kofun period and afterward. The term is a modern scholarly convenience rather than a label these people necessarily used for themselves, and it should not be read as implying a single, unified ethnic or national identity, since migrants came from distinct kingdoms — Baekje, Silla, Gaya, and occasionally directly from Chinese contexts — with different languages, political affiliations, and cultural practices.

Students should avoid two common distortions when using this term. First, toraijin should not be imagined as a marginal or foreign underclass; as Section 9.5 explains, migrant-descended lineages included some of the most prominent aristocratic families of later centuries. Second, the term should not be used to flatten genuinely different migration streams, occurring across a broad span of time and originating from distinct peninsular and continental sources, into a single undifferentiated "immigrant" category. Whenever possible, historians specify the particular kingdom, period, and social role associated with a given migrant community rather than relying on the general term alone.

Section 9.7

Writing Comes into Wider Political Use

Chapter 8 introduced the earliest surviving inscribed objects associated with the Yamato world, including inscribed swords bearing dated formulas in Chinese characters, and asked what such objects revealed about emerging political authority. This chapter returns to that evidence with a more pointed question: who could actually produce such inscriptions, and how did the capacity to write enter archipelagic political life in the first place?

The answer follows directly from the migration and specialist-recruitment patterns already described. Literacy in Chinese characters was a specialized, continentally trained skill, and while character-bearing objects had already reached the archipelago earlier, the fifth and sixth centuries saw sustained, court-centered scribal use become substantially more visible and politically consequential, carried by literate migrants most closely associated in later tradition with Baekje, which maintained its own scholarly tradition of Chinese-style learning and administration. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki preserve later traditions crediting specific individuals, most famously a scholar remembered as Wani (Korean: Wang In), with coming from Baekje during the reign traditionally assigned to Ōjin and bringing Chinese classical texts to instruct at the Yamato court. Wani's exact historicity and date are not established by independent contemporary fifth-century evidence, and students should treat the specific narrative details of this story with appropriate caution, since it survives only in later chronicle compilation. What the tradition likely reflects, even if the specific individual and chronology cannot be verified, is a genuine underlying process: the arrival of literate specialists, probably over an extended period and involving more than one person, who brought functional literacy in Chinese into Yamato court service.

A crucial conceptual distinction must be made clear at this point, one the outline for this chapter specifically emphasizes. The introduction of Chinese characters, known as *kanji*, did not mean the immediate emergence of a system for writing the Japanese language. For a substantial period extending well beyond this chapter's chronological scope, Chinese characters were used in the archipelago primarily to write in literary or administrative Chinese itself — the shared written language of diplomacy and elite learning across much of East Asia in this period — for purposes including royal genealogies, diplomatic correspondence with peninsular and Chinese courts, commemorative inscriptions such as those discussed in Chapter 8, and religious or scholarly texts. Systems for using Chinese characters to represent the sounds and grammar of the Japanese language itself, eventually producing the phonetic *kana* scripts examined in Chapter 19's discussion of Heian court literature, developed only gradually over subsequent centuries. Students should therefore resist imagining fifth- and sixth-century Yamato as a "literate society" in a broad sense; literacy in this period was a narrow, specialized, court- and elite-centered skill practiced by a small number of trained scribes, not a general capacity distributed across the population.

Section 9.8

Scribes, Records, and Court Administration

The arrival of functional literacy, narrow as it was, had real consequences for the organizational capacity of the Yamato court described in Chapter 8. Literate scribes, often referred to using titles such as *fuhito*, could maintain diplomatic correspondence with peninsular and Chinese courts in the shared written language those courts expected, draft and preserve genealogical records establishing and defending the status claims of elite lineages (a function of obvious importance given the *kabane* rank system and the negotiated, prestige-based authority described in Chapter 8), and potentially assist in tracking inventories, labor obligations, or other administrative details relevant to organizing the *be* groups and dependent households already discussed.

Students should be careful not to overstate this development into a claim that Yamato had become a fully bureaucratic state by this period; Chapters 12 and 13 will show that the more systematic administrative institutions associated with later reforms lay still generations in the future. What this chapter's evidence supports is more modest but still significant: the availability of literate specialists, recruited and retained through the same migration and patronage networks already described, gave Yamato rulers and allied elites new tools for record-keeping, diplomatic communication, and status documentation that had not existed in the same form during the periods discussed in Chapters 7 and 8. These tools reinforced rather than replaced the kinship-based, prestige-good, and ritual-authority foundations of Yamato power; a written genealogy, in this period, functioned as one more prestige resource in the same political competition that horses, iron, and Chinese titles also served, rather than as evidence of an impersonal bureaucratic apparatus.

The presence of scribal specialists at court also had a subtler but important effect: it meant that Yamato rulers now had direct access, through translation and interpretation as well as written correspondence, to continental political concepts, diplomatic conventions, and models of kingship circulating among Baekje, Chinese, and other regional courts. This access mattered for the chapter's next section, which turns to why continental recognition, titles, and cultural forms carried such weight in Yamato's internal politics.

Section 9.9

Continental Prestige and Political Legitimacy

Chapter 8 explained that Yamato authority in this period rested heavily on the ability of rulers to attract and reward loyalty from regional elites through prestige goods and ritual status rather than through coercive bureaucratic control. This chapter's evidence shows that continental connections were among the most valuable sources of exactly this kind of prestige, making foreign relations an integral part of Yamato's internal political competition rather than a separate sphere of "foreign policy" distinct from domestic concerns.

The clearest documented example of this dynamic involves the sequence of rulers conventionally known to modern historians as the "Five Kings of Wa." An early Wa embassy is recorded under the Eastern Jin dynasty in 413, and the principal, best-documented sequence of missions runs from 421 to 478 under the Liu Song court, culminating in a detailed petition from the ruler Bu in 478 and his investiture with a Liu Song military title. Separate notices in 479 (Southern Qi) and 502 (Liang) record promotions of Bu's title tied to the founding of each new dynasty; these should not automatically be read as evidence of fresh Wa embassies in those specific years, since dynasties commonly reconfirmed or upgraded existing titles upon their own founding. Historians debate the precise identification of these kings with rulers named in the Nihon Shoki and with archaeologically documented tomb occupants, and this identification should be presented to students as a genuine scholarly puzzle rather than settled fact. What is far better established is the underlying pattern: Wa rulers actively sought recognition from a Chinese imperial court, valued the titles that recognition conferred, and used the resulting prestige to bolster their authority relative to rival elites within the archipelago itself. A title granted by a distant and prestigious Chinese emperor carried weight precisely because it was difficult for a domestic rival to obtain or dispute, giving the recipient a genuine, if intangible, political advantage at home.

This same logic applied more broadly to imported goods, migrant specialists, and diplomatic relationships generally. A regional chieftain or Yamato-affiliated elite who could display continental prestige goods, patronize a skilled migrant craftsman, host a literate scribe capable of producing an impressive genealogical record, or claim a documented diplomatic relationship with a peninsular court gained standing relative to rivals who could not. Continental connections, in other words, functioned within the same competitive system of gift-giving, patronage, and negotiated status that Chapter 8 described as the foundation of Yamato political order — they simply extended that system's reach across the sea. This helps explain why Yamato rulers invested such sustained effort in cultivating peninsular alliances, recruiting migrant specialists, and seeking Chinese recognition: these were not incidental foreign-policy activities separate from the "real" business of domestic politics, but were themselves among the most effective tools available for winning that domestic political competition.

Section 9.10

From Continental Exchange to Religious Transformation

By the early sixth century, the patterns this chapter has described — diplomatic relationships with Baekje in particular, sustained migration of specialists and their descendants, the spread of continental technologies, the narrow but consequential spread of literacy, and the ongoing pursuit of continental prestige as a tool of domestic legitimacy — had become deeply embedded features of Yamato political life. The Yamato court by this point maintained regular, personal, and increasingly institutionalized relationships with the Baekje court specifically, including the exchange of scholars trained in Chinese classical learning and, before long, in a further body of continental knowledge that would prove far more disruptive to the existing order than ironworking or ceramics ever had: Buddhism.

Buddhism, as Chapter 10 will explain in full, did not arrive in the archipelago through some entirely new channel. It traveled through precisely the same Baekje-Yamato diplomatic relationship, the same networks of migration and specialist recruitment, and the same currents of continental prestige-seeking that this chapter has traced across ironworking, ceramics, equestrian culture, and writing. What made Buddhism different was not its mode of transmission but its content: a comprehensive religious and philosophical system, backed by its own institutions, images, and texts, that raised questions about cosmology, ritual authority, and political legitimacy that went considerably further than a sword, a ceramic kiln, or a genealogical record ever could. Those questions would soon divide the Yamato court itself, as Chapter 11 will show in its account of the Soga-Mononobe conflict. This chapter's purpose has been to establish, in careful and unglamorous detail, the continental networks of exchange, migration, and legitimacy-seeking through which that far more dramatic religious and political transformation would travel.

Primary Source Window

The Gwanggaeto Stele

Creator: Commissioned by King Jangsu of Goguryeo in commemoration of his father, King Gwanggaeto · Date: Erected 414, describing events largely from Gwanggaeto's reign (391–413) · Type: Monumental stone inscription, still standing near Ji'an in present-day northeastern China

Context: The Gwanggaeto Stele is one of the very few contemporary, or near-contemporary, inscriptional sources bearing on Wa activity on the Korean Peninsula in this period. It was erected by Goguryeo, a rival power to both Baekje and, at times, Silla, to commemorate the military achievements of a specific Goguryeo king, and it describes campaigns involving Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Wa forces in the southern peninsula. Because it was created for Goguryeo's own commemorative and dynastic purposes, it should not be read as a neutral battle report.

Paraphrase of the inscription's general content (not a verbatim translation): The inscription describes Wa forces as active participants in conflicts in the southern peninsula during Gwanggaeto's reign, at times fighting in connection with Baekje and at times opposed by Goguryeo and allied Silla forces, and frames Goguryeo's king as having achieved decisive military successes against these combined forces.

Analysis: Historians have long debated how to read this inscription's claims about Wa's role and scale of involvement, and the debate involves two distinct kinds of difficulty that students should learn to tell apart. The first is the stele's obvious political purpose: it was commissioned by Goguryeo's own dynasty to commemorate a Goguryeo king's victories, and a triumphal inscription of this kind has strong incentives to emphasize the scale and menace of the enemy forces it describes defeating. Some earlier interpretations, particularly in early twentieth-century Japanese scholarship, read the stele as confirming a large, organized Wa military presence supporting a claim of territorial control in the peninsula's south; more recent scholarship treats such a reading as going well beyond what the inscription can support. The second difficulty is physical and textual: portions of the stone are worn or illegible, and one especially famous passage, conventionally dated to the year 391 (the "Sinmyo year" passage), survives with missing or disputed characters whose grammatical subjects and precise meaning remain actively contested among specialists — some readings have it describing Wa subjugating Baekje and Silla, others read the damaged text quite differently. This particular passage should not, by itself, be treated as establishing that Wa conquered or ruled Baekje and Silla, let alone that Yamato held a territorial colony; other, less damaged portions of the inscription describing later campaigns provide somewhat more secure evidence that Wa forces were militarily active on the peninsula during Gwanggaeto's reign, even though the stele's political purpose still requires that this evidence be read cautiously. The stele must therefore be read alongside archaeological evidence and other textual traditions rather than taken as a straightforward factual record.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a monument commissioned by Goguryeo to celebrate its own king's victories exaggerate rather than understate the scale of the Wa forces it describes defeating?
  2. Why does the damaged and disputed "Sinmyo year" passage require even more caution than the inscription's political bias alone would suggest?
  3. What does this source confirm about Wa activity on the peninsula, and what does it leave uncertain?
  4. How might a Baekje source, had one survived, be expected to describe the same events differently from this Goguryeo inscription?
  5. Why is a near-contemporary inscription still not automatically a neutral or fully reliable source?
Historical Debate

What Was the Nature of Yamato Involvement in Southern Korea?

The question: How should historians characterize Wa's relationship with the Gaya region between roughly the late fourth and mid-sixth centuries: as territorial colonial rule, as military alliance and intervention, or as a looser network of shifting coalitions among autonomous powers?

Interpretation A — The older territorial-administration model: Drawing primarily on a literal reading of the Nihon Shoki's account of "Mimana," this interpretation, most influential in earlier twentieth-century Japanese scholarship, holds that Yamato established and maintained a direct administrative outpost with appointed officials governing part of the Gaya region for an extended period, comparable to a colonial territory. This interpretation is now rejected by the great majority of historians, but students should understand it as a historically influential position whose political uses, discussed in Section 9.4, help explain why the topic remains sensitive.
Interpretation B — The alliance and intervention model: This interpretation, closer to current scholarly consensus, holds that Wa maintained close trade, migration, and military-cooperation relationships with Gaya polities, including periodic direct military intervention in alliance with Gaya and Baekje against Silla and Goguryeo, but did not exercise sustained territorial administration. Gaya's own rulers retained independent political agency and pursued the relationship for their own strategic reasons.
Interpretation C — The network/coalition model: A related but distinct interpretation emphasizes that Gaya was never a single unified polity, and that Wa's relationships varied considerably among individual Gaya communities and shifted over time as circumstances changed, cautioning against any single characterization — whether "colony," "alliance," or otherwise — applied uniformly across the whole Gaya region and the whole of this roughly 150-year period.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same core evidence — the Nihon Shoki, the Gwanggaeto Stele, the Samguk sagi, and archaeological findings from Gaya and Kyushu tomb sites — but differ in how much weight to give the Nihon Shoki's later, politically interested account relative to contemporary inscriptional and archaeological evidence.

Why it matters: This debate is not merely academic. The older territorial-administration model was used to help justify Japan's twentieth-century colonization of Korea, examined in Chapter 62, giving the historiographical question direct modern political stakes alongside its scholarly ones.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence discussed in this chapter that supports Interpretation B or C over Interpretation A, and explain why the distinction between "military intervention and alliance" and "territorial colonial rule" matters for how historians describe this period responsibly.

Historical Debate

Immigrants, Refugees, Specialists, or Elite Networks?

The question: What is the most historically accurate way to describe people who moved from the Korean Peninsula into the Japanese archipelago in this period — as immigrants, refugees, specialists, settlers, or participants in a transregional elite network?

Interpretation A — Migration as displacement: Some historians emphasize that much of this movement, particularly following events such as Baekje's 475 defeat, was driven by warfare, political collapse, and danger, making terms like "refugee" or "displaced person" historically appropriate for at least some migration streams.
Interpretation B — Migration as specialist recruitment: Other historians emphasize that many migrants, particularly literate scribes, craft specialists, and their descendant lineages, moved in response to active demand and patronage from Yamato rulers and regional elites, making terms like "specialist" or "recruited expert" more appropriate for these particular streams.
Interpretation C — Migration within a shared elite world: A third view stresses that, at the highest social levels, movement between peninsular courts and the Yamato court occurred within a single transregional network of elite families, diplomatic hostages, and intermarried lineages who did not experience the peninsula and the archipelago as fully separate "foreign" worlds in the way modern national categories might suggest.

Evidence: These interpretations are not mutually exclusive; different migration streams, occurring at different times and involving different social groups, likely fit different descriptions.

Why it matters: How historians label this movement shapes whether students imagine ancient Japan-Korea relations through the lens of modern immigration, modern refugee crises, elite diplomacy, or something else entirely — and modern categories, however useful for other purposes, do not map cleanly onto the fluid political geography of the fifth and sixth centuries.

Student task: Choose one migration example discussed in this chapter (for example, the Hata or Aya lineages, or the aftermath of Baekje's 475 defeat) and explain which interpretation, or combination of interpretations, best fits the evidence for that specific case.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Chapters 7–8 to Chapter 9

What Changed

  • Migration of continental specialists and their descendants became more sustained and politically consequential than in earlier periods
  • Literacy in Chinese characters entered court life for the first time in an organized, if still narrow, form
  • Diplomatic relationships with named peninsular kingdoms and Chinese dynasties became more regular and better documented
  • Technological transfer intensified, particularly in ironworking, ceramics (Sue ware), and equestrian equipment

What Stayed the Same

  • Political authority continued to rest on kinship networks, ritual status, and prestige-good competition rather than centralized bureaucratic control
  • Continental connection continued to function as an extension of, rather than a replacement for, the domestic political competition described in Chapters 7 and 8
  • The archipelago remained connected to continental exchange networks that had operated in some form since the Yayoi period examined in Chapter 5

Why the pattern matters: Treating continental exchange as an intensification of existing patterns, rather than a sudden new departure, helps students see Yamato's political development as continuous rather than as a story of sudden foreign "civilizing" influence arriving from outside an unchanging archipelagic tradition.

Cause and Effect

Continental Entanglement and Yamato Power

Long-term causes

Geography placed the Kinai region at the end of a maritime corridor connecting the archipelago to the Korean Peninsula and, beyond it, to China, a corridor already in use since the Yayoi period.

Short-term causes

Peninsular political instability, especially Goguryeo's military pressure on Baekje and Gaya's vulnerability as a decentralized cluster of small polities, created strong incentives for peninsular actors to cultivate relationships with Wa, while Yamato rulers sought specialist labor, prestige goods, and continental recognition to strengthen their domestic standing.

Effect

Sustained migration, technology transfer, narrow but consequential literacy, and diplomatic engagement with named peninsular kingdoms and Chinese dynasties became defining features of Yamato political life.

Long-term consequence

The same continental networks that carried iron, ceramics, horses, and writing into the archipelago in this period would soon carry Buddhism as well, setting up the religious and political transformation examined in Chapters 10 and 11.

Historical complexity: Continental exchange did not mechanically "modernize" or "civilize" the archipelago in a simple, one-directional sense; it gave Yamato rulers and rival elites new resources to deploy within their own ongoing, locally rooted political competition, and Gaya, Baekje, Silla, and Goguryeo pursued their own interests through the same relationships rather than acting merely as sources of influence upon Wa.

Everyday Life and Environment

Life Around the Korea Strait

Beyond the diplomatic missions and elite genealogies that dominate the surviving textual record, this period's continental connections shaped everyday life for a range of ordinary people. Fishing and coastal communities on both sides of the Korea Strait, in northern Kyushu, Tsushima, Iki, and the southern Gaya coast, would have had direct, regular, and probably long-familiar contact with people from the other shore, quite apart from any court-level diplomacy — a reminder that "international relations" in this period operated at multiple social levels simultaneously, not only through named rulers and envoys.

For migrant craft specialists and their families settling in the archipelago, daily life likely involved navigating an unfamiliar social order, learning or adapting to new languages and customs, and working within the *be* labor structures or elite household arrangements described in Chapter 8, while also maintaining, where possible, elements of their own craft traditions, religious practices, and family customs. Archaeological evidence, including distinctive continental-style dwelling structures and cooking equipment found at some Kofun-period settlement sites in the archipelago, suggests that migrant communities in some cases maintained recognizably distinct household practices for at least a generation or more after settlement, offering a rare, physically grounded glimpse into daily life beyond what the elite-focused textual sources describe.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has placed the emerging Yamato polity described in Chapters 7 and 8 within the interconnected East Asian world of the fourth through sixth centuries, arguing that continental relationships were not peripheral to Yamato's political development but central to it. Geography made this possible: sea routes crossing the Korea Strait via Tsushima and Iki, and continuing through the Seto Inland Sea, connected the Kinai region to a Korean Peninsula divided among the rival kingdoms of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla and the smaller, decentralized Gaya polities.

Wa's relationships with these kingdoms were neither uniform nor static. Baekje generally emerged as Wa's closest and most durable diplomatic partner, cultivated through gift exchange and, later, scholarly and religious exchange. Relations with Silla shifted between cooperation and conflict according to circumstance. Gaya's relationship with Wa, closest of all in material and human terms, has also proven the most historiographically contested, owing to the later, politically motivated claim, rooted in the Nihon Shoki, that Yamato maintained direct territorial administration there under the name Mimana. This chapter has shown why the great majority of modern historians reject that older model in favor of interpretations emphasizing alliance, military intervention, and shifting coalition rather than sustained colonial rule, while still affirming that Wa forces were genuinely and repeatedly militarily active on the peninsula.

Migration has occupied a central place in this chapter's account. People moved from the peninsula into the archipelago for reasons including warfare, political collapse, and active recruitment, and they became farmers, craftspeople, scribes, military specialists, and, in a number of documented cases, ancestors of prominent aristocratic lineages, rather than remaining an undifferentiated foreign category. This migration carried with it the specialist knowledge behind key technologies of the period, including advanced ironworking, equestrian culture, Sue ware ceramics, weaving, and construction techniques, each tied to specific migrant communities rather than to an abstract process of "influence."

Writing entered archipelagic political life through these same channels, carried by literate migrants associated above all with Baekje. This chapter has taken care to distinguish the introduction of Chinese characters, used initially and for a long period afterward primarily to write in Chinese for diplomatic, administrative, and commemorative purposes, from the much later development of systems for writing the Japanese language itself. The resulting scribal capacity strengthened the Yamato court's ability to maintain diplomatic correspondence and document elite genealogies, reinforcing rather than replacing the kinship-based and prestige-good foundations of Yamato authority already described in Chapter 8.

Continental prestige, this chapter has argued, was not a peripheral concern but a central resource in Yamato's internal political competition. The "Five Kings of Wa" who sought and received titles from southern Chinese dynasties, and the regional elites who sought imported goods, migrant specialists, and documented continental relationships, were all engaged in the same underlying project: converting continental recognition and resources into domestic political advantage.

Throughout, this chapter has insisted on avoiding modern national categories when describing a genuinely multipolar and fluid fifth- and sixth-century world, and it has used the Gwanggaeto Stele and the historiography of the Mimana question as extended case studies in the careful, source-critical evaluation of politically interested evidence — a methodological lesson connecting directly to this textbook's broader concern with historical evidence and interpretation, introduced through the very different case of the Fujimura fraud in Chapter 2.

The chapter closes by pointing directly toward its successor. The same Baekje-Yamato diplomatic relationship, the same migration networks, and the same continental prestige-seeking described throughout this chapter would soon carry a far more disruptive import into the archipelago: Buddhism, whose arrival and consequences are the subject of Chapter 10, and whose domestic political fallout at the Yamato court is the subject of Chapter 11.

Students who understand the fifth and sixth centuries as a period of active, multidirectional, and politically contested continental engagement — rather than as a story of passive reception from a culturally superior continent, or as a story of ancient Japanese territorial dominance over Korea — are well prepared to follow the more sharply defined religious and political conflicts examined in the chapters that follow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Wa
The continental exonym for the polities and peoples of the Japanese archipelago in this period, used in Chinese and Korean sources.
Baekje
A southwestern Korean kingdom and Wa's closest, though not exclusive, continental diplomatic partner in this period.
Silla
A southeastern Korean kingdom whose relationship with Wa shifted between cooperation and conflict and which eventually absorbed the Gaya polities.
Goguryeo
The most militarily powerful of the peninsular kingdoms, based in the north, whose campaigns repeatedly reshaped the balance of power among Baekje, Silla, and Gaya.
Gaya
A cluster of smaller, politically decentralized polities on the peninsula's southern coast, closely tied to Wa and eventually absorbed by Silla.
Mimana / Imna
Japanese and Korean readings of a debated geographical/political designation in the Gaya region; basis of the "Mimana Nihon-fu" theory of direct Yamato territorial administration, now largely rejected by historians.
Toraijin
A modern historiographical term for migrants and migrant-descended populations who came to the archipelago from the Korean Peninsula and, indirectly, from China.
Sue ware
A gray, high-fired stoneware ceramic tradition introduced through migrant potters, associated with kiln and wheel technology previously unavailable in the archipelago.
Haji ware
The earlier, softer, low-fired archipelagic ceramic tradition that continued alongside Sue ware after its introduction.
Kanji
Chinese characters, present in the archipelago before this period but increasingly used in sustained court, diplomatic, administrative, and commemorative contexts during the fifth and sixth centuries; later adapted more systematically to record Japanese.
Fuhito
A title associated with literate scribes serving the Yamato court in record-keeping and correspondence.
Kabane
The hereditary rank system organizing elite lineages within the Yamato political order, introduced in Chapter 8 and reinforced by written genealogical documentation discussed in this chapter.
Investiture / Chinese titles
Formal titles and recognition granted by Chinese imperial courts to regional rulers, including Wa rulers, in exchange for tribute and nominal acknowledgment of Chinese imperial superiority.
Five Kings of Wa
A modern label for Wa rulers recorded as sending missions to Eastern Jin (413) and, in the best-documented sequence, the Liu Song court (421–478), with further promotions of the title of the ruler Bu recorded by Southern Qi (479) and Liang (502) at those dynasties' founding.
Gwanggaeto Stele
A 414 Goguryeo monumental inscription describing military campaigns involving Wa, Baekje, Silla, and Goguryeo forces; a contemporary but politically interested source.
Nihon Shoki
An early eighth-century Japanese court chronicle whose accounts of this period were compiled centuries later and require careful source criticism.
Samguk sagi
A twelfth-century Korean chronicle of the Three Kingdoms period, an important but similarly retrospective source for this era.
Seven-Branched Sword
An inscribed iron sword, traditionally associated with a Baekje-Wa gift exchange, held today at Isonokami Shrine.
Hata lineage
An aristocratic lineage that later traced its ancestry, according to genealogical tradition, to continental migrants associated with weaving and other craft traditions.
Aya (Yamato-no-Aya) lineage
An aristocratic lineage that later traced its ancestry, according to genealogical tradition, to Chinese lineages that had settled on the peninsula before moving to the archipelago.
Wani (Wang In)
A scholar whom later Kojiki/Nihon Shoki tradition credits with bringing Chinese classical learning from Baekje to the Ōjin-era court; his historicity and exact date lack independent contemporary attestation.
Korea Strait
The body of water separating the southern Korean Peninsula from northern Kyushu, broken by Tsushima and Iki.
Seto Inland Sea
The sheltered sea corridor linking northern Kyushu to the Kinai region, facilitating onward travel from the continent toward Yamato's political center.
Be
Specialized hereditary labor and service groups organized under Yamato-affiliated elite patronage, discussed in Chapter 8 and central to organizing migrant craft specialists in this chapter.
Source criticism
The historical method of evaluating a source's origin, purpose, and potential bias before accepting its claims, central to this chapter's treatment of the Gwanggaeto Stele and the Nihon Shoki.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the emerging Yamato political order described in Chapters 7 and 8 outward into the wider East Asian world that helped make it possible. It has shown that the prestige goods, specialist craftsmen, horses, ironwork, and eventually the written records underwriting Yamato authority arrived through concrete human relationships with named peninsular kingdoms and Chinese dynasties, not through some abstract process of cultural diffusion from a more advanced continent to a passive archipelago.

The chapter's treatment of the Mimana question matters well beyond its immediate subject. It demonstrates how a later chronicle's politically interested account can harden into an influential historical claim, how that claim can be put to explicitly modern political use, and how careful, multi-source historical scrutiny can responsibly revise such a claim without either denying genuine military intervention or reinstating a discredited colonial narrative in a different form. Students should carry this lesson into every subsequent chapter addressing contested Japan-Korea history, including the modern colonial period examined in Chapter 62 and the persistent questions of historical memory that recur throughout this textbook's later chapters.

The chapter has also reinforced an argument this textbook has made since Chapter 1: that "Japan" and "the Japanese people" are historical constructions built gradually over centuries, not fixed categories projected backward onto the past. The fifth- and sixth-century Yamato world described here was porous, cosmopolitan by the standards of its time, and thoroughly entangled with peninsular and Chinese political life — a reality obscured by later nationalist narratives on more than one side of the Korea Strait, and one this chapter has tried to recover through careful attention to evidence.

Students who understand this period as one of active, multidirectional continental engagement — rather than passive reception or ancient territorial dominance — are well prepared to follow the chapters that come next, in which the same networks of exchange this chapter has traced will carry Buddhism into the archipelago and, with it, a religious and political controversy that will reshape the Yamato court itself.
