Part IV — Conquest and the Umayyad Arab Empire
20

Conquerors, Garrisons, and Provincial Societies

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Some time after the surrender of the fortress of Babylon, near the point where the Nile divides toward the Delta, an Arab-Muslim army pitched its camp beside the older Byzantine stronghold. Later tradition explained the name of the settlement that grew there, Fustat, by a tent. Whatever the truth of that story, the camp did not remain a camp. Within a generation it had become a city beside an Egyptian administrative landscape that remained largely in place, and the two coexisted in ways that shaped both.

Chapter 19 explained how Arab-Muslim armies overturned Byzantine and Sasanian rule across the Near East. This chapter asks a different question: what did conquest look like on the ground once those armies held populated provinces? A relatively small ruling elite, itself divided by tribe, precedence, and region, had taken control of territories whose peoples spoke Greek, Syriac, Coptic, Middle Persian, and other languages, followed Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other religious traditions, and were accustomed to established systems of taxation and government. How such a minority could rule, feed, pay, house, and restrain its own soldiers, while extracting revenue from much larger populations, is the problem this chapter examines.

The chapter's central argument is that the early conquests did not replace the societies of the former Byzantine and Sasanian provinces. They placed an Arab-Muslim military and political elite over existing populations, institutions, economies, languages, and religious communities. The early caliphate survived because it combined new military settlement and political authority with extensive use of inherited provincial structures and negotiated local arrangements. There was no single, universal "Arab conquest administration." Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Iran each developed different arrangements, and even within a region the situation varied from city to city.

Several developments recur throughout the chapter. Conquerors were often concentrated in garrison cities such as Basra, Kufa, and Fustat rather than dispersed among the conquered. Their soldiers were organized by tribe and, in the tradition, paid from revenue collected from the provinces. The revenue itself was gathered largely by local officials who had served earlier regimes, and it was recorded in Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian well after the conquest. Remembered surrender agreements shaped later claims about land and rights, though the surviving texts of such agreements are often later reconstructions. And non-Muslim communities continued to run their own religious and communal affairs under conditions that varied by place and period.

The chapter also insists on the limits of the evidence. Arabic sources describing the early garrison cities, stipends, and taxes were compiled in the eighth and ninth centuries, and they often project later legal categories back onto the first decades. Terms such as jizya and kharāj acquired precise technical meanings only over time. Against this stand documents that survived because they were written on papyrus in dry Egyptian and Palestinian soil, a few Sasanian and Arab-Sasanian coins and seals, archaeological evidence for early urban development, and non-Muslim writings. The chapter attempts to build its account from the convergence of these different kinds of evidence, and to say plainly where they do not converge.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It does not retell the conquests, which belong to Chapter 19. It does not narrate the political struggle that erupted in the caliphate after ʿUmar's death, which belongs to Chapter 21, though it identifies the tensions of the conquest order from which that struggle grew. It does not examine the Umayyad state, the administrative Arabization of the late seventh century, or the eventual conversion of the majority of the population, which are the subjects of Chapters 22 through 24. Its purpose is to explain how a conquest state actually worked in its first generation, and how far it remained a rule over old societies rather than the creation of new ones.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the problems of holding and governing populated provinces after military victory.
  2. Describe the garrison-city system and analyze why Arab-Muslim forces were concentrated in new or expanded centers.
  3. Compare the settlement patterns of Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Iran, and explain why no single model applied.
  4. Assess the role of remembered treaties and surrender agreements in later claims concerning land, taxation, and local rights, while recognizing the limits of the surviving texts.
  5. Distinguish early tribute-taking from the later technical categories of jizya and kharāj, and explain why applying later legal terminology mechanically is misleading.
  6. Analyze the continued role of Byzantine, Sasanian, Coptic, Greek, Syriac, Persian, and local administrative personnel in collecting revenue.
  7. Interpret documentary evidence, especially papyri, as evidence for local administration and everyday relations between conquerors and conquered.
  8. Describe how Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other communities negotiated with new rulers, emphasizing regional variation.
  9. Explain why the early conquest period should not be described as a period of mass conversion.
  10. Identify tensions within the conquest order that would contribute to later political conflict.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether the early caliphate primarily inherited existing institutions or created a new order.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a relatively small conquering population rule much larger and culturally diverse societies?
  2. Why were so many Arab-Muslim soldiers concentrated in garrison cities, and what did this arrangement make possible?
  3. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct early taxation and settlement, and where do later sources distort the first decades?
  4. How should historians use surrender agreements whose surviving texts were written down or edited long after the conquests?

Historical Context

Chapter 19 established that, within roughly two decades of Muhammad's death, the caliphate had taken Syria, Palestine, Iraq, and Egypt, defeated the principal Sasanian forces, and begun operations in North Africa. It also established that the newcomers were a small minority among the ruled and that conquest was not conversion. Students should carry these points forward rather than relearn them. This chapter concerns what happened after the armies stopped, or, in many regions, while campaigning continued elsewhere.

The provinces the caliphate inherited were already governed. The Byzantine provinces had a tax system organized in Greek and, in Egypt, in Coptic-speaking villages under local officials, a hierarchy of governors and city councils in decline, and a church that was itself a major landholder and social institution. The Sasanian realm had a system of provincial administration, a landholding gentry known as dihqans who mediated between the state and the countryside, and a Zoroastrian priesthood alongside large Christian and Jewish communities. In both empires the practical work of government was carried out by local people. A conqueror who wished to collect revenue without displacing the people who knew how to collect it had strong incentives to keep much of this machinery in place.

The conquerors themselves came from a society organized around kinship and, since Muhammad, a new religious community. They had no tradition of governing large agrarian populations and no bureaucracy of their own beyond what Medina had developed. Their military forces were composed of tribal contingents whose loyalty to the caliph, to their own leaders, and to their tribes did not always coincide. The challenge was to keep such forces together, supplied, and paid, while avoiding two dangers: that they would disperse and become absorbed into the countryside, and that they would fragment along tribal lines. The solutions developed in these years shaped the caliphate for generations.

Section 20.1

From Conquest to Occupation

Military victory created a set of problems that battle could not solve. The armies of the caliphate had to be fed, housed, and paid. Their leaders had to decide where to place them, since the same mobility that made them formidable in the field made them difficult to control in peacetime. Revenue had to be extracted from populations that had paid taxes to previous rulers and would now pay someone else, and it had to be extracted without provoking rebellion. And the conquerors, who had entered these lands as a minority, had to find some working relationship with the communities they had taken over, most of which they could neither convert quickly nor replace.

The earliest solutions were improvised. In the years around the conquests of Syria, Iraq, and Egypt, commanders made agreements with cities and districts, left existing officials in place, and settled their troops in camps and towns that suited the military situation. Later Arabic tradition, compiled from the later eighth century onward, describes these measures as if a single plan had been devised in Medina and carried out uniformly, often crediting ʿUmar. Historians regard this picture with caution. The evidence suggests that many arrangements emerged gradually, differed from province to province, and were later systematized and attributed to the caliph who presided over the conquests. This chapter therefore reads its sources for what they can show about practice and for what they show about later memory.

Two basic strategies can nevertheless be discerned. The first was to concentrate the military population in settlements of its own, separate from the local inhabitants. The second was to rely on existing local structures to collect revenue and maintain order, while claiming a share of what they produced. Both strategies shaped the political economy of the early caliphate, and both created tensions that would later erupt. The following sections examine the first, then the second, then the way they interacted.

Section 20.2

The Garrison-City System

Historical Vocabulary
Miṣr (plural amṣār)

Miṣr, in the sense used here, refers to a settlement established or expanded to house the Arab-Muslim military population, and the plural amṣār is the standard Arabic term for the great early garrison cities. The word has older meanings of "town" or "frontier region," and later Arabic sources used it as the label for Basra, Kufa, and Fustat, among others. Historians often translate it as "garrison city," a useful shorthand that should not obscure the fact that these were not only military encampments. They were also residential cities, markets, religious centers, and eventually centers of administration and scholarship. The term itself belongs largely to later usage, and students should treat it as a modern organizing category as much as an early historical one.

The most distinctive feature of the early conquest order was the garrison city. In Iraq, the tradition remembers the foundation of Basra in the far south, near the head of the Gulf, in roughly 636–638, and of Kufa on the edge of the desert near the old Lakhmid center of al-Hira, around 638. In Egypt, the tradition places the foundation of Fustat beside the fortress of Babylon after its capitulation, in about 641–642. These dates come from later tradition and vary among the sources, but the outline is supported by the eventual prominence of the cities themselves.

The reasons for concentrating soldiers in such places were partly military and partly political. A large army dispersed among conquered villages would be hard to assemble for the next campaign, and its members would be vulnerable to local hostility and to the disease and hardship of unfamiliar surroundings. Placed at the edge of the desert, or on a river close to it, the garrison cities gave the Arab-Muslim forces a base with access to their own pastoral hinterland, to routes back to Arabia, and to the agricultural provinces from which revenue flowed. They also kept soldiers together under the eye of their commanders and away from the cultural influence of the conquered. Whether this last motive was deliberate policy or a later rationalization is uncertain, and the tradition attributes such reasoning to ʿUmar in ways that historians treat carefully.

The cities were not simply camps. Each had a central mosque and a governor's residence, sections allotted to different tribal groups, and markets that served both soldiers and the surrounding population. They grew quickly as families, clients, and local workers joined the original settlers. Section 20.5 and Section 20.4 examine the Iraqi and Egyptian cases in more detail, and Section 20.6 explains why Syria followed a different path.

Section 20.3

Arab Tribal Settlement and Military Organization

The people who settled in the garrison cities were organized by tribe. The Arabic sources describe how each city was divided into quarters (khiṭaṭ, singular khiṭṭa) assigned to tribal groups, and how, in Kufa and Basra, tribes were sometimes combined into larger units for administrative and military purposes, in arrangements later remembered as divisions into fifths, sevenths, or similar fractions. The details are reconstructions from later accounts, but the general principle is well supported: the tribe remained the basic unit of military organization and settlement, and the caliphate worked through, rather than against, tribal structures.

Military service was connected to payment. Later tradition credits ʿUmar with establishing a register (dīwān) of soldiers and paying them a stipend (ʿaṭāʾ) drawn from revenue, with rates said to reflect precedence in the community, including early conversion and participation in the first campaigns and battles. The tradition describes this as a deliberate and systematic act, and it may reflect a real effort to put payments on a regular footing. Historians, however, doubt that a comprehensive and stable register existed in the 640s in the form the later sources describe, and many think the system developed over the following decades. What seems reasonably clear is that soldiers received regular payments from provincial revenue, and that the arrangement bound them financially to the caliphate and to the taxpayers who sustained them.

This system had consequences that shaped the rest of the conquest period. It made the army dependent on the tax revenue of the provinces and gave soldiers an interest in maintaining the existing tax base. It created a hierarchy of status among the conquerors, based on precedence, that would later generate resentment. And it reinforced the separation of the military population from the local one, since the soldiers lived on stipends rather than on land and had, at least in principle, no need to become farmers.

Cause and Effect

Why Garrison Cities Emerged

Long-term causes

A society of kin-based groups accustomed to mobility, with no tradition of governing large agrarian populations, and the existence of frontier towns and camps linking Arabia to Syria and Iraq.

Short-term causes

The need to keep armies together after victory, to supply and pay them, to defend against counterattack, and to hold conquered territory with limited numbers.

Event or transformation

The founding or expansion of Basra, Kufa, Fustat, and other settlements, and the organization of soldiers by tribe and stipend.

Immediate effects

A separation between a military population and provincial society, regular payments from provincial revenue, and the growth of new urban centers.

Long-term consequences

The garrison cities became centers of politics, commerce, religious and legal learning, and, in later chapters, of factional conflict and revolt.

Historical complexity

The system was neither uniform nor entirely planned. Syria developed differently, later tradition credits central policy with what may have been gradual local arrangements, and the motive of keeping soldiers apart from the conquered may be partly a later rationalization.
Section 20.4

Fustat and Egypt

Egypt offers the clearest case of a garrison city rising beside an older administrative landscape. The fortress of Babylon stood at a strategic point, where the Nile Valley narrows before the Delta, and it had been a Byzantine military and administrative center. After its capitulation, the Arab-Muslim forces camped nearby, and the settlement that grew there became Fustat. Later tradition holds that ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs wished to make Alexandria his base, and that the caliph refused because he did not want the army separated from Medina by water. The story explains the choice by a single decision, and historians treat it as a plausible but unverified narrative. What is certain is that the capital of Egypt under Arab-Muslim rule was not Alexandria but a new settlement on the river.

The consequences were substantial. Alexandria remained a large and important city, a center of Greek-speaking Christian life and of maritime trade, but it ceased to be the seat of government. The new center of political authority was located in the interior, at the point where the Nile Valley met the Delta, and it drew soldiers and officials from the provinces. Meanwhile, the local administrative system centered on the districts and their officials, the pagarchs, continued to function. Papyri show local officials writing in Greek, and sometimes in Coptic, addressing the governor and his representatives, and receiving demands for supplies and taxes. The Arab governor and his military entourage stood at the top of a system that remained largely in local hands.

Fustat itself grew. Tribal groups were assigned areas, a mosque was built, and a market developed. The city became home to soldiers, their families, their clients, and increasingly, Egyptians who came to work, trade, and serve. It would in time become a major city of the Mediterranean world, and the later Fatimid foundation of Cairo nearby, examined in Chapter 31, belongs to the same urban region. What matters here is that it began as an addition to Egyptian society, not a replacement for it.

Section 20.5

Basra and Kufa in Iraq

In Iraq, the two garrison cities stood at the edge of the alluvial plain, between the desert and the cultivated lands of al-Sawād. Basra, in the south, was located near the head of the Gulf and the mouth of the great rivers. It served as a base for operations toward Khuzistan and Fars and for connections with the Gulf and, eventually, with the Indian Ocean. Kufa, in the northwest, lay on the desert side of the Euphrates near al-Hira, and it looked toward the Sasanian heartland, the central plateau, and northern Mesopotamia. Both were rooted in the frontier landscape that had linked Arabia and Iraq for centuries, and Kufa in particular drew on a population already familiar with the region.

The two cities developed distinct characters. Their populations reflected different tribal groupings from Arabia, and each became associated with its own political tendencies and, later, its own traditions of legal and religious scholarship. Their armies were also the base for the campaigns onto the Iranian plateau described in Chapter 19, and for the settlement of soldiers and their families in Iranian lands in later decades. The revenue of the Sawād and of the Iranian provinces supported their stipends, giving each city a stake in the extraction of revenue from the conquered.

Persians and other local people quickly became part of these cities' populations. The Arabic sources mention groups of Sasanian troops, including cavalrymen known as asāwira, who joined the Arab-Muslim forces and settled in the garrison cities, one group reportedly at Basra around 642, and they mention laborers, craftsmen, and servants who accompanied or joined the settlers. The relationship between these newcomers and the Arab tribal core, including their legal and social standing, would become an important question in later chapters, especially in the discussion of clients in Chapter 24. For now, it is enough to note that the garrison cities were never populated exclusively by the conquerors.

Section 20.6

Syria and a Different Settlement Pattern

Syria did not follow the Iraqi pattern. Arab-Muslim forces settled in and around existing cities and in military camps, and no single new garrison city rose to overshadow the older urban network. Damascus, Homs, and other cities remained centers of population and administration, and Jerusalem retained its religious importance. A military camp at Jabiya, in the south, served as a focus of command in the tradition, but it did not become a lasting center of the type of Basra or Kufa. Later administrative units, the ajnād (singular jund), were organized around existing districts, though the timing and form of their creation are disputed.

Several reasons explain the difference. Syria was closer to Arabia, and its northern steppe had long been home to Arab groups who had served the Byzantine frontier system, so Arab presence in the region was not new. It contained many established cities, with walls, markets, and institutions that could house and supply an army. And the Byzantine provincial and military structure, including its fortifications and road network, provided a framework that the conquerors could inherit. The result was a pattern in which Arab-Muslim soldiers settled among existing communities, often in separate quarters or adjacent to the cities, rather than in wholly new foundations.

The first generation of settlement in Syria was also disrupted by natural disaster. A plague, remembered in the Arabic tradition by the name of ʿAmwas, struck Syria and Palestine around 638–639 and, according to the tradition, killed a number of prominent commanders. The scale of mortality and its effect on the settled population are uncertain, since the evidence comes largely from later accounts, but the event is a reminder that the demographic and economic circumstances of the first decades of conquest were not stable.

Section 20.7

Treaties, Capitulation, and Local Agreements

Chapter 19 introduced the distinction between ṣulḥ, a territory that surrendered on terms, and ʿanwa, a territory taken by force, and cautioned that it was a later legal categorization. This section examines what the remembered agreements meant in practice. Many cities and districts did negotiate, sometimes after a siege, sometimes before one, and the agreements typically involved a payment of tribute, a promise of security, and some guarantees for the inhabitants' property and religious institutions. The obligations of the conquerors, and the rights of the conquered, thus began as local bargains, and they varied by place and circumstance.

The texts of these agreements that survive in Arabic sources are problematic. Many are known only from historians of the ninth century and later, who preserved them in forms shaped by the legal debates of their own time. Texts attributed to ʿUmar concerning Jerusalem, or to commanders concerning other cities, may contain authentic elements, but they also reflect later concerns, including the status of land and the taxation of non-Muslims. A well-known document called the Pact of ʿUmar, which sets out restrictions on Christians, is in the form we have it a later legal compilation projected back onto the conquest generation, and it cannot be used as evidence for the terms of the earliest agreements.

What the memory of surrender agreements did do was shape later claims. Whether a district had capitulated or been taken by force was invoked by jurists and officials in disputes over the taxation of land, the rights of local populations, and the disposition of property. The categories thus acquired a life of their own, even when they did not accurately describe what had happened. Historians who use them to reconstruct the conquest itself run the risk of reading later legal arguments as if they were first-generation facts.

Section 20.8

Taxation and Revenue

Historical Vocabulary
Jizya and Kharāj

Jizya and kharāj are the two Arabic terms most often associated with taxation under early Muslim rule. In later legal usage, jizya came to mean a poll tax paid by non-Muslim adult men, and kharāj a tax on land. In the first decades after the conquests, the terms were much less fixed. Both could refer to tribute paid collectively by a community or district, and both were applied to payments that later jurists would have classified differently. Papyri and other documents from Egypt, Syria, and Iraq use Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian terms for taxes and demands, and their relationship to the later Arabic categories is often uncertain. Students should therefore avoid reading the later legal distinction back into the earliest arrangements. Chapter 28 examines the formal legal development of these categories.

The revenue that supported the army and the emerging state came from several sources. The main one was the taxation of the agrarian population. In many places, the conquerors took over the existing systems, including the assessment of land and the collection of payments in coin, kind, or both. In Egypt, the Byzantine system of land tax and payments in kind continued to operate, with demands now directed toward the Arab governor and his subordinates. In Iraq and Iran, the Sasanian system of land assessment and poll taxes provided a model. In Syria and Palestine, Byzantine taxation likewise continued, adapted to the new circumstances. Payments in kind, especially grain, oil, and livestock, supplied the army directly, as the sheep-receipt papyrus examined below shows.

Other sources of income included tribute paid by cities and districts under their agreements, the booty of conquest, including movable goods, captives, and, in some cases, estates that had belonged to the previous rulers, and the revenue from state lands. The tradition holds that in Iraq the former Sasanian crown lands and abandoned estates passed into the control of the new state, and that ʿUmar declined to distribute the lands of al-Sawād among the fighters, leaving them with their cultivators and taxing them instead. This account is important in later legal argument, and it is likely to reflect a real decision, but its details and its motivations are known through later sources and are debated.

The overall picture is of a system in which taxation was extensive, and at least in its administration largely inherited, but in which a new group of recipients had taken the place of the old. The revenue no longer went to Constantinople or Ctesiphon but to the soldiers and the officials of the caliphate. Whether the tax burden on the cultivators rose, fell, or stayed the same varied by place and year, and it is difficult to measure. Historians should resist the old claim that conquest lightened taxation, and equally the view that it invariably increased it. What the evidence supports is continuity of the basic mechanisms alongside a change in who benefited.

Section 20.9

Who Collected the Taxes?

The people who assessed, collected, and recorded taxes in the first generation of conquest were largely the same people who had done so before. In Egypt, local pagarchs and village officials, many of them Christian and Greek- or Coptic-speaking, continued to write and receive documents in Greek and, to a lesser extent, Coptic. In Palestine and Syria, Greek-speaking officials and local notables maintained administration. In Iraq and Iran, dihqans and Persian-speaking scribes carried on the work of the Sasanian fiscal system, using Middle Persian records. The conquest did not instantly create an Arabic-language bureaucracy, and Arabic appears in the early documents chiefly as an accompaniment to Greek or Middle Persian.

Surviving papyri and coins show this continuity. Bilingual Greek and Arabic documents, of which the papyrus in the Primary Source Window is an example, reveal a system in which Arab officials and Greek-writing local administrators worked side by side. Arab-Byzantine coins, minted in Syria, and Arab-Sasanian coins, minted in Iraq and Iran, imitated the earlier imperial coinage, often with additions of Arabic phrases or the names of governors. The new rulers were, in effect, taking over the machinery of their predecessors and adding their own names to it.

This reliance on inherited personnel had consequences. It meant that local elites retained a role in government and a share in its proceeds, and that the new rulers depended on them for information and cooperation. It gave non-Muslim officials influence in the new state. And it meant that the shift to Arabic as the language of administration, which occurred later and is examined in Chapter 23, was a deliberate change rather than an immediate consequence of conquest.

It shows that the new rulers were already using the Arabic language in writing and dating documents by the early 640s. It is also limited. Section 20.10

Landholding and the Countryside

Most of the population of the conquered provinces lived in villages and worked the land, and their lives were tied to agricultural cycles, irrigation systems, and landholding arrangements that predated the conquest. As Chapter 3 explained, the productivity of Egypt and Iraq depended on the management of river water through canals, embankments, and drainage, and on the coordinated labor that such management required. These systems continued to function under the new rulers. The state's interest in revenue gave it a reason to maintain them, and the local officials and village communities who ran them remained in place.

In Iraq, the countryside was organized around estates and villages, many controlled by dihqans and other landowners, alongside land that had belonged to the Sasanian crown or to the church and priesthood. In Egypt, land was held by large estates, by the church, and by villagers, and the Byzantine system of assessing land and collecting taxes in kind remained in operation. In Syria and Palestine, villages and estates, monasteries and towns, continued to farm, and archaeology suggests that many rural settlements were not immediately disrupted. Change occurred in some districts, where estates were abandoned or passed into state hands, and where local elites lost or gained influence, but the general picture is one of continuity in production and social organization.

The conquerors did not, in general, become landlords in the first generation. Living in cities on stipends, they had no immediate need to. Over time, this changed, as members of the elite acquired estates and as the distinction between military and landed status blurred. But in the years covered by this chapter, the countryside remained the domain of local farmers, elites, and institutions, and the caliphate stood above it as a claimant to its revenue. Chapter 26 follows the later development of agriculture and landholding under Abbasid rule.

Section 20.11

Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Other Communities

The populations of the conquered lands belonged overwhelmingly to non-Muslim religious communities, and these communities were among the principal social institutions of the provinces. Churches owned land, ran schools and charities, and provided leadership and legal adjudication for their members. Synagogue communities and rabbinic authorities did the same for Jews, and the Zoroastrian priesthood and fire temples served the Iranian population. The new rulers, in general, left these institutions in place, and their leaders became intermediaries between their communities and the authorities.

Experience varied by region. In Egypt, the Miaphysite church, which had been out of favor under Byzantine imperial policy, continued under its own leadership, while the Chalcedonian community, tied to the Byzantine state, lost its imperial patron. In Syria and Palestine, Christian communities of several kinds continued, with Jerusalem's Christian institutions remaining central to the city's religious life. In Iraq and Iran, the Church of the East continued to function. Its patriarch Ishoʿyahb III (c. 649–659), writing around 650–652, portrayed the Arab rulers as not opposing Christianity and as honoring clergy and aiding churches and monasteries, while criticizing Christians in Mazun who had abandoned their faith. His account is a contemporary Christian assertion with its own rhetorical purposes, not an independent description of state policy. Jewish communities in Iraq, Palestine, and elsewhere continued their own institutions. Zoroastrian communities remained the dominant religious group in much of Iran and retained their priests and temples, though they lost the support of a Zoroastrian state.

The sources for these communities' relations with the conquerors are limited and mixed. Christian, Jewish, and other writings provide contemporary or near-contemporary perspectives, but they are few and reflect specific concerns. Later legal literature, including the terms later attributed to the conquest generation, projects the regulations of a later age. This chapter describes the relationship in terms of practice, arrangement, and negotiation, and it leaves the formal legal category of dhimma, the protection of non-Muslim communities under Islamic law, to Chapter 28. The main point here is that provincial religious institutions survived, kept their leadership, and functioned as recognized communities within the new order.

Section 20.12

Conversion Was Not Yet the Main Story

This chapter's account of rule over old societies depends on a point already established in Chapter 19: in the years after conquest, most of the people of Syria, Palestine, Iraq, Egypt, and Iran remained non-Muslim. Some individuals and groups converted, especially those in close contact with the garrison cities, such as captives, clients, and people who joined the settlers, but the great majority of the population continued in its inherited religions. There is no evidence of a systematic policy of mass conversion in this first generation, and the state's heavy dependence on revenue from overwhelmingly non-Muslim provincial populations complicated the fiscal consequences of conversion, a problem Chapter 24 examines.

The practical relations between Muslims and non-Muslims in this period were thus those of a ruling minority and a subject majority, mediated by agreements, taxes, and local institutions. Muslims lived largely in garrison cities and, in Syria, in existing towns and quarters, and non-Muslims lived in the countryside and in other quarters and cities. Contact occurred in markets, in the administration, and through households, but the two populations were largely distinct.

The longer processes of conversion, adoption of the Arabic language, and social integration belong to later chapters, especially Chapter 24, which distinguishes Arabization from Islamization and examines the tensions over converts. They unfolded over generations, at different speeds in different regions. Readers should not project them backward onto the first decades. The society this chapter describes was still a society in which the ruling and ruled populations remained largely separate in religion, in language, and in residence.

Section 20.13

Everyday Contact between Conquerors and Conquered

Despite the separation, conquerors and conquered met constantly. Soldiers needed food, clothing, animals, housing, and services, and the cities and villages around them supplied these things through markets, requisitions, and taxes in kind. Local craftsmen, laborers, merchants, and servants worked in and around the garrison cities. Local scribes and officials served the governors and their staff. The result was a daily exchange in which each population depended on the other, even as they remained distinct.

Marriage and household ties also linked the two populations. Arab men, who often arrived without their families or in smaller numbers, took local wives and concubines, and captives taken in the conquests joined households as servants and dependents. The children of such unions grew up in contact with both cultures, and their status and identity would become an important issue in later decades. Language contact followed. Bilingual and multilingual documents show Greek, Coptic, Middle Persian, and Arabic in contact, and the sources mention interpreters and local intermediaries. The result was an early stage of the linguistic mixing whose later development is examined in Chapter 24.

The conquests also involved the taking of captives and the movement of enslaved people, whose labor and presence shaped households and cities. The sources record this unevenly, from the perspectives of the conquerors and their descendants, and they rarely permit a clear view of the experiences of the enslaved themselves. Historians should acknowledge both the reality and the difficulty of the evidence. Chapter 29 returns to slavery in the Abbasid world.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living Alongside the New Rulers

For a village farmer in Egypt or Iraq, the arrival of the new rulers might mean, in the first instance, a change in the person to whom taxes were paid and in the demands made on the village for supplies. The village official, the tax assessor, and the local priest or elder often remained the same, and the agricultural year continued. For a townsperson in Fustat, Kufa, or Basra, the changes were more immediate, since the arrival of a large military population created new markets, new demand for labor and goods, and new tensions over housing and status.

For the soldiers and their families, life in the garrison cities meant living in a new kind of community, organized by tribe, dependent on stipends, and separated from the surrounding population. Women, whose presence in these cities is recorded unevenly, managed households in a setting that combined tribal ties with new circumstances. For non-Muslims, life continued within their religious communities, which provided law, worship, and social support, while they paid taxes and dealt with the new authorities through their own leaders. Almost all surviving sources were written by officials, chroniclers, and clerics, and they rarely record the daily concerns of ordinary people, which historians must infer from papyri, archaeology, and comparison.

Section 20.14

Provincial Variation

Comparing the provinces makes the chapter's central point concrete: there was no single Arab conquest administration. In Iraq, the conquerors concentrated in two new garrison cities on the edge of the alluvial plain and drew revenue from the Sawād through Sasanian-era officials and landowners. In Egypt, they founded a single garrison city at the head of the Delta and worked through Byzantine-era local officials, in Greek and Coptic, with Alexandria continuing as a major Greek-Christian center. In Syria, they settled among existing cities and drew on Byzantine structures, with no comparable new garrison city, and with a different tribal and military composition shaped by the region's Arab populations.

Iran differed again. There, Arab-Muslim rule was established through a mixture of agreements with local rulers and dihqans, tribute, and periodic campaigns, and control was uneven and in some regions nominal. Garrisons were settled in some strategic places, and later, in the Umayyad period, soldiers from the Iraqi garrison cities were settled in Khurasan. But the first generation of Arab-Muslim rule in Iran was, in most areas, a matter of tribute and overlordship rather than direct administration. Chapters 22 and 24 examine the later transformation of these arrangements.

These differences reflected the differing structures the conquerors inherited, the different geographies and populations they encountered, and the different forces available to them. They should discourage any attempt to describe the early caliphate as if it were a single, uniformly governed empire. It was a collection of provinces governed by governors and commanders, with varying arrangements, linked by their common obligation to the caliph and by the flow of revenue and troops between them.

Section 20.15

From Military Occupation to Provincial Government

Over the first generation, improvised arrangements began to harden into institutions. Governors, at first largely military commanders, acquired staffs and routines. The registers of soldiers and the payment of stipends became more regular. Tax collection came to be organized through recognized channels, and the record-keeping of the earlier administrations was adapted to the new regime's needs. Mosques, governors' residences, and markets gave the garrison cities physical form, and the cities themselves became centers of provincial administration as well as of military life.

This process was gradual and uneven, and it should not be confused with the more deliberate reorganization of the state under the Umayyads, which Chapters 22 and 23 examine. In the first generation, the caliph in Medina exercised authority over provincial governors through appointment, correspondence, and the flow of revenue and troops, but the degree of central control varied, and governors and commanders often acted with considerable autonomy. The structure that emerged was one of provinces, largely self-administering, tied to the caliph by personal loyalty, shared religious community, and shared interest in the proceeds of conquest.

This structure was durable enough to hold the conquests together in the 640s, and it provided the basis on which later rulers built. But it also had built-in tensions, including the reliance on governors of independent standing, the dependence of soldiers on provincial revenue, and the competing claims of different groups within the conquering population. The next section examines these.

Section 20.16

Tensions in the Conquest Order

The conquest order created inequalities among the conquerors themselves. Those who had joined the community early or fought in the first campaigns claimed precedence over later arrivals, including many who had joined after the Ridda wars or during the conquests, and stipends, at least in the tradition, reflected this hierarchy. Different tribal groups had different numbers, different histories, and different relationships to the caliph and to one another. The garrison cities each had their own tribal balances, and the interests of the soldiers in Kufa and Basra did not always coincide with those of the governors appointed from Medina.

There were also tensions between the conquerors and the provincial populations, and between the soldiers who depended on stipends and the taxpayers who supplied them. The revenue of the provinces was finite, and the claims of a growing military population could exceed it. Disputes over the distribution of revenue and land, over the appointment of governors, and over the precedence of different groups became increasingly important. The conquest of new territory eased some of these pressures by providing new revenue, but its slowing would increase them.

These tensions did not by themselves produce the political crisis that followed ʿUmar's death. That crisis had causes of its own, examined in Chapter 21. But the structure of the conquest order, with its separation of soldiers from provincial society, its dependence on provincial revenue, and its hierarchy of precedence and tribal interests, provided the conditions in which political conflict could arise and spread. This chapter therefore ends by pointing forward: the arrangements that allowed a small elite to rule large populations were also arrangements that made that elite vulnerable to division.

Section 20.17

Continuity or Transformation?

Historical Debate

Did the Early Caliphate Inherit Its Structures or Create a New Order?

The question: How far did the early caliphate simply take over Byzantine and Sasanian institutions, and how far did the conquests already create a fundamentally new political order?

Interpretation A — Continuity: Emphasizes that provincial administration, taxation, record-keeping, coinage, and local elites remained largely in place, and that the caliphate for its first generation was essentially a new military overlay on inherited structures. Documentary evidence, such as bilingual papyri and imitative coinage, supports this view.
Interpretation B — Transformation: Emphasizes that the placing of a new ruling elite over the provinces, the garrison-city system, the payment of soldiers from provincial revenue, and the emergence of a new religious and political community together constituted a new order whose long-term effects were far-reaching, even if its early administration looked familiar.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same evidence, including papyri, coins, non-Muslim writings, archaeology, and later Arabic tradition, and differ in how they weigh administrative continuity against the new political and military arrangements. They also differ in how much weight to give later accounts that describe the conquest generation as creating systems that may in fact have developed more gradually.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the pace of change. A stress on continuity suggests that transformation came later and was driven by internal developments, such as the Umayyad reforms and the growth of a Muslim majority. A stress on transformation suggests that the conquest itself set decisive patterns. Most historians combine both views, recognizing continuity in administration and selective innovation in military and political organization.

Student task: Choose one province and identify which of its early arrangements you would classify as continuity and which as innovation. What further evidence would help decide the borderline cases?

The debate should not be settled by choosing a side. The early caliphate inherited an administrative machinery and continued to use it, but it also introduced a new military settlement, a new pattern of payment, and a new political and religious framework. Continuity in tools did not mean continuity in purpose, since the same taxes now supported a different ruling group. Conversely, innovation in organization did not mean the immediate transformation of provincial society. The chapter's evidence points to a combination that historians describe in different proportions.

What Remained Continuous

  • The basic mechanisms of land assessment and tax collection
  • The local officials, scribes, and notables who ran them
  • The languages of administration, including Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian
  • The religious communities and their institutions
  • Agriculture, irrigation, and village life

Who experienced change most directly? Members of the former imperial elite, soldiers and families who moved into the garrison cities, and townspeople near them. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers, local officials, and religious leaders. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the caliphate's stability depended on preserving much of the existing order while replacing its apex, and it explains why the changes examined in Chapters 22 through 24 came later and required deliberate action.

Section 20.18

A New Ruling Order over Old Societies

By the mid-640s, the shape of the conquest order was visible. A military and political elite, organized by tribe and paid from provincial revenue, lived in garrison cities and, in Syria, in existing towns, and ruled through governors appointed from Medina. Beneath them stood provincial societies whose administration, economies, languages, and religious institutions had largely persisted. The two were connected by tribute and taxes, by local officials and negotiated agreements, and by daily exchange, but they remained distinct.

This arrangement gave the caliphate both strength and weakness. Its strength lay in its ability to draw on existing structures, keep its soldiers together, and avoid the burdens of directly administering a vast, diverse population. Its weakness lay in the dependence of its military elite on provincial revenue, the inequalities among the conquerors, and the limited machinery of central control. Chapter 21 turns to the political conflict that these conditions helped make possible, beginning with the crisis following ʿUmar's death and the struggle over the caliphate that followed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how a relatively small Arab-Muslim military and political elite actually ruled the populated provinces it had conquered. Its central argument is that the early conquests did not replace the societies of the former Byzantine and Sasanian provinces. They placed a new ruling order over existing populations, institutions, economies, languages, and religious communities, and the early caliphate survived because it combined new military settlement and political authority with extensive use of inherited provincial structures and negotiated local arrangements.

The chapter began with the problem of occupation: feeding, housing, and paying armies, collecting revenue, and finding a working relationship with populations that could be neither converted nor replaced. It emphasized that later Arabic tradition presents the solutions as the deliberate plan of a single caliph, while the evidence suggests that many arrangements emerged gradually and varied by province.

The garrison cities were the most distinctive feature of the new order. Basra and Kufa in Iraq, and Fustat in Egypt, were traditionally founded in the late 630s and early 640s, at the edge of cultivated land and close to routes back to the desert. They housed soldiers organized by tribe, paid through stipends drawn from provincial revenue, and they grew into political, commercial, and cultural centers. The dates and motives given by the tradition are uncertain, but the pattern is well supported.

Syria followed a different path. Arab-Muslim forces settled among existing cities and camps rather than founding a single dominant new city, reflecting the region's dense urban network, its earlier Arab populations, and the Byzantine structures the conquerors inherited. The comparison shows that there was no single Arab conquest administration, a point reinforced by Iran, where control was established through agreements with local rulers and dihqans and was uneven for decades.

Taxation and administration showed continuity beneath a change in recipients. Remembered surrender agreements shaped later claims about land and rights, but their surviving texts are often later reconstructions, and later legal categories such as ṣulḥ and ʿanwa, jizya and kharāj, cannot be read back mechanically into the first decades. Local officials, scribes, and notables continued to collect revenue and keep records in Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian, as papyri and imitative coinage show. The bilingual papyrus of 643 illustrated a system in which an Arabic element appeared alongside inherited administrative forms.

The countryside continued to function under existing landholding and irrigation arrangements, with the conquerors living on stipends in cities rather than becoming landlords. Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities retained their institutions and leaders and negotiated with the new authorities, with regional variation. Conversion was not yet the main story. Most people remained non-Muslim, and the longer processes of Arabization and Islamization belong to Chapter 24.

Everyday contact between conquerors and conquered occurred through markets, labor, supply, households, and language, and it also involved the taking and movement of captives, a reality the sources record unevenly. Over the first generation, improvised arrangements began to harden into institutions, though central control varied and governors often acted with considerable autonomy.

The conquest order also created tensions: inequalities of precedence among the conquerors, the dependence of soldiers on finite provincial revenue, tribal and regional rivalries, and limited machinery of central control. These conditions did not by themselves cause the political crisis that followed ʿUmar's death, but they helped create the conditions for it, a subject taken up in Chapter 21. Historians disagree about the balance of continuity and transformation in the early caliphate, and this chapter's evidence supports a combination: continuity in administration and daily life, and selective innovation in military settlement and political organization.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how conquest became rule. Chapter 19 explained how armies overturned two empires; this chapter shows that the result was not the replacement of provincial societies but the placing of a new elite over them. That distinction explains much of what follows: why the caliphate could hold together, why its early administration looked so familiar, and why later changes required deliberate action.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. It shows how a minority can rule a majority by concentrating its own members, paying them from the revenue of the ruled, and relying on inherited local institutions. It shows how memory can reshape events, as later authors attributed to a single caliph arrangements that may have emerged gradually. And it shows how documents such as papyri and coins can correct or complicate narrative sources.

Later developments grew directly from these arrangements. Chapter 21 will examine the political conflict that arose within the caliphate after ʿUmar's death, in a setting shaped by the tensions described here. Chapters 22 and 23 will show how the Umayyads reorganized administration, coinage, and language. Chapter 24 will explain how conversion and Arabization gradually transformed the societies the conquerors had taken over. The garrison cities themselves, Basra, Kufa, and Fustat, became major centers of the early Islamic world.

Continuities survived beyond the chapter: local officials, village communities, religious institutions, languages, and agricultural rhythms persisted, and in many cases shaped the societies of the Arab world for centuries. What the conquests created was a new political and military order that had to learn to govern old societies, and the history of how those societies and that order gradually changed one another is the subject of the chapters that follow.
