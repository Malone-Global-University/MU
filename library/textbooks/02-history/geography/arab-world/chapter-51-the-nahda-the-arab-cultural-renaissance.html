Part IX — Reform, Nahda, and European Expansion
51

The Nahda: The Arab Cultural Renaissance

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In 1826 a young Egyptian religious scholar, Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, sailed from Alexandria to France as imam to a group of students sent by Muhammad Ali. Within a few years he had learned French, read widely, and begun writing an Arabic account of what he saw. Nearly seventy years later, in 1892, a Syrian-born woman in Alexandria, Hind Nawfal, published the first issue of a magazine for women. Between those two moments, Arabic readers acquired printing presses, newspapers, modern schools, translated books, serialized novels, public theaters, and a new vocabulary for talking about society, science, and the past.

This chapter studies that transformation, usually called the Nahda, a term often translated "awakening," "renaissance," or "revival." It asks how Arabic-speaking teachers, translators, editors, publishers, poets, novelists, journalists, actors, and readers changed the way knowledge was produced and circulated across the nineteenth century, and how those changes reshaped Arab cultural life from Cairo and Alexandria to Beirut, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, Tunis, and Arabic-reading communities abroad.

The chapter's central argument is that the Nahda was not a single coordinated movement, nor a sudden awakening from centuries of intellectual sleep, nor simply the arrival of European ideas in a passive region. It grew out of the interaction of several things: older Arabic traditions of scholarship, literature, and religious learning; Egyptian state-building and Ottoman reform; missionary and communal schools; commercial publishing; European intellectual influence; and countless local initiatives by people who found that the new media allowed them to reach readers they could never have reached through manuscripts or lecture halls alone.

The chapter's chronology runs from the early nineteenth-century foundations laid in Egypt and Mount Lebanon to approximately 1914, with the most sustained analysis concentrated between the 1840s and the eve of the First World War. Its subject is intellectual and cultural production: printing, journalism, language, translation, education, science, literature, theater, women's participation, and the communities and networks that sustained them. The institutional history of Egypt's state under Muhammad Ali (Chapter 49) and the Ottoman Tanzimat (Chapter 50) provides the political setting, but is not retold here.

The Nahda also needs to be kept distinct from the political movements of the same decades. Many Nahda figures wrote about the homeland, about reform, and about the Arabic language as a shared inheritance, and some of their ideas later fed into political projects. But cultural revival is not the same thing as Arab nationalism, and treating it as such would obscure how many writers were loyal Ottoman subjects, Egyptian patriots, religious reformers, or simply educators and entrepreneurs. The systematic analysis of Ottomanism, Arabism, constitutionalism, Zionism, and reformist Islam belongs to Chapter 54.

Historians reconstruct the Nahda from an unusually rich body of evidence: printed books and their prefaces, newspapers and periodicals, school records, missionary reports, memoirs, letters, travel accounts, biographical dictionaries, and surviving manuscripts. This evidence overrepresents literate, urban, and often elite participants, a limitation the chapter returns to repeatedly. Recent scholarship has also warned against treating the Nahda as simply an Arabic copy of the European Renaissance or as a story of a few famous writers, and the chapter's final sections evaluate those debates.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand how a society of manuscripts, teaching circles, and oral performance became, within about three generations, one in which newspapers, magazines, novels, plays, and schools were central to public life, and why this transformation, uneven and unequal as it was, prepared the ground for the political developments of Chapters 54 through 60.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what historians mean by the Nahda and why its definition, chronology, and geography remain debated.
  2. Describe the older Arabic scholarly, literary, and religious traditions on which the Nahda built, and explain why it should not be treated as a revival from nothing.
  3. Trace the expansion of Arabic printing from limited early presses to a commercial publishing industry.
  4. Distinguish among official gazettes, private newspapers, scientific and literary magazines, and women's periodicals as different kinds of print media.
  5. Analyze how translation, linguistic reform, and new curricula changed Arabic as a language of journalism, science, and education.
  6. Compare the development of poetry, prose, fiction, theater, and popular cultural forms, and explain why each followed a different path.
  7. Assess the contributions of women writers and the women's press to the Nahda and to debates about gender and society.
  8. Evaluate the roles of Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities, and of missionary, state, and private institutions, in shaping Arabic cultural life.
  9. Contextualize the differences among Cairo, Alexandria, Beirut, Damascus, Aleppo, Jerusalem, Baghdad, Tunis, and the migrant communities of the mahjar.
  10. Interpret primary sources from the period with attention to authorship, audience, and the perspectives they leave out.
  11. Evaluate competing historians' interpretations of the Nahda and its limits.
  12. Connect the cultural transformations of this chapter to the political movements examined in Chapter 54.

  2. Why did printing, journalism, and schooling expand when and where they did, and who financed and controlled them?
  3. What was gained and what was lost when Arabic writers adopted new literary genres such as the novel and the play?

Historical Context

At the start of the nineteenth century, Arabic was the language of religion, law, scholarship, and poetry across a vast area, but most Arab provinces were Ottoman, governed largely in Ottoman Turkish at the imperial level and in a mixture of languages locally. Books circulated almost entirely as handwritten manuscripts, copied by professional scribes and scholars and sold or donated through endowed libraries. Learning passed through teaching circles in mosque-colleges such as al-Azhar in Cairo, in Ottoman madrasas, in Sufi lodges, in church and monastery schools, and in the households of scholars and notables. Chapter 46 examined this world of religion, learning, and communal life in the Ottoman centuries.

Three developments set the stage for the Nahda. First, Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 brought a French press with Arabic type and a corps of scholars, and its aftermath brought Muhammad Ali's program of military and educational modernization (Chapter 49). Second, the Ottoman Tanzimat reforms, beginning in 1839, expanded state schools, official translation, and administrative publication across the empire (Chapter 50). Third, European and American missionary societies, and later Catholic and Protestant educational orders, opened schools and presses in the Levant, especially in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, where they interacted with long-established Maronite, Greek Catholic, and Orthodox communal institutions.

Two cautions guide this chapter. First, it does not repeat the political, military, and administrative histories assigned to Chapters 49 and 50, and it only introduces the political ideas that Chapter 54 will analyze. Second, it treats the Nahda as an Arab-speaking and Ottoman phenomenon at once: many of its participants were Ottoman subjects, read and sometimes wrote in Turkish and French as well as Arabic, and worked in Istanbul and Paris as well as Cairo and Beirut. Its history is also parallel to similar print and literary developments among Ottoman Turks, Armenians, Greeks, and Persians, with whom Arab writers were sometimes in close contact.

Division I — Origins and Intellectual Foundations · Section 51.1

What Was the Nahda? The Meaning of Arab Cultural Revival

The Arabic word nahda comes from a root meaning to rise or stand up. Nineteenth-century writers used related words for the revival of learning and of the Arabic language, and the term came to be applied widely to the period in the twentieth century, notably in George Antonius's The Arab Awakening (1938) and Albert Hourani's Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age (1962). It is thus partly a retrospective label, one that later writers used to give coherence to developments that those living through them did not experience as a single movement.

Its boundaries are contested. Some historians emphasize the decades from the 1830s or 1840s to 1914; others begin with al-Tahtawi or even with Napoleon's invasion, and some extend the period into the 1930s. Its principal centers were Cairo and Alexandria, Beirut and Mount Lebanon, and Istanbul, but important activity also occurred in Damascus, Aleppo, Jerusalem, Baghdad, Tunis, and abroad. Debates about origins concern how much weight to give European contact, Egyptian state reform, missionary education, Ottoman reform, and older Arabic traditions; recent scholarship gives all of these a role and warns against assigning the Nahda a single cause or a single geography.

Three misunderstandings are worth avoiding. The Nahda was not a European-style Renaissance, since Arab writers were not recovering a classical past that had been lost, but reworking a living Arabic tradition. It was not an awakening from stagnation, a picture that rests on a one-sided reading of the eighteenth century. And it was not identical to Arab nationalism, which emerged later and was only one of several political outcomes of cultural revival.

Section 51.2

Intellectual Life before the Nahda: Continuity and Transformation

The Arabic intellectual world of the eighteenth century was active and varied. In Cairo, scholars such as Murtada al-Zabidi worked on lexicography and the study of the Arabic language, and the chronicler 'Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti recorded the French occupation. Al-Azhar's teaching circles trained jurists, theologians, and scholars; Sufi lodges transmitted devotional knowledge; and private and endowed (waqf) libraries preserved thousands of manuscripts. Authority in this world came from ijaza, a teacher's certificate permitting a student to transmit a text, and from the reputation of scholarly families. Poetry circulated through recitation and manuscript anthologies, and in cities such as Damascus, Aleppo, and Baghdad, scholarly and literary households maintained their own traditions.

Printing was not wholly absent. The earliest printed Arabic texts were produced in Italy in the early sixteenth century. In the Ottoman lands, Ibrahim Müteferrika's press in Istanbul (1727) printed works in Ottoman Turkish, and Christian communities in the Levant, notably Greek Orthodox in Aleppo (1706) and Greek Catholic and Maronite monasteries in Mount Lebanon, produced Arabic religious books in small numbers in the eighteenth century. Maronite education in the Levant and at Rome also trained scholars of Arabic and Syriac. These early efforts were limited, but they created experience and personnel on which the nineteenth-century presses drew.

For historians, the evidence for this earlier world comes from manuscripts and their colophons, ijaza records, waqf documents, biographical dictionaries, and chronicles. The most important point for the Nahda is continuity: the new print culture was built upon, and for decades coexisted with, a scholarly culture of manuscripts and teaching circles, and many Nahda writers were products of that older training.

Section 51.3

Egypt and the Early Foundations of Cultural Reform

Muhammad Ali's schools in medicine, engineering, and languages, and his government's translation and printing efforts, created a new group of Arabic-reading and writing specialists. Chapter 49 treats the state-building; here the focus is cultural consequence. The school of medicine (1827), the School of Languages (1835), and a translation bureau working from French and Turkish produced technical manuals and scientific works in Arabic and trained a generation of translators who then had to invent Arabic equivalents for new concepts.

Rifa'a al-Tahtawi was the central figure. A student and protégé of the Azhar scholar Hasan al-'Attar, he spent five years in Paris (1826–1831) and published an Arabic account of France in 1834 (Takhlis al-ibriz fi talkhis Bariz). He later directed the School of Languages and its translation program, supervised the translation of many works, and wrote on education, patriotism, and Egyptian history. Under Abbas I (r. 1848–1854) the educational program was curtailed and Tahtawi was posted to Khartoum, but under Khedive Isma'il (r. 1863–1879) a renewed program brought the periodical Rawdat al-Madaris (1870), the Dar al-Kutub library (1870), and the teacher-training college Dar al-'Ulum (1872), associated with Ali Mubarak.

These institutions created a new reading and writing class, often called the effendiyya, trained in state schools and employed in the administration, the schools, and the press. They also encouraged a new vocabulary of the watan (homeland) and of Egypt's ancient past. This early Egyptian foundation was a state-sponsored and relatively elite phenomenon, and it is one reason why the broader cultural movement later centered on private initiatives in Beirut and Alexandria.

Section 51.4

Beirut and Greater Syria: Schools, Scholarship, and Intellectual Associations

Beirut grew rapidly in the nineteenth century from a modest port into the commercial and intellectual hub of the Syrian coast. The Egyptian occupation of Syria (1831–1840) opened the country more fully to foreign merchants and missionaries, and Ottoman restoration after 1840 left that opening largely in place. Steamship connections, European consuls, a mercantile class of Christian and Muslim families, and silk exports from Mount Lebanon created both the money and the demand for schools, printing, and reading. In 1888 Beirut became the capital of its own Ottoman province (vilayet), confirming a status it already held in cultural life.

Schools came first, and from several sources. Maronite, Greek Catholic, and Greek Orthodox communities already ran church schools, among them the Maronite college at 'Ayn Waraqa (founded 1789). American Presbyterian missionaries arrived in the 1820s and moved their press from Malta to Beirut in 1834; Eli Smith and, from the 1840s, Cornelius Van Dyck supervised textbooks in geography, arithmetic, astronomy, and chemistry printed in Arabic. French Catholic orders, above all the Jesuits, who opened a press that produced the weekly al-Bashir (1870) and Arabic-language books, competed with the Protestants for students. These institutions were driven by missionary and denominational aims, but they depended on Arab teachers and translators, who gained training, income, and access to type and presses.

The most consequential collaboration was the Arabic Bible. Smith and Van Dyck, working with Arab scholars, above all Butrus al-Bustani, Nasif al-Yaziji, and the Azhar-trained Yusuf al-Asir, produced a translation whose New Testament appeared in 1860 and whose complete Bible appeared in 1865. The project was a linguistic as well as religious enterprise: its translators argued about idiom, avoided the ornate rhymed prose of much formal writing, and aimed at a clear modern Arabic that could be read widely. The Jesuits later produced a Catholic Arabic Bible (Section 51.11). The translations show how missionary projects became sites of Arab scholarship, and how Arabic stylistic debates were shaped partly in them.

Learned societies provided a second foundation. The Society of Arts and Sciences (1847) brought together American missionaries and Arab scholars, including Bustani and Nasif al-Yaziji, to hear and publish papers in Arabic on science, history, and literature. Its successor, the Syrian Scientific Society (1857, revived in 1868), recruited a wider membership of Christians, Muslims, and Druze and gave Arabic a public role in scientific discussion. Such societies were small, their membership elite, and their relations with missionary sponsors sometimes uneasy, but they made Beirut a place where learned argument happened in Arabic and in public.

The violence of 1860 in Mount Lebanon and Damascus gave these efforts new urgency. Bustani responded with the Nafir Suriyya pamphlets (see the Nafir Suriyya source window following Division II) and in 1863 opened the National School (al-Madrasa al-Wataniyya), which accepted students of different religions and taught Arabic, sciences, and European languages. Higher education followed with the Syrian Protestant College (1866, later the American University of Beirut), founded by Daniel Bliss, which at first taught in Arabic, and the Jesuit Université Saint-Joseph (1875). Muslim notables created their own institutions: the newspaper Thamarat al-Funun (1875), edited by 'Abd al-Qadir al-Qabbani, and the Maqasid Islamic charitable society (1878), which opened schools. Beirut's cultural life thus involved several communities, motives, and funders, and the Ottoman state, which regulated schools and presses, was always a presence.

Beirut's importance should not be overstated: it was one center among several, and its prominence owed much to a particular combination of foreign resources, commercial wealth, and Ottoman tolerance that did not exist everywhere. By the 1880s many of its leading journalists were moving to Egypt, a migration examined in Sections 51.7 and 51.24.

Historical Vocabulary
Nahda

Nahda (Arabic al-nahda) means "rising" or "awakening." It is used by historians for the cultural and intellectual transformation of the Arabic-speaking world from roughly the nineteenth century to the early twentieth, involving printing, journalism, education, language, and literature.

The term is a retrospective label rather than the name of an organized movement, and it came into wide use only later. English translations such as "Arab Renaissance" or "Awakening" can mislead: "Renaissance" suggests a recovery of a lost classical past on the European model, and "Awakening" suggests prior sleep. Many scholars now prefer to speak of the Nahda as a set of overlapping cultural transformations. The term is also sometimes extended beyond cultural change to political projects, a practice this chapter avoids.

Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, Takhlis al-ibriz fi talkhis Bariz (1834)

Creator: Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, Egyptian scholar and translator · Date: written after 1826–1831, published 1834 · Type: travel narrative and descriptive report on France

Context: Tahtawi served as imam of Muhammad Ali's student mission to Paris. He studied French and read French books, and wrote his account with the support of Egypt's government, which saw the work as useful for officials and students.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): The book follows the voyage from Alexandria to Marseille, then describes Paris: its streets, institutions, and customs; the French political order, including the constitutional charter, which Tahtawi explains to Arabic readers; and French learning, with its schools, libraries, and scientific societies. Throughout he urges Egyptians to take up European sciences and arts while remaining within Islamic and Arabic frameworks, and, as scholars have noted, he explains unfamiliar political ideas such as liberty and equality by relating them to Islamic conceptions of justice and equity.

Analysis: The book was printed at Bulaq in 1834 and, it is reported, was translated into Turkish for officials at Muhammad Ali's direction, which shows that the government regarded it as useful for administrators and students. It is valuable as an early, sustained Arabic encounter with modern Europe by a religious scholar rather than a diplomat or soldier, and its method of translating the unfamiliar into familiar concepts anticipates later Nahda practice. Historians read it together with Tahtawi's later works on education and the homeland, such as Manahij al-albab al-misriyya (1869) and al-Murshid al-amin (1872), to see how his ideas developed. Its limitations are equally important: Tahtawi wrote as a state-sponsored author with a patron, so the work is partly an argument for reform; it describes Paris mainly through the experience of a man in an official mission, selecting what to praise and what to pass over; and it says little about the views of Egypt's peasants, artisans, and women.

Division II — Printing, Journalism, and the New Public Sphere · Section 51.5

The Expansion of Arabic Printing

Arabic printing expanded in three overlapping phases. In the first, from the 1820s, the Bulaq Press in Cairo, founded by Muhammad Ali, printed mainly military, medical, scientific, and administrative works, many of them translations. Its early directors included the Syrian-born Niqula Masabki, who had learned printing in Italy, and its output was prepared by correctors drawn from al-Azhar, among them Nasr al-Hurini, whose task was to establish sound texts. A press was therefore a meeting point of foreign technology, Syrian and European printers, and Egyptian scholars.

In the second phase, from the 1830s onward, mission presses in Beirut and Malta and Jesuit presses produced school books, Bibles, devotional works, and eventually general reading. The American mission's press attended closely to type design, and its Arabic fonts were admired and imitated. Faris al-Shidyaq, who worked at the mission press in Malta before moving to Paris and Istanbul, is a reminder that skilled Arab writers and correctors moved among presses across the Mediterranean.

In the third phase, especially from the 1860s and 1870s, private printers and publishers in Beirut, Cairo, Alexandria, Istanbul, and other cities competed for readers, producing newspapers, magazines, novels, and editions of classical texts. Bulaq itself increasingly printed classical Arabic works, including the Arabian Nights (1835), Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddima (1857), and the great dictionary Lisan al-'Arab (1880s). These editions made rare and costly texts widely available, and because correctors collated manuscripts, they tied the new industry directly to the learned world of the old one.

The material problems were considerable. Arabic script's joined letters and variable forms required large fonts with many ligatures and sorts, making type costly to cut and slow to set; paper was imported; and trained compositors were scarce. Different regions therefore used different methods. Movable type predominated in Egypt and the Levant, while lithography, which reproduced a calligrapher's handwritten page and avoided the cost of type, was widely used in the Maghreb, for example in Morocco, and for some religious and school texts elsewhere. Print runs were small by modern standards, and editions of a few hundred to a few thousand copies were typical; precise figures for most titles are not reliably documented.

Early resistance to printing should not be exaggerated: religious scholars were soon among the largest users of the press, and classical Islamic texts were among its most important products. The decisive changes were economic and organizational: cheaper paper, improved type, telegraph and postal services, and growing readership allowed print to move from state and mission sponsorship toward commercial publishing. Historians reconstruct this history from colophons, prefaces, press accounts, missionary reports, and the books themselves, which show who printed them, with what fonts, and for whom.

Section 51.6

The Rise of Arabic Newspapers

The first Arabic newspaper in the Ottoman world was the Egyptian official gazette, al-Waqa'i al-Misriyya, begun in 1828 and initially published in Turkish and Arabic. It reported government decisions, appointments, and foreign news, and its readership was chiefly officials; it was also an instrument for spreading a standard administrative Arabic. Official gazettes soon appeared elsewhere in the empire, such as Suriya in Damascus (1865), al-Furat in Aleppo (1867), and al-Zawra' in Baghdad (1869), usually bilingual and under the governor's direction. Muhammad Abduh edited al-Waqa'i briefly in 1880, showing that official papers could also host reformist argument.

Private newspapers followed. Mir'at al-Ahwal (1855), published in Istanbul by Rizqallah Hassun, and Hadiqat al-Akhbar (1858), published in Beirut by Khalil al-Khuri, are among the first private Arabic papers. al-Jawa'ib, begun in Istanbul in 1861 by Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq, was widely read across the empire and was supported by the Ottoman authorities, which valued a literate, loyal readership. In Egypt, private papers such as Wadi al-Nil (1867) appeared under Isma'il. The most successful was al-Ahram: the Lebanese brothers Salim and Bishara Taqla established their enterprise in Alexandria in 1875 and issued the first number on 5 August 1876. It combined telegraph news, commercial and shipping notices, advertising, and commentary, and it was financed by subscriptions and advertisers rather than by the state. Later papers such as al-Mu'ayyad (1889) took a more explicitly reformist and political line.

Newspapers differed in purpose. Gazettes announced policy; commercial papers sold information, such as arrivals of ships, market prices, and foreign news by telegraph, to merchants and professionals; and political papers argued about reform, the Ottoman state, and, after 1882, British rule in Egypt. Editors also developed the form: they separated news from editorials, added serialized stories and letters, and trained a corps of journalists. The style of newspaper Arabic, shorter and plainer than much older prose, became a model for general writing.

All were shaped by censorship. Ottoman press regulations (1864 and later), the harsher controls of Abdulhamid II's reign, and Egyptian press laws such as that of 1881 limited what could be printed, and papers were suspended or closed when they offended authorities. The Ottoman constitutional restoration of 1908 relaxed these controls and produced a surge of new titles. The contrast between Ottoman restrictions and Egypt's relatively greater latitude helps explain why so many Syrian journalists, including the Taqla brothers, worked in Egypt, and why the British occupation, whatever its politics, gave some of them a congenial place to publish.

Section 51.7

Magazines, Periodicals, and Intellectual Debate

Magazines were the Nahda's principal medium for sustained intellectual argument, and each had a distinct editorial purpose. al-Jinan (1870), edited in Beirut by Butrus al-Bustani with his son Salim and his relative Sulayman, was a fortnightly of essays on history, literature, politics, and science, with a motto linking love of the homeland to faith and with serialized fiction; it also reflected Bustani's educational aims. al-Muqtataf (1876), founded in Beirut by Yaqub Sarruf and Faris Nimr, both teachers connected with the Syrian Protestant College, was devoted to science, industry, and practical knowledge, and moved to Cairo in 1885, where it became a leading monthly. al-Hilal (1892), founded in Cairo by Jurji Zaydan, a Beirut-born writer who had worked in Egyptian journalism, combined history, biography, literature, and fiction and offered readers a general cultural education.

Other periodicals served specialized communities: the educational Rawdat al-Madaris (1870), edited under Tahtawi's supervision; the medical al-Tabib; and the reformist Islamic al-Manar (1898), founded in Cairo by Rashid Rida, a scholar from the Tripoli region, which addressed Muslim readers in many countries on questions of reform, law, and religion. Its readership extended to North Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and elsewhere, illustrating that Arabic periodicals could reach beyond the Arab provinces.

Magazines created networks in concrete ways. Editors recruited contributors in different cities and published their letters and articles; readers in Syria, Egypt, Iraq, North Africa, and the Americas received the same issues, often through agents; and question-and-answer columns invited readers to write in with queries on science, language, and religion and to see their names in print. Bound annual volumes served as reference works and were collected in libraries. A schoolteacher in a provincial town, a physician in Alexandria, and a migrant in New York could thus participate in a single conversation, even if circulation was small by modern standards.

Magazines also carried controversy. al-Muqtataf's coverage of evolutionary theory in the 1880s drew responses from religious scholars; al-Manar argued for a reformist Islam and answered critics in its pages; and women's periodicals, discussed in Section 51.21, opened debates on gender. The magazine form therefore made intellectual disagreement visible to a broader reading public than any earlier Arabic medium. Because periodicals were the main vehicle for fiction, scientific popularization, and essays alike, they also tied different literary and intellectual fields together, a point developed in Divisions III and IV.

Section 51.8

Publishers, Booksellers, Libraries, and Reading Audiences

Print was a business, and its economics shaped what was published. Publishers had to buy or cast type, import paper, hire compositors and correctors, and find buyers. Many financed their work by subscription, collecting payments in advance, and distributed through agents, booksellers, and, as Egypt's postal and telegraph services developed in the 1860s and after, by mail. Newspapers relied on subscriptions and advertising, and magazines on subscriptions and the reputations of their editors; books depended on patrons, institutions, or the author's own funds. Unauthorized reprinting was common, and authors rarely lived on royalties, so many worked as teachers, officials, translators, or journalists.

Established firms specialized in different markets. The Cairo enterprise later known as Mustafa al-Babi al-Halabi began in 1859 as al-Maymunah, founded by Ahmad al-Halabi, and adopted the Mustafa al-Babi al-Halabi name later; it became a major publisher of classical and religious texts. Other houses sold schoolbooks, novels, and translations, and mission and Jesuit presses supplied school and religious books. Booksellers clustered in the commercial districts of Cairo, Beirut, and Istanbul and served as intermediaries who stocked new titles and relayed news. For historians, publishers' catalogues, advertisements in periodicals, and prefaces are important evidence of what was available and at what cost.

Libraries multiplied as well. Egypt's Dar al-Kutub, founded in 1870 as the Khedival Library, gathered manuscripts from mosques and other collections along with printed books; the Zahiriyya in Damascus, established in the 1880s, brought together manuscripts from city institutions; and in Jerusalem the Khalidi Library was opened in 1900. Reading rooms and society libraries made books available to those who could not afford them. These new institutions complemented rather than replaced the older waqf libraries, and in some cases they preserved manuscripts that would otherwise have been dispersed.

Who actually read? Literacy was low throughout the Arab provinces, with only a small minority able to read fluently and wide differences by gender, region, and class; no reliable overall figures exist for most of the century. Books and newspapers were nonetheless affordable to a widening group of teachers, clerks, merchants, and students, and audiences were larger than readership figures suggest, because newspapers and books were read aloud in coffeehouses, shops, and homes to listeners who could not read. Evidence on circulation is limited and often unreliable, since publishers' own claims were promotional, and historians use it with caution. The social base of print remained urban and male, a limit that Section 51.25 considers further.

Section 51.9

Literary Societies, Salons, Reading Rooms, and Public Discussion

Print was accompanied by new places of discussion. Learned societies such as Beirut's Society of Arts and Sciences and Syrian Scientific Society organized lectures, held meetings in Arabic, and published their proceedings, and later associations, among them charitable and educational societies in Cairo, Beirut, and elsewhere, combined cultural and social aims. Literary salons met in private homes: in Aleppo the household of the Marrash family hosted writers; in Cairo, Princess Nazli Fazil's salon brought together intellectuals, including Muhammad Abduh and European visitors; and in other cities, scholars' majlises continued older traditions of discussion in a new setting.

Reading rooms, schools, and coffeehouses gave less affluent men access to newspapers and conversation. Public lectures, theatrical performances, and school ceremonies, some reported in the press, brought writers and audiences together. In these spaces, individuals argued about literature, education, science, and politics in ways that were new to the Arab provinces and were shaped by the habits of the press: reading the same text, replying in print, and appealing to "public opinion" as a judge.

Historians sometimes describe such settings using Jürgen Habermas's concept of the public sphere, a space in which private people discuss matters of common concern. The concept is useful but needs adaptation. The Nahda's public was limited by literacy, income, and gender, and was constrained by censorship and surveillance, especially under Abdulhamid II, when informers and press controls inhibited open debate. It also coexisted with older public spaces, such as mosques, markets, and religious gatherings, where information and opinion also circulated, and many participants moved between the two. Some historians prefer to speak of several overlapping publics, divided by language, religion, class, and gender, rather than a single one.

The result was a more diverse and argumentative cultural life, and many of the habits of debate and association developed in these settings carried over into the political organizations discussed in Chapter 54.

Cause and Effect

Printing, Literacy, and the Emergence of Reading Publics

Long-term causes

Arabic's long literary and religious tradition; the early Christian and Ottoman printing experiments; expanding Mediterranean commerce; and Egyptian and Ottoman state reform.

Short-term causes

State schools and presses in Egypt; mission and communal education in Beirut; cheaper print technology; the telegraph and mail; and the political crises of mid-century that made public argument urgent.

Immediate effects

Newspapers and magazines multiplied; classical texts became available in print; new genres reached readers; and new networks of writers and readers developed across the empire and abroad.

Long-term consequences

A shared modern written Arabic for journalism and education; a larger and more argumentative public; and the conditions in which the political ideas of Chapter 54 could circulate.

Historical complexity: Print did not by itself create reading publics. Literacy, schooling, income, censorship, and the existence of authors and audiences with shared interests all mattered, and the resulting publics were fragmented by religion, class, gender, and region.

Division III — Language, Translation, Education, and Knowledge · Section 51.10

Reviving Arabic: Grammar, Lexicography, and Linguistic Reform

Language was central to the Nahda because the new media needed an Arabic that could serve journalism, science, and schooling as well as poetry and religion. Nasif al-Yaziji (1800–1871) wrote on grammar and rhetoric and composed Majma' al-Bahrayn, a collection of maqamat in the manner of the classical rhymed-prose narratives, which presented the tradition to nineteenth-century readers as a living model. His son Ibrahim al-Yaziji (1847–1906) worked on lexicography, journalism, and Bible translation, and wrote on the Arabic language and its reform. Butrus al-Bustani produced the dictionary Muhit al-Muhit (1867–1870), which reorganized the classical lexicon for contemporary readers, adding explanations of terms in use and modern vocabulary. Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq criticized earlier dictionaries and wrote extensively on Arabic vocabulary, including a critique of the classical dictionary al-Qamus, and defended the borrowing of European terms.

Writers faced practical choices. To express new objects and ideas, such as the telegraph, the constitution, or chemistry, they revived old words with new meanings, derived new words from Arabic roots, borrowed words from Turkish and European languages, or translated foreign expressions literally (calques). Journalists also moved away from the ornate rhymed prose (saj') that had dominated much formal writing toward plainer styles suited to newspapers, though ornate styles persisted for decades, and the same writers might use both. Arguments over such choices were often public, as writers criticized one another's coinages in periodicals.

A related question was the relationship between the formal written language (fusha) and the colloquial dialects. Most writers held that formal Arabic was a shared inheritance that connected readers across regions, and that it should be modernized rather than replaced. A few, including the British engineer William Willcocks in the 1890s, argued that Egyptians should write in their spoken dialect, and some satirists and popular writers, such as Yaqub Sannu and Abdallah Nadim, used colloquial in print; the argument was strongly resisted by most intellectuals, for whom colloquial writing threatened the unity of Arabic readers and the link to the classical heritage. Arabic also competed and interacted with Ottoman Turkish in administration and with French and English in education and commerce, so linguistic reform was bound up with questions of status and opportunity.

Print itself helped standardize written Arabic by giving readers across the empire a common modern style, an effect later institutions such as the language academies formalized (Chapters 56 and 60). Historians reconstruct these debates from dictionaries and their prefaces, grammar books, periodical controversies, and the works of writers experimenting in print.

Section 51.11

Translation and the Transformation of Knowledge

Translation was both a government program and a literary practice. In Muhammad Ali's Egypt, translators working from French and Turkish produced technical, military, and medical manuals, creating Arabic scientific vocabulary in the process, often with the help of interpreters, many of them Syrian, who knew European languages. Under Tahtawi's direction the translation program broadened to include history, geography, and law, and the translators' work was checked and edited by Azhar scholars. Tahtawi himself translated Fénelon's Télémaque (published 1867), reading a work of French literature in a way that fitted it to Arabic readers' own moral and political concerns about rulers and the common good.

Literary translation was often free adaptation. Egyptian writers such as Muhammad 'Uthman Jalal rendered plays by Molière and fables by La Fontaine into Arabic verse and colloquial settings, changing names, places, and customs for local audiences. Lebanese writers such as Salim al-Bustani translated and adapted French and English fiction, often in periodicals, and Sulayman al-Bustani's Arabic verse translation of the Iliad (1904), with a long introduction on Homer and on Arabic poetry, showed the form's ambitions. Translators from Ottoman Turkish also brought works of Ottoman reform writing and literature into Arabic, a reminder that translation was not only from Europe.

Religious translation also mattered. The Arabic Bible produced by Eli Smith and Cornelius Van Dyck with Arab collaborators (New Testament 1860, complete Bible 1865) and the Catholic Arabic Bible produced by the Jesuits and published across the late 1870s and early 1880s influenced Arabic prose style and showed how Christian communities contributed to Arabic's modernization. Their translators debated whether to keep traditional wording or to use the vocabulary of contemporary readers.

Treating this activity as passive borrowing misses its creativity. Translators chose what to translate, shortened or expanded texts, wrote prefaces explaining them, localized settings and names, and invented vocabulary. In many cases the texts they selected reflected their own concerns: education, government, family, or national history. Translation was thus a way of making new knowledge in Arabic, not simply of importing it. The evidence for this includes the translations themselves, their prefaces, and the reviews and controversies they provoked in periodicals.

Section 51.12

Education, New Curricula, and the Expansion of Literacy

Older educational institutions remained important: the village kuttab taught reading and recitation, and al-Azhar and the madrasas taught religious sciences, law, and Arabic through established texts and commentaries. Alongside them grew new kinds of schools: state schools in Egypt and the Ottoman provinces; communal schools run by Greek Orthodox, Maronite, Jewish, Coptic, and Muslim associations; missionary schools by American Protestants, Jesuits, French orders, and others; and private schools run by individual teachers. Chapter 50 examined state education policy; here the focus is on what students learned and how it changed cultural life.

New curricula added arithmetic, geography, natural sciences, foreign languages, and modern history to Arabic and religion. Examinations, graded classes, and printed textbooks replaced the individual teacher's authority with standardized programs, and the textbook itself became a new genre. Teacher training, as at Dar al-'Ulum in Cairo, tried to combine traditional Arabic scholarship with modern subjects, and its graduates became influential teachers and writers. Instruction in foreign languages became both an opportunity and a source of controversy, since French and English-language schools opened careers but also raised fears about loyalty and identity, a tension visible in the growth of rival Muslim and communal schools.

Girls' education grew more slowly, beginning in mission and communal schools and, in Egypt, with early state-supported girls' schools in the 1870s. Most girls who learned to read did so in such schools, at home with tutors, or in small circles, and their education usually stressed religion, domestic subjects, and languages; the women writers of Sections 51.20 and 51.21 largely came from these backgrounds.

Literacy rose but remained limited, with large differences between urban and rural areas, men and women, and regions. Educated graduates formed the audience and staff of the press, and the schools were often where writers first met one another. But the schools also created gaps: between those educated in religious, state, and foreign institutions, who might speak different languages and have different assumptions, and between the educated minority and the unlettered majority. Historians reconstruct this history from school records, curricula, textbooks, memoirs, and missionary and official reports, all of which reflect the perspective of institutions more than of pupils.

Section 51.13

Science, Medicine, Technology, and Intellectual Modernity

Science entered Arabic cultural life through schools, translations, and periodicals. Egypt's medical school trained doctors and produced Arabic medical texts and translations; at the Syrian Protestant College, medical teaching began in Arabic in the late 1860s, and Cornelius Van Dyck wrote Arabic textbooks on science and geography. The Egyptian astronomer Mahmud al-Falaki worked on astronomy, mathematics, and surveying and published in Arabic and French, and technologies such as steam power, the telegraph, and photography were described and explained in the press. These efforts also required vocabulary: translators had to decide how to name chemical elements, anatomical parts, and instruments in Arabic.

Yaqub Sarruf and Faris Nimr's al-Muqtataf made scientific discussion a regular part of Arabic reading. It explained inventions, natural history, medicine, and theories, combined translation from European sources with original writing, and encouraged readers to send questions. Its editors believed that knowledge of science would benefit society, and many of its pages were practical, covering agriculture, health, and industry as well as theory.

The best-known controversy concerned evolutionary theory. In 1882 a lecture on Darwin at the Syrian Protestant College provoked a dispute within the college, and al-Muqtataf, whose editors were then connected with it, discussed Darwin's ideas, as did the writer Shibli Shumayyil, who championed evolution in Arabic essays. Religious scholars replied: Husayn al-Jisr of Tripoli, in a work of 1888, argued that Islam was compatible with modern science, while others questioned particular theories. Muhammad Abduh and other reformers took up the relationship of reason and revelation in related terms. These debates show scientific ideas becoming matters of public argument that crossed communal lines, with Christians, Muslims, and others on varied sides.

These developments linked science to literary production, as popular science writing used the essay form and a clear Arabic style, and fiction and poetry occasionally took up scientific themes. They also illustrate the limits of the movement: scientific research itself was limited, institutions for original work were few, and Arabic science writing remained largely a matter of popularization and translation before 1914.

Section 51.14

Encyclopedias, Historical Scholarship, and the Rediscovery of the Past

Nahda writers collected, edited, and reinterpreted the past. Bustani began the Arabic encyclopedia Da'irat al-Ma'arif in 1876; he completed several volumes, and his family continued the work after his death, though it was never finished. It aimed to put the sciences, history, and geography into Arabic, drawing on European encyclopedias as well as Arabic sources. Ali Mubarak's topographical and historical encyclopedia of Egypt (al-Khitat al-Tawfiqiyya, 1880s) described its cities, villages, and institutions, drawing on earlier chronicles and on his own administrative experience. Tahtawi wrote on Egypt's ancient and Islamic history in terms of a patriotic past, connecting the pharaonic and Islamic periods in one national story.

Jurji Zaydan's History of Islamic Civilization (1901–1906) and History of Arabic Literature (1911–1913) offered general readers a modern survey of the Arab-Islamic past organized by themes such as institutions, arts, and sciences, not only by rulers, though some Muslim and Orientalist critics disputed his interpretations and sources. His historical novels, discussed in Section 51.17, used fiction to teach history.

At the same time, presses at Bulaq and elsewhere edited and printed classical texts in history, geography, law, and literature, so that readers could consult them directly, and European orientalists produced competing editions. Together these projects made the past available in a new form and encouraged new questions about Arab, Islamic, Egyptian, and ancient histories. Writers did not agree about which past mattered most: some emphasized pharaonic Egypt, others the Arab-Islamic centuries, and others, in Mount Lebanon, the ancient Mediterranean world, a diversity that later political movements would draw on in different ways (Chapter 54). Historians use these works both as sources on the past they describe and as evidence of how nineteenth-century writers understood themselves.

Historical Vocabulary
Adab

Adab originally meant good conduct and refinement, and in classical Arabic came to mean the literary and cultured learning appropriate to an educated person: poetry, prose, rhetoric, and ethical writing. In the Nahda the word came increasingly to mean "literature" in the modern sense, and the term adib came to mean writer or man of letters.

Because the same word covered learned culture and literary production, adab links the Nahda to older traditions, and English "literature" does not capture its sense of cultivation. The shift of its meaning reflects the wider transformation of what counted as culture.

Division IV — Literary Renaissance and New Cultural Forms · Section 51.15

Poetry between Classical Revival and Innovation

Nineteenth-century Arabic poetry is usually described as a neoclassical revival, but the label hides real invention. The Egyptian poet and statesman Mahmud Sami al-Barudi (1839–1904), an officer and official, edited anthologies of Abbasid poets and composed verse in their style, rejecting what he regarded as the ornamental and trivial verse of the preceding centuries. His later poetry, written during his exile in Ceylon after the 1882 Egyptian crisis (Chapter 53), gave the classical ode a personal voice of loss and longing, showing that revival could carry modern experience.

Ahmad Shawqi (1868–1932), educated in law in France and attached to the Khedival court, and Hafiz Ibrahim (1872–1932), an officer turned writer of public poetry, wrote in classical meters on contemporary subjects, from public events, funerals, and anniversaries to the condition of Egypt and its peasants. Hafiz was called the poet of the Nile for his social themes. In the Levant, poets of the Yaziji circle, including Warda al-Yaziji, wrote in similar traditions, and in Iraq, poets such as Jamil Sidqi al-Zahawi and Ma'ruf al-Rusafi began to write on social reform and the position of women, often in newspapers. These poets differed in political outlook and social position, from court poets to critics of authority.

The revival was not mere imitation. Poets addressed new audiences through newspapers and public recitation, wrote about topics such as railways, education, and national events, and adapted the classical ode to changing circumstances. Poetry published in periodicals reached more readers than manuscripts ever had, and occasions such as the opening of a school or a ruler's death became occasions for public verse. Critics debated whether such work was too bound to old models; later generations, in turn, attacked the neoclassicists' formal conservatism. The classical forms nevertheless carried prestige and linked poets to a shared Arabic heritage, and poetry remained the most prestigious literary form through the period, while newer forms such as the novel and the play were still gaining acceptance. Some of the major poets' most celebrated work, such as Shawqi's verse drama, falls after 1914 and belongs to later chapters.

Section 51.16

Modern Arabic Prose: Essays, Travel Writing, Biography, and Satire

Journalism reshaped prose. Writers produced essays on social and political subjects for newspapers, travel narratives about Europe, biographies of notable figures, and satirical commentary. The rhetorical resources of the classical tradition remained in use, but a plainer and more direct style gradually became dominant in newspapers, because editors wanted to be read by clerks and merchants as well as scholars. Writers often moved between forms, composing a poem, an essay, and a serialized story for the same periodical.

Travel writing is a good example of the experiments involved. Tahtawi's account of Paris combined description with argument; Ali Mubarak's 'Alam al-Din (1882), a long fictionalized narrative in which an Azhar scholar and an English orientalist travel together, set Egyptian and European learning in conversation and was also an encyclopedia of knowledge. Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq's travels to Malta, France, and England provided the material for writing about European life and manners, and Egyptian and Syrian writers produced accounts of journeys to Europe that tested what could be borrowed.

Al-Shidyaq was among the most inventive prose writers. His al-Saq 'ala al-saq (published in Paris in 1855) is a hybrid of autobiography, social satire, linguistic play, and argument about language, marriage, and women's status that does not fit conventional genre categories and draws on the lexicon of the classical tradition. He later edited the Istanbul newspaper al-Jawa'ib (from 1861), one of the most widely read Arabic papers of its time. Muhammad al-Muwaylihi's Hadith 'Isa ibn Hisham, serialized from 1898 and published as a book in 1907, revived the classical maqama form: a pasha of Muhammad Ali's time returns to life in contemporary Cairo and, guided by the narrator, encounters courts, markets, entertainments, and foreign influence, and the work satirizes Egyptian society under British occupation. These works show writers experimenting with inherited forms rather than abandoning them, and critics still debate whether they should be read as continuations of the classical tradition or as early modern literature.

Section 51.17

The Emergence of Modern Arabic Fiction

The modern Arabic novel developed through translation, adaptation, and original writing, often serialized in magazines. Early narrative experiments include Khalil al-Khuri's Wayy... idhan lastu bi-ifranji (1860), Fransis Marrash's allegorical Ghabat al-haqq (1865), which uses a dream-vision to argue for freedom and learning, and Salim al-Bustani's novels, such as al-Huyam fi jinan al-Sham (1870), serialized in al-Jinan. These works borrowed from European fiction but also from Arabic narrative traditions, including the maqama and popular tales, and they were often didactic.

Jurji Zaydan's historical novels, serialized in al-Hilal from the 1890s, were extremely popular. He wrote more than twenty, beginning with al-Mamluk al-Sharid (1891), and set their love plots against episodes of Islamic and Arab history, from the early caliphate to the Mamluk and Ottoman periods, in order to teach history to readers who would not read a chronicle. They were accessible, formulaic, and widely read, and critics disagreed about whether their historical accuracy and literary quality were adequate. They helped form a mass fiction audience that included men and women.

Later works moved toward contemporary social subjects. Zaynab Fawwaz's Husn al-'awaqib (1899) is often cited as an early novel by an Arab woman, and Muhammad Husayn Haykal's Zaynab, with its contemporary Egyptian rural setting, appeared in 1913 or 1914 depending on how its first publication is dated, and is often cited as a landmark Egyptian novel; because it was first published under a pseudonym, its date and authorship were treated cautiously at first, and bibliographies vary.

Historians disagree about which work was the "first" Arabic novel, and the disagreement depends on definitions: whether the work must be original rather than adapted, long rather than short, realist rather than allegorical or historical, and published in book form or serialized. Most now regard the question as less useful than tracing how a range of narrative experiments gradually established the form. Serialization mattered because it allowed publishers to retain readers from issue to issue, and the fiction audience included men and women of varying education. Some critics worried that novels were frivolous or morally dangerous, while others saw them as instruments of education; and translations and adaptations, including of detective and adventure stories, shaped readers' expectations alongside original works.

Section 51.18

Theater, Drama, and the Modern Stage

Modern Arabic theater began in Beirut in 1847, when Maroun al-Naqqash produced an adaptation of Molière's The Miser (al-Bakhil) in his home, to an invited audience of merchants, officials, and consuls. Naqqash wrote in Arabic, explained his purpose in a preface, and combined European dramatic structure with songs and music, creating a musical drama suited to Arab audiences. After his death in 1855, his relatives, notably his brother Niqula and his nephew Salim, continued his work.

Theater moved outward. In Damascus, Abu Khalil al-Qabbani produced musical drama in the 1870s, drawing on historical and literary stories, before leaving for Egypt in the 1880s following local opposition. In Egypt, Yaqub Sannu, of Egyptian Jewish background and educated in Europe, established a theater troupe in Cairo around 1870–1871 (accounts differ on the exact date of his first performances) under Khedive Isma'il's patronage, writing plays in colloquial Arabic, and Salim al-Naqqash brought a troupe of Syrian players to Egypt in 1876, where they performed in Alexandria and Cairo. Khedive Isma'il's Cairo Opera House (1869) provided a European-style venue, but it largely served foreign and elite audiences, and Arabic theater found its audience in other halls.

The theater drew on existing performance traditions, including storytellers, shadow plays, and musical performance, as well as European models. It built its audience gradually and unevenly, depended on individual patrons and entrepreneurs, and faced censorship and religious criticism; in Damascus and elsewhere, some objected to performances on moral grounds. Singing actors such as Salama Hijazi (1852–1917) made musical drama widely popular in Egypt, and the repertory included translations, adaptations of European plays, and plays on historical and Arabic subjects. Theater thus extended the Nahda to people who were not readers, since it could reach audiences through performance rather than print. Evidence for it comes from play texts, newspaper notices, memoirs, and official records of licensing and closure.

Section 51.19

Satire, Illustrated Journalism, Music, and Popular Cultural Expression

Not all Nahda culture was in books. Satirical journalism used humor to criticize authorities and society. Yaqub Sannu's Abu Naddara Zarqa' (1877), published in colloquial Egyptian Arabic with caricatures, attacked official abuses and foreign influence; after Sannu's expulsion from Egypt in 1878, he continued publishing from Paris, in Arabic and European languages, and smuggled copies into Egypt. Abdallah Nadim, a speaker and journalist, edited satirical and educational papers such as al-Tankit wa-l-Tabkit (1881) in a lively, partly colloquial style, addressing artisans and others who read less often, and was involved in the political events of the early 1880s.

Illustrations, photographs, and caricature appeared increasingly in magazines, and commercial studios in Cairo, Beirut, and other cities produced portraits and views. Such images reached audiences that text could not, and they made visible changes in dress, architecture, and public life.

Music thrived in cafés and theaters, with performers such as Abduh al-Hamuli and Muhammad 'Uthman becoming celebrated singers and composers of the Egyptian repertoire; commercial recording of Arab singers began in the early twentieth century and later changed how music circulated. Film screenings in Alexandria and Cairo began within a year or two of the first public shows in Paris in 1895. These forms often reached audiences that books did not, including illiterate and poorer city dwellers, and they show that the cultural change of the period included visual, oral, and performative life as well as writing. They also expanded commercial entertainment as an industry, creating new careers for performers, printers, and impresarios.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Manuscript Scholarship, Classical Arabic Literature, and Modern Print

What Changed

  • Books were produced by presses, not copyists, and in larger numbers
  • New genres, including the newspaper, the novel, and the play, appeared
  • Readerships widened beyond scholarly and administrative elites
  • Authority shifted partly from teachers' certificates to editors, publishers, and the market

What Stayed the Same

  • Arabic remained the language of learning and shared culture, and classical texts remained prestigious
  • Manuscripts and teaching circles continued alongside print
  • Poetry retained its high status, and religious scholars remained central
  • Patronage and personal networks continued to shape careers

Who experienced change most directly? Urban, educated men in Cairo, Alexandria, Beirut, and Istanbul, and women of elite urban families, experienced the change most directly; rural populations and the poor, who lived mainly in an oral and local culture, experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: it shows the Nahda as a transformation of existing culture, not a replacement of it.

Everyday Life and Social History

Readers, Teachers, Booksellers, and Audiences

The following is a composite illustration, based on the kinds of lives historians reconstruct from memoirs, school records, and periodicals, and not a portrait of one named person. A young man in a provincial town might begin in a kuttab, move to a state school, and become a clerk or a teacher, buying the newspaper when he could afford it or reading a shared copy at a coffeehouse. A bookseller in a market district sold lithographed or printed copies of classical works, schoolbooks, and the latest novels, and acted as an informal source of news. A schoolteacher at a missionary or communal school taught Arabic alongside French or English, and often wrote for newspapers on the side to supplement a modest salary.

Women's participation varied greatly. Wealthy women read and sometimes wrote in the household, often through tutors or relatives, while few poor women had access to schooling. Most evidence comes from literate urban men, and historians must read it for what it indirectly reveals about the daily lives of women, rural families, and workers, using memoirs, school records, and other sources. Audiences at the theater and listeners at public readings, who might include people who could not read, also formed part of Nahda culture, and their reactions are often visible only in the complaints of critics and the accounts of censors.

Division V — Women, Communities, and the Geography of the Nahda · Section 51.20

Women Writers, Education, and Intellectual Participation

Women took part in the Nahda as writers, translators, teachers, and hosts, although the sources record them less fully than men. Aisha al-Taymur (1840–1902), from an elite Egyptian family of Turkish-Circassian background, wrote poetry in Arabic, Turkish, and Persian and prose in a classical style, including essays on the condition of women; she was educated at home, and her works circulated among elite readers and in print. Maryana Marrash (1848–1919) of Aleppo, sister of the writer Fransis Marrash, wrote poetry and hosted a literary salon, and published her work in Beirut. Warda al-Yaziji (1838–1924), daughter of Nasif, was a poet in the Beirut circle, and published a collection of poetry. Zaynab Fawwaz (1860–1914), a Lebanese-born writer in Egypt, contributed to newspapers, compiled a biographical dictionary of famous women (al-Durr al-manthur, 1894), and wrote a novel and a play.

Their obstacles were considerable. Access to education was limited, social norms discouraged women's public presence, and publication often required a male relative's support or the use of a pseudonym or initials. Many were from privileged families, with access to tutors and libraries, so their experience was not that of most women. Fawwaz's career is instructive: she came from modest origins, built her reputation through journalism, and argued in print for women's capacities, but was also criticized and had to defend her right to write.

Biographical dictionaries and periodicals, many compiled by women or devoted to them, are central sources, as are salons such as that of Princess Nazli Fazil in Cairo, which made her a patron of writers. Zaydan's al-Hilal also published biographies of notable women. These works helped establish a canon of women writers, though they also reflect the editors' own assumptions. Their work shows that the Nahda's literary and intellectual life was not exclusively male, and that women contributed to debates about their own education and role.

Section 51.21

The Women's Press and Debates over Gender and Society

The women's press deserves attention in its own right. Hind Nawfal, born in Tripoli (in present-day Lebanon) and living in Alexandria, founded al-Fatat in 1892, a magazine edited by a woman for women readers, and it is often counted the first Arabic women's journal. In its inaugural program, as scholars and archival editions of the journal describe it, Nawfal presented the magazine as a place for women's participation in writing, offering articles on science, history, and literature, and she undertook to avoid political and religious controversy. That choice reflects both a concern to keep the journal acceptable to a mixed readership and the constraints of censorship. Others followed: Anis al-Jalis (1898), edited in Alexandria by Alexandra Avierino, and Fatat al-Sharq (1906), edited by Labiba Hashim in Cairo. These magazines carried articles on education, household management, health, and women's biography, along with poetry and fiction by women.

They also joined broader debates. Qasim Amin's books on women's emancipation (1899 and 1900) provoked a controversy, with critics and defenders writing in newspapers and magazines, including women such as Zaynab Fawwaz and, soon after, Malak Hifni Nasif. Debates concerned girls' education, marriage, veiling, polygamy, and household responsibilities, and drew on Islamic arguments, European ideas, and local social experience. Many women writers emphasized education and "domestic science" as a route to respect, sometimes in terms that fit conservative expectations while expanding women's public role.

Most contributors were urban, educated, and from relatively privileged families, and the debates did not reach all women equally. Many Syrian Christian women worked in Alexandria and Cairo, and Muslim and other women increasingly took part. The women's press nevertheless showed that gender was a central question of the Nahda, and it laid foundations for the organized women's movements of the twentieth century (Chapter 60).

Section 51.22

Religious Communities and Shared Intellectual Networks

The Nahda was neither a Christian project nor a uniformly secular one. Christians of several denominations, trained in missionary and communal schools, were prominent as publishers and journalists, in part because of their access to such schools and to foreign contacts, and because they often had links to Egypt and Europe. Muslim scholars and writers also used the press extensively: Azhar-trained scholars edited classical works at Bulaq and elsewhere; Muhammad Abduh and Rashid Rida used journals to argue for Islamic renewal; and Husayn al-Jisr in Tripoli and Tahir al-Jaza'iri in Damascus promoted new learning on Islamic foundations. In Beirut, Muslim notables created Thamarat al-Funun and the Maqasid society.

Jews took part through communal schools such as those of the Alliance Israélite Universelle, established in Baghdad in the 1860s, and as writers and entrepreneurs, such as Yaqub Sannu in Egypt. Copts too established schools and newspapers, among them the Cairo paper al-Watan (1877). Different communities thus built different institutions and had different relations with the state and with foreign sponsors.

Shared Arabic language and culture created common ground, but relations also involved competition and suspicion. Missionary activity prompted Muslim and other communities to found their own schools and societies; Catholic and Protestant missions competed with each other and with Orthodox and Maronite authorities; and debates on science, women's status, and foreign influence often cut across religious lines instead of following them. Many writers saw Arabic as a bridge across religious boundaries, as Bustani did; others emphasized communal or religious loyalties. The Nahda is therefore better understood as a shared field in which different communities, with different aims, took part, and as a field in which communal identity and common language were both being redefined.

Section 51.23

Beyond Cairo and Beirut: Regional Variations in Cultural Revival

Cairo and Beirut were exceptional. In Damascus, the Zahiriyya library, the scholarship of reformers such as Tahir al-Jaza'iri, and the official gazette Suriya sustained a vigorous but more constrained intellectual life, especially under Abdulhamid II. Aleppo had its own press and gazette (al-Furat) and literary families such as the Marrash family, whose members, Fransis and Maryana, illustrate how a single household could produce both writers and a salon. In Jerusalem, the Khalidi Library (1900), mission and Ottoman schools, and early twentieth-century newspapers, such as al-Karmil in Haifa (1908), formed the basis of a growing Palestinian press (Chapter 59).

In Baghdad, the governor Midhat Pasha established the gazette al-Zawra' (1869) and opened schools, and scholarly families such as the Alusis maintained a strong tradition of religious scholarship, while poets such as al-Zahawi and al-Rusafi took up reformist themes; printing and new schools developed more slowly than on the Mediterranean coast, and Jewish communal schools of the Alliance, from 1864, formed one of the most important new educational channels. In Tunis, the reformer Khayr al-Din established the Sadiqi College (1875) and wrote a major work on political reform (1867), the Zaytuna mosque-university remained a center of learning, and an official gazette appeared in 1860.

In Algeria, French rule (Chapter 52) restricted Arabic education and printing, though an official Arabic gazette existed; in Morocco, lithographic printing predominated. In the Arabian Peninsula and the Gulf, Yemen, and much of the Sudan, printing and new schools were very limited before 1914, and the Hijaz received a press only late. Differences in literacy, educational resources, economic development, political constraints, and access to publishing explain much of this variation. They show that the Nahda's most concentrated centers were not representative of all Arab societies, and that "the Nahda" in Aleppo or Baghdad took distinct forms, shaped by local elites, communities, and officials.

Section 51.24

Migration, the Mahjar, and Transregional Arab Intellectual Networks

Migration reshaped Arab intellectual networks. Many Syrians and Lebanese moved to Egypt, especially after the 1860s and 1880s, where they founded newspapers and magazines, taught, and worked in the press, administration, and trade, and others went to Europe and, in large numbers from the 1880s, to the Americas. Causes included economic pressure, such as the decline of Mount Lebanon's silk industry, the wish to avoid military service, political restrictions, and the search for opportunity. Egypt itself was a kind of mahjar for the Syrian press, and Paris and London hosted Arabic exile journalism, such as Sannu's Abu Naddara and the reformist al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa (1884), published in Paris by Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh.

In the Americas, Arabic newspapers appeared from the 1890s: Kawkab Amrika in New York (1892), founded by Ibrahim al-'Arabili; al-Huda, founded in 1898 by Na'um Mukarzil and later based in New York; and Mir'at al-Gharb (1899), and later periodicals in Brazil and Argentina. These served migrants, who also sent letters and money home and carried newspapers and books across the Atlantic, and published poetry, news from home, and arguments about politics in Syria and the Ottoman Empire. Because they were beyond Ottoman censorship, some printed criticism that could not be published at home.

Writers such as Khalil Gibran and Ameen Rihani began to publish before 1914, but the major literary achievements of the migrant writers, including the Pen League (1920), belong to the post-1914 period and later chapters. Overall, the mahjar illustrates that the Nahda's public existed across borders, and that the press could connect distant Arabic readers; it also complicates any assumption that the Nahda was confined to the Arab provinces, since migrants helped fund, read, and shape it.

Historical Vocabulary
Mahjar

Mahjar (from a root meaning to emigrate) refers to the lands of Arab emigration, especially the Americas, and to the Arab migrant communities there. In literary history it is associated with the Arabic-language press and literature produced by emigrants, though the term is sometimes applied only to the post-1914 literary movements.

Students should note that most nineteenth-century emigrants came from the Ottoman provinces of Syria and Lebanon and described themselves as Syrians, Lebanese, or by religion or village; modern national labels do not map neatly. Migrants also moved within the Arab world, and Egypt served as another destination.

Division VI — Historical Interpretation and Legacy · Section 51.25

The Limits and Contradictions of the Nahda

The Nahda was unequal. Literacy was concentrated among urban men, and women, peasants, pastoralists, and the urban poor were mostly outside the readership of books and periodicals, though they might hear them read. Geography mattered: some regions had presses and schools while others had few. Cost and access to foreign languages shaped who could take part.

Writers also worked under constraints. Ottoman and Egyptian censorship limited what could be said, and Abdulhamid II's regime was especially restrictive; newspapers depended on subscribers, advertisers, patrons, and governments; and many writers relied on state salaries or foreign institutions. European influence was powerful and double-edged: it supplied models, institutions, and funding, but it was bound up with missionary activity and imperial expansion, which many contemporaries criticized, and later scholars have questioned whether some Nahda writers took European categories too much for granted. It was also concentrated among particular urban groups, including Christian Levantine intellectuals and Egypt's educated effendiyya, so claims that it was a broad social transformation need to be weighed carefully against evidence of its limited reach.

Section 51.26

The Nahda and the Transformation of Arab Cultural Life

By 1914 Arabic cultural life had been transformed. Modern standard Arabic served journalism, education, and science; newspapers and magazines linked readers across the empire and abroad; schools produced a larger educated class; and poetry, prose, fiction, and theater had developed new forms. Intellectual networks crossed religious, regional, and national lines, and women had taken their place as writers and editors.

These changes created conditions for later political thought without determining it. A public that read the same newspapers could debate reform, Ottoman citizenship, Egyptian independence, Arab identity, Islamic renewal, and Zionism, as Chapter 54 shows. The Young Turk revolution of 1908 produced an immediate surge of new Arabic periodicals, and the First World War (Chapter 55) would bring wartime repression and the ending of the Ottoman order. The Nahda's legacies, in language, institutions, and debate, persisted through the mandate and independence periods covered in the remaining chapters.

Historical Debate

Was the Nahda a Cultural Renaissance, Intellectual Modernization, or Several Overlapping Transformations?

The question: How should historians describe and explain the cultural change of the nineteenth-century Arab world, and does the term "Nahda" describe a single coherent phenomenon?

Interpretation A — A single movement of liberal modernization: Influential mid-twentieth-century accounts, notably Albert Hourani's Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age, presented the Nahda as the story of Arab thinkers responding to Europe and developing modern liberal ideas about society, government, and religion, centered on a series of major intellectuals.
Interpretation B — Several overlapping and unequal transformations: More recent scholarship, including work edited by Jens Hanssen and Max Weiss, emphasizes continuity with older Arabic and Ottoman traditions, the multiple sites and communities involved, the role of institutions, print economies, and women's participation, and the connections to Ottoman and global history. It questions the label "renaissance" and the focus on a handful of thinkers.

Evidence: Both draw on the same periodicals, books, and memoirs, but differ in which writers they treat as central, how they weigh European influence relative to local and Ottoman developments, and how much they emphasize the limits of the movement's reach.

Why it matters: The interpretation decides whether the Nahda appears as a European-inspired awakening, a continuation of existing traditions, or a complex of changes whose consequences were uneven. It also affects how historians assess the roles of women, religious scholars, and rural populations. These views overlap and are better seen as shifts of emphasis than as opposed camps.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Nahda as a cultural and intellectual transformation of the Arabic-speaking world from the early nineteenth century to 1914. It argued that the Nahda was not a single coordinated movement, not a European-style Renaissance, not an awakening from stagnation, and not identical with Arab nationalism, but a set of overlapping changes arising from the interaction of older Arabic traditions, Egyptian and Ottoman reform, missionary and communal institutions, commercial publishing, and European influence.

The foundations were laid in an Arabic scholarly world of manuscripts, teaching circles, and endowed libraries, and in early state-sponsored experiments in Egypt under Muhammad Ali and his successors, with Tahtawi, schools, and translation programs creating a new educated class. In Beirut and Mount Lebanon, missionary, communal, and private schools, learned societies, and presses created a second center, in which Butrus al-Bustani and others combined education, publishing, and reflection on the common life of a mixed society after the violence of 1860.

Printing expanded in phases: state presses such as Bulaq, mission and Jesuit presses, and then private commercial enterprises. Newspapers developed from official gazettes to private and political papers, and magazines such as al-Jinan, al-Muqtataf, and al-Hilal created networks of writers and readers across regions. Publishers, booksellers, libraries, societies, salons, and coffeehouses gave this print culture a social setting, though one limited by literacy, income, gender, and censorship.

Language, translation, education, and science were central. Grammarians and lexicographers worked to adapt Arabic to new needs; translators selected, adapted, and reshaped European and Turkish works; new curricula and schools widened the educated public; and periodicals brought scientific controversy, notably over evolution, into public view. Historical scholarship, encyclopedias, and printed editions of classical works reconsidered the Arab, Islamic, Egyptian, and ancient pasts.

Literature changed in different ways. Poetry was revived in neoclassical forms by writers such as al-Barudi, Shawqi, and Hafiz Ibrahim; prose was transformed by journalism and by experimenters such as al-Shidyaq and al-Muwaylihi; fiction developed through adaptation and serialization, with Zaydan's historical novels and later works such as Zaynab; and theater began with Maroun al-Naqqash and developed through the work of Sannu, al-Qabbani, and others. Satire, illustration, music, and early film extended cultural change beyond readers.

Participation was wider than often supposed. Women such as Aisha al-Taymur, Maryana Marrash, Warda al-Yaziji, Zaynab Fawwaz, and Hind Nawfal wrote and edited, and the women's press opened debate on education, marriage, and family. Muslims, Christians, and Jews took part through different institutions, with cooperation and competition. Regional differences were large, with Damascus, Aleppo, Jerusalem, Baghdad, and Tunis developing in distinct conditions and the Arabian interior hardly touched by print; and migration created a mahjar press and network across the Atlantic.

The Nahda was also unequal and contradictory. It reached mainly urban and educated groups, was constrained by censorship and dependence on patrons, and was entangled with European influence and imperial expansion. Historians disagree about whether it is best described as a renaissance, as intellectual modernization, or as several overlapping transformations, and recent scholarship tends toward the last.

Its significance lies in what it made possible: a shared modern written Arabic, a larger and more argumentative reading public, new institutions of education and publication, and networks across borders. These did not determine later politics, but they shaped the setting in which Ottomanism, Arabism, constitutionalism, Zionism, and reformist Islam developed (Chapter 54), and they provided resources for responses to the First World War and its aftermath (Chapters 55 and 56).

MGU Library Research Project

Studying a Nahda Periodical

This project asks you to study one periodical or publishing enterprise from the chapter and assess what it reveals about the Nahda.

  1. Choose a periodical. Select one, such as al-Jinan, al-Muqtataf, al-Hilal, al-Ahram, al-Fatat, or al-Manar.
  2. Research its background. Identify its founders, place and date of publication, sponsors, language style, and intended readers.
  3. Examine its content. Using library resources, translated excerpts, or secondary scholarship, summarize its main subjects and controversies.
  4. Assess its audience and limits. Consider circulation, cost, censorship, and who read or could not read it.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay or presentation, explain what the periodical shows about the Nahda, noting where evidence is incomplete.

Library resources to use: The MGU History Library's materials on Arabic print and journalism, scholarly studies of the Nahda, digitized periodicals where available, and translated collections of Arabic writings.

Chapter Reflection

The Nahda matters because it changed how Arabic speakers produced, shared, and argued about knowledge. Printing, journalism, schools, translation, and new literary forms made Arabic a medium of modern public life, and they created networks of readers and writers that crossed the boundaries of cities, religions, and states.

It also shows how historical change operates through institutions and ordinary labor: teachers, translators, printers, editors, booksellers, actors, and readers, as well as famous authors. It shows continuity with older traditions, uneven participation across regions, gender, and class, and the interplay of Ottoman and Egyptian reform, missionary education, commerce, and European influence.

Its consequences extended beyond culture. The habits of public debate, the shared written language, and the reading public provided the setting for the political arguments of Chapter 54, the responses to the First World War and the mandates of Chapters 55 and 56, and the cultural life of the interwar and postcolonial Arab world. The Nahda's limits, including its concentration among urban elites, are equally part of the story.

Finally, the chapter shows that the Arab world's modern history cannot be told as politics alone. Cairo and Beirut, Alexandria and Istanbul, Damascus, Tunis, and the mahjar were linked by words as well as trade and empire, and understanding those links is essential to understanding the world that the following chapters describe.
