Part VII — The Late Medieval Arab World
38

Trade and Society in the Mamluk Age

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Chapter 37 explained how the Mamluk Sultanate was governed: how slave-soldiers became rulers, how emirs, judges, and administrators divided authority, and how sultans sought legitimacy. This chapter turns to the far larger population on whom that state rested. Peasants who cleared canals and paid taxes, artisans who wove linen and inlaid brass, merchants who moved pepper from the Red Sea to Alexandria, scholars who lived on endowments, women who owned and bequeathed property, and Christians and Jews who staffed offices and ran businesses all made the Mamluk world function, from positions of very unequal power.

Consider a single loaf of bread in fourteenth-century Cairo. The grain came from fields along the Nile, where cultivators had managed the annual flood and paid revenue to the holder of an iqta', an assignment of tax revenue that supported a Mamluk officer. It was carried by boat and by animal to river ports and city granaries, milled, sold through regulated markets, baked in a neighborhood oven, and eaten in households ranging from the courtyard residences of emirs to the single rooms of day laborers. Behind that one loaf stood a village, a landholding system, a fiscal bureaucracy, a transport network, and a market inspector charged with checking its weight. This chapter is about those linked systems and the people inside them.

The chapter's central question is how agriculture, cities, trade, craft production, scholarship, and religious institutions sustained everyday life in Mamluk Egypt and Syria, and how those regions took part in commercial networks linking the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, and the Indian Ocean. Its central argument rejects an older picture of the Mamluk period as an era of military rulers living off a stagnant economy. The Sultanate rested on a complex agrarian and urban economy: Nile agriculture and Syrian farming supported the state and its cities; Cairo and Damascus were major centers of consumption, production, learning, and exchange; and merchants connected Alexandria to Mediterranean buyers and the Red Sea to markets in Yemen, India, and beyond.

The chapter also insists that this prosperity was uneven. Peasants bore heavy fiscal burdens. Merchants faced state intervention and political risk. Women participated in economic life under legal and social conditions that were unequal to those of men. Enslaved people entered the region through several distinct trades and occupied very different positions. Christians and Jews lived within a structured hierarchy that combined protection, opportunity, restriction, and, at times, coercion. Religious institutions such as mosques, madrasas, Sufi lodges, hospitals, and waqf foundations were at once spiritual, educational, charitable, and economic institutions, and they converted private wealth into durable urban infrastructure.

Unlike Chapter 37, this chapter is not organized by reign. It is thematic and covers roughly 1260 to the fifteenth century, while recognizing that conditions did not stay fixed across so long a span. Individual sultans, such as Baybars, al-Nasir Muhammad, Barsbay, and Qaitbay, appear as case studies when their policies illuminate economic and social life rather than as the chapter's subject. The chapter describes the dense and productive economy as it existed before the plague of 1347–1349. The Black Death and later plague recurrences profoundly altered these systems, but Chapter 41 examines those transformations, and this chapter does not narrate them.

The chapter also observes the division of labor with its neighbors. Chapter 39 owns the Maghreb, including its Mediterranean commerce. Chapter 40 owns Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and the Indian Ocean societies and states in their own right. Here, Indian Ocean trade is examined from the perspective of how it reached and affected Mamluk lands. Pilgrimage appears as a social and economic movement linking Cairo and Damascus to the Hijaz, while Arabia itself remains for Chapter 40.

Historians reconstruct this world from an unusual range of evidence. Narrative chronicles by al-Maqrizi and others record prices, markets, and disturbances. Biographical dictionaries preserve the careers of scholars, including women. Waqf deeds list properties, salaries, and building plans. Court records, notarial documents, and treaties show how property, commerce, and diplomacy operated in practice. Manuals for market inspectors describe the ideals of urban regulation. Travelers' accounts offer outsiders' impressions, and standing buildings, textiles, glass, and metalwork survive as material evidence. Much of this evidence reflects the concerns of literate, urban, and often elite men, and peasants and the urban poor are visible mainly through the eyes of others. The chapter returns to this limitation repeatedly.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand the Mamluk Sultanate not as a military regime floating above society but as part of an interconnected system: villages produced food and revenue; cities concentrated population, craft production, administration, learning, and consumption; merchants linked those cities to wider networks; waqf converted wealth into religious, educational, medical, and civic institutions; and people of every rank and faith took part in it from profoundly unequal positions.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Nile agriculture and Syrian farming supported Mamluk cities and the Mamluk state, and why agriculture rather than long-distance trade was the foundation of the economy.
  2. Analyze the economic consequences of the iqta' system, distinguishing it from European feudal landholding.
  3. Describe the lives and obligations of peasants and the limits of the evidence for rural society.
  4. Compare Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, and Alexandria as economic and social centers.
  5. Trace the movement of commodities between the Indian Ocean, the Red Sea, Egypt, and the Mediterranean, and explain the Mamluk position as a transit region rather than a controller of the Indian Ocean.
  6. Assess the relationship between Mamluk authorities and Italian and other Latin merchant communities, distinguishing political and military rivalry from continuing commerce.
  7. Explain how market regulation, the hisba, and artisanal organization worked, and why "guild" is an unreliable label.
  8. Evaluate the economic and legal position of women, enslaved people outside the military system, and Christian and Jewish communities.
  9. Analyze the waqf as a legal, economic, religious, and social institution.
  10. Describe how madrasas, legal schools, Sufi institutions, hospitals, and book production sustained scholarly and religious life.
  11. Interpret a source on a Mamluk endowed institution and identify what it can and cannot establish.
  12. Contextualize the pre-plague economy within Part VII and recognize which later transformations belong to Chapter 41.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did villages, cities, and the Mamluk military elite depend on one another economically?
  2. Why should the iqta' be understood as an assignment of revenue rather than as private feudal landownership?
  3. Why did Egypt occupy such a strategically valuable position between Indian Ocean and Mediterranean commerce, and what limits are there to saying the Mamluks "controlled" that trade?
  4. How could Mamluk authorities fight Latin Christian powers while hosting Latin Christian merchants?
  5. What were the economic, legal, and moral purposes of market regulation?
  6. In what ways did the waqf convert private wealth into public institutions, and what private interests did it also serve?
  7. How did women, enslaved people, Christians, and Jews experience the same economy differently from male Muslim merchants and scholars?
  8. What kinds of evidence reveal the lives of ordinary people, and whose lives remain largely invisible?
  9. Why should the fifteenth century not be treated automatically as a story of decline?

Historical Context

The Mamluk Sultanate emerged in Egypt in 1250, as Chapter 36 explained, and consolidated after the victory at Ayn Jalut in 1260 and the reign of Baybars. At its height it ruled Egypt and most of greater Syria and exercised suzerainty over the Hijaz, where the Hasanid sharifs retained local political authority, and it was recognized as protector of Mecca and Medina. Chapter 37 examined its military system, its offices, its succession politics, and its efforts at legitimacy. The present chapter takes that political framework as given and asks about the society and economy beneath it.

The society inherited much from earlier periods. Egypt's economy had been shaped by Fatimid and Ayyubid rule (Chapters 31 and 35), including the growth of Cairo, the commercial importance of the Red Sea route, and the institution of the iqta', which developed in earlier Islamic states and was adapted by the Ayyubids and Mamluks. Syria had been fragmented by the Crusader states, Ayyubid principalities, and Mongol campaigns (Chapters 34 to 36), and the Mamluk conquest of the remaining Crusader coastal strongholds in the late thirteenth century reoriented Syrian trade. The madrasa, the Sufi lodge, and the pious endowment had all spread widely under the Zengids and Ayyubids, and the Mamluks expanded all three on a large scale. Cairo had also become a refuge for scholars and craftsmen fleeing the Mongol destruction of Baghdad in 1258 (Chapter 36).

Two cautions frame the chapter. First, it spans about two and a half centuries, and economic and social conditions changed within them: the world of Baybars was not the world of Barsbay or Qaitbay. Second, the plague that reached Egypt and Syria in 1347 marks a boundary for this chapter's narrative. Where this chapter discusses the fifteenth century, it does so to follow institutions, commodities, and policies, such as the monopolies of Sultan Barsbay, without making population collapse and demographic change its organizing story. The Mamluk centuries should not be reduced to a decline preceding Ottoman conquest, a point Part VII as a whole insists upon.

Section 38.1

From Political System to Social World

The Mamluk military elite was small. Estimates of its size vary and should be treated cautiously, but it was certainly a minority of the population of Egypt and Syria, which was overwhelmingly composed of peasants, artisans, merchants, scholars, religious specialists, laborers, and the women and children of their households. Mamluk rulers depended on those people for revenue, food, labor, and the administrative skills that kept the state running. The Sultanate was therefore not an alien layer resting on an inert society but one element within a complex and changing social order.

The population was also diverse. Most inhabitants were Muslims, and Arabic was the main language of administration among the civilian population and of religious scholarship, while the military elite often spoke Turkic languages at home, a distinction examined in Chapter 37. Alongside them lived Coptic Christians in Egypt, Syrian and other Christian communities in the Levant, and Jewish communities in Cairo, Damascus, and many smaller towns. Bedouin groups occupied the margins of cultivated land, moving between pastoral and agrarian life in patterns Chapter 3 described, and their relationship to settled society is a recurring theme. The Mamluk world also included merchants and pilgrims from the Maghreb, Anatolia, Iraq, Yemen, and beyond, and enslaved people from the Black Sea steppe, the Caucasus, and Africa.

Geography organized this society. As Chapter 3 emphasized, Egypt's wealth rested on the Nile, whose annual flood made intensive agriculture possible in a narrow valley and wide delta. Syria offered a more varied landscape of coastal plains, hills, interior cities, irrigated oases, and steppe. Egypt and Syria were linked by the land route across Sinai and Gaza, which the Mamluks maintained with relay posts for official mail, and by sea. Beyond them lay the Red Sea, the Mediterranean, Anatolia, and Iraq. This chapter moves from the land to the cities, from the cities to commerce, and from commerce to the institutions through which wealth was converted into religious and social life.

Section 38.2

The Nile, Land, and the Iqta'

Egyptian Agriculture

Egyptian agriculture in the Mamluk period still depended on basin irrigation, the system Chapter 3 described, in which the Nile's annual flood was trapped behind embankments and then drained, leaving fertile silt for planting. A good flood meant a good harvest and a good tax yield; a flood too low or too high could cause hardship. Officials measured the river at the Nilometer on the island of Roda in Cairo, and the announcement of the flood level was a public event. At the same time, the Nile did not simply determine outcomes. The flood supplied the possibility of intensive farming, but whether canals were cleared, embankments repaired, and labor mobilized depended on institutions: village communities, local officials, iqta' holders, and the state. The Mamluk government invested in canal digging and dike maintenance, sometimes through forced labor, and al-Nasir Muhammad's reign was notable for such works.

The principal crops included wheat, barley, and legumes, which fed the population, along with flax for linen production, an important export material, and sugar cane, grown especially in Upper Egypt and refined in dedicated workshops. Orchards, vegetable gardens, date palms, and livestock added to the agricultural mix. Cattle, water buffalo, sheep, and goats supplied labor, milk, meat, and leather, and the Bedouin of the margins supplied animals, transport, and military service. Grain was the political commodity: its price affected the entire urban population, and shortages could produce unrest, which is why the state took an interest in granaries and markets.

Land, Revenue, and the Iqta'

Chapter 37 explained the iqta' politically as the way the Mamluk state paid its military. Its economic consequences were equally important. An iqta' was an assignment of the right to collect land revenue from a specified area, in return for military service. It was not private ownership of the land in the European feudal sense. The holder did not own the villages, usually did not reside in them, and could be moved to another assignment; the cultivators were not serfs in the European manorial and legal sense, although some scholars describe the restrictions and corvée imposed on settled peasants under parts of the Mamluk iqta' system as serf-like. Their relationship to the land and to the state was governed by different arrangements. The word "feudalism" obscures more than it clarifies when applied to this system, because it imports assumptions about hereditary landholding, manorial jurisdiction, and legal bondage that did not characterize the Mamluk iqta'.

Because iqta's were revenue assignments, the state had a strong interest in knowing what each was worth. Cadastral surveys, known as rawk, measured land and assessed its productivity. The survey of 1298 under Sultan Lajin and the more thorough rawk al-Nasiri of 1315 under al-Nasir Muhammad reassigned revenue and redistributed iqta's. These surveys also strengthened the sultan's hand against his emirs, since the redistribution of revenue was a means of political control. Revenue was collected in coin and in kind through a bureaucracy of scribes, many of whom were Copts or Muslim converts from administrative families, a subject returned to in Section 38.7.

The system also had weaknesses. A holder with a short and uncertain tenure had an incentive to extract as much as he could rather than invest in improvement, and local agents and Bedouin intermediaries could add their own exactions to official taxes. The state periodically attempted to abolish irregular levies, and chroniclers record such decrees, but their repetition suggests they were not always effective. Historians accordingly debate how heavily the system pressed on cultivators in different periods, and the answer varied by region, decade, and the character of the officials involved.

Section 38.3

Peasants, Villages, and Syrian Agriculture

Peasants and Village Life

Most people in the Sultanate were rural cultivators, yet they are the hardest to see. The chronicles were written in cities by and for literate men, and they mention peasants chiefly when they pay taxes, flee, riot, or are affected by flood and famine. Historians reconstruct village life indirectly: from tax and survey records, from legal opinions, from the notices of administrators, from archaeological work, and from the comparative study of later rural societies.

The evidence suggests a life organized around the agricultural calendar and the water. Villages cooperated to maintain local embankments and canals, to apportion water, and to guard harvests. Households combined cultivation with livestock and with household production such as spinning, weaving, and food processing, and women's labor in the fields, in animal care, and in the household was integral to the rural economy, though largely invisible in the sources. The village was also a fiscal unit: its tax obligation was assessed collectively, so that neighbors bore responsibility for one another's liabilities, and flight by some residents increased the burden on those who remained. Cultivators could and did leave heavily taxed villages for others, for towns, or for the margins, which gave them a limited form of leverage and gave officials a constant concern with controlling movement.

Rural relations with the Bedouin were similarly mixed. Pastoral groups supplied animals, transport, and sometimes security, while their raiding or their demand for payments could threaten crops. The Mamluk state granted influence and revenue to some Bedouin leaders and attempted to control others by force. As Chapter 3 emphasized, pastoral and agrarian life were interdependent rather than separate worlds.

Agriculture in Syria

Syria's agriculture was more varied than Egypt's and less dependent on a single river. Rain-fed cultivation of wheat and barley prevailed in much of the interior plains and hills, with olives, vines, and fruit trees in suitable areas, and irrigated gardens near rivers and springs. Around Damascus, the Ghuta oasis, watered by the Barada River and its channels, produced fruit, vegetables, and grain for the city, and the intensity of its irrigation made it one of the most productive districts in the region. Sugar cane was grown in suitable areas such as the Jordan Valley, though Egypt was the larger producer. In the steppe and the edges of cultivation, pastoralism persisted alongside farming.

Syrian villages were closely tied to the cities. Damascus, Aleppo, and the coastal and inland towns drew grain, fruit, oil, and livestock from the surrounding countryside, and in return supplied credit, manufactured goods, and administrative and legal services. The Mamluk conquest of the Crusader coastal strongholds in 1291 ended a long period of political fragmentation and, together with the later deliberate neglect or destruction of some coastal fortifications, shifted commercial emphasis toward other ports. Here, as in Egypt, the state's fiscal interest and the productivity of villages were intertwined: when the state maintained order and irrigation, villages could prosper, and when its demands grew excessive, cultivators could withdraw.

Section 38.4

Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, and Alexandria

Cairo

Cairo was the political capital, but its significance for this chapter is social and economic. It was one of the largest cities in the Mediterranean world, though precise population estimates are uncertain and scholars disagree about them. Its urban area included the old walled city founded by the Fatimids, the older settlement of Fustat to the south, the Citadel on its heights, and the river port of Bulaq, which grew in importance in the fourteenth century as a Nile harbor for goods arriving from the delta and Upper Egypt. Streets of shops and workshops, public baths, mosques, madrasas, and residential quarters were densely packed, and the principal thoroughfare was lined with markets.

The presence of the military elite shaped the city's economy. Emirs and their households were large consumers of food, textiles, horses, weapons, building materials, and luxury goods, and their construction projects employed masons, carpenters, and laborers. Their waqf endowments, discussed in Section 38.8, tied agricultural revenue to urban institutions. Merchants' markets, including covered markets for textiles and spices, clustered near the main streets. Among those that survive in memory is the Khan al-Khalili, founded in 1382 by the emir Jaharkas al-Khalili on the site of earlier buildings. Cairo's merchants dealt in goods from Europe, the Mediterranean, Africa, and the Indian Ocean, so that the city lay at a junction rather than at an end of long-distance routes.

Damascus and Its Ghuta

Damascus was the great city of Syria and a center of crafts, scholarship, and trade. Its Umayyad Mosque, discussed in Chapter 22, remained the religious heart of the city, and its madrasas, mosques, and Sufi lodges gave it a reputation for learning. Its artisans produced textiles, metalwork, and glass, and its markets drew grain, fruit, and animal products from the Ghuta and from the hinterland. Damascus was also the gathering point for the Syrian pilgrim caravan to Mecca, so that the annual hajj brought traders, animals, and provisioning activity into the city. The quarter of Salihiyya on the slopes of Mount Qasyun, originally settled by Hanbali refugees from Palestine in the twelfth century, became a center of scholarship and of the Hanbali school, and illustrates how migration shaped urban topography.

Aleppo and the Northern Routes

Aleppo lay at the junction of routes linking Syria with Anatolia, Iraq, and the Mediterranean. Caravans brought goods overland from the east and north, and Aleppo's merchants served as intermediaries between regions with different political systems, including, at times, the Mongol-ruled lands of Iran and Iraq. The city's political role as a frontier fortress belongs to Chapter 37; here the point is that its location gave it a distinctive commercial character, tied to overland as well as maritime exchange.

Alexandria

Alexandria was Egypt's principal Mediterranean port and the main point of contact with Latin Christian merchants. Ships from Venice, Genoa, Catalonia, and other ports arrived to buy spices, textiles, sugar, alum, and other goods and to sell metals, timber, cloth, and other products. The city had customs houses and warehouses, and its administration was closely supervised by Cairo. Its activity fluctuated with politics: the Cypriot raid of 1365 sacked the city, and papal prohibitions on certain trade with Muslim powers complicated Latin commerce. Yet exchange continued, as the following sections show.

Section 38.5

Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean Commerce

The Red Sea Route and the Indian Ocean

Egypt stood between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean. Goods such as pepper and other spices, aromatics, fine textiles, and precious items moved from India and Southeast Asia by sea to Red Sea ports, then overland to the Nile and downriver to Cairo and Alexandria, where Latin merchants bought them for European markets. In the earlier Mamluk period, a route through Upper Egypt, using Qus on the Nile and the Red Sea port of Aydhab, was important; over time, other ports and routes took on greater importance, and Aydhab declined. Merchants in the Red Sea trade used ports in Yemen, which Chapter 40 examines in its own right, and in the fifteenth century the port of Jeddah gained prominence as a western terminus.

It is important to describe the Mamluk position accurately. The Mamluks did not control the Indian Ocean, whose commerce was carried by merchants and ships of many regions and governed by many rulers. They governed crucial western termini and the transit routes to the Mediterranean, collected customs and taxes on the goods that passed through, and intervened in particular commodities at particular times. Their wealth benefited from that position, but it did not depend on it exclusively, as the Historical Debate feature in this chapter discusses.

The Karimi Merchants

The Karimi merchants were a group of prominent Muslim traders associated especially with the spice trade between the Red Sea and Egypt, active mostly in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Their origin and the meaning of their name are debated. They are best understood as a network of wealthy merchants, with family and business connections, who financed and organized trade, rather than as a corporation or an official monopoly. They also acted as patrons, founding and endowing religious and charitable institutions. Their position later weakened as the state intervened more directly in the spice trade, discussed below.

Latin Merchants and Mamluk Authorities

Latin merchant communities, notably Venetians and Genoese and, later, Catalans and others, traded in Alexandria and in Syrian ports under negotiated conditions. Their presence was framed by treaties, such as those that Sultan Qalawun concluded with Genoa and Aragon in 1290, and by arrangements that allowed them consuls, customs agreements, and lodging in funduqs (the Italian fondaci), buildings that combined warehouse, lodging, and place of business. Residence was regulated and often confined, and merchants were subject to customs and to local authority. These were not isolated outsiders excluded from society but participants in a system of negotiated privileges, and they depended on it.

The relationship was not free of tension. The Mamluks and Latin powers were adversaries in the aftermath of the Crusades (Chapter 34), and the Church at times forbade the supply of war material, including metals and timber, to Muslim powers, though popes also granted licenses for limited trade. The Mamluk state, for its part, needed timber, iron, and other goods that Latin merchants supplied, and it profited from customs. Commercial interest repeatedly outweighed political and religious hostility, even though the Alexandria raid of 1365 and similar episodes showed how fragile that balance could be.

State Intervention

The Mamluk state taxed trade and sometimes directly intervened in it. In the fifteenth century, Sultan Barsbay imposed monopolistic measures on commodities including pepper and sugar, purchasing at set prices and reselling, and he sought to direct Indian Ocean trade toward the port of Jeddah. These policies raised revenue for the state but irritated merchants, including Europeans and the established Muslim merchant families, and they illustrate the political risks of commerce. Historians continue to debate the long-term effects of such intervention, and the measures should not be taken as proof of general decline.

Section 38.6

Markets, Regulation, and Artisans

The Suq and the Hisba

The suq was the organizing space of urban commerce, with trades often concentrated in particular streets or markets. Its regulation fell largely to the muhtasib, an official who supervised weights and measures, the quality and pricing of foodstuffs, the honesty of dealings, and aspects of public conduct. The office, called the hisba, combined economic, legal, and moral functions: the same official might inspect a baker's loaves, check a scale, and enforce standards of public behavior. Manuals written for market inspectors, such as one by Ibn al-Ukhuwwa, who lived in Egypt in the early fourteenth century, describe ideal practice, though they should be read as statements of norms and not as direct records of how markets actually worked. Al-Maqrizi himself served as a market inspector in Cairo in the early fifteenth century.

The state also intervened in prices, especially of grain and bread, because shortages threatened public order. Such intervention could be effective or ineffective, and merchants and bakers sometimes hoarded or evaded controls. The relationship between regulation and the market was therefore a continuing negotiation rather than a fixed system.

Artisans and Workshops

Mamluk cities supported a wide range of crafts: textile workers, metalworkers, leatherworkers, glassmakers, builders, potters, food processors, booksellers, copyists, and many others. Workshops were typically small, often family-based, and combined production with retail sale. Scholars have long debated how artisans were organized. European guilds had formal charters, legal privileges, and control over entry and prices, and medieval Islamic craft organization did not map neatly onto them. Occupational groups existed and had recognized leaders and shared interests, and the muhtasib dealt with them, but the evidence does not support casually calling them guilds. This chapter therefore speaks of trades and occupational groups and treats questions of formal organization as a matter of evidence.

Textiles and Luxury Production

Textiles were a cornerstone of urban production. Egyptian linen, based on local flax, was well known, and silk, wool, and cotton goods were produced and traded, along with embroidered fabrics and carpets. Mamluk metalwork, notably inlaid brass and bronze, and enameled glass, such as mosque lamps, are among the best-known material products of the period, many of them made for elite patrons. These objects illustrate an important economic link: the consumption of the military elite, who commissioned buildings, furnishings, and display items, supported skilled artisans and the merchants who supplied them. Elite spending was therefore a source of urban employment, even as it reflected sharp inequality.

Section 38.7

Women, Slavery, and Religious Minorities

Women and Economic Life

Women are underrepresented in narrative chronicles but appear regularly in legal documents and waqf records. Under Islamic law, women could own, inherit, buy, sell, and bequeath property, retain their own wealth after marriage, and receive a dowry, though they typically inherited smaller shares than men, and social norms limited their public activity. Women appear as property owners, litigants, and founders of endowments. Elite women, including relatives of sultans, established pious foundations, and women of more modest means worked in spinning, weaving, food preparation, trade in the household sphere, and service. Women also participated in religious and scholarly life: biographical dictionaries record women who transmitted hadith and taught, including Zaynab bint al-Kamal in Damascus. The evidence shows that women were neither invisible nor equal; they operated within a framework that gave them genuine legal capacity but limited their opportunities relative to men.

Slavery Beyond the Barracks

Chapters 36 and 37 explained military slavery, in which boys purchased from the Qipchaq steppe, the Caucasus, and neighboring regions were trained as soldiers and could rise to command. That system must be distinguished from other forms of slavery in Mamluk society. Enslaved people also worked as domestic servants, concubines, and laborers, and were brought from Africa, the Caucasus, and other regions through different trading networks. Women and men occupied different positions, and enslaved status did not predict a single social outcome: some enslaved people lived in elite households with considerable influence, while others endured heavy labor and had little recourse. Islamic law regulated slavery, permitted manumission, and treated the children of an enslaved woman and her master as free in certain circumstances, but it did not abolish the institution. This chapter treats slavery as part of the economy, not only as a feature of the military system, and the military and non-military forms should not be blurred.

Christians and Jews under Mamluk Rule

Christians and Jews lived under the legal framework of dhimma, which granted them protection and communal autonomy in exchange for a tax, the jizya, and acceptance of certain restrictions. Coptic Christians in Egypt and various Christian communities in Syria, and Rabbanite, Karaite, and other Jewish communities, maintained their own religious institutions and leaders, and their members were prominent as scribes and officials in the fiscal bureaucracy, as merchants, physicians, and craftsmen. Their position was legally unequal: they were subject to special taxes and dress and building regulations, and could be excluded from certain offices. In practice, enforcement varied.

Periodically, pressure intensified. In 1301, decrees imposed restrictions on dress and employment for non-Muslims, and in 1321 anti-Christian violence, including attacks on churches, occurred in Egypt, with some conversion to Islam among Copts in the aftermath. These episodes should not be read as the whole story, nor should the story be reduced to uninterrupted tolerance. Nor should dhimma be equated with modern religious tolerance, since it was an unequal legal framework. A fair account includes integration and specialization, commerce and state service, communal life, legal inequality, and periodic coercion. Religious conversion over the Mamluk centuries also changed the proportions of Muslims, Christians, and Jews, a process that Chapter 24 and later chapters help contextualize.

Section 38.8

Religious Institutions and the Waqf Economy

Mosques, Madrasas, Lodges, and Hospitals as Social Institutions

Chapter 37 treated religious foundations as instruments of legitimacy. They were also working institutions. Mosques held congregational prayer and served as places of teaching and assembly. Madrasas taught law and religious sciences and housed students and teachers. Sufi lodges, called khanqahs in Egypt and Syria, housed mystics and offered devotional practice and hospitality. Shrines attracted visitors. Hospitals, called bimaristans, treated the sick. Together, these institutions provided education, employment, welfare, and spiritual life for the population, and many were located together in large architectural complexes.

The Waqf Economy

What made these institutions durable was the waqf. A waqf was a legal arrangement by which the founder dedicated property, such as agricultural land, shops, baths, or urban buildings, so that its income would support specified purposes in perpetuity. The founder set out the terms in a deed, appointed an administrator, and specified salaries, expenditures, and beneficiaries. Revenue from the endowed property paid for the maintenance of a building, the salaries of teachers, preachers, Qur'an readers, doctors, and staff, and the support of students and the poor. A waqf could also provide for the founder's family, who might be named as administrators or beneficiaries, and in this way it served as a means of preserving wealth and status as well as of pious giving.

The waqf should therefore not be equated simply with charity. It combined religious merit, civic benefit, and family strategy, and it transformed rural revenue into urban infrastructure. Mamluk emirs and sultans were among the most prominent founders, but merchants, scholars, and women also established endowments. The result was an enormous quantity of property tied up in endowed institutions, which supported religious and educational life and which administrators and, at times, rulers could also manipulate or appropriate. Chapter 28 examined the legal basis of the institution; here its economic role is the emphasis.

Madrasas and the Four Legal Schools

Madrasas were centers of legal training in the four Sunni schools of law (madhhabs): Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. Chapter 37 noted the political role of the four chief judges; the educational reality was that each school had its own teachers, texts, and endowed positions. Students traveled in search of teachers, studied texts with them, and received certificates of study. Appointments to teaching posts and the revenues attached to them were objects of patronage and competition. Madrasas were not simply medieval universities: they lacked the corporate structure and degree-granting powers of European universities, and much instruction depended on the personal relationship between teacher and student. They were nonetheless important institutions of learning and employment.

Sufism and Popular Religion

Sufism was woven into urban religious life. Lodges supported mystics, and shrines of saints were visited by people of all ranks. Scholars could be both jurists and Sufis, and the opposition between "Sufi" and "orthodox" that older writing sometimes assumed does not fit the evidence. Debates did occur, and figures such as Ibn Taymiyya criticized certain popular practices, which led to controversy and, at times, imprisonment. Popular devotion, saint visitation, and communal festivals formed part of social life.

Hospitals and Welfare

The hospital in Qalawun's complex illustrates the integration of medicine and endowment. It was supported by endowed revenue and employed physicians and other staff, and its endowment provided for the treatment of Muslims in need, a restriction discussed in the Primary Source Window. Such institutions provided real medical care and a degree of urban welfare, but their capacity was limited, and access should not be assumed to have been universal. They are best understood as important but partial elements of urban health provision, alongside private physicians, household care, and religious healing.

Section 38.9

Learning, Books, Pilgrimage, and Mobility

Books, Manuscripts, and Knowledge

Cairo and Damascus were major centers of textual production. Copyists, booksellers, binders, and paper makers supplied a lively trade in manuscripts, and libraries attached to madrasas and mosques preserved them. Scholars worked in an environment of encyclopedic and historical writing. Al-Nuwayri, who worked in the administration, compiled a large encyclopedia; al-Maqrizi wrote on the topography and institutions of Cairo; Ibn Khaldun, who arrived in Cairo from the Maghreb, wrote and taught in the city; and Ibn Hajar, a judge and hadith scholar, produced major works of scholarship. These individuals illustrate a broader network of scholars, students, and patrons rather than constituting a list of great men to be admired for its own sake; their work depended on the endowed institutions, libraries, and urban markets in which they lived.

Education Beyond the Elite

Education reached further than the madrasa. Children in many towns learned the Qur'an in small schools, and apprentices learned trades in workshops and households. Women learned in domestic settings, and some gained scholarly credentials. The evidence for popular literacy is limited, and modern percentages should not be assigned, since no reliable data exist. What can be said is that literacy and learning varied by class, gender, and occupation, and that the written record overrepresents those with the most education.

Pilgrimage as Social and Economic Movement

Each year, caravans from Cairo and Damascus carried pilgrims to Mecca. The hajj linked the Mamluk lands to the Hijaz, and the Mamluk Sultan's role in organizing and protecting the caravans was a source of prestige, with the ceremonial litter known as the mahmal becoming a prominent symbol. For the economy, the pilgrimage meant demand for animals, provisions, and transport services; markets and charitable activity along the routes; and opportunities for trade, as many pilgrims carried goods. It also connected scholars and ordinary travelers from distant regions. Arabia itself is examined in Chapter 40.

Mobility and Migration

People moved constantly. Merchants, scholars, pilgrims, craftsmen, refugees, soldiers, and enslaved people traveled across Egypt, Syria, Anatolia, Iraq, the Hijaz, the Maghreb, and the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean. Scholars from the Maghreb and Andalusia settled in Cairo; refugees from the Mongol invasions arrived in Syria and Egypt; North African pilgrims often lingered en route. This mobility gave Mamluk cities a cosmopolitan character and moved ideas, skills, and goods across regions.

Section 38.10

Material Life, Inequality, and the World Before 1347

Consumption and Material Life

Material life varied widely. Bread, the staple food, was supplemented by legumes, vegetables, and, for those who could afford it, meat, dairy, fruit, sugar, and spices. Elite households ate varied diets, wore silk and fine linen, and lived in large houses built around courtyards, while poorer residents lived in small rooms or shared lodgings, wore coarse cloth, and ate mainly grain and legumes. Public bathhouses provided hygiene and sociability, water carriers supplied households without access to cisterns or fountains, and endowed fountains offered water to the public. Lighting, utensils, and clothing were products of urban workshops, and the choice among them depended on rank.

Urban Inequality

Prosperous cities contained profound inequality. Emirs and wealthy merchants occupied one end of the social scale; at the other were wage laborers, porters, servants, beggars, and households with no margin against a rise in grain prices. Charitable endowments, almsgiving, and communal solidarity cushioned some of the poorest, but they did not remove precarity. The chronicles record bread riots and protests when prices rose, which indicates that ordinary city dwellers could and did express grievances.

Rural–Urban Interdependence

Cairo and Damascus could not exist without grain, livestock, raw materials, taxes, and labor from villages. Rural producers, in turn, relied on urban markets, finance, administration, and demand. Revenue extracted from villages funded the military and endowed institutions in cities, and that flow sometimes strained cultivators while enabling urban construction and learning. The relationship was therefore both interdependent and unequal, and it connects the agricultural themes of Sections 38.2 and 38.3 with the urban and religious themes that followed.

Mediterranean and Indian Ocean Worlds Meet

Bringing the economic geography together, Alexandria faced the Mediterranean; Cairo linked Mediterranean and Red Sea networks; Syrian cities faced Anatolia and Iraq; pilgrimage routes ran to the Hijaz; and Indian Ocean goods moved westward through Red Sea ports. Mamluk Egypt and Syria were therefore points of articulation among several commercial worlds. From the Mamluk perspective, the Indian Ocean trade reached and affected their lands; how Arabian and Indian Ocean societies and states themselves operated is the subject of Chapter 40, and the Maghreb's Mediterranean links belong to Chapter 39.

Limits of Prosperity

Prosperity had limits even before the plague. Taxation could be heavy, warfare was costly, and flood levels and harvests varied. Prices fluctuated, and state intervention, monopolies, and unequal distribution of wealth created risks for merchants and cultivators. These were structural features of the economy and should not be read as signs of inevitable collapse.

The World Before 1347

On the eve of the plague, Egypt and Syria presented a dense and dynamic system: highly urbanized centers, productive agriculture, long-distance commerce, strong religious and charitable institutions, and a large and varied population, together with deep inequality and vulnerability. The Black Death of 1347–1349 and subsequent plague recurrences profoundly altered these systems; Chapter 41 examines those transformations. This final pre-plague snapshot stops here deliberately, so that the world it described can be understood in its own right rather than only as a prelude to what came after.

Historical Vocabulary
Waqf

A waqf (plural awqaf) is a legal endowment in which property is dedicated so that its income supports stated purposes indefinitely. The founder specifies the purposes, the beneficiaries, and the administrator in a deed.

In the Mamluk period, waqf income supported mosques, madrasas, Sufi lodges, hospitals, fountains, tombs, teachers, students, and Qur'an readers, and sometimes the founder's descendants. It matters because it turned agricultural and commercial wealth into lasting urban institutions.

The modern word "charity" is a misleading equivalent. A waqf could serve worship, education, health, civic infrastructure, and family interests all at once, and it was also a strategy for preserving property and prestige.

Historical Vocabulary
Hisba and the Muhtasib

The hisba was the duty of supervising public morals and market conduct, and the muhtasib was the official who performed it. He inspected weights and measures, food quality and prices, and the standards of trades, and enforced norms of public behavior.

The institution matters because it shows that Mamluk markets were regulated by an overlapping economic, legal, and moral authority. "Market inspector" is a useful but partial translation, since the office also had a religious and ethical dimension.

Historical Vocabulary
Karimi Merchants

The Karimi were a group of wealthy Muslim merchants associated with the spice trade between the Red Sea and Egypt, active especially in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Scholars still debate the origin and meaning of the name.

They matter because they illustrate how long-distance commerce was organized through merchant networks, and how merchants served as financiers and patrons. They should not be imagined as a modern corporation or as a state monopoly.

Primary Source Window

Qalawun's Hospital Waqf, 12 Safar 685 / 9 April 1286

Creator: Sultan Qalawun, through his administration and legal officials · Date: 12 Safar 685 AH / 9 April 1286 CE · Type: Endowment (waqf) deed for the hospital (bimaristan) of his Cairo complex · Archive: Cairo, National Archives, Hujaj Umara' wa-l-Salatin, no. 367, which preserves this document together with a second deed dated 21 Safar 685 / 18 April 1286; a published version appears in Muhammad Muhammad Amin's appendix of Qalawun waqf documents to Ibn Habib's Tadhkirat al-nabih fi ayyam al-Mansur wa-banih

Context: Qalawun was a Mamluk sultan who reigned from 1279 to 1290. His complex on the main street of Cairo, built in 1284–1285, combined a hospital, a madrasa, and his tomb. The surviving principal hospital deeds date from shortly after construction, in 1286, with a further document from 1287.

Paraphrase, not a quotation: the deed endowed revenue-producing property to support the hospital and specified treatment for Muslims in need, male and female, local residents and foreigners, while providing for physicians, medicines, food, and other institutional expenses.

Analysis: The deed shows how a ruler converted revenue into a lasting institution and joined medicine, endowment, and commemoration in one complex. The properties it names show where the founder's wealth lay. It also shows that the institution was charitable but not universal across religious communities: its provisions concern Muslims, and scholarship notes that Christians and Jews were excluded from treatment and employment there. This matters in a chapter that examines Christian and Jewish participation in Mamluk economic life, because the same society could host minority physicians and officials while endowing a major institution for Muslims. The deed states intentions; it does not show how well the hospital functioned, who actually used it, or how administrators managed the endowment over time, and it cannot reveal the experience of patients, staff, or the cultivators whose labor produced the endowed revenue.

  1. What does this deed say the hospital was intended to provide, and for whom?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purposes beyond charity?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the relationship between wealth, religion, and the state?
  4. What can historians not establish from the deed alone?
Historical Debate

Was Mamluk Prosperity Built on Transit Trade or on the Productive Base of Egypt and Syria?

The question: How much did the Mamluk economy depend on the spice corridor and long-distance commerce, as against agriculture, urban production, and internal markets?

Interpretation A — Transit trade as the engine: Some accounts emphasize Egypt's position between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean, the wealth of the Karimi merchants, customs revenue, and the state's later monopolies as evidence that long-distance commerce was central to Mamluk wealth.
Interpretation B — The productive base: Others stress that agriculture, craft production, and domestic consumption supported most of the population and the state, and that long-distance trade, though spectacular, was a smaller share of the whole.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on chronicles, customs and fiscal records, merchant and treaty documents, waqf deeds, and the material evidence of urban production. Quantitative evidence is limited, so conclusions often rest on inference.

Why it matters: If transit trade was central, changes in Red Sea routes or state intervention would have had major economic effects. If the agrarian base was central, then flood levels, taxation, and village conditions matter more. Most historians now treat the two as complementary, with the agrarian base sustaining the state and population while long-distance trade added revenue and prestige.

Student task: Identify what kinds of additional evidence, such as customs figures or production records, would help estimate the relative importance of trade and agriculture.

Multiple Perspectives

One Economy, Many Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and the groups did not hold equal power.

An Egyptian peasant household: The harvest, the flood, and the tax assessment set the year. Its labor feeds the city and supports the revenue holder, and its main protection against a bad year is the village community and the possibility of moving.
A Cairene textile artisan: Employment depends on demand from elite households, merchants, and exporters, and on the market inspector's standards. A change in patrons or in cloth prices affects the household's income.
A Venetian merchant living in a funduq in Alexandria: Business depends on treaty privileges, customs payments, and good relations with local officials, in a setting where residence is regulated and political tension can interrupt trade.
A Muslim scholar supported by a waqf: A teaching post and stipend make a scholarly career possible, but they depend on patrons, appointments, and the continued income of the endowment.
A Coptic administrator or a Jewish physician: Professional skill brings employment and standing, but status is legally unequal, and periodic restrictions or violence can threaten security.
A woman managing inherited property: Legal capacity to own and bequeath property gives economic agency, although social norms and unequal inheritance shares set limits.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Cairo and Alexandria Trade around 1300 and around 1450

What Changed

  • The Karimi merchants were prominent around 1300, while by the mid-fifteenth century state-directed trade and new merchant arrangements had grown in importance
  • Routes shifted, with Aydhab declining and Jeddah rising
  • State intervention became more direct, as in Barsbay's monopolies on pepper and sugar
  • The balance among Latin merchant groups and their arrangements changed over time

What Stayed the Same

  • Egypt remained a transit zone between Indian Ocean and Mediterranean markets
  • Alexandria remained the main meeting point with Latin merchants under negotiated privileges
  • Customs and taxation remained important sources of state revenue
  • Agriculture remained the foundation of the economy

Who experienced change most directly: large merchants and traders in controlled commodities felt the effects of monopolies most, while peasants and many artisans experienced greater continuity in their daily routines.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that change in commerce reflected policy, routes, and merchant networks, and it avoids reducing the fifteenth century to an automatic story of decline.

Cause and Effect

The Growth of Cairo as a Commercial Metropolis

Long-term causes

The agricultural productivity of the Nile Valley and delta, earlier urban growth under the Fatimids and Ayyubids, and the city's position between the Mediterranean and the Red Sea.

Short-term causes

The establishment of Mamluk rule and the concentration of the military elite and administration in Cairo, the arrival of scholars and craftsmen after the Mongol destruction of Baghdad, and the expansion of building and patronage.

Immediate effects

Rising demand for food, materials, and luxury goods, expansion of markets and workshops, growth of the river port, and construction of madrasas, hospitals, and lodges.

Long-term consequences

Cairo became a leading center of learning and commerce in the Mediterranean world, and its institutions, many supported by waqf, shaped urban life for centuries.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Cairo's growth. Agriculture, political centralization, trade routes, elite consumption, and religious and scholarly institutions reinforced one another, and the city's size also depended on the rural revenue that flowed into it.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Day in a Mamluk City

The following is a reconstruction based on patterns documented in many sources, not a record of one person's day. In early morning, bakers fire their ovens and households send out dough or buy bread, the item whose price and weight the market inspector watches most closely. Water carriers supply households without their own cisterns, and shopkeepers open workshops that combine production and sale. The call to prayer shapes the rhythm of the day, and mosques and madrasas hold lessons, while students gather around teachers whose stipends come from endowments.

Through the day, porters carry goods from river ports and caravan stops, artisans work at looms, forges, and benches, and women manage households, produce food and cloth, and, for those with property, deal with agents and relatives. The bathhouse offers a place of cleanliness and sociability, with different times for women and men. In the evening, markets remain active, and poorer residents return to crowded lodgings while emirs' households dine in courtyard residences. The day makes visible both the shared institutions of the city and the sharp differences in how people experienced them. Because the sources largely come from literate, urban, male authors, historians must read them for indirect traces of the work of women, children, laborers, and the poor, and supplement them with legal records, material culture, and archaeology.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has shown that the Mamluk Sultanate rested on a complex agrarian and urban economy rather than on military power alone. Nile agriculture and Syrian farming fed the population and supplied revenue, while cities such as Cairo and Damascus concentrated consumption, craft production, scholarship, and exchange. Chapter 37 explained how the state worked; this chapter has explained how the society and economy beneath it worked.

On the land, basin irrigation made intensive farming possible in Egypt, but outcomes depended on institutions and labor as well as on the flood. The iqta' was a revenue assignment that supported the military, not private feudal ownership, and cadastral surveys such as the rawk of 1298 and 1315 illustrate the state's effort to measure and redistribute revenue. Peasants bore heavy fiscal burdens but retained some leverage through mobility and village solidarity, and the evidence for their lives is limited and indirect.

In Syria, a more varied landscape of rain-fed farming, orchards, irrigated gardens such as the Ghuta of Damascus, and pastoralism supported the cities. Aleppo's position tied it to Anatolia and Iraq, while Alexandria connected Egypt to Mediterranean markets. Cairo, with its river port, markets, and workshops, was a metropolis whose growth reflected agriculture, political centralization, trade routes, and elite consumption together.

Commercially, Egypt and Syria occupied a transit position. Indian Ocean goods reached the Red Sea and crossed to the Nile, while Latin merchants bought them in Alexandria. The Mamluks did not control the Indian Ocean; they governed key termini and transit routes, taxed the goods that passed, and sometimes intervened directly, as in the monopolies of Barsbay. Merchant networks such as the Karimi, and Latin communities operating under treaties, consuls, and fondacos, show that commerce continued despite political and religious rivalry.

Markets were regulated through the hisba, which combined economic, legal, and moral functions, and artisans worked in small workshops whose organization should not be described casually as guilds. Textile, metal, and glass production illustrate how elite consumption supported urban employment. Women had legal capacity to own and bequeath property and appear in legal and endowment records, though they lived under unequal conditions. Enslaved people entered the region through several trades and held very different positions, and the distinction from military slavery must be maintained.

Christians and Jews lived under dhimma, an unequal legal framework that combined protection, specialization, and participation with periodic restrictions and violence, and neither uninterrupted tolerance nor uninterrupted oppression describes their history. Religious institutions functioned as social institutions, and the waqf converted private wealth into mosques, madrasas, lodges, and hospitals, serving worship, education, health, and family interests together.

Scholarly life flourished in the madrasas of the four legal schools, in Sufi settings, and in the book trade, while the hajj linked Cairo and Damascus to the Hijaz and moved goods, people, and ideas. Mobility brought merchants, scholars, refugees, and enslaved people across the region. Material life varied sharply between elite households and poor residents, and urban inequality sat alongside real prosperity.

Rural and urban life were interdependent but unequal: villages supplied food, labor, and revenue, and cities supplied markets, credit, and institutions. Prosperity had limits in taxation, warfare, variable floods, price fluctuations, and the distribution of wealth, but these were features of a functioning system and not signs of inevitable decline.

The chapter has stopped deliberately at the eve of the Black Death. The plague of 1347–1349 and its recurrences profoundly altered population, agriculture, the economy, and institutions, and Chapter 41 examines those transformations. Chapter 39 turns to the post-Almohad Maghreb, and Chapter 40 to Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and the Indian Ocean as regions in their own right.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the Mamluk Sultanate cannot be understood from the Citadel alone. The state's military and political structures, examined in Chapter 37, depended on villages that produced food and revenue, on cities that organized labor and consumption, and on merchants and institutions that connected those cities to wider worlds. Seeing that dependence changes how the period is read: not a military regime above society, but a state embedded within a productive and diverse society.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that run through Part VII and beyond: the conversion of agricultural revenue into urban institutions through the waqf, the importance of transit positions in long-distance commerce, the negotiation of coexistence among religious communities under unequal legal frameworks, and the interdependence of town and countryside. These patterns echo the interplay of water, settlement, and pastoral life examined in Chapter 3, and they shaped the Ottoman cities and provincial societies examined in Chapters 43 to 47.

Finally, the chapter connects regions. Egypt and Syria were linked to the Hijaz by pilgrimage, to Anatolia and Iraq by caravans, to the Mediterranean through Alexandria, and to the Indian Ocean through the Red Sea. Chapter 39 follows the western Islamic world after the Almohads, and Chapter 40 returns to Arabia and the Indian Ocean on their own terms. Chapter 41 then examines how plague transformed the dense and dynamic society described here. Understanding the world before 1347 on its own terms is the necessary first step.
