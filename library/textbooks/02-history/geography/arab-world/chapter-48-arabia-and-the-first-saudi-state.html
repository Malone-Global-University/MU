Part VIII — The Ottoman Arab World
48

Arabia and the First Saudi State

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the eighteenth century Arabia was not a single political unit waiting to be unified. It was a set of competing religious traditions, tribal confederations, oasis emirates, coastal commercial states, and regional dynasties, each with its own relationship to the Ottoman Empire.

Around 1740, a traveler would have crossed very different worlds. In Najd there were walled oasis towns ruled by local families. The Hijaz had the holy cities, governed by Sharifs of Mecca under Ottoman suzerainty. In Oman, the port of Muscat looked toward India and East Africa. In Yemen the Zaydi imams ruled a highland state and coffee flowed to Mocha. On the Gulf coast, new ruling families were building pearling and trading ports. This chapter studies these worlds together.

Its central transformation is the rise of the First Saudi State. An alliance between Muhammad ibn Saud, the ruler of Diriyah, and the preacher Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, conventionally dated 1744, produced a political-religious state that expanded across Najd, eastern Arabia, and the Hijaz before Ottoman-Egyptian forces destroyed it in 1818. Historians disagree about how to explain that expansion, and the chapter presents the debate between those who emphasize religious doctrine and militancy and those who emphasize firearms, trade, and resources.

The chapter also insists that other Arabian societies had their own histories. Oman's Al Bu Sa'id dynasty arose from resistance to Persian intervention and from older Omani political institutions. Yemen's Qasimi imamate declined while its coffee port flourished and then faced new competition. The Gulf's ruling families were forming within networks of trade, pearling, and tribal migration. Saudi expansion touched each of these regions without defining them.

The evidence is uneven. Najdi chroniclers such as Ibn Ghannam and Ibn Bishr wrote as supporters of the movement. Critics wrote treatises against it, Ottoman and Egyptian officials left records of their own, and European travelers and the British East India Company produced reports that were shaped by commercial and strategic interests. Omani, Yemeni, and Gulf sources survive in varying quantities, and the voices of farmers, pastoralists, women, divers, and enslaved people mostly appear only at second hand.

The chapter covers roughly 1700 to 1818. Chapter 47 gives the broader Ottoman provincial context, and Chapter 49 treats Muhammad Ali's Egyptian state-building, so here the Egyptian campaigns appear only as an Ottoman-Egyptian intervention in Arabia. The chapter follows the water and settlement patterns of Chapter 3 and prepares for the Gulf and Arabian developments of later chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political, religious, and economic diversity of Arabia c. 1700.
  2. Explain the main teachings of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab and the objections of other Muslim scholars.
  3. Distinguish the 1727 and 1744 dates in the founding narrative of the First Saudi State.
  4. Analyze how religious legitimacy, taxation, and military mobilization sustained Saudi expansion.
  5. Trace expansion into Najd, al-Ahsa, the Iraqi frontier, and the Hijaz.
  6. Assess Ottoman responses and the Egyptian campaigns that ended the state in 1818.
  7. Explain the rise of the Al Bu Sa'id dynasty and Omani maritime power.
  8. Compare Yemen's Zaydi politics and coffee economy with those of Najd and Oman.
  9. Analyze Gulf state formation and contested ideas of "piracy."
  10. Evaluate sources and interpretations, identifying whose perspectives are missing.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a reform movement in a small Najdi town become the basis of a regional state?
  2. How did the Saudi alliance combine religious and political authority, and why does the founding date matter?
  3. Why did Ottoman authorities respond slowly, and why did the Hijaz change their calculations?
  4. How did Oman, Yemen, and the Gulf develop institutions independently of Saudi expansion?
  5. How did commerce (coffee, pearls, Indian Ocean shipping, pilgrimage) shape political power?
  6. Whose voices survive in our sources, and whose do not?
  7. Which changes after 1818 were durable, and which were temporary?

Historical Context

The Ottomans conquered the Hijaz and the Red Sea coast in the sixteenth century (Chapter 43), and their presence in Arabia was layered and uneven. It was strongest in the holy cities, where it rested on garrisons, payments, and the annual pilgrim caravans from Damascus and Cairo. It was weak in the interior, and in Yemen the Ottomans had been expelled by 1636. Chapter 47 explains the wider Ottoman provincial order that this chapter assumes, in which governors and local elites governed with considerable autonomy.

Arabia's geography and settlement patterns (Chapters 2 and 3) shaped its politics. Oasis towns, highland terraces, and pastoral ranges produced small political units, while pilgrimage, caravan routes, and sea trade produced the wealth of the Hijaz and the coasts. Settled and mobile populations were interdependent, and tribal identities cut across them.

The period was also one of changing commercial and military conditions. Firearms were spreading through Arabian warfare, and trade connected Najd to the Gulf and the Indian Ocean. Coffee, pearls, textiles, and horses moved through networks that included Indian, Persian, Ottoman, and European merchants. British and French rivalry in the Indian Ocean drew outside powers into Gulf and Omani politics.

Religious diversity was part of the setting. Sunni Muslims, Twelver Shi'a, Ibadis, Zaydis, and Jewish communities lived in different regions, and Sufi and saint-venerating practices were widespread. The Wahhabi movement's challenge to those practices, and the opposition of other Muslim scholars to the movement, belong to this setting.

Division I — Arabia's political and social landscape
Section 48.1

Arabia at the Beginning of the Eighteenth Century

Arabia c. 1700 was a set of regions linked by trade, pilgrimage, and kinship rather than a single political unit. The Hijaz, with Mecca, Medina, Jidda, and Ta'if, lived on the hajj, Red Sea commerce, and Ottoman subsidies, and its Sharifs balanced Ottoman officials against Bedouin allies. Najd, the central plateau, was a country of walled oasis towns and tribal ranges. Eastern Arabia (al-Ahsa, Qatif, and the Gulf coast) combined date agriculture with ports and a mixed Sunni and Twelver Shi'a population. Oman, Yemen, and the southern and eastern coasts looked outward to the Indian Ocean.

Ottoman presence was real but uneven. It rested on garrisons and payments in the Hijaz, on the annual pilgrim caravans from Damascus and Cairo, and on provincial governors in Baghdad and Basra, but not on any administration of the interior. Yemen had been lost to the Zaydi imams in the 1630s, and the Gulf coast was contested among local rulers, Persia, and European and Indian merchants.

Settlement patterns followed water (Chapter 3). Farmers worked oasis groves and highland terraces; pastoral tribes moved between wells and pastures; merchants, sailors, and artisans lived in towns and ports. Households crossed these categories, and tribal and religious identities cut across them. Our sources for the period include local chronicles, Ottoman records, European traveler and company reports, and later gazetteers, and each sees only part of the peninsula.

Power followed water and movement. Where wells, springs, and wadis supported groves, towns grew; where pilgrims, caravans, and ships passed, merchants and rulers collected fees. The Hijaz's wealth came from people moving to the holy cities, Najd's from the control of scattered oases and grazing, and the coasts' from shipping. Different resources produced different political forms: a sharifate under imperial suzerainty, small town emirates, an elective imamate, a highland imamate, and merchant-ruler families. A movement that could combine several of these resources, as the Saudi state eventually did, would change the balance among them.

Section 48.2

Najd: Tribes, Oasis Towns, and Local Authority

Najd is a plateau of rocky uplands, sand belts, and long dry valleys, with settlement concentrated along wadis such as the Wadi Hanifa, where Diriyah, Uyayna, and later Riyadh lay. Towns were walled clusters of mud-brick houses around groves of date palms and fields of wheat and barley irrigated from wells, often drawn by animals. Further north and east, the towns of the Qasim, such as Buraydah and Unayzah, built wealth from caravan trade with Basra, Syria, and the Hijaz, and merchants from these towns carried goods, news, and money across Arabia.

Each town normally had its own ruling family, a council of leading households, a judge (qadi), and a mosque teacher. Authority was personal and negotiated. Emirs depended on the loyalty of kin and neighbors, income from fields and trade, and agreements with nearby tribes. Najd's scholars were mostly Hanbali, and local scholars trained in town schools were often judges, teachers, and landholders. Rivalries among ruling lineages, and among towns, produced frequent raiding and shifting alliances, which kept any one town from dominating its neighbors for long.

Pastoral tribes such as the Anizah, Mutayr, and Utaybah moved their camels and sheep across the plateau according to rainfall. Their relations with townspeople combined trade, kinship, and conflict. Bedouin bought dates, grain, and cloth, and sold animals, wool, and transport. Settled communities often paid protection money (khuwwa) to powerful tribes in return for safe passage, a practice that shows that mobile groups held real political leverage (Chapter 3). Many people moved between the two ways of life. Men's and women's labor was divided along lines that varied by household and community: women worked in food preparation, weaving, and animal care, while men handled long-distance herding and trade. We have almost no direct written testimony from women or from farmers and pastoralists, and our narrative sources are the work of town scholars.

These conditions mattered for what followed. Najd had no overarching ruler, and the Ottomans did not govern it. A ruler who could unite towns and tribes under one set of rules could therefore expect to gain from the fragmentation of the existing order, but only if he could overcome the rivalries that had always prevented it.

Najd's environment limited what any ruler could do. As Halevi summarizes it, water was scarce enough that the largest oasis towns held only several thousand people, and the date palms, wheat, barley, and other crops sustained them in years free of locusts, drought, cholera, or smallpox. The chronicles of Ahmad al-Manqur (d. 1713) and Muhammad ibn Rabi'a (d. 1745) are extremely brief but dwell on disasters, such as the great famine of 1674, when survivors migrated east, and the famine of 1724 in which forty-two people died in Unayzah. Fortified walls and the lack of an agricultural hinterland for besiegers made towns hard to take. Halevi notes a wave of fortification building before the Saudi expansion, with Unayzah, Thadiq, and Riyadh raising fortified structures around 1678, 1723, and 1737 and Buraydah building its first wall around 1740.

Several chiefdoms held power in this landscape. Halevi accepts that they existed and were strong enough to resist Saudi subjugation for decades in some cases, but notes that they struggled to master even neighboring settlements: Unayzah itself consisted of four walled settlements with numerous clans. The political fragmentation described here is therefore not an absence of states so much as a competition among many small ones.

Division II — Religious reform and Saudi state formation
Section 48.3

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab and Religious Reform

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (c. 1703–1792) was born in Uyayna, a Najdi town, into a family of Hanbali scholars; his father served as a judge. He studied in Najd and, according to the traditions, in Medina, and traveled in Iraq and perhaps elsewhere before returning to teach. He drew on the writings of the medieval Hanbali jurist Ibn Taymiyya and his student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, and he regarded many popular practices as incompatible with God's oneness. These included seeking help from saints, tombs, trees, and stones, and ritual customs that he treated as innovations (bid'a).

His preaching made enemies. Opponents included scholars in Najd, among them his own brother Sulayman ibn Abd al-Wahhab, who wrote a refutation, as well as scholars in the Hijaz, Iraq, Yemen, and Egypt. Their central objection was that he was too ready to declare other Muslims unbelievers (takfir) and to treat them as legitimate targets of war. His defenders, and later historians who have studied his own writings, argue that his treatises are more restrained than the practice of the later movement. Both positions rest on the sources, and the controversy is discussed in the Historical Debate below.

The movement's religious program had social consequences. It stressed regular prayer, payment of zakat, and punishment under Islamic law, and it challenged the authority of local saintly families and of scholars who defended the older practices. In small oasis towns, where such families held land and local standing, those claims could be politically as well as religiously significant.

Four terms organize the movement's argument. Tawhid, God's oneness, was for Ibn Abd al-Wahhab above all a rule about worship: prayer, supplication, vows, and sacrifice belong to God alone. Shirk, associating others with God, was therefore the opposite, and he applied it to appeals to saints or the dead for help. Bid'a, innovation, covered practices without scriptural basis, such as building domes over graves. Takfir, the declaration that a person has left Islam, was the most consequential, because it determined whether a Muslim who persisted in such practices could be fought, taxed, or have property seized. The Hanbali tradition gave him a legal framework, and the works of Ibn Taymiyya a medieval precedent for attacking practices he saw as idolatrous, though scholars disagree about how closely he followed them.

Here the distinction among four layers matters. The first is the founder's own writings, which stress doctrine and argue from texts. The second is his teaching in letters and answers to questions, which at times is more cautious about who may be called an unbeliever. The third is the practice of the Saudi rulers and their commanders, who applied these categories to towns and tribes in war. The fourth is the response of critics, who argued that the founder and his followers misused texts to treat ordinary Muslims as idolaters. Historians such as Bunzel read the early movement as militant from the start, while others stress the founder's restraint; both readings rely on selecting among these layers, and the Historical Debate below returns to the question.

Relations with other scholars were mostly hostile outside Diriyah. Opponents in Najd included men trained in the same Hanbali schools, and scholars in the Hijaz, Iraq, and Yemen also wrote against him. The disputes were doctrinal, but they also concerned who could speak for religion in a town. The sources do not allow us to say which social groups tended to support or oppose him.

Historical Vocabulary

Tawhid

Tawhid is the Islamic doctrine of God's absolute oneness. All Muslims affirm it; the historical question is how it is applied. For Ibn Abd al-Wahhab it required that worship be directed to God alone, which led him to condemn intercession practices that many Sufi and popular traditions accepted. "Wahhabi" was originally a label used by opponents; supporters preferred "muwahhidun" (those who affirm God's oneness). Students should avoid treating either label as neutral.

Primary Source Window

Ibn Abd al-Wahhab, Kitab al-Tawhid (Book of Divine Oneness), mid-eighteenth century

Context: Written by the preacher in Najd as a teaching text for followers. Source type: religious treatise organized as chapters of Qur'anic verses and hadith with brief comment.

Paraphrase: the work argues that worship, supplication, and reliance belong to God alone, and that appeals to saints or the dead, charms, and similar practices contradict this duty.

Passage examined: the opening of the book. In published Arabic texts it begins with the heading Kitab al-Tawhid and, immediately after, the Qur’anic verse 51:56 (“I did not create jinn and humankind except to worship Me”). That verse is scripture, not the author’s words. The first numbered chapter, “The merit of tawhid and the sins it expiates,” opens with Qur’an 6:82 and a hadith. The author’s own contribution is in the chapter headings and in the lists of numbered points (masa’il) that follow the scriptural passages, such as one stating that the wisdom of creating jinn and humans is worship. The book contains 66 chapters; later headings include chapters on fear of shirk, on wearing rings or threads to avert harm, on amulets, on seeking blessing from trees or stones, on vows to other than God, on intercession, and on worship at the grave of a righteous man.

Analysis: The structure shows a teaching method: a thesis, scripture, and points of benefit. It also shows what the author treated as the central problem, namely devotion directed to anyone but God, and it names the practices he considered as violating it. It does not show how these ideas were enforced. The book contains no account of warfare or taxation, and its rulings on shirk do not themselves establish how rulers applied them. Opponents such as Sulayman ibn Abd al-Wahhab disputed his reading of these same texts. Passages in the book are quoted in Arabic editions of different dates and with later commentaries, so a historian also needs to know which edition and commentary is being used.

  2. What does the choice of 51:56 as the first verse suggest about the book’s purpose?
  3. Which chapter headings show practices the author considered shirk, and what social settings might they refer to?
  4. What can the book not tell historians about the Saudi state, and which other sources would they need?
Section 48.4

Diriyah, Muhammad ibn Saud, and the Political-Religious Alliance

Diriyah lay in the Wadi Hanifa, a small town of farmers and traders ruled by the Al Saud, whose emir Muhammad ibn Saud took power in 1727 in the later Saudi reckoning. Before 1744 it was one of several small emirates in the valley, and its rival Uyayna was larger. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's first patron was Uthman ibn Mu'ammar, the ruler of Uyayna, who supported him for a time. According to the chronicles, he acted against a revered tomb, enforced punishments, and attracted the hostility of rival rulers, including the Banu Khalid emir of al-Ahsa, whose pressure led Ibn Mu'ammar to send him away. The details come from supporters' accounts and cannot be confirmed independently.

Ibn Abd al-Wahhab moved to Diriyah, where the chronicles say that Muhammad ibn Saud, urged by family members, offered him protection and agreed to support his mission. The date conventionally given is 1744. The agreement gave the preacher security and an armed patron, and it gave the emir a religious cause that could unite towns and tribes under his authority and justify war on neighbors who would not accept it. Historians stress that both men gained, and that the alliance gave the Saudi house a way to expand beyond the usual limits of an oasis emirate.

The two dates in the Saudi founding narrative therefore represent two claims. The official Saudi chronology dates the state to 1727, connecting the dynasty to Muhammad ibn Saud's accession. Others see 1744 as the beginning of the political-religious state that expanded across Arabia. The question is partly one of definition: whether the state begins with a ruling house or with the project that transformed it.

A further episode complicates the founding narrative. According to Ibn Bishr's brief chronicle of events before 1744, the ruler of Diriyah, Zayd ibn Markhan, was shot dead with two muskets in 1139 AH (1726 or 1727) while negotiating in the castle of Uyayna, and Muhammad ibn Saud succeeded him. Halevi observes that, had the first histories of the dynasty not been written by Wahhabi authors, historians might begin the story with this event rather than with the 1744 pact. The same chronicle records that in 1721 the Banu Khalid chief Sa'dun ibn Muhammad had shown the cannons of al-Ahsa in an invasion of Najd that plundered three quarters of Diriyah. Both reports are from a chronicler writing long after the events, and neither can be independently confirmed, but they place Diriyah in a world of firearms and external powers before the alliance.

Historical Debate

Religion, Statecraft, and the Origins of Saudi Expansion

The question: What made a small oasis emirate expand so rapidly after 1744: religious doctrine, military and commercial resources, or both?

Interpretation A (religious doctrine as the engine): Cole M. Bunzel, in Wahhābism: The History of a Militant Islamic Movement (2023), argues that militancy was part of the movement from its origins. In his reading, Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's doctrines on tawhid and takfir made it legitimate to fight Muslims who did not accept them, and so the movement's wars follow from its theology. He disagrees with scholars who portray the founder as more moderate than his followers.
Interpretation B (military and commercial resources): Leor Halevi, in “Arabians for guns: Wahhabi matchlocks, world trade, and the rise of the first Saudi state” (Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society, 2023), argues that matchlock firearms obtained through Gulf and Indian Ocean trade networks were critical to Saudi expansion, supplementing explanations that concentrate on ideology. His argument is that Saudi expansion cannot be explained by doctrine alone and that the evidence for imported matchlocks, and for the Hijaz and other regions' use of them, shows how the state fits into world trade; he does not deny that doctrine motivated warfare. The anonymous chronicle Lam' al-Shihab (c. 1817) is central to his case.
Interpretation C (sociological and political explanations): Halevi surveys earlier explanations. Michael Cook, studying the long conflict over Washm, considered commercial causes but doubted them for lack of evidence and suggested that Saudi warriors' main advantage was an obstinacy of faith. The Saudi historians Abdulaziz Fahad and Khalid al-Dakhil argued instead that Wahhabism was an urban ideology of state formation, and Michael Crawford wrote that the initial aim was a regime of godliness rather than a state. Halevi doubts the sociological readings, since the sequence of urban coalition followed by Bedouin pacification did not occur, and he doubts any ideological unity among the state's subjects.

Evidence: Bunzel works mainly from Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's writings, those of his followers, and the Najdi chronicles. Halevi uses accounts of weapons and trade, together with narrative sources, to reconstruct how the Saudi state armed itself. The two kinds of evidence answer different questions: what the movement said it was doing, and what it needed to do it. Their arguments address different aspects of state formation and are not directly contradictory.

Why it matters: The interpretations are not necessarily rivals. Bunzel explains why the movement regarded war on other Muslims as lawful, and Halevi helps explain why Diriyah could win. Which one a historian emphasizes affects whether the state appears as a religious project, a conventional emirate, or an unusual combination.

Student task: Identify one claim that each historian's evidence can support and one that it cannot, and say what additional evidence would test them.

Section 48.5

Government, Religious Authority, and the First Saudi State

The ruler of Diriyah governed as emir and, in time, took the title imam, which expressed a claim to lead the community in religious as well as political affairs. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab and the scholars who followed him taught, judged, and advised, and the movement's chroniclers, Ibn Ghannam and Ibn Bishr, present the two as a unified leadership. In practice the ruler decided on war and tribute, and the scholars supplied the doctrinal justification.

Subject towns received governors, judges, and preachers sent from Diriyah. Revenue came from zakat on crops and herds, from the state's share of booty (ghanima), and from tribute from submitting groups. Armies combined town levies with Bedouin contingents, and men fought under local commanders, so mobilization depended on persuasion and shared interest. Recent research in the Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society argues that matchlock firearms obtained through world trade networks were important to Saudi military strength, which suggests that this inland state was tied to global commerce more closely than the older picture of isolation allows.

Authority was therefore layered. Tribes could submit, withdraw, and submit again, and towns could rebel when Diriyah seemed weak. The state's durability depended on keeping these bargains and on continued success, which also meant it was vulnerable once an outside power could match its resources.

Judges and teachers were a principal instrument of rule. A submitting town normally received a qadi who applied the movement's Hanbali law, a preacher who taught the doctrine, and often a governor chosen from the ruling house or from a trusted local family. Chroniclers say that attendance at prayer and payment of zakat were tests of submission. In practice the line between religious and political obligation was thin, since refusal to pay was regarded as a sign of disobedience to the religious order as well as to the emir.

The firearms question has been newly examined. Leor Halevi's 2023 article, which is open access, is built on a chronicle, Lam' al-Shihab, composed by an anonymous author around 1817, shortly before the fall of Diriyah. That text, which Halevi describes as a non-Wahhabi Gulf observer's account apparently written for a British official, says that Saudi Najd had become a gunpowder state and that it produced gunpowder. Halevi regards the claims of local manufacture as exaggerated, but argues that matchlocks played an absolutely critical role in the state's expansion, that they were probably imported, and that Najd was more closely connected to world trade than a revisionist reading of the older picture of an isolated interior allows. He notes that other Arabian societies, in the Hijaz, Tihama, Yemen, and Oman, and the Banu Khalid, had adopted matchlocks as well. A documented point, therefore, is that guns were widely used; the claim that they decided the outcome of Saudi expansion is Halevi's interpretation, and he allows that doctrine may have motivated the wars.

Other work adds detail. A Leeds doctoral study of the state's administrative, military, and economic features draws on Arabic and European sources, and Nicholas Roberts's 2025 article on 'Oceanic Wahhabism' shows Najdi merchants engaged in Indian Ocean trade, including a ruling by a Diriyah scholar, around 1810, allowing Wahhabi merchants to do business in non-Wahhabi lands. They suggest that the Saudi state's fiscal and military systems relied on commerce as well as on tribute and zakat, and that doctrine on relations with outsiders was adjusted to commercial need.

European observers, as quoted by Halevi, described a notably small apparatus. Burckhardt reported that the state relied on a few shaykhs to enforce law in their districts and recruit troops, appointed salaried judges everywhere, sent tax collectors across the realm, and kept an elite guard of just 300 armored cavalry. It had no standing army, since a system of requisition sufficed, and no conventional police force, and Burckhardt noted that the Wahhabis had no coin of their own. Soldiers were expected, unless poor, to equip themselves and bring their own provisions. Order was kept through heavy fines and expropriations backed by the threat of force. These are an outsider's descriptions: Burckhardt traveled in the Hijaz in 1814–1815 and died in 1817, and his notes on the Bedouins and Wahhabis were published posthumously in 1830. Halevi uses them with that caution.

Mobilization figures must be read the same way. European and Arab sources agree that nearly every Bedouin and Wahhabi infantryman or camel-rider carried a matchlock, while elite horsemen did not necessarily do so. Halevi notes that estimates of the army at Karbala (about 15,000 by one contemporary account) and at the battles of al-Safra (1811, about 18,000 and 800 horsemen according to Ibn Bishr) and Basal (1815, 24,000 to 30,000) show that the state could deploy more than 10,000 men, although it rarely mobilized more than that. Estimates of the total pool of men able to bear arms vary enormously, from about 84,000 to over 450,000, and Halevi doubts their operational meaning. He also records that one Hijazi woman, Ghaliya of Turaba (d. 1818), ruled her tribe and became a notable commander.

Whether the guns came from Najdi workshops or from trade is where documented findings and inference diverge. The documented points are the chronicle evidence of gunfire in Najdi warfare from the 1720s and 1740s on, the observers' reports of widespread matchlocks, and the claim in Lam' al-Shihab that a matchlock cost five to ten riyals. Halevi's inference is that a local industry could not have equipped tens of thousands of men, since he estimates that a smith working about 150 hours per gun would produce perhaps 15 a year, and that the weapons were therefore probably imported. He also argues that horses were Najd's most valued export overseas and that camels carried the weapons trade and the infantry.

A historiographical correction is worth noting. M. S. M. El-Shaafy's 1967 Leeds dissertation, a study of the state's administrative, military, and economic features, described early Saudi warriors as ill equipped and preferring swords, lances, and darts. Halevi shows that this rested on a misreading of the French writer Corancez, who was describing the situation around 1804, not 1744, and who had not visited Najd. The dissertation remains a useful study of the state's administration, but its picture of early weaponry is not secure.

Section 48.6

The Consolidation of Najd

The expansion that followed was slow, contested, and often reversed. The chronicles describe the first campaigns after 1744 as defensive and then as acts of jihad against neighbors who rejected the movement. The decisive rival was Dahham ibn Dawwas, the ruler of Riyadh, who for almost three decades fought Diriyah, often in coalition with other towns and tribes and at times with outside support from the Banu Khalid of al-Ahsa. The long struggle followed a pattern of raids, sieges, and temporary agreements. It ended in 1773, when Dahham left Riyadh and the town came under Saudi authority.

Under Abd al-Aziz ibn Muhammad (r. 1765–1803) Saudi power grew in several regions at once and not in a single line after 1773. Campaigns in the Washm, Sudayr, Kharj, and the Aflaj, and in the Qasim, where merchant towns like Buraydah and Unayzah resisted for years, overlapped over several decades, and control of each district was won, lost, and regained. In the south, Najran, whose Isma'ili leaders rejected the movement, and the Banu Khalid of al-Ahsa were Diriyah's major external rivals. Halevi notes that firearms helped Saudi forces against invaders from Najran and al-Ahsa from 1775; Najran was not simply conquered. The pattern in the districts that did submit was repeated: a campaign, a negotiated submission or a defeat, the appointment of a governor, judge, and preacher, the payment of zakat and tribute, and, if the town rebelled, a second campaign. The traveler Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, observing in 1814–1815 (his notes were published posthumously), reported that powerful Bedouin groups sworn to the state still refused full subjection and that Saud imposed heavy fines on those who absconded from campaigns.

The movement's war was financed by what it captured and taxed, and by trade. If Halevi's argument is right, imports of matchlocks through Gulf ports, and the revenue to pay for them, gave Diriyah an advantage over towns with only traditional weapons, though the details of this supply system are still being reconstructed. Defeated towns faced destruction of tombs and shrines, obligatory instruction, and, in some cases, loss of property and political independence.

Experiences differed and the evidence is uneven. Towns that submitted paid taxes and received a judge and a teacher; those that rebelled, such as Thadiq, were taxed more heavily afterward. Bedouin groups were treated as allies or opponents according to their choices. We know little of women's experience beyond occasional mentions in the chronicles of captured households, and historians should not assume that all inhabitants experienced the state in the same way.

Halevi's reading of Ibn Ghannam and Ibn Bishr gives the clearest picture of how the early campaigns worked. In the first battle between Diriyah and Riyadh, around 1746 at Manfuha, Saudi shooters fired from rooftops at invaders holding a castle and wounded Dahham ibn Dawwas's horse. In 1748 the battle of al-Bunayya began with a day of musket fire from a distance. Raids targeted livestock: in 1752 about forty Saudi raiders from Uyayna took the flocks of al-Dilam in al-Kharj, and when some hundred Dilamis caught up with them, the raiders charged and killed about thirty men. In 1756 Abd al-Aziz ibn Muhammad led a campaign against Thadiq, where musket fire in the palm groves allowed the Saudis to cut down trees; the town, which Mengin later said held about 1,200 people, lost eight men, submitted, was assigned a missionary, rebelled two years later, and was forced to accept a heavy agricultural tax.

Several tactics recur. Because fortified walls usually held, fighting took place outside them, in ambushes, livestock raids, and the destruction of crops and irrigation. Saudi forces cut down date palms and counted them as they counted dead men: four palms were felled in an expedition against Riyadh in 1748, a dam on the torrent was demolished at Manfuha in 1756, and about 2,000 palms were reportedly cut at al-Dilam in 1781. Attempts to breach walls with tunnels, ladders, or ruses mostly failed, as at Tharmada in 1757, where the guards, alerted to a tunnel, sent slaves out to trap the raiders. Artillery was not decisive in this period: Banu Khalid cannon failed against Diriyah in 1764 and at al-Bid' in 1781, and Sharif Ghalib's seven cannons failed to take a small Saudi fort at al-Sha'ra in 1791. Only the Egyptian siege artillery of 1816–1818 breached Diriyah's walls.

Halevi's central point about consolidation is that towns submitted less because they were persuaded than because they feared the cost of resistance and saw benefits in joining. He also cautions that the sequence was not neat: some Bedouin groups became Saudi subjects earlier than some towns. The chronicles' battle stories cannot all be confirmed, as he acknowledges, but he finds that the patterns of warfare they show are plausible.

Cause and Effect

Expansion of the First Saudi State

Long-term causes: the political fragmentation of Najd, with no ruler able to dominate it; the absence of Ottoman power in the interior; and the importance of trade and tribute in oasis politics.

Short-term causes: the 1744 alliance, which gave Diriyah an ideology and a mission; access to firearms and revenue; and a succession of rulers, notably Abd al-Aziz, who could keep the alliance together.

Event: Between the 1740s and 1790s, Najd's towns and tribes were brought under a single authority.

Immediate effects: new taxes and appointees, the destruction of shrines, resistance and rebellion, and the movement of Saudi power toward al-Ahsa, Iraq, and the Hijaz.

Long-term consequences: the precedent of a Najdi state resting on a religious program, which survived the defeat of 1818.

Historical complexity: religion, weapons, trade, leadership, and the weakness of enemies all contributed, and historians such as Bunzel and Halevi emphasize different parts of the story.

Division III — Expansion and Ottoman confrontation
Section 48.7

Expansion into al-Ahsa and Eastern Arabia

The Banu Khalid, rulers of al-Ahsa and the Qatif coast since the seventeenth century, were Bedouin lords who governed a prosperous region of date groves, springs, and Gulf ports with a mixed Sunni and Twelver Shi'a population. They were not a weak rival: Halevi notes that Banu Khalid rulers used cannon in Najd as early as 1721. Saudi forces raided and besieged the region during the 1790s, and the campaigns, which Saudi sources place mostly in 1793–1796, ended Banu Khalid rule. Hofuf, the main town, fell in 1795 according to the usual reckoning. Durable control came only after a governor and garrison were installed and local notables were reconciled, and the region continued to experience revolt.

The conquest gave the Saudi state agricultural revenue, trade through Qatif, 'Uqayr, and nearby ports, and a base on the Gulf that linked it to Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman. Shi'a communities were subject to a doctrine that condemned shrines, mourning rituals, and certain legal practices. Our evidence for their treatment comes mainly from Najdi chronicles and hostile outside accounts, so historians treat claims of uniform persecution or of full toleration with caution. It seems clear that public observance was restricted and that taxation and the need for local intermediaries shaped how far the restrictions were applied.

Halevi's reading of Ibn Bishr shows that the Banu Khalid used artillery against Najd well before the Saudi conquest of al-Ahsa. Sa'dun ibn Muhammad showed the cannons of al-Ahsa in 1721, and by 1764 Urayir ibn Dujayn had cannon that could not damage Diriyah's thick walls. In 1781 his successor Sa'dun ibn Urayir fired at the hastily built Saudi fort at al-Bid' without breaching it, and the next year his army marched to al-Qasim and besieged Buraydah, then under Saudi control, where the smiths tried to cast guns from scrounged brass without success. Halevi concludes that the Saudi conquest of Banu Khalid territory in the late 1780s and early 1790s depended on firepower accumulated earlier inland. He does not discuss the experience of al-Ahsa's Sunni or Shi'a residents, and the sources used here do not either.

Lorimer's gazetteer, compiled for the British Indian government in 1915, helps to separate the stages. He reports that Hasa was reduced for the first time in 1792 and finally conquered in 1795, when it was placed under Wahhabi governors and religious instructors, and that the capture of the port of Qatif in 1800 completed the conquest and brought Saudi power to the Gulf shore. The Banu Khalid, whom he describes as a possible bulwark for Ottoman Iraq, were broken in these campaigns. The sequence can therefore be read as incursion and first reduction (1792), conquest (1795), and consolidation of the coast (1800). Lorimer wrote from a British imperial standpoint and relied on earlier compilations, so his dates should be checked against the Najdi chronicles. Al-Ahsa then became a Saudi base for influence on Bahrain and the Gulf, and it was the first place where Saudi and Ottoman forces met in 1798–99.

Section 48.8

The Hijaz, Mecca, Medina, and the Politics of Pilgrimage

The Hijaz was a different political world. The Sharifs of Mecca, recognized by the Ottoman sultan, governed Mecca, Jidda, and Ta'if, drew income from pilgrimage, customs, and Ottoman subsidies, and balanced Bedouin allies against Ottoman officials. Saudi forces had raided the region for years. The TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi, an Ottoman-Turkish reference work, lists the Sharif of Mecca, with the Isma'ili Makarima of Najran and the Muntafiq of Iraq, among the early opponents of the rising Saudi power.

The chronology of 1802–1806 shows control passing back and forth. The TDV entry on Abd al-Aziz ibn Muhammad ibn Saud reports that Ta'if was taken and plundered in February 1803, after which Saudi forces turned to Mecca. A related account says that Sharif Ghalib's brother Abd al-Mu'in invited them into the city to prevent a massacre, and they entered on 30 April 1803. (The TDV article on Mecca names Saud ibn Abd al-Aziz as the commander, while the TDV entry on his father describes Abd al-Aziz's withdrawal to Najd; the discrepancy is not resolved here.) After about four days they marched on Jidda, where Ghalib and the governor of Jidda, Sharif Pasha, defeated them. Ghalib's siege of Mecca began on 12 July, and he recovered the city in August 1803. Abd al-Aziz was killed at Diriyah in late 1803. The Saudis did not weaken but became more aggressive: the Turkish historian Erhan Afyoncu dates their occupation of Medina to June 1805 and their second occupation of Mecca to January 1806. The Ottoman government, he notes, was occupied with the French invasion of Egypt, a Russian war, and internal disturbances, and could not intervene.

Saud's forces destroyed domes over tombs and enforced prayer and a ban on tobacco and certain rituals. A large Saudi contingent was present at the pilgrimage, as the Spanish traveler Ali Bey observed in 1807. The Syrian caravan, which brought pilgrims, subsidies, and trade goods, reached the Hijaz under difficult conditions in 1804–1805, according to Robert Schick's research, and was obstructed in 1806. Saudi authorities objected to some caravan ceremonies as forbidden practices. Ghalib remained sharif in the intervals of Saudi control, his loyalties shifted with circumstances, and he cooperated with the Egyptian forces when they came. According to a TDV biographical entry, he was deposed in 1813 and went to Salonika with his son; other accounts mention a transit through Egypt, and sources differ on the exact sequence.

For the Hijazis the effects were economic as well as religious: fewer pilgrims meant lost income for guides, merchants, lodging-keepers, and the many people who depended on the hajj. The Muslim world noticed, and that is why the seizure of the holy cities carried weight beyond Arabia.

Hijazi politics included armed tribes as well as the Sharif. In 1791 Ghalib besieged a small Saudi fort at al-Sha'ra with seven cannons whose shot had been reinforced with iron bars and chains, and the army retreated to Mecca without taking it. Burckhardt, who traveled in the Hijaz in 1814–1815, estimated that the Hudhayl lost over 300 of their best men before submitting to the Saudis, that the 'Adwan were nearly exterminated by Muhammad Ali's army after allying with the Saudi state, and that the various branches of the Harb had 30,000 to 40,000 guns. These are an outsider's estimates, but they indicate how widely firearms were held. Ali Bey, in Mecca in 1807, estimated 45,000 Wahhabi pilgrims among about 83,000 pilgrims.

Section 48.9

The Iraqi Frontier and Religious Conflict

The Iraqi frontier was where Saudi power first pressed on Ottoman provincial authority. Lorimer reports that by 1798 even the town of Hillah suffered from Wahhabi raids, that Baghdad's governors could not afford to protect the frontier, and that inhabitants paid blackmail to the raiders. The tribes of Iraq were not a single bloc. In May 1802 Lorimer describes the attitude of several, including the Khaza'il and the Shammar Toqah, as doubtful. The Muntafiq confederation, whose Sa'dun shaykhs were Ottoman clients and Basra's chief defense, led several responses, and the chronology of those responses is disputed.

Lorimer places a Baghdad-sponsored expedition under the Muntafiq chief Thuwaini after the attack on Karbala. An Arabic-language study, as summarized in its abstract, dates Thuwaini's campaigns to 1786 and 1797, says he was killed, and describes a campaign under Thamir ibn Sa'dun in 1798, and research by Giovanni Bonacina is reported to place his fatal campaign in 1797, before Karbala. One British-era compilation places it in 1801. The weight of the scholarship therefore favors 1797, and this chapter treats Lorimer's post-Karbala placement as unreliable while noting the disagreement. The failed Baghdad response in 1802 that Lorimer reports is not confirmed by the other sources used here.

The attack on Karbala belongs to this frontier war. Lorimer and Encyclopaedia Iranica give 20 or 21 April 1801. Halevi, using Ibn Bishr's report of a cavalry setting out in Dhu al-Qa'da 1216 AH, which ended on 3 April 1802, argues for 20 April 1802. The two traditions rest on different kinds of source: a British compilation drawing on earlier European accounts, and a Najdi chronicle written later. This chapter therefore gives the date as 1801/1802. The accounts agree that Saudi forces attacked the shrine city, plundered it, and killed many inhabitants. Estimates of the dead vary widely and are unreliable, and Shi'a sources record the event as a massacre while Najdi chronicles present it as a campaign against shirk.

Contemporary diplomatic records show that Karbala was quickly a regional matter. British Library records (IOR/L/PS/9/76/248, digitized by the Qatar Digital Library) include a record of a 6 June 1802 meeting between the British envoy Harford Jones and Sulayman Pasha in Baghdad that discussed the attack on Karbala and a possible Persian expedition, and a 12 July letter discussing routes for Persian intervention, including via Bahrain. They show that Ottoman and Persian responses were being discussed. They do not show that the proposed Persian expedition took place.

Raids continued afterward. Saudi forces attacked Najaf and Samawa without success, and about 1810 Abdallah ibn Saud, son of the ruler, reached a short distance from Baghdad, according to Lorimer. Lorimer also reports that valuables from a Shi'a shrine were, it is said, temporarily moved to Kadhimayn near Baghdad, and that Sulayman Pasha tried to placate the sovereigns of Turkey and Persia, whose subjects were angered by the attack. Baghdad's response was largely defensive, because the province could not match the Saudi state in the desert, and the attack gave an Iranian and Shi'a dimension to what had been a frontier dispute.

Section 48.10

Ottoman Responses to Saudi Expansion

The Saudi state challenged Ottoman interests in three ways: by raiding the Iraqi frontier, by absorbing al-Ahsa, whose Banu Khalid rulers had been a buffer, and, after 1803, by taking the holy cities that the sultan claimed to protect. The response depended on provincial governors with limited means. A Turkish popular account says that Istanbul first learned of the movement in 1749 from a letter of the Sharif of Mecca and treated it as religious deviance, sending a scholar to advise. The Ottomans' later problem was practical: a Turkish reference summary says that the provinces of Baghdad and Damascus could not prevent Saudi forces from taking the Hijaz.

The first provincial effort was in 1797–98. According to the abstract of an Arabic-language study, Istanbul was urging Sulayman Pasha to act in 1797, and he restored the Muntafiq shaykh Thuwaini to his post to lead tribal forces. Whether Thuwaini was killed in 1797 or later is disputed (see 48.9). Lorimer reports that for two or three years the Porte could not induce Sulayman Pasha to mount an expedition, and that one finally set out in 1798, when raiding had reached Hillah.

Sulayman Pasha organized the 1798–99 expedition, but it was commanded by Ali Pasha, his kehiyah, and marched from Basra, which it had reached by early December, with Diriyah as its objective by way of al-Ahsa. According to Lorimer, the armies faced each other for three days without fighting, and a six-year truce was arranged at Thaj during the Ottoman retirement toward Basra, in May 1799, and ratified at Baghdad with a Saudi envoy present. Lorimer judged it an ill-managed expedition that inspired Saudi contempt for Ottoman troops, and the truce was not kept. He is a British writer relying on earlier sources, and an Ottoman account would add detail on command, supply, and tribal support. The expedition preceded Karbala, whatever the date of that attack. Lorimer reports a second Baghdad response after Karbala that also failed, but the leadership and date of any 1802 expedition remain uncertain.

Imperial constraints affected the response. Lorimer notes that the Ottoman government was paying for wars with Austria, Russia, and France, and that Britain and Russia assisted it after the beginning of 1799, and Afyoncu adds internal disorder. In the Hijaz, the governor of Damascus and the sharif were the main authorities, and Syrian pilgrim caravans passed with difficulty in 1804–1805 and were obstructed in 1806 (Schick). Ghalib informed Istanbul of the situation in 1805, according to one Turkish study. The sultan then turned to Muhammad Ali, governor of Egypt, whose resources made an expedition possible; a Turkish reference summary says he was assigned the task after the provinces of Baghdad and Damascus failed to stop the Saudis. Chapter 49 treats the Egyptian side.

Section 48.11

The Ottoman-Egyptian Campaigns and the Fall of Diriyah

The war that ended the first Saudi state fell into four stages, and each depended on a different balance of force and loyalty. In 1811 Muhammad Ali's son Tusun landed at Yanbu with an Egyptian army. Ottoman forces had to fight inland, away from their ports, against an enemy that knew the ground. At al-Safra the Egyptians were trapped in the gorges and defeated, with about 18,000 Saudi warriors and 800 horsemen taking part, according to Ibn Bishr. The defeat showed that a campaign in the Hijaz could not succeed by force alone.

The second stage was political as much as military. Reinforced, the Egyptians took Medina in late 1812, and Mecca and Ta'if in early 1813, with Mecca's recovery dated by the TDV article to 23 January 1813. Ghalib, who had cooperated with the Egyptians, was deposed that year and sent to Salonika. Hijazi tribes that had submitted to the Saudis changed sides or were punished: Burckhardt estimated that the 'Adwan were nearly exterminated by Muhammad Ali's army after allying with the Saudi state. Muhammad Ali himself came to the Hijaz in 1813, and the keys of Mecca were sent to Istanbul in one account. The Ottoman sultan could now present the Hijaz as recovered, and Muhammad Ali's sons later received titles and offices in Jidda and Mecca.

The third stage took the war to Najd. Saud ibn Abd al-Aziz died in 1814 and his son Abdallah succeeded him. In 1815 an Egyptian expedition under Tusun marched into central Arabia from mid-March to the end of June. It won the battle of Basal near Turaba, where Saudi forces, estimated at 24,000 to 30,000, many from the tribes of Asir, were defeated by a ruse, and it halted for four weeks in al-Qasim. A treaty with Abdallah followed, but Muhammad Ali did not ratify it. Lorimer suggests that he disliked his son's disobedience and success, or thought the terms too lenient, and the sources do not allow a firmer judgment. The settlement failed because both sides could treat it as a pause.

The final stage was a methodical conquest. Muhammad Ali sent Ibrahim Pasha in 1816 with orders to crush the Saudis by advancing on Diriyah, and operations in Najd began in 1817. Ibrahim's campaign was a series of operations against fortified towns, and it combined sieges, negotiation, and the winning of tribal leaders. Halevi observes that by the 1810s the Saudis possessed large-caliber guns but used them mainly for defense, and that it took the siege artillery of Ottoman Egypt, bombarding Najd's cities between 1816 and 1818, to breach their mud-brick walls. The Saudi forces withdrew to Diriyah, which surrendered in September 1818 after a siege. Ibrahim took Abdallah and many others captive, according to Afyoncu, and Abdallah was sent to Istanbul and executed. The Egyptians demolished Diriyah's fortifications.

The campaigns had immediate and longer-term consequences. The Hijaz returned to Ottoman-Egyptian administration, which meant that Muhammad Ali's power now extended into Arabia, a subject for Chapter 49. In Najd the Saudi family and the religious tradition survived, and, looking forward, Turki ibn Abdallah regained Riyadh in 1824 and re-established the Saudi emirate (TDV, on Wahhabism). Estimates of the armies come from Ibn Bishr, Burckhardt, and Finati, differ, and should be treated as estimates. Logistics undoubtedly shaped the campaigns, since supply came by sea to Red Sea ports and overland into the interior, but the sources used here give little detail on it.

Division IV — Other Arabian political and commercial worlds
Section 48.12

Oman's Imamate and the Rise of the Al Bu Sa'id Dynasty

Oman's Ya'aruba imams, in power from 1624, drove the Portuguese from Muscat in 1650 and built a naval and commercial state. In the eighteenth century the imamate was torn by rival claimants. Imam Saif ibn Sultan II called on Persian help against a rival elected imam, Sultan ibn Murshid, and Persian forces under Nader Shah's commander Taqi Khan arrived from 1737, took Muscat, and besieged Sohar. Ahmad ibn Sa'id, governor of Sohar and a leading merchant of that port, defended it. Sultan ibn Murshid was killed in the defense of Sohar in 1743, and Ahmad, after a siege of about nine months, negotiated a surrender in which the Persian commander confirmed him as governor of Sohar and Barka in return for tribute. A rival imam, Bal'arab ibn Himyar, held the interior.

Dates for what followed are contested. The orientalist C. F. Beckingham argued in 1941 that the commonly given dates of 1741 for Ahmad's election and 1775 for the end of his reign were wrong, and that his election probably occurred in 1749, and his death in 1783; other works give 1744. Jones and Ridout's A History of Modern Oman gives 1749. The sequence is clear enough: Ahmad emerged as the military and political leader of resistance to the Persians in the 1740s, Bal'arab ibn Himyar died in 1749, and Ahmad's election as imam followed, according to Beckingham a Persian garrison still held Julfar in 1748. The distinction between his emergence as governor and his election as imam is important because the second gave him religious legitimacy and the first gave him power.

Succession was a recurring source of conflict. Ahmad's son Said ibn Ahmad kept the title of imam while real power passed to his son Hamad, and two of Ahmad's sons, Sultan and Saif, seized the forts guarding Muscat's harbor in 1781. Sultan ibn Ahmad ruled from 1792 to 1804. When he died, his sons Salim and Said were young and a regent, Muhammad ibn Nasir al-Jabri, was named; Sultan's brother Qais ibn Ahmad tried to seize power, and Badr ibn Saif, a nephew of Sultan, emerged as the effective ruler. In July 1806 Said ibn Sultan lured Badr to Barka and killed him, and Said became ruler. His accession is therefore dated 1806, not 1804. Said's long reign belongs mostly to the nineteenth century.

Muscat's politics cannot be separated from its commerce. An Arabic-language study of trade in Muscat under Ahmad ibn Sa'id (1744–1783), known here through its abstract, says that he was one of the largest merchants in Muscat before and after he took power, that he kept a war fleet to protect merchant ships, and that he also owned a trading fleet carrying goods between the Arabian and Gulf coasts and the wider world. His authority thus rested on a coastal commercial base as well as on election as imam, which helps explain why the relationship between the coast and the Ibadi interior, whose tribal leaders and scholars regarded the imamate as elective, remained a central tension. The sources consulted here say little about the interior's farmers and the aflaj that watered their fields (Chapter 3), and the later nineteenth- and twentieth-century evidence that dates were the farmers' main source of income should not be projected back without caution.

Historical Vocabulary

Ibadi Imamate

Ibadism is a branch of Islam distinct from Sunnism and Shi'ism, with a long presence in Oman. The imamate is the office of the community's leader, traditionally chosen for merit rather than inherited. Under the Al Bu Sa'id the office and the ruling title diverged over time. "Imam" and "sultan" are not interchangeable.

Section 48.13

Muscat, Maritime Power, and the Indian Ocean

Muscat's commerce rested on a partnership between rulers and merchants. Ahmad ibn Sa'id was himself a merchant, and the merchants of Muscat included Omani Arabs, Baluch, Persians, and Indian traders, notably Banyans from Gujarat and Kutch and Khojas. Historians of the Indian community note that Indian merchants played a role in Muscat's entrepot trade under the Portuguese and the Ya'aruba, and that under the Al Bu Sa'id, toward the end of the eighteenth century, they became more important as Muscat grew into an emporium of the Indian Ocean and Gulf trade (Fukuda). Calvin Allen, studying Banyans in Oman over a longer period, describes them as brokers, importers, exporters, retailers, and financiers who connected the Omani interior with the wider Indian Ocean economy. Those findings concern a long period, and they should be applied to the eighteenth century with caution.

The goods that passed through Muscat linked several regions. Gulf pearls, Mocha coffee, Indian textiles and rice, and dates from Oman's own groves all moved through the port, and Muscat's fleet carried goods between the Gulf, India, and East Africa. East Africa supplied ivory and enslaved people, who were used as domestic servants, sailors, and agricultural workers in Oman and elsewhere. The evidence for the scale of slavery in this period is limited. We have no reliable figures for sailors, whose labor kept the fleets moving, or for the women of coastal and interior households, and the commercial records that survive mostly concern merchants and rulers.

Commerce also determined political relationships. The imam's war fleet protected merchant shipping, customs revenue supported the ruler, and Indian financiers were drawn into government finance. Politics in the early nineteenth century was tied to the same networks. Sultan ibn Ahmad ruled from 1792 to 1804, as described in 48.12. After his death Badr ibn Saif held effective power until Sultan's son Sa'id ibn Sultan took it by force in 1806. According to one account, Badr arranged for Saudi forces to attack Sohar, then held by his rival Qais ibn Ahmad, which shows that Najd's power reached into Omani affairs. Sa'id also had to deal with the Qawasim and with British and French interests, a story continued in 48.17. His long reign belongs mostly to the nineteenth century, when Oman's East African domains, including Zanzibar, became far more important, and before 1818 the Omani system was centered on Muscat, the Gulf, and India.

Section 48.14

Yemen: Zaydi Imams, Tribal Politics, and Regional Authority

The Qasimi imams, beginning with al-Mansur al-Qasim (r. 1597–1620) and his successor al-Mu'ayyad Muhammad, led a revolt against the Ottomans that drove them from Yemen by 1636, and the imams then expanded into the coastal lowlands and the eastern stretch of Hadramawt. Zaydi doctrine required an imam to be descended from the Prophet's family and to claim office publicly, and the imams based their legitimacy on that descent. The northern highland tribes, notably the Hashid and Bakil, were the imamate's military base and also its chief constraint.

In the eighteenth century the Qasimi state lost the territory and stability of its predecessors. Historians note that the imamate had no cohesive rule of succession, that family quarrels and tribal insubordination weakened it, and that the territory it controlled shrank from the later seventeenth century, with lowland and southern districts such as Lahij becoming separate polities. By the mid-eighteenth century the imams had become dynastic and a more formal bureaucracy had been established, and the older Zaydi idea of rising up against unjust rule was less prominent. Some historians argue that the imams, adopting the style of other Middle Eastern monarchies, became distant figures and lost some of their charismatic standing among the tribes. These are summaries from reference works, and the underlying chronicles and archival studies would give a more detailed picture.

The imams also had foreign relations. A conference abstract, drawing on Yemeni chronicles, European travel writing, and Dutch East India Company archives, reports that early in the eighteenth century Imam al-Mahdi Muhammad received French and Dutch merchants and a Safavid embassy at his court near Dhamar, and that his successor al-Mutawakkil al-Qasim received an Ottoman embassy from Jidda that tried to discourage him from letting European merchants buy coffee. The episode shows that the imams could act as rulers of a commercial state as well as religious leaders. Sunni Shafi'i populations in the Tihama and the south and Jewish communities in highland towns lived under the imams' rule or under neighboring rulers, but the sources say little about their experience.

The imamate's weakness was also external. Reference works note that Saudi attacks on Yemen after 1800 contributed to its decline, but the sources consulted here do not allow a detailed account. The Qasimi state remained a region of competing powers: the imams in Sanaa, tribal confederations in the northern highlands, and separate lowland and southern rulers. Historians of the period draw mainly on Yemeni chronicles, which were written for or about rulers, and on European merchants' records, so the political history is much better documented than the social history of highland villages.

The political system depended on exchange among three groups: imams, who claimed religious legitimacy and drew revenue from the ports; tribal leaders, who supplied armed force and could withhold it; and scholars, who administered law and justified the imam's claim. None could govern without the others. That helps explain the pattern the sources record: when succession was disputed or tribal support was withdrawn, the state lost territory, and lowland and southern rulers became independent.

Section 48.15

Yemen's Ports, Coffee Commerce, and Social Economy

Mocha was a commercial society and not simply a coffee port. Nancy Um's study of its merchant houses shows that after the Qasimi imams ousted the Ottomans in the 1630s, the port's trade turned eastward toward the Indian Ocean and coastal India. Merchants and shipowners from Asian, African, and European shores came to buy coffee and aromatics and to sell Indian textiles, Asian spices, and silver from the New World. Her study treats Mocha as a hub linking overseas cities, agricultural hinterlands, and inland market centers, and examines the ship captains (nakhudas) and merchants who worked there. Academic research on the port stresses that textiles, spices, minerals, and aromatics were important alongside coffee.

Coffee remained the main export and the basis of the imams' revenue. Until the end of the seventeenth century the world's supply came almost entirely from Yemen. In the eighteenth century Dutch plantations on Java and the rise of the Atlantic plantation economies competed with Yemeni coffee, and the Dutch, who had kept a factory at Mocha from 1614, closed it in 1738. Competition reduced Yemen's share, but it did not end the trade, and Britannica reports that Mocha still had about 20,000 inhabitants in the early 1800s. The more decisive decline came in the nineteenth century, when trade was diverted to other ports.

The commercial system also had political effects. A conference abstract reports that early in the eighteenth century Imam al-Mahdi Muhammad received French and Dutch merchants, and that his successor received an Ottoman embassy from Jidda that tried to discourage him from allowing Europeans to buy coffee. Customs and brokerage gave local governors an interest in the ports, while the same trade depended on foreign merchants. The sources are largely European company records, travelers' accounts, and Yemeni chronicles written for rulers, and they say more about merchants and rulers than about highland farmers, porters, or women in highland and port households. Enslaved people lived in Yemen as in the wider region, but the surviving evidence does not allow their numbers or conditions to be described reliably.

Section 48.16

Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and Emerging Gulf Polities

The Gulf's ruling families emerged from migrations of Arab tribes to the coast. The Utub, a federation of clans from central Arabia, are traditionally said to have settled near Kuwait Bay in 1716, an area influenced by the Banu Khalid. Clans including the Al Sabah, Al Khalifa, and Al Jalahma shared power there, and one account says that the Al Sabah held governance while the Al Khalifa managed trade. The Utub had a pearling fleet of about 800 boats by 1765, according to one account. About 1766 the Al Khalifa and Al Jalahma moved to Zubara in northwestern Qatar, leaving the Al Sabah as the sole rulers of Kuwait. A SOAS study of the Utbi states suggests that the commercial success of Kuwait and Zubara aroused the jealousy of other Arab tribes, especially on the Persian coast.

Zubara became a pearling and trading center and by 1790 its port was larger than that of Qatif. It was attacked in 1778 and 1782 by Nasr Al Madhkur, the Persian-appointed ruler of Bahrain and Bushehr, and from about 1780 it was threatened by Saudi raids on the Banu Khalid. In response, Utub forces from Kuwait and Zubara captured Bahrain in 1782–1783 under Ahmad ibn Muhammad Al Khalifa, with Al Bin Ali and other clans, and the accounts differ on the exact dates of the fighting. The Al Khalifa have ruled the islands since, but the shift from Zubara was gradual and links with Zubara continued, as UNESCO's documentation of the site describes. Saudi forces occupied Zubara in 1809, and control of Bahrain was again contested in the early 1800s; this chapter does not narrate those changes.

The Gulf's societies were organized around the sea. Merchants financed boats and trade, captains led the fleets, and divers and sailors did the work, but little eighteenth-century evidence survives on their wages or debts, and claims about indebtedness are inferences from later records. Ruling families depended on merchants' revenue and loyalty and on the ability to protect shipping, which made them politically vulnerable to the rivals whose interests ran across the water. These polities negotiated among Persia, Basra, Muscat, Saudi Najd, and the British, and none was a mere object of any one of them. Accounts of the period are often brief, and a fuller treatment would use Gulf chronicles and the British records on which later historians drew.

Section 48.17

The Qawasim, Maritime Rivalries, and Foreign Intervention

The Qawasim, based at Ras al-Khaimah, with a branch at Lingah on the Persian coast, built a fleet and were Muscat's rivals for control of the lower Gulf. Their conflict with Muscat predates both the Saudi alliance and the British campaigns: after fighting in the 1760s, all but Ras al-Khaimah made peace in 1763, Ras al-Khaimah submitted in 1771 and revolted in 1775. From the 1790s the Qawasim came increasingly under Saudi influence, and later accounts say that the Saudi state supported them. The relationship helped them against Muscat, and it also brought the Saudi state onto the Gulf coast, where it met British and Omani interests.

The East India Company (EIC) was concerned with its trade between India and the Gulf and with possible French influence, and it allied with Muscat and Persia. It attributed a series of attacks on shipping, including those off Sindh in 1808, to Qasimi 'pirates'. In 1809 it sent a force that took Ras al-Khaimah, Lingah, Luft, and Shinas, but, according to Lorimer, no permanent measures followed, and 'piracy' soon reappeared. Said ibn Sultan of Muscat mounted an expedition against Ras al-Khaimah in 1813, intended to restore the deposed Sultan ibn Saqr as a ruler acknowledging Muscat's suzerainty, and an attempted blockade of Ras al-Khaimah in 1816 was ineffective. Hassan ibn Rahma ruled Ras al-Khaimah from 1814, and a British arrangement with the Qawasim was made in 1815, which had broken down by 1819.

Interpretation depends on whose evidence is used. British Indian records, including those compiled by Lorimer, describe piracy and blame Wahhabi influence. Sultan bin Muhammad Al Qasimi, the ruler of Sharjah, argues in The Myth of Arab Piracy in the Gulf, according to the publisher's description, that British piracy allegations supported the East India Company's commercial ambitions. That is a later political argument by a descendant of the Qasimi rulers, as well as a historical interpretation, and it must be tested against the documents. British records were produced by an interested administration, and Qasimi accounts survive less fully, so neither side's narrative can be taken without checking. The 1819–1820 British expedition and the General Maritime Treaty of 1820 fall just beyond this chapter and are mentioned only as a forward-looking point.

Division V — Historical synthesis and review
Section 48.18

Arabia Transformed: Political Authority, Society, and Regional Legacies

Comparing the regions shows that the peninsula's political authority took several different forms. In Najd, the state came from a coalition of a ruling house and a reform movement and relied on negotiated submission and firearms. In the Hijaz, the Sharifs governed under Ottoman sovereignty and depended on the pilgrimage economy. In Oman, an elective imamate gave way to a dynasty whose strength lay in Muscat's commerce. In Yemen, a Zaydi imamate with a dynastic tendency rested on tribal support and the coffee trade. On the Gulf coast, tribal migrants became merchant-ruler families whose revenues came from pearls, shipping, and customs.

Religion operated differently in each of these settings. For the Saudi state it supplied a mission and a legal order, and its opponents, from Najdi scholars to Shi'a communities in Iraq and al-Ahsa, defined themselves against it. In Oman, the Ibadi tradition gave the imamate its legitimacy while the dynasty drew on commerce. In Yemen, the Zaydi idea of the imam as a descendant of the Prophet's family who claims office was a source of both authority and instability. Sunni and Shi'a communities in the Gulf and Iraq lived under rulers who claimed different religious bases, and the chapter's sources rarely give their own voices.

Commerce and imperial pressure shaped all these systems. Firearms, horses, and caravan trade connected Najd to global networks; Mocha and Muscat connected Yemen and Oman to the Indian Ocean; and Gulf pearls and shipping tied the coast to India. Outside powers entered in different ways: the Ottomans through Baghdad, Damascus, and finally Egypt, Persia on the Gulf, and the East India Company in maritime disputes. In each case local rulers negotiated with outside powers as well as resisting them.

What lasted was mostly what was rooted in local society. The Saudi state fell, but its dynasty and religious tradition survived, while Omani, Gulf, and Hijazi ruling families kept or recovered their positions. The social history behind these institutions remains the least known part: farmers, pastoralists, merchants' agents, sailors, divers, women, and enslaved people sustained these societies, and the records give only glimpses of them. That limitation is itself a historical finding about whose experience became part of the record.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Arabian Political Authority

What changed? Najd gained a state; Gulf and Omani dynasties consolidated; the Ottoman-Egyptian presence in the Hijaz deepened; Britain emerged as a Gulf power.

What remained continuous? Local authority, tribal alliance, oasis agriculture, pilgrimage and pearl economies.

Who experienced change most directly? Residents of the holy cities, Shi'a communities in al-Ahsa and Iraq, and Najdi towns.

Who experienced continuity? Pastoral groups, highland Yemeni farmers, and Gulf divers.

Why does it matter? It shows how political change can coexist with enduring social structures.

Chapter Summary

Eighteenth-century Arabia was politically plural. Najd's oasis emirates, the Hijaz's pilgrimage cities, Oman's maritime imamate, Yemen's highland and port societies, and the Gulf's emerging ruling families each had their own institutions and economies, and none was waiting for unification.

In Najd, small walled towns competed under local ruling families, with Bedouin tribes as both partners and rivals. Environmental limits, fortified settlements, and the absence of an outside ruler had kept any one town from dominating its neighbors.

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab taught that worship must be directed to God alone and condemned practices he regarded as shirk. He drew on the Hanbali tradition and Ibn Taymiyya, and was opposed by many scholars, including his own brother, who objected to his readiness to declare Muslims unbelievers.

The alliance with Muhammad ibn Saud, conventionally dated 1744, gave the movement a state and the ruler a mission. Official Saudi chronology dates the state to 1727, and historians treat the two dates as answering different questions.

The state expanded over decades through campaigns, negotiated submissions, taxation, and appointed judges and governors. Dahham ibn Dawwas's resistance in Riyadh ended in 1773, and expansion elsewhere overlapped and was contested. Historians such as Cole Bunzel stress the movement's militant doctrine, and Leor Halevi stresses firearms and trade, and the two arguments address different aspects of state formation.

Saudi forces took al-Ahsa in the 1790s, attacked Karbala in 1801 or 1802 (the date is disputed), and took Ta'if (1803), Mecca (April 1803, retaken that August, taken again in January 1806), and Medina (1805). These actions challenged the sultan's custody of the holy cities and disrupted the pilgrim caravans.

The Ottoman provinces had responded earlier, through a Muntafiq-led effort in 1797 (whose chronology is disputed) and the 1798–99 Baghdad expedition under Ali Pasha, which ended in a truce, but the loss of the holy cities led the sultan to turn to Muhammad Ali of Egypt. His forces recovered the Hijaz in 1812–1813, and under Ibrahim Pasha took Diriyah in September 1818, ending the first state.

Elsewhere in Arabia, Ahmad ibn Sa'id led resistance to Persian forces and became imam in 1749, founding the Al Bu Sa'id line, and Sa'id ibn Sultan took power in 1806. Yemen's Qasimi imamate shrank while Mocha's coffee trade faced new competition, and Utub clans founded Kuwait and took Bahrain in 1782–83.

The chapter's lesson is that Arabia's transformation was regional and multi-centered. Conquests were temporary, but ruling families, port economies, and religious networks lasted, and the Saudi tradition survived defeat to return in later chapters.

Key Terms

  • Tawhid: God's oneness, understood in the movement as exclusive worship of God.
  • Shirk: Associating partners with God, which the movement applied to appeals to saints and the dead.
  • Bid'a: Religious innovation without scriptural basis.
  • Takfir: The declaration that a Muslim is an unbeliever.
  • Hanbali school: One of four Sunni legal schools, dominant among Najdi scholars.
  • Emirate: A territory ruled by an emir.
  • Imam (Saudi use): Title adopted by Saudi rulers to express religious and political leadership.
  • Qadi: A judge applying Islamic law; the Saudi state appointed qadis to subject towns.
  • Zakat: Almsgiving that also served as a tax.
  • Ghanima: Booty, a share of which went to the ruler.
  • Khuwwa: Protection payments from settled communities to powerful tribes.
  • Matchlock: An early firearm central to Halevi's argument about Saudi expansion.
  • Banu Khalid: Rulers of al-Ahsa and rivals of Diriyah.
  • Sharifate of Mecca: The office of the Sharif, governor of the Hijaz under Ottoman suzerainty.
  • Hajj caravan: The annual pilgrim caravans from Damascus and Cairo.
  • Kehiyah: A deputy or steward, such as Ali Pasha under Sulayman Pasha.
  • Ibadi imamate: Omani system in which the imam was traditionally chosen, not born to the office.
  • Al Bu Sa'id: Dynasty founded by Ahmad ibn Sa'id in Oman.
  • Zaydi imamate: Yemen's highland state led by a descendant of the Prophet who claims office.
  • Qasimi: The dynasty of Zaydi imams that expelled the Ottomans.
  • Mocha: Yemen's leading coffee port.
  • Utub: A federation of clans including the Al Sabah and Al Khalifa.
  • Pearling: Diving for pearls, financed by merchants and boat captains.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that Arabia's modern political order did not arise from a single center. Reform, trade, dynasty-building, and imperial intervention each pulled in different directions.

The First Saudi State shaped later history by establishing a model of political-religious authority and a memory of both achievement and destruction. Oman, Yemen, and the Gulf equally shaped later chapters through their dynasties, commerce, and relations with Europe.

The chapter also shows how historians work. The same events appear differently in Najdi chronicles, Shi'a memory, Ottoman and British records, and modern scholarship, and the evidence is least generous about the farmers, pastoralists, women, divers, and enslaved people who made these societies work.

Chapter 49 turns to Egypt, where the power that ended Diriyah was building a new state, and later chapters follow Arabia's political order through the nineteenth century and beyond.
