Part VI — Regional Powers and the Crusading Age
36

The Mongol Shock and the Rise of the Mamluks

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1249 a French king landed in Egypt, a dying sultan was carried to a camp on the Nile, and a corps of purchased soldiers stood ready to decide who would rule. Within eleven years, Baghdad, seat of the Abbasid caliphs for nearly five centuries, had been stormed and its caliph killed; Aleppo and Damascus had fallen to a Mongol army; and a regime of former slave soldiers in Cairo had defeated part of that army in Galilee. This chapter explains how those events fit together.

The narrative of this chapter begins in Egypt rather than Iraq, because the two crises it joins were developing in parallel. In the 1240s the Ayyubid dynasty founded by Saladin was divided among rival principalities in Egypt, Syria, and the Jazira. Its Egyptian sultan, al-Salih Ayyub, had come to rely on large numbers of purchased soldiers, the mamluks, because he distrusted the free-born troops and relatives who had repeatedly betrayed his predecessors. At the same moment, far to the east, Mongol armies that had already overrun Central Asia and Iran were preparing a new campaign westward. No one in Cairo or Damascus in 1249 could have known that these two developments, one internal and one external, were about to converge.

The chapter's central question is how the collapse of the Ayyubid political order and the westward expansion of the Mongols transformed the eastern Arab lands, and how a military elite created within earlier Islamic states became the rulers of Egypt and Syria. Answering it requires resisting three familiar simplifications. First, the Mongols did not appear suddenly at Baghdad in 1258; the destruction of the caliphal capital was one stage in a campaign planned over years and part of an expansion that began four decades earlier. Second, the Mamluks were not created by the Mongol invasion; military slavery had a history of roughly four centuries in Islamic states, and Chapter 35 closed with Ayyubid Egypt already dependent on it. Third, the Battle of Ayn Jalut in 1260, though enormously important, did not permanently stop the Mongols; it ended the Mongol occupation of Syria and secured Egypt, but warfare between Mongols and Mamluks continued for decades.

The chapter also asks students to see the period as a redistribution of political power rather than as the destruction and miraculous rescue of a single unified Islamic civilization. The Abbasid political order centered on Baghdad disappeared. Mongol rule became entrenched in Iraq and Iran. The Ayyubid order collapsed in Egypt, and a military elite became sovereign. Cairo and Damascus were drawn into one Egyptian-Syrian state. Arabic learning, Islamic law, commerce, and urban life continued through all of this, though under changed patronage and in changed places.

The evidence for these events is rich but uneven and often partisan. Arabic chronicles written in Cairo and Damascus, such as those of Ibn Wasil, Ibn Taghribirdi, and al-Maqrizi, supply much of the narrative of the Egyptian side, though some were composed decades or generations after the events. Persian chronicles produced under Mongol patronage, above all the work of Rashid al-Din, present the Mongol viewpoint. Latin, Armenian, and Syriac sources add the perspectives of Franks, Armenians, and Eastern Christians. Each tradition has its own loyalties, its own tendency to exaggerate numbers, and its own stake in explaining why events turned out as they did.

Geographically, the chapter moves among the Nile Delta, Cairo, the Iranian plateau, the Tigris plain around Baghdad, northern Syria and the Jazira, the Galilee, and the Frankish coast around Acre. It treats Egypt, Syria, and Iraq together because their fates were tied, and it keeps non-Muslim and non-Arab actors in view: Turks and Kipchaks who supplied the mamluk soldiery, Mongols and their Turkic and Persian subordinates, Armenians of Cilicia, Franks, Georgians, Kurds, and the Syrian and Iraqi Christian and Jewish communities who lived through each change of rule.

The chapter is a transition and crisis chapter. It stops around 1260–1261, with Baybars established as sultan and an Abbasid caliph installed in Cairo. The mature Mamluk state, its institutions, its long confrontation with the Mongols, and its claims to legitimacy are the subject of Chapter 37; the society and economy of the Mamluk age belong to Chapter 38. Here the task is to explain how the world that Chapter 37 inherits came into being.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Ayyubid Egypt and Syria were politically unstable in the 1240s and how reliance on mamluk troops grew out of that instability.
  2. Distinguish mamluk as a social and military status from the Mamluk Sultanate as a state.
  3. Trace the sequence from al-Salih Ayyub's death in 1249 through Mansura, Fariskur, Shajar al-Durr's reign, and Aybak's rise.
  4. Describe the Mongol expansion up to 1256, and explain why it should be understood as a long process rather than a sudden event.
  5. Analyze the siege and destruction of Baghdad in 1258, treating casualty figures and rhetorical accounts with appropriate caution.
  6. Assess what the events of 1258 did and did not end.
  7. Explain why Hülegü withdrew much of his army in 1260 and why this matters for interpreting Ayn Jalut.
  8. Interpret the alliances of 1260, including the Mongol cooperation of Armenian Cilicia and Antioch and the neutrality of the Franks of Acre, as evidence against a simple Christian-versus-Muslim binary.
  9. Evaluate competing explanations of the Mamluk victory at Ayn Jalut.
  10. Analyze how the assassination of Qutuz exposed the central political problem of military rule.
  11. Connect Baybars's installation of an Abbasid caliph in Cairo to earlier discussions of caliphal legitimacy in Chapters 25, 30, and 33.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a dynasty founded by Saladin come to depend on purchased soldiers, and what risks did that dependence create?
  2. How should historians weigh the role of Shajar al-Durr, whose reign is often treated as a curiosity but who was central to the transfer of power in 1250?
  3. In what sense was the destruction of Baghdad the culmination of a long Mongol expansion, and in what sense was it a sudden shock?
  4. What did the Abbasid caliph still represent in 1258, and what was lost when he was killed?
  5. How do different source traditions, Arabic, Persian, Latin, Armenian, and Syriac, shape our understanding of the same events?
  6. Why did the Mongol force at Ayn Jalut face the Mamluks with reduced strength, and what does this imply for how the battle should be remembered?
  7. Why did different Christian powers behave differently toward the Mongols and Mamluks in 1260?
  8. What problem of political succession did armies that could defend a state also create?

Historical Context

Chapter 35 traced the Zengid and Ayyubid consolidation that reconquered Jerusalem in 1187 and left Saladin's descendants ruling Egypt, Syria, and parts of the Jazira and Yemen as a confederation of rival princes. After Saladin's death in 1193 the family fought repeatedly over inheritance, though it also produced capable rulers such as al-Adil and al-Kamil. By the 1240s the Ayyubid realm consisted of competing principalities centered on Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, and Kerak, each ruled by a different branch of the dynasty and each willing, at moments, to ally with Franks or other outsiders against relatives.

The Frankish states survived as a coastal strip centered on Acre, with Antioch and Tripoli to the north, weakened but diplomatically active. The Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad, discussed in Chapters 30 and 33, had recovered a degree of independence in Iraq in the generations after the decline of the Seljuks, but they did not rule the old empire. Their authority was religious and symbolic across the Sunni world, and politically effective chiefly in central Iraq. Beyond them, in Iran and Central Asia, the destruction of the Khwarazmian Empire by the Mongols in the 1220s had already reshaped the eastern frontier of the Islamic world.

This chapter assumes that earlier background and moves forward from it. It does not retell the Ayyubid succession struggles or the history of the Seljuks, which belong to earlier chapters, and it does not describe how the Mamluk state functioned over the following two and a half centuries, which belongs to Chapter 37.

Section 36.1

The Eastern Arab Lands in the 1240s

The regional order of the 1240s was already unstable before Hülegü left Mongolia. Ayyubid princes in Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Kerak, and Cairo competed for territory and prestige, and several made alliances with Frankish rulers against their own kin. In 1244 the Egyptian sultan al-Salih Ayyub, then fighting for Damascus, allied with displaced Khwarazmian military bands, Turkic horsemen who had moved west before the Mongol advance. These Khwarazmian forces sacked Jerusalem that year and then helped defeat a coalition of Franks and Syrian Ayyubids at La Forbie near Gaza. Their alliance demonstrated both the military value and the political danger of such forces: after the victory they could not be reliably controlled, and within a few years they were destroyed or dispersed.

Alongside these unstable external alliances, al-Salih increasingly relied on mamluks whose careers were tied directly to his household. This was a broader feature of his reign rather than a simple reaction to La Forbie. The policy fit a long tradition, but at this moment it had unusual consequences, because the dynasty had few reliable alternatives and because the mamluk establishment he built was large and close to the capital. Meanwhile, the Frankish coastal states remained a factor in every calculation, and the new danger from the east was still distant. The point for students is that the Ayyubid system was fragile from the inside before any outside force tested it.

Section 36.2

What Was a Mamluk?

The Arabic word mamluk means "owned" and refers to a slave, but in the political vocabulary of the medieval Islamic world it denoted a specific kind of military slave: a boy or young man, usually of non-Muslim origin from the Eurasian steppe, the Caucasus, or Anatolia, purchased, brought into the Muslim world, converted, and trained as a cavalryman. Earlier states had used similar soldiers. The Abbasid caliphs of the ninth century recruited Turkic slave soldiers, a development that contributed to the decentralization traced in Chapter 30, and the Seljuks and Zengids relied on slave commanders. Ayyubid rulers continued the practice alongside armies of Kurdish and Turkmen free soldiers and Arab tribal levies.

The system had a distinctive logic. A mamluk was bought as a youth, received religious instruction and military training in barracks or a household, and was eventually freed, usually when his training was complete. Manumission did not end the relationship: the freed soldier remained bound by loyalty to the master who purchased him and to his comrades who had been trained and freed alongside him. Rulers valued this because the soldier had no local kin, no tribal base, and no alternative patron. The risk, apparent from the ninth century onward, was that the same dependence could reverse once the soldiers were numerous and the ruler weak.

Students should keep two meanings of the word apart. A mamluk is a status, applicable under many dynasties over several centuries. The Mamluk Sultanate is a particular state, which came into being in Egypt in the 1250s and 1260s, whose rulers and officers were mostly mamluks and which lasted until the Ottoman conquest of 1517. This chapter is concerned with the transition from the first to the second.

Historical Vocabulary
Mamluk, Ghulam, and Manumission

Mamluk (from the Arabic for "owned") designates a purchased soldier who was trained for military service. The related term ghulam (plural ghilman), meaning "boy" or "youth," was used for slave soldiers in earlier centuries and sometimes overlapped with mamluk in usage.

In the Mamluk context, manumission, being freed, was a normal stage in a military career rather than an exit from the system. The freed soldier belonged to a household of loyalty tied to his purchaser and to fellow trainees. This meant that military slavery in this setting was unlike the plantation or domestic slavery found elsewhere, though the mamluk was nonetheless acquired through the slave trade and began his life in the Muslim world without freedom or kin.

The term matters here because modern English "slave" suggests powerlessness, whereas mamluk officers could hold great wealth and command. Students should avoid both romanticizing the system and treating it as ordinary servitude.

Section 36.3

Al-Salih Ayyub and the Bahriyya

Al-Salih Ayyub came to power in Egypt in 1240 after a succession struggle and, after 1245, controlled Damascus as well. He purchased mamluks in large numbers, many apparently Kipchak Turks from the Black Sea steppe, a region then being devastated by Mongol campaigns, which increased the supply of captives for the slave markets. He housed them in a fortress he built on Rawda Island in the Nile at Cairo. Because the Nile in Arabic sources is the bahr, the regiment came to be called the Bahriyya, the "river" mamluks.

The Bahriyya became unusually powerful for three reasons. Not every early Mamluk ruler was Bahri: Baybars was, but Aybak headed the broader Salihiyya and Qutuz belonged to a different military network. They were concentrated in one place close to the capital, rather than dispersed across garrisons. They were a major elite corps within al-Salih's increasingly mamluk-dominated army, itself part of a larger Salihiyya establishment. And their lord ruled a dynasty whose other forces were unreliable. Al-Salih gave their officers rank and iqta, revenue assignments, and they formed bonds of solidarity that survived their master. Chronicles written later by Mamluk historians emphasize the corps' prestige, so some details of their organization may reflect later retrospective tradition, but the broad picture of a sizable, politically significant regiment under al-Salih is well supported.

Section 36.4

The Seventh Crusade, Mansura, and Fariskur

In June 1249 Louis IX of France landed near Damietta in the eastern Nile Delta, after wintering in Cyprus. The Ayyubid garrison abandoned the city, which fell almost without a fight. Al-Salih, already seriously ill, was at Mansura, and he died there in November 1249. His widow Shajar al-Durr and key officials concealed his death, continued to issue orders in his name, and sent for his son Turanshah, who was in the Jazira. This should be understood as an Egyptian political crisis as much as a Crusade: the army was under threat at its most delicate moment, and control of the succession mattered as much as battlefield success.

Louis advanced slowly and, in February 1250, his forces met the Egyptians at Mansura. The Frankish vanguard under the king's brother Robert of Artois entered the town and was destroyed in street fighting, an engagement in which the Bahri mamluks played a prominent role and in which the Ayyubid commander Fakhr al-Din ibn Shaykh al-Shuyukh was killed. Turanshah arrived in the camp shortly afterward. Disease and shortages then weakened the crusaders, who attempted to retreat to Damietta in April. At Fariskur the Egyptians caught and destroyed the retreating army, and Louis was captured. He was released after paying a large ransom and surrendering Damietta, then sailed to Acre.

For the Egyptian regime the victory brought a decisive political consequence. The mamluk commanders who had delivered it expected a reward, and Turanshah, a newcomer with his own entourage, began promoting men from outside the corps. The tension between the soldiers who had saved the dynasty and the sultan who now ruled it was explosive.

Section 36.5

Shajar al-Durr and the End of Ayyubid Rule in Egypt

Shajar al-Durr ("Tree of Pearls") was a former slave woman whose origins are variously described as Turkic or Armenian; she had been al-Salih's concubine and then his wife. The sources show her acting with authority during the concealment of his death, and her role is central to the survival of the army at Mansura. She is not a footnote but a principal political actor, a point sometimes obscured by later chroniclers uneasy about female rule.

Turanshah's alienation of the Bahriyya led to his murder in early May 1250, at Fariskur, by a group of mamluk officers. The army then recognized Shajar al-Durr as ruler, with a title that included reference to her late husband. She issued coins and was named in the Friday sermon. Her accession was unprecedented in Egypt and difficult to defend. The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad is reported in later tradition to have sent a letter objecting to the situation, though the details of that tradition should be treated cautiously. Syrian Ayyubid princes also refused to accept her. Within weeks, she stepped aside, apparently by agreement with the army leadership.

The solution was to marry her to the senior officer Aybak al-Turkmani, who took the post of atabak, commander of the army, and eventually the title of sultan. To satisfy those who insisted on Ayyubid legitimacy, a young Ayyubid prince was briefly named co-sultan. Thus the first mamluk regime emerged not through a clean break but through improvised arrangements designed to legitimate rule by soldiers.

Section 36.6

Aybak and the First Mamluk Regime

The year 1250 did not produce a stable Sultanate. Aybak had to contend with the Bahriyya, whose leaders resisted his authority, with Ayyubid rulers in Syria who threatened to invade Egypt, and with the question of what made his rule lawful. In 1254 he had the Bahri commander Aqtay killed, and many Bahri mamluks, including the officer Baybars, fled Egypt. Some took refuge with Syrian Ayyubids, whose rulers distrusted but used them. Aybak consolidated power around his own mamluks, the Muizziyya, who would later be rivals of the Bahriyya.

The relationship with Shajar al-Durr deteriorated, and in 1257 Aybak was killed, apparently at her instigation. Shajar al-Durr was herself killed days later by supporters of Aybak's other wife, and her death marked the end of her political career. Aybak's young son was made sultan with the senior officer Qutuz as regent. The pattern was already visible: every transfer of power was a contest among military households, and legitimacy was borrowed from dynastic forms that the soldiers did not fully believe in.

Section 36.7

The Mongol Expansion Before Baghdad

The Mongols did not suddenly appear in 1258. Chinggis Khan united the steppe peoples in 1206 and, after a diplomatic incident in which the Khwarazmian governor of Otrar killed Mongol merchants or envoys, invaded the Khwarazmian Empire around 1219. Within three years the great cities of Transoxiana and Khurasan, Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv, and Nishapur, had been captured, and several suffered heavy destruction and depopulation. The Khwarazmian ruler's son Jalal al-Din fled westward and campaigned in the Caucasus and northern Iraq before his death in 1231, and his followers became the displaced Khwarazmian military bands that appeared in Syria in the 1240s. The Mongol army at the same time penetrated the Caucasus and the Russian steppe.

Mongol expansion was not a single march but a series of campaigns under successive Great Khans. After Chinggis, his successor Ögedei continued the conquest of Iran and the Caucasus, and in 1243 the Mongols defeated the Seljuk sultan of Rum at Köse Dag, making Anatolia a tributary. This is background only for the Arab-world narrative, but it shows that by the 1250s the Mongols were already a neighboring power to Iraq, Syria, and Armenia. A cautionary point: contemporary Arabic and Persian writers, such as Ibn al-Athir, who wrote about the first Mongol invasions, described them in terms of cosmic catastrophe, and modern historians weigh their rhetoric against archaeological and demographic evidence of recovery in many regions.

Section 36.8

Hülegü and the Caliphate on the Eve of 1258

In 1251 Möngke became Great Khan. He commissioned his brother Hülegü to lead a major campaign to subdue the remaining powers of Iran and the Islamic West, starting in 1253 with the Nizari Ismaili strongholds and then the caliphate. Hülegü assembled a very large army, drawing on Mongol units and on Turkic, Georgian, Armenian, and Iranian contingents; sources give differing and unreliable figures. He moved methodically, with siege engineers and administrators. In 1256 the Nizari fortresses, including Alamut, surrendered or were reduced, and the Nizari imam Rukn al-Din Khurshah submitted, ending the Nizari state in Iran as an independent power. Sunni authors often welcomed this, which is a reminder that Muslim reactions to the Mongols were not uniform.

The Abbasid caliph al-Mustaʿsim ruled from Baghdad with a small military establishment and a court rent by factions, in which the vizier Ibn al-Alqami, a Shiʿi, and the Sunni military leaders were rivals. The caliph still carried major religious legitimacy across the Sunni world: sultans sought his investiture and his name was mentioned in the Friday sermon in much of it. But Baghdad was no longer master of an empire. We should avoid both extremes: it was neither the unchallenged center of the Muslim world nor a politically meaningless shell. Hülegü demanded submission, negotiations failed, and the campaign against Baghdad followed. Ilkhanid and later narrative sources often portray al-Mustaʿsim as fatally overconfident, but his precise calculations cannot be reconstructed securely.

Section 36.9

The Siege and Destruction of Baghdad, 1258

Mongol columns converged on Baghdad in January 1258, from several directions. The caliph's forces attempted to resist outside the city and were defeated, and the city was encircled and bombarded. After about two weeks, the caliph sent out the vizier and notables to negotiate, and on 10 February al-Mustaʿsim himself surrendered. Mongol troops entered, and for roughly a week the city was sacked. The caliphal palace, mosques, libraries, and markets suffered damage, and a large part of the population was killed or enslaved. Accounts say that Christians sheltering in certain houses and churches were spared at the intercession of Hülegü's Nestorian Christian wife, Doquz Khatun, an episode that shows how Mongol decisions could follow religious politics.

Al-Mustaʿsim was put to death, according to Persian and Arabic chroniclers, a few days after the surrender. Later tradition says he was rolled in a carpet and trampled, a story whose details may reflect a Mongol taboo against spilling royal blood but cannot be verified. Casualty totals in the sources range from tens of thousands to figures in the hundreds of thousands and beyond, but these are rhetorical or unverifiable estimates; the devastation was undeniably enormous, yet exact numbers cannot be recovered. Stories of the Tigris running black with ink from destroyed books are likewise literary images and should be treated as such rather than as documented fact.

After the sack, Hülegü ordered the city's partial restoration and placed an administration under Mongol authority with local Iraqi officials, and he established his base in Azerbaijan. The distinguished scholar Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, who had joined his service, supported projects of learning, including an observatory at Maragha, showing that the same conquest that destroyed one center of scholarship patronized another.

Primary Source Window

Al-Ḥawādith al-jāmiʿa, an Anonymous Baghdadi Chronicle of the Conquest

Creator: an anonymous Baghdad chronicler (the work was formerly attributed to Ibn al-Fuwati but is now generally treated as anonymous) · Date: composed in the decades after 1258 · Type: local narrative chronicle

Context: Al-Ḥawādith al-jāmiʿa ("The Comprehensive Events") records events in Baghdad year by year and is valued by modern scholars for its intimate knowledge of the city and for being an early local account of the conquest and its aftermath. It was written by someone who knew Baghdad, not by a distant observer, though not necessarily by an eyewitness to every event described.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation), based on the chronicle's account for 656 AH/1258 (Arabic edition: M. Jawād, ed., Baghdad, 1932–33, p. 331; English translation: Hend Gilli-Elewy, "Al-Ḥawādiṯ al-ǧāmiʿa: A Contemporary Account of the Mongol Conquest of Baghdad, 656/1258," Arabica 58.5 (2011), pp. 353–371, especially p. 368): After an amnesty was announced, survivors emerged from hiding to find bodies lying through Baghdad's streets and markets. The chronicler reports an enormous and unreliable death toll and describes survivors suffering amid hunger, contaminated water, decaying corpses, flies, and spoiled food. Supplies later arrived from nearby Iraqi towns, whose merchants exchanged food for books, metalwork, furnishings, and other valuables.

Analysis: The chronicle is important because it comes from the city itself and shows how Baghdadis understood the catastrophe. Its limitations are equally important. Its reported death toll cannot be used as a literal demographic count, and modern scholars have used this passage to show how such figures were repeated and enlarged in later chronicles. It was written after the event, and its author's own position and sympathies shape what is recorded. Historians compare it with Persian accounts produced at the Ilkhanid court, notably Rashid al-Din's Jāmiʿ al-Tawārīkh, which stress Hülegü's demands for submission and the caliph's failure to comply, and with Arabic chronicles written in Syria and Egypt under Mamluk rule. Together these traditions support the sequence and general severity of events, but not exact numbers, and they say little about ordinary residents' experience or the pace of recovery.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this chronicle say, in outline, about Baghdad after the conquest?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from a local account of this kind?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Section 36.10

What Did 1258 End?

The sack ended the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad, the line of rulers who had held the title since 750, and it ended Baghdad's role as the capital of Sunni caliphal authority. It did not end Islam, Arabic culture, urban life, or the history of Iraq. Muslim political and intellectual institutions continued elsewhere, from Cairo and Damascus to the Maghreb and Anatolia. Baghdad itself remained a city with a Muslim population, schools, and trade, though it was no longer the center it had been, and southern Iraq's irrigation economy suffered from disruption and neglect in the following decades, a question modern historians continue to debate.

Equally, 1258 was not "the end of the Islamic Golden Age" in a single day. Chapter 30 traced the long fragmentation of Abbasid power, and Chapters 31 through 35 followed new centers of learning and politics. The fall of the capital was a profound shock and a symbolic rupture, but it came after centuries of political decentralization.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of Baghdad, 1258

Long-term causes

Four decades of Mongol imperial expansion westward, and the long erosion of Abbasid military power that left the caliph dependent on a small army and on prestige.

Short-term causes

Möngke's decision to send Hülegü west, the elimination of the Nizari strongholds, failed diplomacy over the caliph's submission, and factional rivalry within Baghdad's leadership.

Event

The siege, surrender, and sack of Baghdad in January–February 1258 and the execution of al-Mustaʿsim.

Immediate effects

Collapse of Abbasid government in Baghdad, heavy loss of life and property, and installation of Mongol authority in Iraq.

Long-term consequences

Entrenchment of Mongol rule in Iraq and Iran (the Ilkhanate), a sharper threat to Syria, and a shift in the center of Sunni political power to Cairo.

Historical complexity

The fall of Baghdad cannot be attributed to one cause or to one ruler's decision. It was neither the sole cause of cultural decline nor an isolated blow; it was one major stage in a long reordering.
Historical Vocabulary
Ilkhan and Ilkhanate

Ilkhan is a Turkic-Mongol title usually translated "subordinate khan," used for Hülegü and his successors, who ruled Iran, Iraq, and neighboring regions under nominal recognition of the Great Khan. The state is called the Ilkhanate. It was a Mongol dynasty ruling a largely Muslim, Persian-speaking and Arabic-speaking population, and it later converted to Islam.

The term matters because it helps students see Hülegü not as an invader who simply left, but as the founder of a durable neighboring state that became the main enemy of Mamluk Egypt and Syria. It also signals a political reality of 1260: the Mongol Empire was no longer one empire, and the western khans had their own interests.

Section 36.11

From Iraq into Syria, and Qutuz's Seizure of Power

In late 1259 Hülegü advanced from Azerbaijan into the Jazira and northern Syria. The Ayyubid ruler of Aleppo and Damascus, al-Nasir Yusuf, failed to organize a defense, and Aleppo fell in January 1260 after a siege and sack. His allies included Hethum I of Armenian Cilicia and Bohemond VI of Antioch and Tripoli, who submitted to Mongol overlordship, while the Frankish barons of Acre took a more cautious path. Damascus submitted in March 1260, and the Mongol general Kitbuqa entered it. Al-Nasir Yusuf fled, was captured, and later killed. Cooperation between Christian rulers and Mongols was real but not universal, and should remind students that alliances in this period did not follow a clean line of religion.

In Egypt, the crisis had a clear logic. Aybak's death left a boy sultan, al-Mansur Ali, under Qutuz, and as the Mongol threat grew the regency became untenable. In late 1259 Qutuz deposed the boy and took the throne himself. This should be explained as a response to necessity rather than palace trivia: Egypt needed unified command and an experienced leader. Qutuz also recalled Bahri exiles, including Baybars, bringing the disunited factions together for the coming confrontation.

Section 36.12

Why Hülegü Withdrew, and the Diplomacy of 1260

In August 1259 the Great Khan Möngke died during a campaign in China. The death opened a succession struggle among Mongol princes, with Hülegü's brothers Khubilai and Ariq Böke contending for the title. Hülegü withdrew toward Azerbaijan with a large part of his force, leaving Kitbuqa with a smaller army in Syria. Möngke's death and the resulting succession struggle are a major explanation, although some historians also emphasize Syria's limited summer pasture and logistical constraints. Tensions with Berke of the Golden Horde were already developing and soon became another major constraint on Ilkhanid strategy. This is essential for avoiding a mythic account of Ayn Jalut in which the Mamluks defeated the entire Mongol imperial war machine at full strength.

Mongol envoys went to Cairo demanding submission. According to the Arabic sources, Qutuz had them killed, a stark signal of refusal, after consulting his commanders. The Franks of Acre then faced a choice. Some may have preferred neither side to win, and the Mamluk army was allowed to pass through or near Frankish territory and to obtain supplies around Acre. Again, the story resists a binary of Christendom against Islam: Armenians and Antiochenes sided with the Mongols, while the Franks of Acre practiced a pragmatic neutrality.

Section 36.13

The Battle of Ayn Jalut, 3 September 1260

The Mamluk army moved up the coast and then inland into Galilee, with Baybars leading the vanguard. The two forces met on 3 September 1260, in the Ayn Jalut, "Spring of Goliath," area in the Jezreel valley near Beisan. Medieval sources give no reliable totals for either army. Kitbuqa commanded only a portion of the Mongol forces that had invaded Syria, but the relative numerical strength of the armies at Ayn Jalut cannot be established securely. The Mamluks reportedly feigned retreat and ambushed the Mongols as they pursued, then rallied under Qutuz in the center of the line. Kitbuqa was killed or captured and executed, and his army was routed. Details of tactics rest on later chronicles and should be taken with caution.

The battle's geography mattered. The Mongol army depended on pasture for its horses, and the Galilee and Jezreel offered pasture, but only for a limited force. Logistics, terrain, and reduced numbers all favored the defenders. The details of the sequence, however, remain debated, as the Historical Debate below explains.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Mamluks Win at Ayn Jalut?

The question: What best explains the Mamluk victory over a Mongol army in 1260? Older accounts, and many popular ones, treat the battle as the moment the Mamluks "stopped the Mongols." More recent scholarship stresses that the Mongol force was reduced and that the outcome depended on several factors working together.

Interpretation A — Mamluk strength: The victory reflected Mamluk military training, discipline, and command, along with the unification of Egypt under Qutuz and the fighting experience gained at Mansura and in factional struggles.
Interpretation B — Mongol limitations: The victory was made possible by Hülegü's withdrawal after Möngke's death, the reduced size of Kitbuqa's army, and the logistical limits of operating in Syria's pasture and terrain, so the Mamluks faced only part of the Mongol force.

Evidence: The debate rests on Arabic chronicles, Persian accounts, and Armenian and Latin sources, which differ in their numbers and emphases, and on inferences from Mongol logistics. None can settle the question precisely.

Why it matters: If the victory is attributed purely to Mamluk prowess, it supports a legend of unique Muslim resistance; if attributed purely to Mongol weakness, it understates the Mamluk achievement. Most historians now emphasize interaction: Mamluk strength mattered because the Mongol army was weakened, and Mongol weakness mattered because the Mamluks were ready to exploit it.

Student task: Identify what further evidence, such as reliable troop numbers or Mongol administrative records, would help decide how much weight each factor deserves.

Section 36.14

What Ayn Jalut Changed and What It Did Not

The battle ended the Mongol occupation of Syria and protected Egypt. Damascus and Aleppo soon passed into Mamluk hands, though Aleppo changed hands again later. Mongol garrisons withdrew and the Ayyubid princes of Syria were removed or reduced. In this sense the Mamluks acquired Syria as a result of Ayn Jalut.

But the battle did not destroy Mongol power in Iran and Iraq, nor end Mongol ambitions toward Syria. The Ilkhans launched further campaigns, and the frontier along the Euphrates would remain contested for decades, the subject of Chapter 37. Students should therefore resist the idea that a single day permanently saved the Islamic world.

Section 36.15

Qutuz, Baybars, and the Problem of Military Rule

On the return from Syria, in October 1260, Qutuz was assassinated by a group of officers including Baybars, reportedly at a halt on the road to Egypt. Baybars then entered Cairo as sultan. Medieval sources attribute the killing to Baybars's personal ambition and to resentment over Qutuz's refusal to give him the governorship of Aleppo, though motives are uncertain and the sources are partly hostile to Baybars.

The episode made the political problem visible: the same military networks capable of defending the state could also determine succession violently. This pattern recurs throughout the Mamluk period, and it shapes the institutions studied in Chapter 37.

Section 36.16

The Cairo Caliphate and a New Regional Order

Baybars began consolidating his rule in Egypt and Syria, restoring administrative order, organizing defense, and seeking legitimacy. He had come to power by killing a sultan and needed religious and legal justification. In 1261 he installed an Abbasid claimant, a man recognized as an uncle or relative of the last caliph, as caliph in Cairo, who invested Baybars as sultan. When that claimant was killed in an attempt to reach Baghdad, another Abbasid was installed in 1262. The caliphs in Cairo retained symbolic and religious value, while the sultan held effective military and governmental power. This tied back directly to the long history of caliphal authority from Chapters 25, 30, and 33.

By 1261 the regional order was clear. Egypt and Syria were joined under a military regime based in Cairo; the Ilkhanate dominated Iran and Iraq; Frankish states remained on parts of the coast; and Mediterranean and Indian Ocean commerce continued. This is the world Chapter 37 inherits.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through 1258–1260

An inhabitant of Baghdad, 1258: For residents the siege meant fear, hunger, and then killing or enslavement; survival depended on chance and on whether one was sheltered by a protected community.

A Syrian Christian: Some communities saw Mongol rule as protection, particularly where Christian figures held influence, but later Mamluk rulers could view such hopes with suspicion.

These groups did not have equal power or equal choices. The purpose is to show that one set of events carried different meanings.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Baghdad and Cairo, Before and After 1258–1260

What changed? Baghdad lost its position as seat of the caliph and a major political center. Cairo became the capital of a powerful military state and later hosted a symbolic Abbasid caliph.

What remained continuous? Arabic scholarship, Islamic law, trade, religious institutions, and urban life continued in both regions, and Iraq remained populated and productive.

Who experienced change most directly? The ruling households of Baghdad and Ayyubid Egypt, and the urban populations who suffered the sack. Who experienced more continuity? Village cultivators, scholars who relocated, and merchants whose networks adapted.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that political collapse and cultural continuity can coexist, and that the shift of power from one center to another is different from the end of a civilization.

Everyday Life and Social History

Displacement, Requisition, and Changing Rulers

For ordinary people, the years 1249–1260 meant disruption. Refugees left Iraq and northern Syria for Damascus, Egypt, and elsewhere, among them scholars and artisans whose movement carried skills and learning westward. Armies on both sides requisitioned grain, animals, and fodder, and towns faced changing demands for taxes and supplies. Urban destruction at Baghdad and Aleppo disrupted markets, and rebuilding was gradual. Religious communities, Muslim, Christian, and Jewish, had to adjust to new rulers, some of whom favored one group or another.

Populations in Syria experienced several changes of rule in a few years, from Ayyubid to Mongol to Mamluk. The surviving sources, mostly elite, say less about women, peasants, and the poor, so historians must infer their experience from patterns of flight, charitable endowments, and later records.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced a thirteen-year transformation of the eastern Arab lands, from the fragile Ayyubid order of the 1240s to the establishment of the Mamluk Sultanate and the Abbasid caliphate in Cairo.

It began by showing that Ayyubid Egypt and Syria were politically unstable before the Mongols arrived. Al-Salih Ayyub's reliance on purchased soldiers, especially the Bahriyya, fit a long tradition of military slavery but concentrated power in a corps that could outlast its master. The distinction between mamluk as status and Mamluk Sultanate as state was central.

The Seventh Crusade tested the regime. The concealment of al-Salih's death, the defeat of the Franks at Mansura and Fariskur, and the capture of Louis IX demonstrated Egyptian military strength, but also turned the soldiers into political arbiters. Shajar al-Durr, the central figure in the crisis, ruled briefly and arranged the transfer to Aybak, whose regime remained unstable.

The Mongol expansion was a decades-long process. Chinggis Khan's conquest of the Khwarazmian lands began it, and Möngke's decision to send Hülegü west led to the destruction of the Nizari strongholds and then to Baghdad. The fall of the city in 1258 ended the Abbasid caliphate there, but not Islam or Arabic culture. Casualty figures are unrecoverable and should be treated with caution.

The Mongol advance into Syria in 1260 produced a complex pattern of alliances. Armenian Cilicia and Antioch cooperated with the Mongols, while the Franks of Acre allowed the Mamluk army to pass. Qutuz's seizure of power unified Egypt, and Hülegü withdrew much of his army, for reasons that include Möngke's death and logistical limits, leaving a reduced force under Kitbuqa.

At Ayn Jalut the Mamluks defeated that force. The battle protected Egypt and gave the Mamluks control of Syria, but did not destroy Mongol power or end the conflict. The explanation of the victory involves Mamluk strength, Mongol limits, terrain, and political timing together.

Qutuz's assassination and Baybars's accession exposed the problem of military rule: armies that defended the state could also decide succession by force. Baybars sought legitimacy by installing an Abbasid caliph in Cairo, whose symbolic authority complemented the sultan's real power.

The result was a new regional order: Egypt and Syria under a Cairo-based military regime, Iraq and Iran under the Ilkhans, and Frankish states on part of the coast. Chapter 37 examines how the Mamluk state worked as a durable institution; Chapter 38 examines its society and economy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regional order was rebuilt in little more than a decade. The Baghdad-centered caliphal order ended, Mongol rule took hold in Iran and Iraq, and a military regime emerged in Cairo. Understanding the sequence prevents both the myth of sudden catastrophe and the myth of miraculous rescue.

It also reveals larger processes: the long reach of steppe politics into the Arab lands, the dependence of rulers on military slavery, and the persistent tension between military power and legitimate authority. These themes continue into Chapter 37 and shape the later history of Egypt and Syria.

The chapter links regions that older histories often separate. Iraq, Iran, Syria, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Frankish coast were parts of a single field of action, and Muslims, Christians, Mongols, and Turks all played roles. The Arab world's late medieval history cannot be understood without that wider setting.
