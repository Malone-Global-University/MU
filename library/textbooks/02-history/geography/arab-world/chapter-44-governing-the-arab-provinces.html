Chapter Opening

Between the conquests narrated in Chapter 43 and the commercial, religious, and political histories of Chapters 45 through 47 lies the question of how an empire actually ruled. A conquest creates a claim; government turns that claim into a daily reality of surveys, taxes, courts, garrisons, and negotiations. This chapter examines that process in the Arab provinces during the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

The central question is how the Ottoman Empire governed territories as different as the Nile Valley, the Syrian steppe, the Iraqi marshes, the holy cities of the Hijaz, the highlands of Yemen, and the coast of North Africa, and how much of its authority depended on cooperation from the societies it ruled. The answer, developed section by section, is that Ottoman government was neither uniformly centralized nor merely symbolic. Istanbul had real power: it appointed governors and judges, commissioned surveys, collected substantial revenue, and maintained standing armies. But the reach of that power depended on provincial resources, geography, military establishments, local elites, and negotiated arrangements.

Consider how an imperial decision traveled. A petition from a village or a complaint about a governor reached the Imperial Council. A decree was drafted, copied into a register, and sent to a governor or judge. There it met a local reality of competing officials, soldiers wanting pay, notables with their own interests, and villagers who understood how to bargain. Whether the order was carried out, delayed, modified, or ignored cannot be known from the decree alone.

The chapter therefore proceeds from general institutions to regional cases. It first explains the Imperial Council, the territorial hierarchy, governors, land registration, taxation, military forces, courts, notables, villages, and tribes. It then applies these concepts to Egypt, Syria and Palestine, Iraq, the Hijaz, Yemen, and the North African regencies, and closes by comparing them.

Historians rely on varied evidence: imperial decrees and registers from Istanbul; survey registers; provincial court records; local chronicles; endowment deeds; travelers' accounts; and archaeological and architectural remains. Each reflects a specific perspective. Imperial sources show what the state wanted; local sources show what happened in particular places; and very few sources record the voices of villagers, women, and pastoralists directly.

This chapter does not narrate later provincial politics, which belongs to Chapter 47, nor the commerce and pilgrimage of Chapter 45, nor the religious life of Chapter 46, nor the nineteenth-century reforms of Chapter 50. It aims to give students the vocabulary and analytical framework that make those later chapters intelligible.

Historical Context

Chapter 43 described the Ottoman conquest of the Arab lands and the circumstances, discussed in Chapter 42, of the late Mamluk and Safavid worlds that preceded it. This chapter assumes that narrative and asks what followed. By the 1570s the empire held a belt of provinces from Algiers to Basra and from Aleppo to Yemen, taken at different times and by different means.

Ottoman rulers inherited institutions that had long histories. The Abbasid and Mamluk states had developed systems of taxation, military service, endowments, and courts (Chapters 26 and 37), and local societies had their own customs. The Ottomans were also heirs to Anatolian and Balkan administrative traditions, including surveys and revenue assignments, and they brought these to the Arab world while adapting to local conditions.

Two mistakes should be avoided. One is to imagine a single uniform Ottoman system; provinces differed profoundly. The other is to project nineteenth-century arrangements back onto the sixteenth century, including a standardized communal structure of non-Muslim subjects. In the period studied here, religious communities had leaders and institutions, but their arrangements were varied and negotiated.

Section 44.1

Governing an Empire of Different Provinces

Chapter 43 narrated how Ottoman armies and fleets acquired the Arab lands: Syria and Egypt in 1516–1517, Iraq from 1534, the Red Sea and Yemeni coasts from the late 1530s, and Tripoli and Tunis in 1551 and 1574, with Algiers entering the empire's orbit around 1519. This chapter asks a different question. Once a territory had been taken, how was it governed? A conquest is an event; government is a set of repeated practices through which orders are issued, revenue is collected, disputes are settled, soldiers are paid and fed, and a population comes to expect (or resist) the presence of a state.

The Ottomans inherited profoundly varied societies. Egypt was a densely settled river valley with a long tradition of centralized taxation and a military elite, the Mamluks, whose political culture the conquerors only partly dismantled (Chapters 9 and 37). Syria combined great trading cities such as Damascus and Aleppo with mountain communities, steppe pastoralists, and a countryside of villages. Iraq was a riverine plain divided between settled cultivators, marsh dwellers, and powerful tribal confederations, and it sat on the frontier with Safavid Iran. The Hijaz held the holy cities of Mecca and Medina and depended on imported grain and the pilgrimage. Yemen offered fertile highlands but a hostile political landscape, with a Zaydi imamate whose authority rested on tribal support. In North Africa, the coastal cities of Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli faced the Mediterranean and a hinterland of Arab and Amazigh tribal societies.

Ottoman administrators did not respond with a single blueprint. A guiding habit of imperial practice was to survey a conquered place, record its existing taxes and customs, and issue a regulation (kanunname) adapting the sultan's law to local conditions. The result was an empire of differently governed provinces, held together by a shared dynasty, a common vocabulary of offices, a body of imperial law, and the circulation of officials, soldiers, scholars, and petitions. Surveys and regulations therefore matter to this chapter not only as administrative tools but as evidence of how the state understood each province.

The chapter separates two things that are easily confused: authority, the recognized right to command, and control, the practical ability to make commands effective. Istanbul's authority in the Arab provinces was real and widely acknowledged. Its control varied with distance, terrain, the loyalty of local elites, the reliability of soldiers, and the state's own finances. Understanding how authority was translated into control, and how much of that translation depended on cooperation from those being governed, is the central problem of the chapter.

Historians approach this problem through several kinds of evidence, each with its own limits. Imperial decrees and registers in the Istanbul archives show what the center wished and recorded. Survey registers and kanunnames show how the state assessed and described local conditions. Court registers show how individuals and communities actually used institutions. Chronicles written by local scholars offer an urban literate perspective, and travelers' accounts add outside observations. Very little survives in the voices of villagers, women, or pastoralists themselves, so their experience must often be inferred from records produced by others. The chapter's main focus is the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, when the arrangements were established; the later rise of powerful provincial households belongs to Chapter 47.

Section 44.2

The Sultan, Imperial Council, and Provincial Authority

Decisions about the Arab provinces were made in Istanbul, above all in the Imperial Council (Divan-ı Hümayun), which met in the Topkapı Palace under the grand vizier. Its members included other viziers, the two chief military judges (kadiaskers), the chief treasurers (defterdars), and the chancellor (nişancı) who authenticated documents with the sultan's monogram. The council heard petitions, appointed and dismissed officials, debated finance and military matters, and issued orders. The sultan was the ultimate source of law and appointment, but daily work was done by a literate bureaucracy that produced and stored an enormous volume of paper.

Imperial instructions took several forms. A ferman or hüküm was a sultanic order addressed to a governor, judge, or other official; a berat was a patent granting an office, revenue assignment, or privilege. Copies of important outgoing orders were entered in registers known as mühimme defterleri ("registers of important affairs"), which survive in large numbers from the second half of the sixteenth century. Many orders responded to petitions from provincial subjects, so the registers record complaints and requests as well as commands. This reflects a political ideal sometimes called the circle of justice: the ruler secures the realm by protecting his subjects from injustice, which keeps them productive, which in turn finances the army and the state.

Communication was slow by modern standards. Orders traveled by courier across Anatolia and Syria and by sea to Alexandria, and a reply could take weeks or longer depending on season, war, and plague. Governors therefore often had to act before instructions arrived, and Istanbul had to verify their reports through other channels: the provincial treasurer, the judge, and complaints from subjects, all of whom could write directly to the capital. This overlapping system of reporting was a deliberate check on any single official, and it also created many opportunities for delay and local bargaining.

Students should read imperial orders with care. A decree shows what the center wished, believed, or had been told. It does not show that the order was carried out. When the registers contain repeated orders on the same subject, such as repairing a bridge, ending an illegal levy, or restraining soldiers, historians may reasonably infer that the problem persisted, or that the center was responding to petitions. Compliance must be checked against local sources: court records, chronicles, endowment deeds, and tax documents. Section 44.3

Eyalets, Sanjaks, and Administrative Geography

The basic territorial unit was the large province governed by a beylerbeyi ("bey of beys"), usually called a province or eyalet in later sources (sixteenth-century documents often say beylerbeylik or vilayet). Each province was subdivided into districts called sanjaks (liva), headed by a sanjak bey. The sanjak was primarily a military and fiscal unit. It was distinct from the judicial district, the kaza, in which a kadi held court. A town might belong to one sanjak, one kaza, and one province, and the boundaries did not always coincide. Students should keep four things separate: the governorship, the territorial district, the judicial jurisdiction, and the military command, since the officials responsible for each were different and did not always cooperate.

Provincial geography changed. Damascus began as the seat of a very large province covering much of Syria and Palestine, including the sanjaks of Jerusalem, Gaza, Safed, and Nablus. Aleppo was a full provincial capital by 1534, and Tripoli on the Syrian coast became a province in 1579. A province centered on Sidon appeared later in the seventeenth century. In Iraq, Baghdad was joined by Mosul, conventionally a provincial capital from 1535, and by Basra, which acknowledged Ottoman suzerainty in 1536 and was directly intervened in during 1546. The Red Sea produced the provinces of Yemen and Habesh, and North Africa the regencies of Algiers, Tripoli, and Tunis. Egypt, by contrast, was a single province with its own distinctive internal organization into districts supervised by kashifs.

Some districts had special status. In the Kurdish borderlands, certain hereditary chiefs were recognized as holding their districts as nearly self-governing sanjaks. In Mount Lebanon, local emirs collected revenue under Ottoman provincial supervision. In the Hijaz, the sharifs of Mecca held an exceptional position, discussed in §44.15. Such arrangements are reminders that administrative maps show claims and categories, not uniform practice. The territorial hierarchy was flexible enough to be redrawn when revenue, security, or political convenience required it, as the creation of Aleppo and Tripoli shows. Chapter 45 turns to the cities as commercial centers; here the point is that the geography of government followed the empire's priorities, not a modern map.

Section 44.4

Governors, Officials, and Provincial Administration

The governor, commonly styled pasha, was appointed by the sultan through a patent and held broad responsibility for security, supervision of the sanjak beys, leadership of campaigns, and in some provinces the safe passage of the pilgrimage caravan. He brought with him a household of retainers, clerks, and soldiers, and the success of a governorship often depended on how well this household was integrated with the existing local order. Governors in the sixteenth century were usually drawn from the sultan's servant elite and reassigned regularly. Short tenures helped prevent a governor from building an independent base, but also meant that many knew little about the province they governed. The Egyptian case shows both approaches: the first governor, Khayr Bey, was a former Mamluk who knew the country, while later governors were sent from Istanbul, and the revolt of Ahmed Pasha in 1523–1524 showed the risk of a powerful governor far from the capital.

Governors did not rule alone. A provincial treasurer (defterdar) supervised revenue and corresponded with the imperial finance department. The judge (kadi), appointed from Istanbul, administered the court and could record complaints and report abuses. Garrison commanders, particularly of the Janissaries, answered to their own chain of command and often to the capital. In Egypt, the governor presided over a Divan that included senior officers of the different regiments, who could limit him. Provincial scribes, many locally trained, kept the accounts. Istanbul encouraged these officials to report on one another, so that overlapping jurisdictions served as a check.

Accountability had limits. The center received petitions, dispatched investigators, replaced governors, and on occasion executed those found to have abused their posts. Yet a governor's ability to collect revenue and keep the peace was the ultimate test of his usefulness, and Istanbul sometimes tolerated misconduct in a successful official. Meanwhile officials had to cultivate local elites: a governor who alienated the notables of a city could find that taxes arrived slowly, that petitions piled up, or that soldiers refused to obey. In practice, provincial administration consisted of continuous negotiation among officials with partly overlapping powers.

Section 44.5

Land, Revenue, and Fiscal Registration

The basis of Ottoman fiscal government was the survey. Imperial commissioners, assisted by scribes and local officials, visited districts and recorded villages, households, cultivable land, crops, mills, markets, and taxes. In much of the empire the results were written in registers called tahrir defterleri. A detailed register (mufassal) listed village by village and often name by name; a summary register (icmal) gave totals. A register typically opened with a kanunname recording the tax customs of the district, so that Ottoman rates were adapted to existing local practice rather than imposed from scratch. Surveys were carried out in Syria and Palestine in the decades after conquest, and historians have used them extensively to reconstruct village economies.

Egypt shows why the same word, survey, should not be applied uniformly. Egypt inherited a distinctive Mamluk fiscal tradition based on village land assessments, and the Ottomans addressed it through the kanunname of 1525 and a general cadastre in the fiscal year 1527–1528. Nicolas Michel's studies indicate that this was the only general cadastre the Ottomans carried out in Egypt. The original registers appear to have been lost, and only copies survive. Michel also emphasizes that land tax was assessed at village level, that cultivators (fallahin) held rights of use while eminent ownership rested with the treasury, and that landownership in the modern sense scarcely existed in sixteenth-century Egypt: what mattered was how the fiscal burden was distributed. Irrigation was documented separately, in registers of dikes (dafatir al-jusur).

Elsewhere in the empire, a general distinction between kinds of land applied, but it should be qualified for each region. Most agricultural land was miri, state land whose cultivators held hereditary rights of use so long as they cultivated it and paid dues. Some land was private freehold (mülk), and a great deal was endowed as waqf, dedicating its income to a religious or charitable purpose. A person who received a revenue assignment from the state did not thereby own the land. In Egypt, the holders of tax-farming rights and the endowments of the older elite complicated the picture further, and the language of ownership must be used cautiously.

Registers are indispensable but must be read critically. They were created for taxation, not demography: they list taxpayers, not the whole population. Elites, religious dignitaries, and certain service groups were often exempt and so under-recorded. Mobile pastoralists were hard to count. Clerks could be misled or bribed, local headmen had incentives to understate, and a survey describes a moment while fiscal reality moved on. Even so, registers preserve a uniquely detailed view of village society, and comparison of successive surveys lets historians trace the expansion or contraction of cultivation, a subject connected to the demographic questions of Chapter 41.

Section 44.6

Taxation, Tax Farming, and Provincial Finance

Ottoman taxes took many forms. Cultivators typically paid a share of the harvest (öşr, a tithe-like levy, though rates varied by place) plus fees on land and households, on mills and presses, on trees and beehives, and on livestock. Non-Muslim adult men paid the cizye, a poll tax that formed part of the legal and fiscal status of non-Muslim subjects under Islamic law; it was not a bargain individually negotiated, though in practice communities often organized its collection. Ports and markets generated customs and market dues. Extraordinary levies (avariz) originally raised in wartime became regular. Taxes could be paid in kind, in cash, or both, varying between provinces and over time.

To assign revenue to those who served the state, the Ottomans used several tools. In the timar system, the revenue of a village or group of villages was assigned to a cavalryman (sipahi), who maintained himself and armed retainers for campaign. Larger assignments (ziamet and has) went to higher officers and governors. Timar holders were supervised by the state, holdings were not normally inherited, and holders did not exercise judicial lordship over peasants. Comparisons with European feudalism can be useful for asking questions about service and land, but the differences, especially the state's control over assignment and the absence of a hereditary lordly jurisdiction, are essential. In the Arab provinces the system was strongest in Syria and Palestine and in parts of Iraq, and weaker or differently structured in Egypt, Yemen, and the North African regencies.

Many provinces, Egypt above all, were administered as salyane provinces, in which revenue was collected by officials and contractors, a portion was spent locally on salaries and garrisons, and the surplus was remitted to Istanbul. The annual Egyptian remittance and the shipments of grain and funds for the holy cities were among the empire's most important transfers. Alongside this, iltizam (tax farming) expanded: a contractor (multezim) bid for the right to collect specified revenues, advanced money to the treasury, and kept what he could collect beyond the agreed sum. In Egypt, Michel traces a passage from the older Mamluk assignment of tax revenues (iqta') toward arrangements in which tax-farmers collected revenue from villages.

Tax farming became more attractive to the state in the late sixteenth century. Firearms and standing infantry raised the cost of war, inflation and the influx of silver eroded the value of fixed cash payments, and the timar cavalry became less militarily useful. Tax farming brought in cash in advance and reduced administrative burdens, but it also created a class of local financiers with an interest in provincial revenues. This is one of the clearest ways in which fiscal and military demands reshaped provincial government, as the Cause and Effect feature shows. The later development of life-term tax farms belongs to Chapter 47.

Historical Vocabulary

Timar and Iltizam

A timar was an assignment of the revenue of a village or district to a cavalryman in return for military service; it was not ownership and was normally not inherited. Iltizam was the contracting of the right to collect specific revenues, often at auction, to a multezim who paid in advance and kept the surplus. Neither gave ownership of the land.

The two systems were not rigid opposites; both could coexist in the same province, and their balance shifted as the state's needs changed. Calling timar "feudalism" misleads because the holder was supervised by the state and did not hold land as a private estate with legal jurisdiction over peasants.

Section 44.7

Garrisons, Janissaries, Cavalry, and Military Authority

Ottoman military power in the provinces rested on three kinds of force. The first was the sultan's own salaried infantry and cavalry, the kapıkulu troops, of whom the Janissaries were the best known. These were stationed in citadels and key towns and paid regular stipends (ulufe). The second was the provincial cavalry of timar holders, who gathered for campaign under their sanjak beys. The third was a changing array of auxiliary and locally recruited troops, including irregular infantry, gunners, and naval crews, whose loyalty was often to a commander or household rather than the sultan.

Garrisons guarded the citadels of Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, and Baghdad; protected roads and fortified stations along the pilgrimage and trade routes; policed markets; supported tax collection; and defended frontiers against the Safavids, the Portuguese in the Red Sea and Indian Ocean, and Christian navies in the Mediterranean. The defense of Jeddah in 1541, in which an Ottoman garrison and Meccan forces under the sharif together repelled a Portuguese attack, shows the military cooperation the system could produce. Egypt maintained several regiments, each with its own officers and finances. Historians distinguish between troops sent from Istanbul and those recruited in the province, especially later, when many soldiers married locally, owned property, and practiced trades.

The relationship between soldiers and civilians was complex. Soldiers needed supplies, lodging, and credit, and the civilian economy provided them. Late payment of stipends could cause unrest, and soldiers' demands sometimes weighed heavily on subjects. At the same time soldiers entered guilds, bought shops, and appeared as litigants in courts. In the North African regencies, Janissary corps became the center of political life (§44.17). The long-term political consequences of this entanglement belong to Chapter 47; what matters here is that the military was an integral part of provincial society rather than a force sitting above it.

Section 44.8

Courts, Judges, Imperial Law, and Petitions

Justice was administered primarily by the kadi, an official judge trained in Islamic law and appointed through the imperial scholarly hierarchy. The Ottoman state favored the Hanafi school and sent Hanafi judges to the major provincial centers, but local populations, many of whom followed the Shafi'i, Maliki, or Hanbali schools, continued to use their own legal traditions. Egypt is a documented example: as Abdurrahman Atçıl's research shows, Ottoman Egypt retained substantial features of the Mamluk judicial arrangements, including practices associated with all four Sunni schools, alongside the chief judge sent from Istanbul. Most judges also had responsibility beyond adjudication: they authenticated documents, registered property transactions, supervised endowments, and reported on conditions.

Three bodies of norms interacted. Islamic law (sharia) governed personal status, contracts, property, and inheritance. Imperial regulations (kanun) were issued by the sultan on administrative, fiscal, and penal matters. Local custom ('urf) governed numerous tax rates, agricultural arrangements, and community practices, and was often recognized in a district's kanunname. In theory these were reconciled: jurists such as Ebussuud Efendi, the chief mufti in the mid-sixteenth century, argued that sultanic law operated within the framework of the sharia. In practice jurisdictions overlapped, litigants chose among courts and schools, and officials and judges sometimes disagreed about which norm applied.

Court registers (sijills) survive for Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Jerusalem, and many other places, and are among the most valuable sources for social history. They record sales, loans, inheritances, marriage contracts, endowments, and lawsuits. Women appear as plaintiffs, defendants, and property owners; non-Muslims frequently used the Islamic court for transactions and disputes. Subjects could also petition the Imperial Council directly, and complaints about officials or tax collectors often produced orders for investigation. These petitions show that ordinary subjects knew how to use the system. The registers must still be read critically, because they record those who came to court, not those too poor, too distant, or too intimidated to do so.

Historical Vocabulary

Kanun, Sharia, and Local Custom

Kanun means imperial regulation issued by the sultan; sharia means Islamic law as interpreted by jurists; 'urf means local custom. Ottoman officials treated these as complementary. Ebussuud Efendi and others argued that kanun operated within the broader framework of sharia.

The distinction matters because students sometimes assume that "Islamic law" was the only law operating in the empire. In practice, tax rates, penalties, and administrative procedures often came from kanun or custom, while the kadi's court applied sharia to personal and commercial matters.

Section 44.9

Scholars, Notables, and Local Intermediaries

No Ottoman governor could have collected a tax, kept a market orderly, or settled a dispute in a major Arab city without the help of local people who understood it. These intermediaries included religious scholars (ulama), descendants of the Prophet (ashraf), leaders of Sufi orders, merchants, guild heads, endowment administrators, village headmen, and local scribes and accountants. In Egypt, Coptic scribes had long been central to tax administration, and their expertise remained valuable. In Syria and Iraq, Christian and Jewish clerks and financiers also took part in fiscal work. These roles were not uniform across the empire and should not be read as a single arrangement for all non-Muslims.

Modern historians often use the term a'yan (singular 'ayn) for the local notables who mediated between rulers and populations, though its use and meaning developed over time. Their power came from religious learning, genealogy, wealth, control of endowments, patronage of craftsmen and markets, and kinship networks. They could represent local interests to the governor, transmit petitions to Istanbul, advise judges, and help collect taxes, and they could be co-opted into the state's service. Notables who cooperated gained status and protection; those who resisted risked dismissal or confiscation.

This cooperation shaped Ottoman government. Local elites did not disappear after the conquest, and families with deep roots in a city could survive political change. Local chroniclers such as Ibn Iyas in Cairo, who witnessed the Mamluk defeat, and Ibn Tulun in Damascus, who recorded daily life after 1516, offer the viewpoint of the urban literate classes. Their perspective must be balanced against those of villagers, soldiers, and pastoralists, whose voices are far less audible.

Section 44.10

Villages, Peasants, Irrigation, and Rural Government

Most subjects of the empire lived in villages, and for them the state appeared mainly as a tax assessment, a surveyor, a judge, or a soldier requisitioning supplies. In many regions the village was the basic tax unit, with the community collectively responsible for payment. This gave village headmen and elders an important role as intermediaries, since they allocated the burden among households, negotiated with tax collectors, and represented the community in court. Michel's work on Egyptian villages stresses that taxation was at the center of relations between public authorities and villagers, so that the village's dealings with the state were above all fiscal.

Peasant strategies took many forms. Villagers petitioned the court or the Divan, bargained over rates, delayed payment, or moved. Egyptian evidence is unusually detailed. Because village land tax depended on resident cultivators, the kanunname of 1525 sought to keep cultivators in their villages and provided means to recover those who had left. Yet migration was extensive. Nicolas Michel's study of the Delta, based on the cadastre of 1527–1528, documents peasants moving in large numbers from the early Ottoman period, and he cautions against reading every recorded departure as flight from taxation or as resistance. Moving was a real option for villagers, and a fiscal problem for a state and tax collectors who needed cultivators on the land. Famine, epidemic disease, and insecurity also affected rural populations, as Chapter 41 discusses.

Water management shaped rural government, as Chapter 3 anticipated. In Egypt, maintaining dikes and canals before the Nile flood was a collective duty regulated by provincial rules and recorded in the registers of dikes, requiring labor from villages under the supervision of kashifs. In Syria and Palestine, spring-fed channels, wells, and rain-fed farming made village agreements on water use essential. Disputes over irrigation turns, boundaries, and inheritance regularly appear in court registers. Rural government was thus not a matter of orders flowing downward but of repeated negotiation among villagers, intermediaries, and officials over who owed what and who would bear the cost of maintaining the systems that made cultivation possible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxation in an Egyptian Village: What the Cadastre of 1527–1528 Reveals

Egyptian villages are among the best-documented rural communities of the early Ottoman period. In the cadastre of 1527–1528, Nicolas Michel found, the agents distinguished in each village between fiscal shares that could actually be collected (al-'amir), corresponding to cultivators resident in the village, and shares whose collection was suspended because cultivators had left. Four fragments of the cadastral registers (the tarbīʿ) survive: one original and three copies made in the second half of the sixteenth century (Michel, Mamlūk Studies Review 25, 2022, p. 58). Much of the cadastre's data is also preserved indirectly, in later registers compiled from 1550 on. The distinction between collectible and suspended shares is drawn from Michel's study of migration (Annales islamologiques 35, 2001, pp. 241–290).

Two things must be kept apart here. Fiscal liability was what the cultivators of a village owed as land tax; revenue assignments were the rights of tax-farmers, endowments, or officials to collect that tax. The cadastre recorded the first as the basis for the second. Because the tax depended on resident cultivators, the kanunname of 1525 tried to prevent cultivators from abandoning their villages and provided ways to recover those who had gone. Even so, Michel documents substantial peasant migration in the Delta from the early Ottoman period, and warns against treating every recorded departure as flight from taxation or as resistance.

The evidence shows the tension between a state that wished cultivators to stay and villagers who sometimes moved anyway. It also shows how the village appeared to officials: as a unit of fiscal shares tied to resident households. Villagers could also appeal to the kadi, and Michel has used the court registers of Asyut to study rural society. What this evidence cannot show: the cadastre records fiscal units, not individual motives or the negotiations behind each assessment, and women, children, and the poorest villagers appear only indirectly.

Section 44.11

Tribes, Pastoralists, and the Limits of Provincial Rule

Beyond the cities and irrigated villages lay steppe, desert, and mountain, where much of the population lived by herding. In Syria, the Jazira, and Iraq these included Arab Bedouin confederations, Turkmen groups, and Kurdish tribes; in North Africa, Arab and Amazigh pastoral communities; in Arabia, many tribal groups. As Chapter 3 argued, pastoralists were neither marginal nor isolated: they supplied animals, transport, and military manpower, and they relied on markets and settled communities.

The Ottomans did not try to administer these regions as they administered a village survey district. Their methods were varied. They recognized tribal chiefs as officials with responsibility for order; paid subsidies for protection of caravan routes; imposed taxes on flocks where they could; built fortified stations along the pilgrimage road; and sent punitive expeditions when negotiation failed. Tribes themselves could be allies, tax collectors, raiders, or all three. Control of the pilgrimage route from Damascus to Mecca depended on payments and agreements with the tribes through whose territory it passed. In Yemen, the highland tribal confederations were the base of the Zaydi imamate that resisted the Ottomans, while in Algiers and Tunis the beys' annual expeditions into the interior collected taxes from tribes by a mixture of bargaining and force.

Geography set limits. Deserts and mountains slowed armies and made supply costly, and mobile populations could withdraw beyond the reach of the state. Yet limits did not mean absence. Tribal leaders sought Ottoman recognition, titles, and tax assignments because these strengthened their positions among their own followers, while the state valued the order and revenue that cooperation produced. The relationship was continually renegotiated, and students should think of the frontier as a zone of bargaining rather than a line.

Section 44.12

Egypt: Imperial Government and Mamluk Continuities

Egypt was the richest Arab province and the one where continuity with the preceding regime was most visible. The Ottomans did not at first replace the Mamluk elite wholesale. Their first governor, Khayr Bey, was a former Mamluk commander who had governed Aleppo and defected to Selim I before the battle of Marj Dabiq. He held office in Cairo from 1517 until his death in 1522, and his career illustrates a deliberate choice: use a figure who knew the country's administration and could reassure its elites. Governors sent from Istanbul followed, and the revolt of Ahmed Pasha, who became governor in 1523 and rebelled against Süleyman before the revolt was suppressed in 1524, showed the danger of leaving a rich and distant province under one powerful official.

The settlement that followed is associated with the grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha, who intervened in Egypt in 1524–1525, and with the Egyptian kanunname of 1525, which Michel describes as fixing the institutional foundations of the province. It should be distinguished from the cadastre of 1527–1528, the only general cadastre the Ottomans carried out in Egypt, which was conducted in accordance with the regulation. Egypt's registration thus rested on a land-based fiscal tradition inherited from the Mamluks, rather than on the household-oriented surveys characteristic of Syria, illustrating why the Ottomans did not simply replicate one system everywhere.

The provincial structure had several layers. The governor resided in the Cairo Citadel and presided over the Divan, a council including senior officers and officials. A treasurer (defterdar) managed finance and answered to the imperial treasury, so that the governor could not use Egyptian revenue without scrutiny. A chief judge was sent from Istanbul, yet the judiciary retained features of Mamluk practice, as Atçıl's research has shown. Several regiments garrisoned the citadel and the main towns, with the Janissaries foremost; each had its own officers and revenues, and their rivalry with one another and with the governor was a recurring feature of Egyptian politics.

Rural government rested on the kashifs, administrators drawn from the Mamluk military elite and its successors, each supervising a district. Their duties included overseeing the repair of dikes and canals before the flood, assisting in tax collection, maintaining security against raiding and disorder, and reporting to the governor. None of this could be done without village headmen, who allocated burdens and mobilized labor; Coptic scribes and accountants, who kept the records on which assessment depended; and cultivators, who held rights of use and paid dues. Failure to maintain the water system endangered both harvest and revenue.

Egypt's fiscal role was central. It sent an annual remittance to the imperial treasury, supplied grain, and financed the provisioning of the holy cities. Tax farming, held by soldiers, beys, and civilians, became increasingly important as a means of collection. The combination of close Ottoman supervision and Mamluk-descended households produced a stable system in the sixteenth century but also created the basis for later developments examined in Chapter 47. Evidence for Egyptian government is unusually rich: imperial decrees, court records, chronicles such as that of Ibn Iyas, and the fiscal documents studied by specialists. Together they show a province where Ottoman power was real and closely supervised, yet exercised through institutions and personnel with deep local roots.

Section 44.13

Syria and Palestine: Provinces, Cities, and Countryside

In Syria the Ottomans introduced the fullest version of the classical provincial structure: a survey-based system of revenue assignment organized in sanjaks. After 1516, Damascus became the seat of a large province including the sanjaks of Jerusalem, Gaza, Safed, Nablus, and others. By 1534, as Bruce Masters has shown, Aleppo was a full provincial capital, though the stages by which it separated from Damascus are disputed. The division reflected geography and commerce: Aleppo faced north toward Anatolia, the Jazira, and the overland trade with Iran, while Damascus faced south toward Palestine, the Hijaz, and the pilgrimage. Each developed its own relations with its hinterland, its garrison, and its merchants.

A sanjak bey's duties combined several functions. He supervised the timar holders of his district and led them to musters, maintained public order, supported the collection of revenue, and escorted or contributed troops to the pilgrimage and to campaigns. He did not hold judicial authority: that belonged to the kadi of the relevant judicial district, who registered the transactions of the district, heard disputes, and reported on conditions. The two officials were meant to check one another, and local communities learned to play them off against each other.

Villages in Syria and Palestine were assessed through surveys beginning in the 1520s, recording households, lands, mills, orchards, and the taxes due. Because the village was a tax unit, its elders and headmen divided the burden among households and negotiated with the timar holder's agent, and a village that could not pay might see its population fall as families left. Historians of Palestine and Syria have used the registers to reconstruct local economies, finding mixed cultivation of grain, olives, fruit, and cotton, along with herding. The registers capture households liable to tax, not the whole population, and mobile groups appear only partially.

Cities were governed through cooperation between officials and local elites. The governor of Damascus and his troops held the citadel, but courts, endowments, and notable families, including scholars and descendants of the Prophet, performed essential governing functions in the markets and quarters. The governor of Damascus also commanded the annual pilgrimage caravan to Mecca, an office of great prestige, which required organizing supplies, agreements with tribes along the route, and troops from several sanjaks. Local chroniclers such as Ibn Tulun preserve the viewpoint of Damascus's literate classes, while Aleppo's connections to Iranian silk and European merchants belong to Chapter 45.

Jerusalem received special imperial attention. Süleyman's rebuilding of its walls around 1537–1541, along with endowments and public works, expressed Ottoman interest in a city sacred to three faiths, and its court records document the daily business of Muslims, Christians, and Jews. In Mount Lebanon, authority was negotiated: local emirs, including the Druze Ma'n family, collected taxes and exercised military power under Ottoman recognition, depending on regular payment of revenue. Their later careers, including the ambitious expansion under Fakhr al-Din II, belong to Chapter 47. Syria thus combined direct administration in cities and plains with indirect rule in mountain and steppe zones, and the creation of the province of Tripoli in 1579 shows that the map was redrawn when control of the coast required it.

Section 44.14

Iraq: Baghdad, Basra, Mosul, and the Eastern Frontier

Iraq was not one province but a set of regions with different administrative and social structures. Mosul became an Ottoman provincial capital in the sixteenth century, conventionally dated to 1535, which made it a center on the road between Anatolia, Syria, and Iran. Baghdad was taken in 1534–1535 and became the seat of the most important province, with a large garrison and responsibility for the frontier with Safavid Iran. Basra shows a two-stage incorporation: its ruler acknowledged Ottoman suzerainty in 1536, but direct Ottoman intervention occurred only in 1546. Distinguishing these cities is essential, because Safavid warfare can obscure the administrative differences among them.

Baghdad's government combined timar assignments, a large salaried garrison, and relations with tribal confederations. The Peace of Amasya (1555) framed relations with the Safavids, but the frontier remained contested, in part because the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala lay within the region and the allegiance of tribes could shift. Revenue came from irrigated agriculture along the rivers and canals, from trade, and from customs, but the costs of defense were high, and the productivity of cultivation depended on canal maintenance and on security from raiding. Mosul, by contrast, drew on dry farming, the caravan road, and a mixed population including Kurdish and Arab tribes and Christian communities.

The events of the 1620s show how divided authority within a province could create an opening for a rival empire. Bakr Subashi, a commander in the Baghdad garrison, challenged the Ottoman governor and took control of the city around 1621. The Ottomans responded militarily, and Bakr appealed to Shah Abbas I of Iran. The prospect of Safavid intervention then led Ottoman officials to recognize him as governor, an act that was itself part of the effort to keep Iran out. Bakr subsequently resisted the Safavids, but Shah Abbas's forces besieged and captured Baghdad, and the city passed to Iran; the Turkish Encyclopedia of Islam notes that accounts differ on the precise circumstances and date of its fall. The crisis is better understood as one of competing local and imperial claims than as a simple betrayal by the Janissaries. Murad IV retook Baghdad in 1638, and the Treaty of Zuhab (1639) established a frontier that proved durable.

Basra developed a distinct pattern. The Afrasiyab family governed Basra from about 1596 until 1668, holding Ottoman office while building local power through control of the port, alliances with tribes, and the Gulf trade. Their position depended on recognition from Istanbul and on their ability to deliver order and revenue, and the Ottomans accepted a family-based government in a distant port rather than risk the cost of replacing it. In the south, tribal confederations such as the Muntafiq gained importance in the seventeenth century, and Ottoman officials negotiated with their leaders. In the northeast, Kurdish chiefs held semi-autonomous districts and served as a buffer against Iran.

Courts in Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul registered property, endowments, and disputes, and non-Muslim communities, including Christians in Mosul and Jews in Baghdad, used them. Iraq therefore illustrates how a province with considerable resources could still be difficult to govern: frontier politics, powerful garrisons, tribal confederations, and the maintenance of irrigation systems all limited central control. The later development of provincial households in Iraq belongs to Chapter 47.

Section 44.15

The Hijaz: Sharifian Government and Ottoman Suzerainty

The Hijaz was unlike any other region because of Mecca and Medina. Protecting the pilgrimage and the holy cities was central to the sultan's legitimacy, and the Ottoman dynasty used the title Servitor of the Two Holy Sanctuaries. After the conquest of Egypt, the sharif of Mecca, Barakat ibn Muhammad, a descendant of the Prophet who had governed the city under the Mamluks, acknowledged Ottoman overlordship. His son Abu Numayy, who had been associated with his father in rule since 1512, represented him before Selim I in Cairo in 1517. The Ottomans confirmed the sharifs in their position rather than replacing them with an appointed governor, and Abu Numayy remained a leading figure for decades.

Authority was divided in specific ways. The sharif belonged to a ruling family of descendants of the Prophet whose members competed for the position, and the Ottoman sultan's confirmation was part of its legitimacy. He governed Mecca and its surroundings, maintained armed retainers, and received revenues from local tolls and a share of the customs collected at Jeddah. The Ottomans appointed a judge for Mecca from the imperial hierarchy and stationed officials and troops, particularly at Jeddah, the port through which pilgrims, grain, and goods entered. Administration of the Hijaz was at first overseen by the governor of Egypt and later by governors of Jeddah. Apart from customs, the inhabitants of the Hijaz generally did not pay regular taxes to the empire, since the region was supported by revenue and supplies from elsewhere.

The system was financed from outside the region. Judges' salaries were assigned from Egyptian funds or Jeddah customs, as the surviving register of judicial appointments for the Hijaz indicates, and the Ottoman administration in Cairo organized the Egyptian pilgrimage caravan, shipped grain, and dispatched the funds and gifts known as the surre to the inhabitants of the holy cities. Medina had its own judge, garrison, and officials. Cooperation was practical as well as symbolic: in 1541 the Ottoman garrison of Jeddah and Meccan forces led by Abu Numayy together repelled a Portuguese attack, and Süleyman rewarded the sharif.

Succession and revenue show the system's workings. Rivalries within the sharifian family over who should rule created disputes in which the imperial center or its representatives could intervene, mediate, or confirm a winner, and in the seventeenth century competition for the emirate of Mecca was intense. Customs revenue at Jeddah, shared between the sharif and the Ottoman administration, was a continual point of negotiation. The Ottomans could not easily replace a sharif without damaging their standing among pilgrims and scholars, while the sharifs could not ignore Ottoman power.

The Hijaz therefore exemplifies suzerainty in a precise sense: the sultan's overlordship was acknowledged and real, but daily governance was shared among the sharifs, Ottoman officials in Jeddah and the holy cities, and the Egyptian establishment. It was not governed as Cairo was, through an appointed governor, and its history shows how imperial legitimacy could depend on preserving local authority rather than displacing it.

Section 44.16

Yemen and the Red Sea: Contested Provincial Authority

Ottoman involvement in Yemen arose from competition with the Portuguese in the Red Sea and Indian Ocean. In 1538, forces under Hadim Süleyman Pasha took Aden on the way to an expedition toward India and killed its ruler; the Ottomans then extended their authority over Zabid, which became an administrative base, and over the coastal plain of the Tihama. In August 1547 Özdemir Pasha took Sana'a, and a province of Yemen emerged, together with a separate province of Habesh on the African coast with ports such as Massawa and Suakin. The undertaking was costly in lives and money, and troops and supplies had to be sent from Egypt.

Yemen had two contrasting zones. In the coastal plain, administrative centers such as Zabid collected revenue from agriculture and trade, and the growth of the coffee trade, with Mocha as a major port, increased the region's commercial importance. The highlands were home to the Zaydi imamate, a tradition in which the imam was a leader chosen for learning and descent from the Prophet's family, and whose authority depended on tribal support. Zaydi leaders and highland tribes regarded Ottoman rule as foreign and fought it repeatedly.

Negotiation marked even the early conquest. After Özdemir Pasha failed to dislodge the imam al-Mutahhar from Thula, an agreement in 1552 (according to Michel Tuchscherer's work, as summarized in reference sources) had al-Mutahhar recognize Ottoman suzerainty while keeping authority over Kawkaban and Thula; in the following year Ottoman authority was established over other highland districts. The settlement did not last. Administrative disorder, including repeated changes of governor, preceded al-Mutahhar's rebellion in 1567, which reduced Ottoman control to little more than Zabid. Sinan Pasha's reconquest ended in a treaty in May 1570, and he left Yemen the following year.

The imamate's recovery came from within. Al-Qasim, who held the imamate from 1597, built a political structure in the highlands, and after his death in 1620 his son al-Mu'ayyad Muhammad succeeded him without a succession dispute and reconfirmed a truce with the Ottomans. Over the following years the Ottomans lost Sana'a, whose keys were handed over to the imam's son under a truce in 1629, and in 1635 an Ottoman counteroffensive was defeated and the Ottomans agreed to surrender Zabid, Mocha, and Kamaran Island, ending the first Ottoman period in Yemen. The details of these final years rest largely on secondary accounts of the Yemeni and Ottoman chronicles, and a precise date for the last evacuation remains to be verified from primary documentation.

Yemen demonstrates the limits of provincial rule when geography, a rooted local political tradition, and logistics worked against the state. Its loss should not be read as a sign of general imperial weakness: the Ottomans gave up a distant and expensive province while holding other, more central regions. It also shows that expansion in the Red Sea was driven by commerce and strategic competition as much as by territorial ambition, a theme developed in Chapter 45. The Ottomans later returned to parts of Yemen in the nineteenth century.

Section 44.17

Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli: The North African Regencies

The North African provinces were created through a combination of corsair enterprise, Ottoman naval power, and local politics, and they developed along different paths. Algiers entered the Ottoman orbit around 1519, when the corsair leader Hayreddin Barbarossa placed his territory under Selim's protection and received imperial support. Tripoli was taken from the Knights of St John in 1551, and Tunis was secured in 1574 after a struggle with Spain. Morocco, ruled by the Sa'dian dynasty, remained outside the empire. The three regencies shared a dependence on Janissary corps and corsair revenue, but treating them as a single system hides the different timing and character of their political development.

In Algiers the early governors were Ottoman beylerbeyis, among them Hayreddin and later commanders sent from Istanbul. Regular provincial government followed, with pashas who ruled for three-year terms. The Janissary corps (odjak) and the corporation of corsair captains (ta'ifat al-ru'asa) had to share power, since the soldiers depended economically on corsair activity. Irregular pay led the Janissaries to revolt repeatedly in the mid-seventeenth century, and the Janissary commander (agha) seized power in 1659. In 1671 the corps, in a revolt in which accounts emphasize the corsair captains' role, killed the agha and installed a leader with the title of dey, which Algiers took from Tunis. From later in the century the dey was elected by Janissary officers, and the Ottoman government retained little effective influence over the regency. Provincial governors called beys administered districts such as Constantine, Titteri, and Oran and led tax-collecting expeditions into the interior.

Tunis followed a different course. After 1574 it was governed by an Ottoman pasha with a garrison. In October 1591 the junior officers of the Janissaries, the deys, revolted against their superior officers and the pasha, and forced the pasha to accept their leader as head of government. Real power then lay with the dey, as Uthman Dey (in office from 1593, and effectively in command from 1598) showed by reducing the pasha to an honorific role. In the early seventeenth century the bey, who commanded the tax-collecting expeditions in the countryside, became a powerful figure. Murad Bey succeeded Ramadan Bey in 1613, held office until 1631, and was eventually also named pasha, founding the Muradid line. Leïla Temime Blili's research warns against reading Tunisian history as an inevitable march toward independence, since the relationship with Istanbul remained meaningful.

Tripoli, taken in 1551, was a smaller and less wealthy regency. Its early leaders included corsair commanders such as Dragut. It was governed by pashas with a Janissary garrison and depended on revenues from the coast, the oases, trans-Saharan trade, and corsair activity. A coup by junior Janissary officers in 1611 forced the pasha to accept their leader, Suleiman Safar, as head of government, so that Tripoli too acquired a dey. The rise of the Karamanli household belongs to the eighteenth century and Chapter 47.

Across the regencies, taxation depended on periodic expeditions into the interior, tribal contributions, customs, and corsair revenues. The kuloğlus, sons of Janissaries and local women, formed a distinctive social group, and local scholars, merchants, and courts operated alongside the military establishment. The regencies maintained recognition of Ottoman sovereignty through the use of the sultan's name, investiture of rulers, and diplomatic and military cooperation, while becoming increasingly locally rooted. Ottoman suzerainty offered legitimacy, access to military resources, and diplomatic standing in the Mediterranean, and regency rulers valued these benefits. The pattern differed in each province: the dey in Tunis and Tripoli arose from junior-officer coups around 1591 and 1611, while in Algiers the sequence ran through the aghas to the dey of 1671.

Historical Debate

Centralized Imperial Power or Negotiated Provincial Authority?

The question: How far did Istanbul control the Arab provinces, and how far did government depend on negotiation with local societies?

Interpretation A — the capable center: The Ottoman state used surveys, registers, appointments, judges, and standing troops to impose a recognizable system. Michel's studies of Egypt emphasize that taxation put the state at the heart of village relations, and the cadastre of 1527–1528 shows a government able to assess an entire province.
Interpretation B — the negotiated empire: Officials relied on local elites, adapted to pre-Ottoman practice, and bargained with tribes and villages. Bruce Masters's comparative treatment of the Arab provinces and Atçıl's work on the Egyptian judiciary stress variation and continuity with Mamluk practice.

Most scholars see these views as overlapping rather than opposed: the state was capable because it negotiated. The debate matters most for the seventeenth century, where it shapes how historians interpret the rise of provincial households.

Evidence: Imperial registers, kanunnames, and cadastres support A; court registers, chronicles, and the survival of local ruling families such as the sharifs, the Ma'ns, and the Afrasiyabs support B. Why it matters: It changes whether later provincial power is read as erosion of central authority or as an extension of long-standing negotiation.

Student task: Choose one province and identify what kinds of evidence would show whether imperial orders were carried out, and whose perspective each source represents.

Section 44.18

Negotiated Empire: Unity, Diversity, and Historical Change

Putting the six regions side by side shows both the unity and the diversity of Ottoman government. Common features included the sultan's authority, the vocabulary of provincial offices, the use of surveys and kanunnames, judges appointed from the capital, standing troops, and the circulation of petitions and orders. Differences included the extent of the timar system, the role of tax farming, the strength of local ruling houses, the presence of tribal confederations, and the nature of the frontier. Egypt had the most intensively documented fiscal administration; Syria had the most fully developed classical provincial structure; Iraq was shaped by the Safavid frontier and by distinct cities; the Hijaz by a sharifian house confirmed by the sultan; Yemen by highland resistance; and the regencies by Janissary corps and corsair enterprise.

The Ottoman empire in the Arab provinces was not a simple structure of command. It was a negotiated empire, in which authority depended on a repeated exchange of recognition, services, and revenue between the center and local societies. Negotiation did not mean that the state was weak. Istanbul appointed officials, wrote the laws, held the registers, and could impose its will when it chose to commit force. Negotiation was an effective method of governing vast and varied territories with limited administrative resources. Where negotiation failed, as in Yemen, the cost of coercion could exceed what the state was willing to pay.

The experiences of those governed differed. Peasants experienced the state as taxation and as an arbiter in disputes; notables as a source of status and risk; soldiers as employers and sometimes as fellow townspeople; tribal leaders as partners and rivals; women and non-Muslims as litigants and property holders in courts that were open to them but not neutral. The sharifs of Mecca, the emirs of Mount Lebanon, and the Afrasiyabs of Basra show that local rulers could hold office within the Ottoman system and still build their own power.

The state's changing fiscal and military needs altered these relationships over time, creating the conditions explored in Chapters 45 through 47. Chapter 45 examines the cities, commerce, and pilgrimage that the provincial systems protected and taxed; Chapter 46 turns to religion and learning; Chapter 47 follows the households and governors who exploited the openings described here. For students, the lesson is to ask of any government document: who wrote it, what did it want, and how do we know what happened next?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Ottoman Empire governed the Arab provinces in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. Its central argument is that Ottoman rule was neither uniformly centralized nor merely symbolic. Istanbul had real authority, expressed through appointments, surveys, taxation, law, and force, but the effectiveness of that authority depended on provincial resources, geography, military establishments, and cooperation from local societies.

The Imperial Council, the sultan's orders, and the registers of decisions formed the center of the system. Provinces (eyalets) were divided into sanjaks, military and fiscal districts, while judicial districts (kazas) formed a separate hierarchy. Governors, treasurers, judges, and garrison commanders shared and contested provincial power, and the center encouraged their mutual oversight. Orders were responses to petitions as often as commands, and their execution has to be checked against local evidence.

Fiscal government rested on surveys and registers adapted to each province. Syria and Palestine were documented through tahrir registers; Egypt through the kanunname of 1525, the cadastre of 1527–1528, and registers of dikes. Students distinguished rights to land from rights to revenue: miri, mülk, and waqf land differed, Egyptian cultivators held rights of use with eminent ownership in the treasury, and timar and iltizam were assignments or contracts for collecting revenue rather than ownership.

Fiscal and military pressures reshaped provincial administration. Rising costs of war, inflation, and the changing character of armies expanded tax farming and salaried troops, creating local financiers and soldiers with stakes in provincial revenue. This process prepared the ground for the provincial households of Chapter 47.

Courts and petitions gave subjects access to government. The kadi administered sharia and registered transactions, while kanun and custom governed tax and administrative matters, and legal pluralism persisted, as the Egyptian judiciary shows. Women, non-Muslims, villagers, and soldiers appear in court records, though the records capture only those who could reach the court.

Notables, village headmen, and local scribes mediated between rulers and the ruled. Villages bargained over taxes, petitioned, and sometimes left; Egyptian cadastral records show that resident cultivators were the basis of collectible revenue. Tribes and pastoralists were recognized, subsidized, taxed, and sometimes fought, and the limits of provincial rule were set by geography and by the need for cooperation.

The regional cases revealed the variety of Ottoman governance. Egypt combined intensive fiscal supervision with Mamluk continuities, beginning with the governorship of Khayr Bey. Syria and Palestine had the fullest classical provincial structure, with Damascus and Aleppo developing separately and local emirs in Mount Lebanon. Iraq was a set of regions, Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul, shaped by the Safavid frontier and powerful garrisons.

The Hijaz was governed through sharifian rule under Ottoman suzerainty, financed from Egypt and Jeddah customs. Yemen showed the limits of control in the face of highland resistance, negotiation in 1552, and eventual loss. The North African regencies were governed through Janissary corps, with different sequences in Algiers (aghas, then the dey of 1671), Tunis (the deys of 1591, then the beys), and Tripoli (a dey after 1611).

The historical debate between centralized and negotiated views highlights how interpretation depends on evidence. Decrees and registers show the center's capacity; court records and chronicles show negotiation. Most scholars see the two as overlapping, with negotiation a method of imperial rule rather than a sign of failure.

Students should continue to treat imperial orders as evidence of intention, not proof of action, and to look for local sources that reveal implementation. The experiences of governors, notables, cultivators, soldiers, women, and non-Muslim subjects differed, and none stands for the whole. These institutions are the foundation for Chapters 45 through 47 and the baseline for understanding the Tanzimat reforms of Chapter 50.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a large premodern empire actually functioned. By examining governors, taxes, courts, soldiers, notables, villages, and tribes, it reveals that government was a continuing process of negotiation as much as command, and that the state's reach depended on the cooperation of the people it governed.

It also reveals that the Arab provinces were governed differently from one another. Treating them as one system would obscure the contrasts among Egypt's fiscal bureaucracy, the Hijaz's sharifian arrangement, Yemen's contested highlands, and the regencies' military corps.

The chapter connects different regions through common instruments: the survey, the kanunname, the kadi, the Janissary, and the petition. It also connects them through the pilgrimage and the flows of grain, funds, and troops among Egypt, the Hijaz, Yemen, and Syria, which Chapter 45 explores.

Many of these patterns persisted. Tax farming, local notables, military households, and tribal arrangements shaped the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and their legacies were visible even when nineteenth-century reformers attempted to centralize. Understanding them helps students read Chapters 47 and 50 with greater care.

Finally, the chapter models a way of reading evidence: asking who produced a document, what it wanted, and whether we can verify what followed. That habit will serve students throughout the remainder of the textbook.
