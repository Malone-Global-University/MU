Chapter Opening

In December 1783 a Berlin monthly printed, in a footnote to an article on marriage, a question it said had not yet been answered: what is enlightenment? Within a year two of the German-speaking world's most respected thinkers had replied in its pages. The question was asked in a periodical, to a reading public, by an editor who expected answers. That fact says as much about the Enlightenment as any reply.

The monthly was the Berlinische Monatsschrift, edited by Johann Erich Biester and Friedrich Gedike. The footnote was added by the Berlin pastor and official Johann Friedrich Zöllner, who worried that talk of enlightenment was unsettling religion and marriage. In September 1784 Moses Mendelssohn, a Jewish philosopher and merchant's bookkeeper turned celebrated author, answered in the same journal, and in December Immanuel Kant, a professor at distant Königsberg, answered in its pages as well. Neither wrote for a prince's council or a university faculty. Both wrote for readers who subscribed, borrowed, or read the journal in a society reading room, and both knew that their answers would be copied, reprinted, discussed, and attacked. The episode condenses the subject of this chapter: an intellectual movement that took shape through journals, correspondents, publishers, clubs, academies, and conversations, and that argued about its own meaning in public.

This chapter therefore studies the Enlightenment less as a set of doctrines than as a culture of communication. Its central problem is how ideas, criticisms, and bodies of information moved between people and across the political, linguistic, and religious frontiers of eighteenth century Europe, and what kinds of people and institutions made that movement possible. The answer involves famous writers such as Voltaire, Diderot, Hume, Lessing, and Rousseau. It also involves booksellers who financed and smuggled their works, translators who rendered them into other languages and often altered them, salon hostesses who decided who met whom, academy secretaries who organized prize competitions, postal clerks and diplomats who carried letters, censors who suppressed some books and tolerated others, and readers whose reactions are often the hardest evidence to recover.

The chapter's scope is roughly 1680 to 1790, with antecedents in the learned networks of the seventeenth century and consequences that stretch into the revolutionary era. Its geography is deliberately wider than Paris. French was the dominant language of cosmopolitan exchange, and Paris was an indispensable center, but the movement also had major centers in Edinburgh and Glasgow, Amsterdam and Rotterdam, Berlin and Königsberg, Milan and Naples, Warsaw, Madrid, Lisbon, Stockholm, and Saint Petersburg, and in Atlantic and imperial settings from Philadelphia to Cap-Français. Greek, Serbian, Romanian, Hebrew, and Ottoman Turkish printing and reading also belong to the story. Historians now commonly speak of Enlightenments in the plural, and Section 82.19 examines why.

The chapter has to explain an apparent paradox. Enlightenment writers often spoke in universal terms, of reason, humanity, and improvement, yet the networks they built were socially narrow. Women were excluded from most academies, though they hosted salons and wrote, translated, and experimented. Jews gained access to the republic of letters through exceptional individuals while living under legal disabilities. Enslaved Africans and Indigenous peoples were subjects of European inquiry, and occasionally authors and informants, but rarely participants on equal terms. Most Europeans were rural, many could not read, and most books sold in the century were devotional works and almanacs rather than philosophy. Taking the Enlightenment seriously means taking both its ambitions and its limits seriously.

Chapter 82 occupies a particular place in Part XIII and in Volume IV. Chapter 81 examined how investigations of nature were transformed into new scientific knowledge and institutions. Chapter 83 will analyze the political theories of Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, and others. The present chapter lies between them as a study of infrastructure. It explains how knowledge and criticism became public, controversial, and influential, and it supplies the setting in which political arguments were composed, printed, and read. It does not narrate how those arguments were later used in revolution, a subject for Part XIV beginning with Chapter 87, and it avoids the assumption that the Enlightenment led inevitably to revolution, secularization, or liberal government.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Printed books and periodicals survive in huge numbers, along with subscriber lists, library catalogues, and the business archives of some publishers, notably the Société typographique de Neuchâtel, whose order books and correspondence show what a Swiss publishing house sold across France. Police files, confiscation records, and censors' reports document attempts at control. Tens of thousands of letters survive, though survival favors famous correspondents. Memoirs, diaries, and marginal notes reveal a few readers' voices. Oral conversation, the heart of salon and coffeehouse life, left only traces. Throughout the chapter, students should ask who is visible in the evidence and who is not.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians treat the Enlightenment as a historical category with contested chronology, geography, and meaning, and distinguish contemporary uses of the term from later interpretations.
  2. Trace the development of the republic of letters from its seventeenth century foundations into the eighteenth century, identifying the people and institutions that sustained it.
  3. Analyze how printing, publishing, translation, and the book trade moved ideas across borders, and distinguish access to books from agreement with their arguments.
  4. Assess the size and limits of the eighteenth century reading public using evidence about literacy, libraries, subscriptions, and reading practices.
  5. Compare salons, coffeehouses, clubs, lodges, and academies as settings for discussion, and explain how each conferred or withheld intellectual authority.
  6. Describe the role of correspondence, postal systems, and patronage in international intellectual exchange, and identify their vulnerabilities.
  7. Evaluate the production, content, and controversies of the Encyclopédie as a case study in collaborative scholarship, commercial publishing, and contested authority.
  8. Distinguish among deism, skepticism, atheism, and reformist religious belief in Enlightenment writing, and explain why the movement cannot be described as uniformly irreligious.
  9. Compare censorship and regulation in several European states and explain why neither prohibition nor toleration alone determined what people read.
  10. Contextualize the Enlightenment in Britain, Ireland, the Dutch Republic, the German lands, Italy, Iberia, Scandinavia, Poland-Lithuania, Russia, and southeastern Europe, resisting a model centered only on France.
  11. Interpret primary sources as evidence, considering authorship, purpose, audience, and perspectives that are absent.
  12. Evaluate competing arguments about the public sphere, the unity of the Enlightenment, and the relationship between universal claims and exclusion, slavery, and empire.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did ideas actually travel between people, institutions, and countries in the eighteenth century, and what did that travel cost in time, money, and risk?
  2. In what sense was there a new public in this period, and what would count as evidence for it?
  3. How did criticism operate under monarchies, churches, and censorship systems that claimed authority over speech?
  4. Who was inside the republic of letters, who stood at its edges, and who was kept out, and how did status, gender, religion, and language shape those positions?
  5. Why did Enlightenment cultures differ across Scotland, France, the German lands, Italy, Iberia, Poland-Lithuania, and southeastern Europe?
  6. How did the continuity of older learned networks shape what is called the Enlightenment, and what was genuinely new?
  7. How did universal claims about reason and humanity coexist with colonial domination, slavery, and social exclusion?
  8. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct reading, conversation, and the circulation of ideas, and what remains out of reach?

Historical Context

The Enlightenment did not begin from nothing. Chapter 81 traced how astronomers, natural philosophers, and experimenters from Copernicus to Newton transformed knowledge of nature and created institutions such as scientific societies to validate and publish it. Those institutions supplied models and personnel for what followed. A society that publishes proceedings, corresponds with foreign members, and organizes competitions for the best solution to a problem has already built much of the machinery of intellectual exchange. Popularizers extended the audience: Bernard de Fontenelle's Conversations on the Plurality of Worlds (1686) explained the new astronomy in dialogue form to a lay readership that included many women, and by the early eighteenth century Newton's work was being translated, summarized, and debated outside specialist circles.

Religious conflict had also shaped the environment. The wars of religion and the Thirty Years' War, examined in Chapters 66 and 67, left Europe divided into confessional states but also produced pragmatic arrangements for coexistence and a widespread weariness with doctrinal violence. In 1685 Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes, which had granted limited rights to French Protestants, and tens of thousands of Huguenots left France for the Dutch Republic, England, the German lands, Switzerland, and colonial settlements. They carried skills as printers, pastors, teachers, and journalists, and they filled the booksellers' quarters of Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, London, and Berlin with French-language talent. The Dutch Republic, examined in Chapter 77, offered unusually permissive conditions for printing, though not unlimited freedom. In England the Glorious Revolution of 1688 to 1689 and the lapse of the Licensing Act in 1695 ended systematic prior licensing of the press, though prosecutions for seditious or blasphemous publication continued.

The political context was one of strong states and expensive wars. Louis XIV's France, the Habsburg monarchy, Prussia, Russia, and Britain competed in the dynastic and balance of power conflicts discussed in Chapters 76, 78, and 80. States wanted useful knowledge, such as maps, navigation, medicine, forestry, and fiscal information, and they sponsored academies and universities. They also feared dissent, and controlled printing through privileges, licensing, and police. Europe's societies remained hierarchical, divided into estates or orders, with nobles, clergy, and corporate bodies holding legal privileges. Intellectual life therefore depended on patrons, offices, pensions, and protection. Even the most critical writers needed noble friends, royal permissions, or distance from the censor.

Material change mattered too. Printing, introduced in the fifteenth century and discussed in Chapters 55, 59, and 60, had by 1700 produced a mature commercial book trade with international fairs, specialized printers, and distribution networks. Roads, canals, post routes, and shipping improved unevenly. Cities grew, and with them the number of people with leisure, money, and a taste for novelty. The commercial and consumer developments of the century are the subject of Chapter 85, and the lived experience of religion and family is the subject of Chapter 86. This chapter touches them only where they explain how books, periodicals, and conversation reached audiences.

Finally, the chapter depends on evidence of kinds that political history usually ignores. Bibliographers count editions and track false imprints, the places printed on title pages that were deliberately false. Historians of the book study print runs, paper, and contracts. Social historians read inventories of estates to discover what ordinary people owned. Literary historians study the manuscripts that circulated alongside print. Digital projects have begun to map surviving letters across Europe, though they reveal chiefly the correspondence that archives preserved. Historians combine these sources with caution, knowing that the visible record overrepresents the educated, the male, the urban, and the western.

Section 82.1

Defining the Enlightenment: Chronology, Geography, and Historical Interpretation

The English word "Enlightenment" is a retrospective label. Eighteenth century writers spoke of light, of enlightened ages and enlightened persons, and in different languages used different words: les Lumières in French, Aufklärung in German, illuminismo in Italian, Ilustración in Spanish. The metaphor of light was old and religious, and it described a process more often than an achievement. To be enlightened was to emerge from darkness, ignorance, or prejudice, and contemporaries argued fiercely about what the darkness was. Some meant superstition, some meant clerical power, some meant the ignorance of the poor, and some meant the narrowness of scholastic learning. The singular, capitalized "Enlightenment" as the name of a historical period or movement became common in English only in the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the idea that a single movement existed owed much to its enemies, who blamed a cabal of "philosophes" for the upheavals of the 1790s, and to its admirers, who celebrated it as the origin of modern liberty.

Even the most famous contemporary definition was a contribution to a debate rather than a settlement of one. In 1784, as the Chapter Opening described, Immanuel Kant answered a journal's question by describing enlightenment as emergence from self-incurred immaturity, the inability to use one's own understanding without another's guidance, and he urged readers to adopt the old Latin motto sapere aude, dare to know. Kant's essay, examined in Section 82.19, also insisted that his own age was an age of enlightenment in progress, not a completed enlightened age. Mendelssohn, answering the same question, distinguished enlightenment as theoretical knowledge and criticism from culture and education as practical refinement. Their answers differ, and neither was universally accepted. The historian's job is to reconstruct the concerns the writers shared without pretending they agreed.

The concerns that recur across many writers include a commitment to reason, understood not as a single method but as a range of practices from mathematical demonstration to empirical observation, comparison, and skepticism; a habit of criticism, applied to texts, institutions, customs, and authority; a preoccupation with improvement, the belief that education, law, commerce, and public policy could be made better; interest in nature as a source of explanation and standard; and attention to sociability, the human capacity for cooperative and polite life. These interests were often in tension. Reason and sentiment were rival sources of moral insight for different writers. Nature could justify reform or hierarchy. Improvement could mean humane reform or more efficient exploitation. Section 82.19 returns to these tensions.

Chronology varies with region and interpretation. Many historians place the origins in the late seventeenth century, with Bayle, Locke, and Newton, and the "crisis of the European conscience" that the French scholar Paul Hazard located between 1680 and 1715. The French philosophes flourished from the 1720s through the 1770s. The Scottish Enlightenment is usually dated from the 1740s to the 1790s, the German Aufklärung from the early eighteenth century, with a high point in the 1770s and 1780s, the Italian reform movement from the 1740s to the 1790s, and eastern European movements often from the 1750s and later. Some historians end the Enlightenment in 1789 or 1794, others at the French Revolution's conservative reaction, and others extend it to the early nineteenth century. Different boundaries reflect different definitions and different questions.

Historical interpretation has itself changed. In the early twentieth century, intellectual historians such as Ernst Cassirer and, later, Peter Gay described a largely unified movement centered on French and British thought. After the Second World War, critics including Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno argued that Enlightenment rationality contained the seeds of domination. From the 1970s and 1980s, historians such as Robert Darnton turned to the social history of publishing, reading, and clandestine literature, and the collection The Enlightenment in National Context (1981) emphasized regional differences. Since the 1990s, scholars such as J. G. A. Pocock have argued for plural Enlightenments, while Jonathan Israel has advanced a contrasting thesis of a "radical" Enlightenment descending from Spinoza. Chapter 82 draws mainly on the social and cultural approach, since its assignment is to explain communication and community, while treating the intellectual content of political thought as the work of Chapter 83.

One further caution is needed. The term "philosophe" in French covered writers who were not professional philosophers: playwrights, naturalists, clerics, officials, and journalists. Not all were critics of religion, and not all were opponents of monarchy. Many Enlightenment figures were clergy, court officials, teachers, or noble landowners. Treating the Enlightenment as the work of marginal outsiders against a united establishment misrepresents it. Much of the movement's energy came from inside institutions, whether Scottish universities, German principalities, Habsburg bureaucracies, or Catholic religious orders, as later sections show.

One reason to distinguish the Enlightenment as a historical practice from the Enlightenment as a later reputation is that contemporaries often disagreed even about the proper audience for criticism. A professor might insist that learned controversy belonged within universities; an editor might solicit letters from educated strangers; a pastor might defend inquiry while warning that certain arguments should not circulate among people without formal training. Such positions need not indicate hypocrisy. They reveal competing ideas about how knowledge acquired authority, who could judge it, and what happened when arguments crossed the boundary between expert exchange and wider publicity. The historical question is therefore not simply whether reason spread, but how different kinds of reasoning became recognizable and consequential in particular institutions.

The language of progress was similarly unstable. Improvements in navigation, farming, medicine, and education were often praised as evidence of human ingenuity, yet their usefulness had to be demonstrated to patrons or paying readers. Antiquarian scholarship could revise inherited accounts without expecting society to break with the past. Religious writers might regard better biblical philology as an improvement in faith, while skeptics regarded it as an instrument for exposing contradiction. Both could publish in the same learned marketplace. This diversity helps explain why attempts to divide every author into enlightened supporters and unenlightened opponents distort the record: institutions and individuals combined apparently conservative and innovative commitments. The categories used later in the chapter describe overlapping relationships rather than memberships in a single ideological organization.

Historical Vocabulary
Enlightenment, Reason, Criticism, and Periodization

Enlightenment names a set of overlapping intellectual and cultural movements of roughly the late seventeenth to the late eighteenth century that emphasized the use of reason and evidence, criticism of inherited authority, and the improvement of knowledge and society. Reason in this period did not mean one method: it included mathematical reasoning, experimental inquiry, and skeptical analysis, and many writers also granted moral weight to sentiment. Criticism meant the careful examination of texts, claims, and institutions, and it often began with philology and history before it reached politics and religion. Periodization is the practice of dividing history into named ages. It is an analytical convenience, and it matters here because the Enlightenment started and ended at different times in different regions and arguably never had clear edges. Modern uses of "enlightened" as simple praise should not be projected backward, because contemporaries disagreed about what enlightenment required.

Section 82.2

The Republic of Letters and the Seventeenth Century Foundations of Enlightenment

The phrase respublica literaria, the republic of letters, originated among Renaissance humanists and denoted the imagined community of scholars across Europe who corresponded, exchanged manuscripts, and judged one another's work. By the later seventeenth century it described a practical network. Marin Mersenne's Paris circle, which relayed letters among mathematicians and philosophers until his death in 1648, and Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society, who corresponded with scholars across the continent and launched the Philosophical Transactions in 1665, supplied models. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz maintained correspondence with well over a thousand people and helped found the Berlin Academy in 1700. Scholars in Catholic and Protestant states traded books and opinions across confessional lines, and Benedictine and Jesuit scholars as well as Protestant professors shared textual methods. The Benedictine Jean Mabillon's work on authenticating medieval documents, De re diplomatica (1681), exemplifies a critical erudition that was ecumenical in its methods.

The republic of letters was not a formal state. It had no constitution, and its "citizenship" was granted by reputation. It rested on practices of reciprocity: scholars sent copies of their books, reviewed one another's work, hosted travelers, and recommended students. It was socially embedded in patronage and rank. Those with noble protectors, university chairs, or court posts had advantages; those without could be isolated. Latin remained the shared language of learned exchange, though French increasingly competed with it. The republic was also shaped by religious frontiers. Scholars could often exchange information across the Catholic and Protestant divide, but they could not assume safety: manuscripts were seized, professors dismissed, and writers prosecuted for heresy or blasphemy. The republic's freedom was therefore always conditional, and it depended on personal trust more than on legal rights.

Periodicals gave the republic a public face. The Journal des sçavans in Paris began in January 1665, followed in March by the Philosophical Transactions, and the Acta Eruditorum in Leipzig began in 1682. The Huguenot refugee Pierre Bayle turned the form into a forum. After losing a chair at the Protestant academy of Sedan when it was closed in 1681, he settled in Rotterdam, where he wrote and edited the Nouvelles de la république des lettres (1684 to 1687), a monthly of book reviews that deliberately crossed confessional lines. The title proclaimed the community it served. Journals in Dutch and French cities, many produced by Huguenot refugees such as Jean Le Clerc, extended the practice, and translations and extracts allowed readers to follow books they could never afford to buy.

The Dutch Republic occupied a special place. Its cities hosted universities at Leiden and Utrecht, wealthy merchants, and printers with international distribution, and the relatively decentralized Dutch state, examined in Chapter 77, offered more latitude than most. Dutch printers produced books for sale abroad, including works banned in France and elsewhere. Spinoza's Theological-Political Treatise (1670), which applied critical scholarship to scripture and argued for freedom of thought, appeared anonymously with a false imprint and was banned across Europe, an early example of the dynamics of underground circulation that Section 82.12 explores. Latitude was not the same as liberty, however. Dutch church authorities and magistrates could and did pressure writers, and the Republic's printers faced diplomatic complaints from neighboring monarchs.

Bayle shows both the republic's critical ambition and its limits. His Historical and Critical Dictionary (1697, expanded in 1702) was organized alphabetically around people and places, but its real argument lay in its annotations. The main text presented a conventional biographical summary, while long commentary and footnotes collected conflicting authorities, exposed errors, and raised unsettling questions about reputations, doctrine, and the reliability of testimony. Readers consulted it for erudition, for amusement, and for its devastating treatment of authority. Bayle's Protestant opponent Pierre Jurieu pursued him, and Bayle lost his professorship at Rotterdam in 1693. After the Dictionary appeared, the Walloon church consistory demanded corrections. Bayle's career shows that critical scholarship could provoke censure from Protestants as readily as from Catholics, and that the republic of letters depended on a tolerant host city as much as on abstract principle. Eighteenth century writers, from Voltaire to Diderot, drew on Bayle as a model and a quarry.

The practical obligations of scholarly reciprocity were demanding. A correspondent who wanted information about a newly discovered manuscript might offer a copy of an inscription, an introduction at court, or a review of another scholar's book. A negative review could damage a career, and an unanswered request could cool a relationship. Journal editors relied on books sent by authors and publishers, but the same dependence raised questions about impartiality. When an editor praised a patron's publication, readers could not always know whether the praise expressed agreement, commercial obligation, or diplomatic tact. Intellectual reputation thus grew out of repeated acts of evaluation in which social credibility mattered as much as the contents of a claim. The idea of a republic without territorial borders expressed an aspiration to impartial judgment; the lived republic remained embedded in specific households, universities, churches, and courts.

This earlier scholarly world also developed techniques that would later appear distinctly enlightened. Editorial comparison of variant manuscripts taught readers to distinguish the age of a document from the age of a story it related. Chronology, numismatics, epigraphy, and archival investigation could undermine prestigious historical legends without rejecting learning inherited from antiquity. A researcher who exposed a forged charter might strengthen a prince's rights in one dispute while threatening another institution's claim in the next. Historical criticism therefore carried practical consequences well before periodical writers began to debate religion and morals for broader audiences. The transition to eighteenth-century publicity lay partly in the extension of critical practices to new genres and readers, not in the sudden invention of skepticism.

Historical Vocabulary
Republic of Letters and Intellectual Sociability

The republic of letters (respublica literaria) was the imagined and partly real community of scholars and writers who exchanged letters, books, and criticism across borders. It was not a political institution but a set of practices and obligations, and its membership was defined by reputation and recognition rather than law. Intellectual sociability refers to the habits and settings, such as correspondence, visits, dinners, salons, and societies, through which intellectual work was shared and judged. Modern readers should avoid imagining the republic as a democracy of equals: it was shaped by rank, patronage, gender, language, and religion, and it relied on the cooperation of governments and churches that could close it down.

Section 82.3

Printing, Publishing, Translation, and the European Book Trade

Books and periodicals did not travel by themselves. They were commissioned, financed, printed, bundled, shipped, smuggled, resold, and translated by a commercial trade whose rules and geography determined what could be read where. Publishing in the eighteenth century was a business of credit and risk. Booksellers, who often acted as publishers, advanced money for paper and labor, hoped to sell editions of a few hundred to a few thousand copies, and relied on exchange with other houses at fairs, particularly the Leipzig fairs, to build stock. Printers owned presses and type but frequently worked for booksellers. Authors, with exceptions such as Voltaire, Hume, or later Smith, usually earned little directly from sales, and depended on patrons, offices, or pensions.

Regulation divided the European trade into distinct regimes. In France, the Paris guild of booksellers and printers held a corporate monopoly over most legitimate publication, and every work needed either a royal privilege or one of several lesser permissions, including the tolerated permission tacite, together with the approval of a royal censor. Chrétien-Guillaume de Lamoignon de Malesherbes, who directed the book trade from 1750 to 1763, used this flexibility to shelter projects such as the Encyclopédie, which shows that the system worked through administrative discretion as well as prohibition. Because privileges excluded rival printers and censors slowed appearance, a large trade grew up beyond France's borders, in Amsterdam, Geneva, Neuchâtel, Lausanne, Bouillon, Liège, and the papal enclave of Avignon, producing books in French for the French market, often with false imprints.

In Britain, no prior licensing existed after 1695, and the Statute of Anne of 1710 granted authors and their assignees limited terms of copyright. London booksellers fought to preserve perpetual rights until the House of Lords ruled against them in Donaldson v. Becket in 1774, opening the way for cheaper reprints. In Ireland, Dublin printers reprinted London books freely, supplying cheaper editions at home and abroad. The Holy Roman Empire had no single market. Hundreds of territories had their own regulations, and unauthorized reprinting (Nachdruck) flourished. Leipzig, which overtook Frankfurt as the empire's chief book fair in the eighteenth century, anchored a trade based on exchange. Reformers such as the Leipzig publisher Philipp Erasmus Reich campaigned to replace barter with cash settlement and to protect authors. In Spain, Portugal, and the Italian states, Inquisition and civil censorship constrained imports, though enforcement varied.

Translation was central. Latin's decline and French's rise as the language of cosmopolitan polite exchange meant that many texts crossed frontiers in French, and from French into German, Italian, Spanish, Russian, and Dutch. English works moved into continental circulation through French translation, Huguenot translators such as Pierre Coste, who rendered Locke's Essay into French in 1700, serving as crucial intermediaries. Newton's Opticks appeared in Latin in 1706 in a translation by Samuel Clarke, and Émilie du Châtelet's French translation and commentary on the Principia was published posthumously in 1759. Translators selected, abridged, adapted, and sometimes rewrote. André Morellet's French translation of Beccaria in 1766 rearranged the text, and the version that spread across Europe differed from the Italian original. Translations therefore shaped how ideas were received, and different publics met different versions of the same book.

The book trade also distributed everything but Enlightenment writing. Devotional works, almanacs, calendars, chapbooks, practical manuals, and sermons filled most shelves and warehouses, and historians counting titles in surviving catalogues find that philosophy and criticism formed a small part of the market. It is important, too, to distinguish between access and agreement. A reader who bought a banned book may have wanted to refute it, to enjoy its scandal, to collect it, or to learn what opponents said. Publishers printed Jesuit and Catholic refutations of Voltaire as well as Voltaire. Records of sales show what people ordered, not what they believed. Historians infer attitudes from marginalia, letters, diaries, or later conduct, always cautiously.

A printed work passed through several hands before it reached a reader. A bookseller might negotiate the manuscript and obtain a privilege, a printer would estimate the number of sheets and the cost of type and paper, compositors would set the text, correctors would check proofs, and binders might prepare volumes only after a buyer ordered them. Illustrations required further specialists and could make an edition prohibitively expensive. Delivery was not immediate. Books traveled in bales alongside ordinary goods through rivers, roads, and ports; damp, customs examinations, seized shipments, and unpaid bills could destroy anticipated profits. Long-distance trade relied on trusted agents who knew local demand and legal restrictions. An author might therefore select a foreign publisher not only to avoid a censor but to obtain reliable distribution or better production quality.

Translation involved another sequence of choices. A translator might abbreviate passages, add explanations for readers unfamiliar with an institution, soften material likely to offend authorities, or adapt currency, measurements, and cultural references. Advertisements sometimes called a book a translation when it was closer to a selective adaptation. Reviews and extracts circulated even more freely than full books. A German-speaking reader might first encounter a French argument through a journal's summary rather than through the original volume. This layering of mediation matters: the international movement of an idea cannot be reconstructed solely by charting where an author traveled or which language appeared on a title page. A book's reception also depended on the version that was actually available.

Price divided audiences. A multi-volume folio might be purchased by wealthy collectors, institutional libraries, or clubs that pooled subscriptions. Less expensive pamphlets, octavo editions, and reprints traveled through a different economy. Publishers combined prestigious works with devotional literature, calendars, school texts, novels, and practical manuals because those commodities paid the bills. The trade was not an autonomous engine of emancipation. It was responsive to buyers, increasingly alert to novelty, and capable of disseminating conservative polemic just as effectively as arguments for reform. Its historical importance lies in the commercial organization of exchange, not in any predetermined politics of print.

Historical Vocabulary
Publishing Privilege, Censorship, and Clandestine Literature

A privilege was an exclusive right, granted by a ruler or authority, to print or sell a work for a set time. Privileges protected investment and also served as a form of licensing, since they implied official approval. Censorship covered a range of practices, including prior examination by appointed censors, ecclesiastical indexes of prohibited works, post-publication prosecution, and seizure of copies. Clandestine literature refers to works printed or circulated without permission, often with false imprints, pseudonyms, or in manuscript. The modern assumption that censorship simply blocked ideas is misleading: authorities negotiated, tolerated, and punished unevenly, and prohibitions sometimes increased demand.

Section 82.4

Literacy, Reading Practices, and the Expansion of Intellectual Audiences

The audience for Enlightenment writing was larger than the circle of scholars but far smaller than the European population. Literacy varied sharply by region, gender, religion, and social position. In general, northern and western Protestant regions had higher literacy than southern and eastern Catholic and Orthodox ones, though the pattern was not simple. Scotland, Sweden, and parts of Germany combined church or state promotion of reading with comparatively high rates, and in Sweden household catechetical examination by the church encouraged reading even where writing was uncommon. In France, the surveys of signature literacy associated with Louis Maggiolo suggest that the share of men able to sign their names on marriage registers rose from roughly three in ten at the end of the seventeenth century to nearly half on the eve of the Revolution, while for women it rose from about one in seven to about one in four. These are approximations based on a proxy measure, and they conceal strong contrasts between a more literate north and east and a less literate south and west. Signing a name also measured writing more than reading, and many people could read who could not write.

Reading itself was varied. A once influential thesis suggested that Europeans moved from "intensive" reading, repeated study of a few sacred texts, to "extensive" reading of many different works in the eighteenth century. Historians now regard that formula as too neat, since people continued to read devotional works repeatedly while also consuming novels, newspapers, and travel accounts. What changed was variety, the commercial supply of new titles, and the growth of reading as a social habit. Novels such as Samuel Richardson's Pamela (1740) and Rousseau's Julie, or the New Heloise (1761) found enormous audiences, and contemporary moralists worried about the "reading mania" they saw spreading among women and the young. Collective reading, aloud in a household, a workshop, or a tavern, meant that one purchased copy could reach many listeners.

New institutions lowered the cost of reading. Subscription publishing, in which authors or publishers collected pledges and printed lists of subscribers' names, financed expensive works and also displayed networks of patronage, so that a subscriber list is both a financial record and a social map. Commercial circulating libraries, which flourished in Britain from the 1740s, charged fees to borrow, and in German lands and elsewhere reading societies (Lesegesellschaften) pooled subscriptions to journals and books, some counting hundreds of members. In Paris and provincial towns, cabinets de lecture allowed paying customers to read periodicals and books on site. Private libraries of nobles, clergy, lawyers, officials, and wealthy merchants were substantial, and inventories show a wide range, from a handful of devotional volumes to thousands of titles. Newspapers, pamphlets, and almanacs reached larger numbers than books.

The social range of readers was widening, but the limits were substantial. Educated elites remained the core of the audience for philosophical and critical works. Merchants, officials, professionals, and some artisans formed a second tier. Women read, and in salons and families often shaped taste, though their schooling was usually narrower than men's, and some genres were considered improper for them. Rural populations, still most Europeans, had far less access to books and to leisure. Serfs in the Russian and Habsburg lands, and the poor generally, rarely owned books. In Catholic regions the Church controlled access to vernacular scripture and promoted devotional literature; in Protestant regions, Bibles and catechisms were more widely owned. Languages mattered: a Hungarian peasant, a Portuguese weaver, and a Scottish ploughman did not read the same texts in the same tongue.

For these reasons historians resist the claim that the Enlightenment was a mass movement. It reached a public in the sense of a communicating audience, but a public of readers and talkers, comparatively well off and urban, gradually larger than before. The next sections show how that audience met in rooms and societies as well as in print.

Counting readers is harder than counting surviving books. In a probate inventory, the presence of three books might indicate ownership without proving their contents were read, while the absence of a book does not establish illiteracy: volumes could be borrowed, inherited by someone else, read at work, or shared among neighbors. Signatures in marriage registers are a further imperfect measure. Someone able to sign might not read a lengthy treatise, and someone unable to sign could listen to a newspaper read aloud. The statistical pattern is nevertheless useful if the categories and regional variation are retained. Local schools, religious teaching, occupation, and the affordability of print produced very different reading opportunities across France, Britain, the German lands, and the Baltic. A single continental literacy rate would conceal precisely the distinctions historians need to understand.

The act of reading also occurred within social relationships. In a workshop an apprentice might hear the master read from a newspaper during a break; a household could share a devotional book; a provincial notable might host a circle of neighbors who discussed a magazine; an urban servant might use borrowed fiction in private. Oral discussion was not an inferior substitute for print but one of the means by which printed ideas acquired meaning. News was repeated, disputed, and modified as it moved from print to conversation. This is why the growth of a reading public should not be imagined as the replacement of an oral society by a purely literate one. Printed texts and spoken exchange reinforced each other, often through people who could not be counted as subscribers or purchasers.

Gender and age affected access in ways not captured by a book catalogue. Girls and boys did not consistently receive the same education; some women learned to read without having comparable opportunities to study Latin or attend university. Yet women could own circulating-library subscriptions, act as booksellers, serve as translators, and shape the books bought for children. The unequal geography of literacy shaped the geography of the Enlightenment's audiences, and the audiences in turn influenced which genres publishers promoted.

Everyday Life and Social History

Who Read, and How? Reading in Ordinary Households

Most evidence about reading comes from the educated, but a few sources let historians glimpse artisans and tradespeople. The Paris glazier Jacques-Louis Ménétra (born 1738) left a memoir, not meant for publication, that describes his working life, his travels as a journeyman, his tavern companions, and his claims of meeting famous writers. Historians treat his book cautiously, since he wrote it long after the events and the encounters he reports cannot always be confirmed, but it shows that a skilled craftsman could read, write, and move in a world where printed matter was part of daily life. Inventories made after death offer another source: they show that many urban households owned a few books, chiefly religious, while a minority held collections that included travel, history, and novels.

Reading was embedded in work and sociability. A newspaper might be read aloud in a tavern, passed between households, or shared in a reading society. Women often read novels and devotional works at home, in lending libraries, or in groups, and in many households a literate member read to others. Rural reading often meant almanacs, devotional texts, and chapbooks sold by peddlers, rather than the books named in this chapter. The expansion of reading was real, but it did not simply carry Enlightenment ideas into every home. It also carried saints' lives, practical advice, and entertainment.

Section 82.5

Salons, Sociability, Women, and the Culture of Conversation

The salon was a regular gathering, held in a private house under the direction of a host, usually a woman, at which invited guests conversed, read aloud, and discussed new works. Its model was the polite society of seventeenth century Paris, and in the eighteenth century salons multiplied in the capital and spread to other cities. The most famous belonged to women of means. Marie Thérèse Geoffrin (1699 to 1777), a wealthy merchant's widow, hosted dinners for artists on Mondays and for writers and scholars on Wednesdays, and supported the Encyclopédie financially in its early years. Marie de Vichy-Chamrond, marquise du Deffand (1696 to 1780), hosted a salon for decades and, after losing her sight, maintained a celebrated correspondence with Voltaire and the English writer Horace Walpole. Julie de Lespinasse (1732 to 1776), who had worked as du Deffand's companion, broke with her in 1764 and set up her own gathering, closely associated with d'Alembert and a group of writers and scientists. Others, such as Suzanne Necker, wife of a Genevan banker and royal minister, hosted salons in later decades.

A salon had a function. It was a place where a writer's work could be read aloud, tested on an audience, praised or damaged, and made known to people with influence. Hosts introduced newcomers, brought together nobles, officials, clergy, artists, and writers who might otherwise not meet, and dispensed favor. Their judgments helped determine who obtained pensions, academy seats, and commissions. Foreign visitors, among them David Hume, Benjamin Franklin, Edward Gibbon, and the Neapolitan diplomat and economist Ferdinando Galiani, treated attendance at Parisian salons as part of an intellectual education. Salon sociability emphasized wit, politeness, and conversation, and rewarded those who could talk well, which gave educated women a significant influence over reputations.

Salons were not formal philosophical institutions, and historians caution against treating them as such. The gatherings varied widely. Some were literary, some artistic, some political, some mainly social. Hosts differed in aims and temperament, and guests came for connections, entertainment, or ambition. The baron d'Holbach's table, where a circle of men including Diderot dined and argued freely, was a different sort of gathering. Etiquette set limits: who was invited, who spoke, and what topics could be pursued depended on rank and the host's judgment. The salon could also be exclusionary. Writers of modest origins depended on invitations, and even celebrated women hosts needed to protect their reputations, since the same social world that gave them influence criticized women who sought public fame. Rousseau, whose Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater (1758) criticized the sociable, theatrical culture of the capital, claimed that women's influence made men effeminate and insincere. Others, including women writers, replied or ignored him.

Elsewhere in Europe, similar sociable circles took local forms. In Britain, the "bluestocking" circle around Elizabeth Montagu and her friends brought women and men together for conversation and learning from the 1750s. In Italy, conversazioni in Venice, Milan, and Naples served comparable ends, and in Spain, tertulias brought together reformers. Berlin's celebrated Jewish salons, hosted by educated women such as Henriette Herz, began in the 1780s and are discussed in Section 82.18. Warsaw's "Thursday dinners," held by King Stanisław August Poniatowski from the 1770s, gathered writers and officials. In all these settings hosts and guests were sorting themselves into networks of acquaintance and obligation.

Historians disagree about the salon's significance. Dena Goodman has argued that salons were institutions of the republic of letters in which women's management of conversation civilized and organized intellectual exchange. Joan Landes argued instead that the shift of political legitimacy to a masculine public sphere marginalized women, and Antoine Lilti has emphasized the mondain, social world of salons, which he saw as a culture of celebrity and sociability closer to fashion than to organized philosophy. These interpretations are not simply opposed. They show that the same gatherings could be intellectually productive, socially exclusive, and a source of reputation as much as ideas.

Salons were social events rather than permanent institutions with uniform rules. Hosts decided whom to invite, when to convene gatherings, and how to manage disagreements among people whose interests and statuses differed. A successful evening might combine music, theater, literature, scientific curiosity, conversation, and patronage. A celebrated guest's remarks could later be repeated in letters or memoirs, but those accounts were written for particular readers and might exaggerate the host's authority or the wit of the participants. The historian must therefore distinguish documented invitations, attendance, and correspondence from retrospective tales that turn every gathering into a center of philosophical strategy. Not every salon was primarily devoted to reform, nor did all Parisian hosts welcome the same writers.

The contrast between informal influence and formal office is essential. A host could make introductions, protect a writer's reputation, arrange employment, or influence which discussion became fashionable, yet she generally could not vote in an academy or award a university degree. Her authority depended on resources and relationships that could be disrupted by scandal, bereavement, or a shift in patronage. The celebrated cases do not demonstrate equal social standing for women. They show how some women exercised considerable cultural agency through forms of sociability that differed from the corporate institutions dominated by men. This also cautions against assuming that literary prominence translated into political power.

Some gatherings crossed social divisions while reproducing others. A titled aristocrat might converse with a professional writer or a foreign diplomat in the same room, and guests could describe the encounter as a community of talent. Servants provided food, heating, and preparation; their labor rarely entered the flattering accounts. Provincial salons, private households, and Jewish sociability in German-speaking cities operated under different constraints than the most famous Parisian gatherings. The salon should be analyzed as one important technique for connecting strangers and organizing attention, not as the sole or universal birthplace of the Enlightenment.

Section 82.6

Coffeehouses, Reading Rooms, Clubs, and Associational Life

If the salon was private and hierarchical, the coffeehouse was commercial and open to anyone who could pay. Coffee, a colonial and imported commodity whose trade is discussed in Chapter 73, reached Europe through Ottoman and Mediterranean commerce in the seventeenth century. The first English coffeehouses appeared in Oxford and London in the 1650s, and by around 1700 London had several hundred, with others in Amsterdam, Vienna, Paris, Venice, and later German cities. The Café Procope in Paris dates from 1686. Customers read newspapers and pamphlets provided on the premises, conducted business, talked politics and literature, and heard news. Some establishments specialized: Lloyd's coffeehouse, where merchants and shipowners gathered, grew into a center of marine insurance, and others attracted lawyers, clergy, scientists, or writers. Coffeehouses were also sometimes seen by governments as dangerous places of sedition, and attempts to suppress them in the seventeenth century failed.

These spaces were open, but openness was limited. Women worked in them as proprietors and servers but were seldom customers in London, though practice differed elsewhere. Social rank affected who was welcome, and the poor or enslaved were generally not. Regulars could dominate conversation. In central Europe, the coffeehouse culture developed later and with distinct patterns, and in the Ottoman world the coffeehouse had its own older history, which European visitors often misread. Taverns, ale houses, and inns were also sites of political and literary talk, and they reached lower social ranks than coffeehouses, so that historians stress the variety of communicative spaces instead of a single model.

Clubs and societies proliferated. In Edinburgh, the Select Society (founded 1754) brought together judges, clergy, professors, merchants, and nobles, including David Hume and Adam Smith, for debate on questions of improvement. London's Literary Club, founded in 1764 by Joshua Reynolds and Samuel Johnson, gathered writers and artists. Provincial gentlemen's societies, philosophical societies, debating societies, book clubs, and reading societies spread across Britain, the German lands, and elsewhere. Birmingham's Lunar Society, which flourished from the 1760s, joined manufacturers, scientists, and physicians, among them Josiah Wedgwood, Erasmus Darwin, and Joseph Priestley. Some London debating societies in the 1770s admitted women as speakers. Such associations offered a setting for discussion that was voluntary, formal in procedure, and often insulated from direct supervision.

Freemasonry deserves special mention. The Grand Lodge of England was formed in London in 1717, and James Anderson's Constitutions (1723) set out regulations and a moral mythology. Lodges spread rapidly across France, the German lands, Italy, the Dutch Republic, Russia, and the Atlantic colonies. Members of different ranks and religions, though usually not women, met in ritual settings with rules of mutual respect, mixing nobles, officers, merchants, officials, and intellectuals. Rome condemned Freemasonry in 1738, but it attracted many Catholics regardless. Historians caution against viewing lodges as engines of radicalism. Most were convivial, charitable, and conventional in politics, and some embraced hierarchy and esoteric ritual rather than rationalism. The Bavarian Illuminati, founded in 1776 by Adam Weishaupt and suppressed in the 1780s, were an exceptional, secretive, and short lived society, and their later reputation for conspiracy owed more to opponents' fears than to their size.

The relationship between commerce and discussion was especially visible in coffeehouses where newspapers, commercial intelligence, shipping reports, and literary controversy were available together. A customer's reason for entering might be to meet a client, inquire about a cargo, or discover a political rumor rather than discuss philosophy. Nevertheless, repeated encounters encouraged the formation of audiences who knew which writers and journals other customers followed. Publishers could use such settings to publicize new books, and groups might organize subscriptions through conversations that began around shared tables. This overlap between business and discourse made the coffeehouse historically significant without turning every cup of coffee into a political act.

Institutions also differed in their expectations of privacy. A public lecture might be advertised broadly but require payment; a club could demand election by existing members; a lodge might admit members only after ritual initiation; a reading society might pool money to purchase books that individuals could not afford. The rules shaped the conversation. Formal minutes and membership rolls provide evidence about who joined a club, but they cannot reveal every informal exchange or disagreement. By contrast, police observations of taverns and coffeehouses preserve complaints and rumors, though surveillance records are biased toward behavior officials considered threatening. Combining these sources yields a more differentiated picture of associational life than either celebration of open debate or accounts of constant repression.

The popularity of learned and sociable associations should not be confused with consensus. Some societies promoted civic improvement or local philanthropy; others were chiefly convivial, commercial, or religious. A group could welcome a debate on public health while treating criticism of the reigning monarch as unacceptable. Historians therefore ask what discussion a given institution enabled, which subjects it discouraged, and what social relationships made continued participation possible. Those questions also distinguish relatively durable organizations from temporary crowds gathered around particular speakers.

Historical Vocabulary
Public Sphere, Reading Publics, and Associational Culture

The public sphere is a concept developed by the German philosopher Jürgen Habermas in 1962 to describe a space in which private people, as a public, argue about matters of common concern, independent of the state. Historians use it as an analytical tool, not as a label that automatically describes eighteenth century Europe. Reading publics refers to groups of readers who consumed and discussed the same works, and associational culture to the voluntary clubs, lodges, and societies in which people met. Critics note that Habermas's model underplays women, the working classes, and counterpublics, and that contemporaries did not use the term. It remains useful for asking who spoke, who listened, and how criticism became public.

Historical Debate

Did a Genuinely New Public Sphere Emerge in Eighteenth Century Europe?

The question: Did coffeehouses, salons, periodicals, and clubs together create a new kind of public, in which private individuals debated common concerns independently of the state, or did they largely extend older forms of sociability and patronage?

Interpretation A: A new critical public emerged. Habermas and many who followed him argued that from the late seventeenth century a bourgeois public sphere took shape in Britain, France, and German lands, grounded in print, voluntary association, and rational debate, and that it gradually claimed the right to judge government. In this view the rise of periodicals, reading societies, and coffeehouses marks a historical shift in how authority was challenged.
Interpretation B: Continuity, exclusion, and variety. Critics respond that the model idealizes rational debate, that older courts, churches, and corporations remained powerful, and that salons and clubs depended on rank and patronage. Feminist historians such as Joan Landes and Nancy Fraser, and scholars of popular politics, emphasize the exclusion of women and the poor and the existence of rival "counterpublics." Antoine Lilti and others argue that the notion of "the public" was itself invoked by contemporaries as a rhetorical authority rather than described as an existing body.

Evidence: Both sides use subscription and membership lists, press runs, police reports, diaries, and the language contemporaries used when they invoked "the public." They disagree about how far such evidence shows independent opinion rather than elite sociability.

Why it matters: If a critical public existed before 1789, then Enlightenment communication helps explain political change. If it did not, or if it was narrower than claimed, then the connection between the Enlightenment and later politics must be argued more carefully, a question taken up in Chapters 87 and 88.

Section 82.7

Academies, Learned Societies, Competitions, and Intellectual Prestige

Where salons and coffeehouses offered informal sociability, academies offered institutional authority. The Académie française (1635) regulated the French language and literature, the Royal Society of London (1660) and the Paris Académie royale des sciences (1666) institutionalized natural philosophy, the Berlin Academy (1700, reorganized in 1744 under Frederick II) and the Saint Petersburg Academy (1724) drew foreign scholars into state service, and the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences (1739), the Royal Danish Academy (1742), the Institute of Sciences in Bologna (1714), and many others followed. France alone had dozens of provincial academies by 1789, at Dijon, Lyon, Bordeaux, Marseille, and many smaller cities. Spain founded royal academies of language (1713) and history (1738). These bodies selected members, published proceedings, offered prizes, advised rulers, and awarded recognition that was valuable for patronage.

Academies provided prestige and constraint at the same time. Membership could bring salaries, access to libraries and instruments, and reputation. It could also require loyalty to the ruler, restrict who could join, and impose standards of decorum. Elections were political: the Académie française included clergy and aristocrats alongside writers, and its choices were negotiated in salons and at court. Voltaire entered in 1746 and d'Alembert in 1754, while Diderot was never elected. Women were excluded from the major academies, a point examined in Section 82.18. Academies also quarrelled. At Berlin, the president Pierre Louis Maupertuis became embroiled in a dispute over priority concerning the principle of least action, which drew in Leonhard Euler and the librarian Samuel König, and Voltaire, then resident in Potsdam, mocked Maupertuis in a satire in 1752, with consequences for his own stay at Frederick's court. The episode shows that intellectual authority was contested and tied to royal favor.

Prize competitions, in which an academy posed a question and awarded a medal for the best essay, were a distinctive feature of Enlightenment culture. Questions ranged from natural science and engineering to agriculture, education, history, and morals, and the essays submitted, with the winners and often the losers published, created a forum open, in principle, to anyone able to write a competent response. Competitions spread debates beyond the academy, rewarded participants outside the capital, and sometimes produced works of lasting influence. Émilie du Châtelet submitted a study on fire to the Paris academy's competition in 1738, though it did not win, and it was printed anyway.

The most famous example is Rousseau's. In 1749 the Academy of Dijon, a provincial body established through a private bequest and active from 1740, announced a question concerning whether the revival of the sciences and arts had improved morals. Rousseau, a Genevan then living in Paris and writing for the Encyclopédie on music, responded in the negative, arguing that cultivated learning and refinement corrupted virtue. The essay won the prize in 1750 and was soon printed, making his reputation. It drew published replies, among them a reply associated with the exiled king of Poland and duke of Lorraine, Stanisław Leszczyński, to which Rousseau responded in turn. The case shows that an intellectual challenge to the culture of learning could arise within one of its institutions, and that provincial academies offered a stage on which an outsider might be heard.

An academy's influence extended beyond its meeting room. Winning a prize could supply the title page of a book with a credential that helped it sell; a successful report might lead to a government consultation; the publication of a memoir could establish priority for a discovery. Secretaries arranged correspondence and selected material for proceedings, acting as editors whose decisions determined which voices traveled beyond their city. In return, rulers could expect useful advice about roads, manufactures, agriculture, sanitation, or mapping. The resulting relationship was neither simple independence nor passive service. Scholars sometimes used official sponsorship to pursue questions that patrons had not anticipated, and governments could ignore the recommendations of their own advisers.

The competition system also creates an archival problem. Prize winners are easy to locate because institutions celebrated them, whereas unsuccessful essays may survive unevenly or not at all. The most famous published response can give a distorted impression of what the academy actually asked and how other entrants understood the problem. Rousseau's Dijon success is instructive not because academies routinely rewarded attacks on learning but because the form of the question opened a space for an unexpected answer. Students comparing the prize question, Rousseau's argument, and later replies can see how an institution generated a controversy that escaped its control.

Academies had varied regional functions. A provincial French society could provide a local elite with a forum independent of Paris while still seeking royal recognition. A German academy might operate under the financial uncertainty of a small court. A Scandinavian scientific association could promote botanical or economic knowledge valuable to merchants and state administrators. These differences caution against treating an academy as a uniformly progressive organization or against assuming that salaried membership always produced more freedom than voluntary association.

Section 82.8

Correspondence, Postal Networks, Patronage, and the International Republic of Letters

Letters were the infrastructure of the republic. Scholars who never met exchanged opinions, drafts, observations, books, and favors by post. Voltaire's surviving correspondence runs to well over fifteen thousand letters, Leibniz exchanged letters with over a thousand people, and Diderot wrote to Sophie Volland, to Catherine II's circle, and to scores of others. David Hume and Adam Smith wrote to one another across Edinburgh, Glasgow, and London, and Benjamin Franklin's letters connected Philadelphia, London, and Paris. The sheer volume shows that correspondence was a form of publication in itself, since letters were copied, read aloud, circulated, and sometimes printed.

Letters moved through postal systems that varied by territory. The imperial post of the Thurn und Taxis family served much of the Holy Roman Empire, national posts connected France, Britain, and others, and Paris introduced a city post in 1760. Postage was expensive, usually paid by the recipient, and a long letter could cost a significant sum, which privileged the wealthy and those with official franking privileges. Diplomatic pouches, merchant houses, travelers, and consuls carried letters outside the regular post, giving mobile intermediaries an essential role. Authorities read mail: France maintained a secret office, the cabinet noir, which opened correspondence, and other states did the same, so writers learned to use codes, couriers, and allusions.

Correspondence worked within patronage. Scholars wrote to nobles and rulers to seek pensions, dedications, introductions, or protection, and rulers wrote back to enhance their reputations. Voltaire corresponded with Frederick II of Prussia, whom he first knew as crown prince, and with Catherine II of Russia, and she corresponded with Diderot and others. Manuscript newsletters also carried intellectual news. The Correspondance littéraire, compiled from the 1750s by Friedrich Melchior Grimm and later others, including Diderot, circulated in manuscript to a handful of subscribers, among them Catherine II, Gustav III of Sweden, and several German princes. Because it was handwritten and sent privately, it escaped the censorship that governed print and could speak more freely.

Language shaped networks. Latin lingered in scientific exchange, French dominated polite correspondence, German grew in importance, and English gained ground gradually. Translators and secretaries were needed whenever correspondents did not share a language. The republic's reach was uneven. Correspondents were heavily concentrated in western and central Europe, and fewer letters connected Iberia, southeastern Europe, and Russia to the main networks, though that partly reflects archival survival. Digital projects such as Stanford's Mapping the Republic of Letters and the Electronic Enlightenment edition have charted thousands of surviving letters, and their maps show the density of links among Paris, London, Geneva, Amsterdam, and Berlin. But such maps record what survives and what has been catalogued, and they omit conversations and the many letters lost.

Correspondence was vulnerable. War interrupted mail and made foreign correspondents suspicious, as in the Seven Years' War examined in Chapter 80. Cost limited the poor. Personal quarrels ended exchanges, and political change could turn a patron into a danger. Letters could be intercepted or used as evidence. A network of letters is thus not a simple picture of intellectual unity: it records collaboration and also rank, interest, and rivalry.

A surviving correspondence collection does not present a neutral image of intellectual Europe. Wealthy individuals could afford to employ secretaries, preserve copies, and bind letters, while households might discard the correspondence of servants or relatives considered unimportant. A letter copied into a published edition could lose details about its route and audience. Editors sometimes cut sensitive passages, regularized spelling, or arranged letters to create a coherent portrait of an admired thinker. The same message might be written differently for a trusted friend, a patron, and a possible future reader. Historical reconstruction therefore requires attention to the material letter: hand, seal, address, date, annotations, and whether the manuscript is an original or a later copy.

Correspondence also mobilized resources, not merely arguments. A scholar might ask a colleague to inspect an unfamiliar book in a foreign library, correct a quotation, obtain a plant specimen, arrange a subscription, or introduce a student to a suitable household. Some letters operated as letters of recommendation, creating chains of credibility by which strangers could cooperate. Postal delays could turn a polite disagreement into a dispute over priority because two writers published without knowing what the other had sent. The same arrangements made transregional projects possible and created occasions for rivalry and misunderstanding.

The movement of letters across imperial and linguistic boundaries complicates the image of a closed western European network. Diplomats and merchants connected ports, courts, universities, and religious institutions across the Mediterranean and the Baltic; not all their exchanges concerned familiar Enlightenment topics. Hebrew, Greek, Italian, and German carried discussions into circuits that a map of elite French-language letters alone would miss. A defensible account must therefore combine intensely documented collections with less complete networks, stating the limits of evidence instead of mistaking gaps in digitized archives for intellectual silence.

Section 82.9

Periodicals, Journalism, Satire, and the New Culture of Criticism

Periodicals changed how ideas were discussed. A book is a finished product, while a periodical is a continuing conversation, with readers, rival editors, and correspondents responding from issue to issue. Learned journals, beginning with the Journal des sçavans, reviewed books and reported discoveries. The Mercure de France combined literary news with court gossip, the Gentleman's Magazine (founded in London in 1731 by Edward Cave) collected extracts from other journals and gave middle class readers a digest of news, verse, and debate, and the Göttingische gelehrte Anzeigen (from 1739) reviewed learned literature on the basis of the university's large library. Weeklies and dailies spread more broadly. In the Berlin Berlinische Monatsschrift (1783 to 1796), editors posed questions, as the opening of this chapter shows, and invited response.

The moral weekly, modeled on Addison and Steele's Spectator, traveled across Europe. Justus van Effen produced De Hollandsche Spectator in the Dutch Republic from 1731. In German lands, moralische Wochenschriften began in Hamburg with Der Vernünftler in 1713, and Johann Christoph Gottsched's Leipzig weekly for women readers, Die vernünftigen Tadlerinnen, appeared in 1725. Olof von Dalin's Then Swänska Argus began in Stockholm in 1732, Spanish writers published El Pensador in the early 1760s, Milan's Il Caffè appeared from 1764, and in Warsaw the Monitor began in 1765. Each adapted the form to local concerns. They aimed to reform manners, promote reading and education, and to criticize superstition, extravagance, and pedantry, often with humor. Collected into volumes, they shaped a shared, pan-European literary form, and a woman reader in Hamburg or a clerk in Stockholm met the same type of essay as a Londoner.

"Criticism" in this period spanned several meanings. It could mean literary and artistic judgment, the review of books, the philological critique of texts, or the evaluation of institutions and beliefs. Periodicals blended these. A journal reviewing a new play, a travel account, or a treatise on commerce taught readers to assess claims rather than to accept them. Satire was a particularly effective vehicle because indirect speech could evade the censor. Montesquieu's Persian Letters (1721), published anonymously in Amsterdam, examined French society through the eyes of two fictional Persian travelers. Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726) mocked politics and science through fantasy. Voltaire's Candide (1759), a brief tale that ridiculed optimistic philosophy against a background including the Lisbon earthquake of 1755, became one of the best selling works of the century and was condemned in several places.

Voltaire was the age's most skillful public polemicist. Born François Marie Arouet, he published under a pseudonym, was twice imprisoned in the Bastille, spent time in England from 1726 to 1728 after a dispute with a nobleman, and thereafter understood how to exploit publication, patronage, and exile. His Philosophical Letters, printed in English in London in 1733 as Letters Concerning the English Nation and in French in 1734 in Rouen, compared English religion, science, commerce, and politics with French practice. The French edition was seized and condemned by the Parlement of Paris in 1734 and Voltaire took refuge at Cirey, the estate of his companion Émilie du Châtelet. In later decades he wrote hundreds of pamphlets, often anonymously, and used his campaign on behalf of the Protestant Calas family, discussed in Section 82.11, to model the writer as public critic of injustice.

The distinctive feature of serial publication was its capacity to make correction and disagreement part of an ongoing enterprise. A journal could publish an essay, receive an objection, print a response, and revive the controversy when a new book appeared. This mattered for the development of intellectual reputation. Readers learned to recognize recurring pseudonyms, editorial voices, and the positions taken by competing titles. Editors cultivated communities of expectation, even when buyers had little opportunity to write back. An article that was first read for entertainment might later be collected in a volume, translated, or cited in a legal or religious controversy. Periodical writing existed between fleeting news and durable books.

Satire complicated this process because the distance between author and narrator was rarely transparent. A fictional traveler could praise a custom in a way that invited readers to see its absurdity, but different readers might take the same passage literally. Religious and political authorities could also exploit ambiguity, accusing a writer of meanings the writer denied. Where overt condemnation invited prosecution, allusion and irony could let a critique circulate without naming its target. These techniques were not infallible protections, and accusations of indecency or impiety could attach even to apparently playful works. The historical evidence is strongest when a literary analysis of the printed text is combined with records of how authorities and readers responded.

Periodicals also transmitted disagreements over language, manners, and taste. Debates about theater, poetry, fashion, and education might appear remote from the great questions of sovereignty, but they trained audiences to evaluate claims publicly. The activity of giving reasons, responding to objections, and invoking examples from other societies could change habits of discussion without producing agreement on an ideology. Literary criticism and public controversy were thus integral to the Enlightenment's social form rather than decorations around its supposedly more serious philosophical core.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of a Public Culture of Criticism

Long-term causes

Humanist and scientific traditions of textual and empirical criticism; confessional division that encouraged comparison of doctrines; commercial printing and learned journals established since the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Short-term causes

Huguenot dispersal after 1685; relaxation of licensing in England after 1695; the growth of cities, coffeehouses, and reading audiences; cheap periodicals and reprints in the early eighteenth century.

Event or transformation

The movement of criticism from specialist scholarship into journals, essays, satire, and pamphlets read by a wider educated public and applied to manners, religion, institutions, and government.

Immediate effects

New forms of writing, such as the moral weekly; sustained controversies conducted in print; new vulnerabilities, since authors were prosecuted, imprisoned, or exiled for publication.

Long-term consequences

Expectation that public policy and belief could be debated in print; later states and churches sought new methods of managing opinion; modern journalism and literary criticism developed from these forms.

Historical complexity

Print did not by itself produce criticism: the same media carried devotional, conservative, and sentimental writing, and critics worked within hierarchies of patronage, so that the culture of criticism was shaped as much by constraint as by freedom.
Section 82.10

Diderot, d'Alembert, and the Encyclopédie

The Encyclopédie, or Reasoned Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts, and Trades was the century's largest collaborative project and the clearest example of how commerce, scholarship, and politics intertwined. It began as a commercial venture. In the 1740s a group of Paris booksellers led by André le Breton planned a French edition of Ephraim Chambers's English Cyclopaedia (1728). After the first arrangement with translators collapsed, the booksellers turned in 1747 to Denis Diderot, a writer and translator of modest means, and to the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert, a member of the Académie des sciences. A prospectus appeared in 1750, and the first volume in 1751. Under Diderot and d'Alembert the project outgrew a translation and became an original attempt to organize and reevaluate knowledge.

The work was also an unprecedented financial and organizational undertaking. By 1772 the original enterprise had produced 17 volumes of text and 11 volumes of illustrative plates, with a later supplement and index brought out by the publisher Charles-Joseph Panckoucke in the late 1770s and 1780s. Roughly four thousand subscribers backed the first edition, and its price was very high. The contributors numbered over a hundred and forty named writers, among them the Chevalier de Jaucourt, who wrote a vast share of articles, physicians, clergy, naturalists, engineers, jurists, and men and women of letters. Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, Turgot, Quesnay, and d'Holbach contributed to different parts. Diderot himself spent decades on editing, writing, and commissioning, and he visited workshops to learn trades. Engravers and draftsmen, among them Robert Bénard, produced the plates, which showed craft processes and tools in detail. Craftsmen supplied information, though their names rarely appear, and some sections drew heavily on earlier published sources, including Chambers and the Académie des sciences's descriptions of the arts, which raised accusations of plagiarism.

The Encyclopédie aimed to gather knowledge scattered across the world and to present it in ordered form. Its "figurative system of human knowledge," derived loosely from Francis Bacon, organized knowledge under memory, reason, and imagination. Alphabetical arrangement mixed fields, and a dense system of cross-references (renvois) allowed editors to guide readers from one article to another, sometimes to produce ironic or critical effects. The work elevated the "mechanical arts," giving manufacturing and crafts the same dignity as learned subjects, and it treated religion, politics, and morality alongside natural science. Diderot's own article "Encyclopédie" described a cooperative effort to alter the common way of thinking, though students should consult the article itself for his exact wording.

Powerful opponents responded. Jesuit and Jansenist journalists attacked the project, and the Paris Parlement and the royal council acted against it. In early 1752, after the first two volumes appeared, the royal council ordered their suppression as harmful to royal authority and religion, though Malesherbes protected the project and the enterprise resumed. Volumes appeared until 1757, when d'Alembert's article "Geneva" provoked a storm, and d'Alembert withdrew from the editorship in 1758. In 1759, after the scandal surrounding Helvétius's On the Mind, the council revoked the work's privilege, the Parlement condemned it, and Pope Clement XIII placed it on the Index of Forbidden Books. Remarkably, work continued in secret. The remaining ten volumes of text appeared together in 1765 with a false Swiss imprint, and the plates were issued in installments through 1772. Diderot learned in 1764 that Le Breton had secretly cut and altered articles in proof to avoid trouble, a discovery that shocked him and shows how commercial prudence shaped the published text.

The Encyclopédie was reprinted, pirated, and revised in several editions in Lucca, Livorno, Geneva, Neuchâtel, Yverdon, and elsewhere, and the cheaper quarto and octavo editions of the 1770s reached many more buyers, which meant that a work conceived in Paris was commercially redistributed across Europe. Its reception varied. Some readers valued its practical information and plates, some its critical articles, others used it as a reference without regard to its politics. It also had critics who saw it as a conspiracy against religion, a charge the editors rejected, and the satirist Charles Palissot mocked its authors in a play of 1760. The project demonstrates the interplay of private capital, censorship, collaborative scholarship, and the deliberate cultivation of a public.

The original project's economics show why an encyclopedia could be both a daring intellectual enterprise and a conservative commercial investment. Subscribers expected an expensive series to appear in a usable form, publishers had capital tied up in unfinished volumes, and contributors needed copy edited and printed. After suppression threatened the venture, secrecy protected the completion of the remaining text but complicated the author's control over it. A careful student should distinguish the editorial intentions of Diderot and d'Alembert, the decisions of booksellers, and the various contributions of individual authors. No single sentence can speak for all of the work's articles. Contradictions across the alphabet were possible because the enterprise combined distinct writers and was issued over many years.

The plates are especially instructive. Their images of tools, workshops, machines, and sequences of operations organized practical activities into ordered stages that could be viewed without attending the workshop. They made technical labor worthy of learned attention, yet they also transformed a process performed by experienced workers into a representation mediated by draftsmen and engravers. The neatness of a plate should not be mistaken for a complete record of working conditions: wages, exhaustion, discipline, accidents, and disagreements among workers often remain out of view. The visual authority of precise engraving could coexist with the social invisibility of those whose knowledge made the illustration possible.

The publication history further demonstrates why a title's intellectual reputation cannot be inferred from the most famous Paris edition alone. Revised and less expensive editions appeared in markets with different regulations and readers, sometimes changing the organization of material. A person consulting a later version might encounter corrections or additions unavailable to an earlier subscriber. The dates matter: the seventeen original text volumes reached completion in 1765, while publication of eleven volumes of plates continued through 1772. Supplements and indexes belong to subsequent publishing enterprises, even when they appear together with the original in surviving library sets.

The viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians have identified. They are not quotations from named individuals.

A Paris bookseller and investor: The project is a very large, risky investment. Seizure or suppression threatens ruin, so he supports cautious cuts and protective friends in the book administration, and he values the subscribers whose advance payments make publication possible.
A royal official in the book administration: He must balance pressure from clergy, magistrates, and court factions against the usefulness of a major national project and the desire to preserve order. His discretion shelters the work while keeping it formally under suspicion.
A Jesuit or Jansenist critic: He sees in the work an attack on religion and a threat to social peace, and uses periodicals and the courts to denounce it, though the two religious parties also disliked one another.
A provincial subscriber: A lawyer or physician in a distant town, he values the work as a reference, a mark of learning, and a window on trades and sciences he could not otherwise follow, whether or not he shares the editors' views.
A craftsman whose trade is described: He may be asked to explain his skills, may resent revealing trade secrets, and will almost never be named. The work honors the mechanical arts in principle, but the artisan is more often source than reader or author.
Section 82.11

Religious Criticism, Toleration, Skepticism, and the Uses of Reason

Religion was the subject on which Enlightenment writing attracted the fiercest conflict, and the movement's attitude toward it varied widely. Some writers attacked intolerance, persecution, and what they called superstition. Others defended Christianity as rational and moral. Still others championed a minimal natural religion or denied the existence of God. Treating the Enlightenment as simply anti-religious distorts it, and treating it as simply religious ignores the radical critics. Chapter 86 will examine lived religion and secularization debates in the age; this section considers criticism and toleration as features of public debate.

A major strand criticized religious coercion. After the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes, Huguenot exiles publicized the persecution of Protestants in France. In 1762, in Toulouse, Jean Calas, a Protestant cloth merchant, was executed on the wheel after being convicted of killing his son, who the court alleged was about to convert to Catholicism. Voltaire took up the case, wrote pamphlets and letters, and published his Treatise on Tolerance in 1763, and in 1765 the verdict was overturned. The affair demonstrates how a writer used print and networks of correspondents to influence the judicial system. France finally granted civil status to Protestants in 1787. In the Habsburg lands, Joseph II issued toleration patents in the early 1780s, discussed in Chapter 84.

Distinguishing positions matters. Deists accepted a creator but rejected revealed religion or church authority. John Toland and Matthew Tindal in England argued for religion's rationality, and Voltaire, who despised clerical power, believed in a creator. Skeptics questioned claims to certain knowledge. David Hume's essay on miracles (1748) argued that testimony for miracles was less reliable than the experience that nature is regular, and his Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion were published only after his death in 1779, because he feared the response; Hume had earlier been denied university chairs in Edinburgh and Glasgow. Atheists were rare and often anonymous. The baron d'Holbach's System of Nature (1770) argued for materialism and atheism and appeared under another man's name, and the priest Jean Meslier, who died in 1729, left a manuscript denouncing religion that circulated clandestinely. Reformist believers were far more numerous. Many Enlightenment authors were clergy or devout laypeople: Anglican latitudinarians, Scottish Moderates, Lutheran and Reformed theologians in German universities, and the Catholic Enlightenment of Lodovico Antonio Muratori in Italy and of Jansenist and Benedictine reformers. Newton remained deeply religious. Enlightenment religion also coexisted with Pietism and Methodism, movements that stressed emotion and personal conversion and that were not opponents of enlightenment in any simple sense.

German debates provide a clear example. In the 1770s Lessing published excerpts from an unpublished work by Hermann Samuel Reimarus, a Hamburg teacher who had privately criticized the historical reliability of the Bible. The publication, called the Fragments, sparked controversy with orthodox Lutheran pastors, notably Johann Melchior Goeze in Hamburg. In 1778 the Duke of Brunswick ordered Lessing to stop publishing on theological matters. Lessing turned to the stage and in 1779 published Nathan the Wise, a drama about religious tolerance set in Jerusalem during the Crusades, which is the subject of the next source window.

Religious criticism became public through specific institutional channels. A pamphlet defending an accused family could circulate through booksellers, salons, and private letters; a clerical journal might publish a rebuttal; legal authorities might attempt to control the case by issuing official summaries. The Calas campaign therefore provides more than an example of Voltaire's personal influence. It shows the work required to turn an unfamiliar provincial prosecution into a controversy that readers across Europe considered relevant to themselves. The process depended on gathering testimony, choosing which parts of the record to publish, winning patrons, and sustaining attention over several years. Readers could be moved by concern for justice while disagreeing about theological principles. Public pressure and judicial reconsideration interacted, but pamphleteering alone did not constitute a modern legal appeal system.

Toleration had multiple meanings. A ruler might promise that a minority congregation would no longer be prosecuted while still denying its members access to office or equal public worship. A theologian might advocate liberty of conscience but expect state supervision of religious teaching. Others defended broader freedom of publication while fearing the effects of atheistic arguments among ordinary people. Those distinctions explain why writers could praise toleration in one country and criticize its limitations elsewhere. They also explain why the legal disabilities of Catholics in Britain or Jews in many states persisted even where enlightened conversation flourished. Arguments over conscience did not automatically produce civil equality.

Source criticism matters particularly in debates about irreligion. A hostile pamphlet could depict its opponent as an atheist without the opponent accepting that description. A manuscript circulated privately may have undergone copying and editing before publication; an anonymous printed book could attract speculative attributions. Historians distinguish what an author actually argued from what critics feared the argument implied. The public culture of religious controversy consequently demonstrates both the power of print to enlarge discussion and the limits of labels such as belief, skepticism, toleration, and unbelief.

Section 82.12

Censorship, Publishing Privileges, Underground Literature, and the Limits of Free Expression

No European state in the eighteenth century allowed unrestricted publication, but the systems differed sharply, and the differences shaped where books were printed and how they circulated. Censorship was multiple. The Catholic Church maintained the Index of Forbidden Books and the Inquisition, which in Spain and Portugal also worked with the crown. Civil authorities appointed censors, and in France the Paris Parlement, the Sorbonne's theology faculty, and the royal council each could condemn books. Authorities burned books publicly, imprisoned authors and booksellers, and in some cases executed offenders, though prosecution was often selective and negotiable. Writers such as Voltaire, Diderot, and Rousseau experienced prison or exile, and booksellers were arrested. Inspector Joseph d'Hémery's files on the Paris book trade in the 1750s record the police's detailed monitoring of authors and dealers.

Elsewhere, rules differed. Britain had no prior licensing after 1695 but prosecuted seditious libel, as in the 1763 case of John Wilkes. In the Dutch Republic, local magistrates and church bodies exercised authority unevenly, and diplomatic pressure sometimes led to action against printers. In Sweden, the Freedom of the Press Act of 1766, associated with the clergyman and writer Anders Chydenius, abolished prior censorship and is often described as the first such law, though it was later restricted. In Denmark-Norway, a decree of 1770 under the minister Johann Friedrich Struensee ended censorship, only to be reversed after his fall in 1772 and 1773. Joseph II relaxed Habsburg censorship in 1781 while retaining many controls, and Catherine II permitted private printing presses in 1783, but later governments tightened restrictions. In Portugal, Pombal's government established a royal censorship board in 1768 that took over much of the oversight formerly exercised by the church, a shift toward state control and not toward liberty.

Underground literature thrived under these regimes. The historian Robert Darnton's research in the archives of the Société typographique de Neuchâtel and in police records revealed a trade in "philosophical books," a category that included works by Voltaire, Rousseau, d'Holbach, and Raynal but also pornography, scandalous court libels, and satires of the French monarchy. Titles such as Louis Sébastien Mercier's utopia The Year 2440 (1771) and the erotic and philosophical Thérèse philosophe (1748) circulated widely. Smugglers took books across borders by river, road, and sea, often with the connivance of local officials. Anonymous publication, pseudonyms, false imprints, and manuscript copying allowed criticism to circulate, and prohibition could raise a book's price and its appeal.

Neither prohibition nor toleration alone determined what people read. Enforcement was uneven, depended on officials' attitudes and connections, and varied by province. A book banned in Paris could be tolerated in a provincial town, and a book formally permitted could be hard to find. Authors and printers calculated risk, sometimes accepting self-censorship. Readers learned to read between lines, and authorities occasionally used the system strategically, allowing some critical books to circulate to manage dissent. The result was a landscape of incomplete control, in which writers and publishers depended on protectors, foreign printing, and discretion more than on legal rights. Historians use confiscation records, police reports, publishers' correspondence, and prosecutions as evidence, though these sources capture mainly the cases authorities noticed.

Censorship commonly operated before and after publication. An official privilege might authorize a particular printer to produce a book, protecting commercial investment while certifying that an approved version had passed examination. A tolerated manuscript might receive informal permission that officials later denied having granted. Even after publication, a court or religious tribunal could order seizure or prosecution. These layers of authority sometimes conflicted with one another. A royal censor could approve what a theological faculty condemned, and a provincial magistrate might ignore an order issued in the capital. The result was uncertainty for authors and booksellers, not simply a binary division between permitted and forbidden texts. Anonymity, a false publishing address, or shipment from abroad reduced some risks while creating others.

Legal permission also has a chronology. Sweden's 1766 law was an exceptional statement of press liberty and access to public records, but changes in political authority narrowed its operation; Gustav III's 1772 coup was followed by a more restrictive ordinance in 1774. Denmark-Norway's abrupt liberalization in 1770 likewise produced an extraordinary burst of pamphleteering before political reaction imposed new controls. These reversals reveal that public discussion was contingent on state power, institutional interests, and the immediate political climate. A history that mentions only the reforming decrees would mistakenly describe an uninterrupted advance toward press freedom.

A publisher deciding whether to print a controversial work might calculate more than the formal penalty. Trade credit, the danger of losing a privilege on profitable schoolbooks, a patron's willingness to intervene, and the possibility of confiscation at the border all affected decisions. Some dealers carried prohibited books alongside innocuous titles; others specialized in low-risk educational works. The surviving correspondence of commercial houses documents these calculations more reliably than later myths of heroic smugglers. The historical achievement of clandestine print was not that it created unbounded freedom, but that the competing jurisdictions of European states prevented any single authority from controlling every route by which a text could circulate.

Section 82.13

Britain, Scotland, Ireland, and the Dutch Republic

Britain was both a source and a recipient of enlightened ideas. English writers, Bacon, Locke, Newton, and later popularizers and essayists, were admired on the continent, and Voltaire's letters made England a point of comparison. Yet English intellectual life had its own institutions and limits. Oxford and Cambridge were restricted to members of the Church of England, so Protestant Dissenters built their own academies, such as Warrington Academy in the 1750s, where Joseph Priestley taught. London was the center of publishing, coffeehouses, and clubs, with a commercial press that, as Section 82.3 showed, operated without prior licensing. Religion remained a boundary: Catholics and Dissenters faced legal disabilities, and blasphemy and sedition could be prosecuted. Enlightened discussion in England was often moderate, concerned with morals, manners, science, and improvement, and compatible with an established church.

Scotland's Enlightenment is the most studied regional case. After the Union of 1707, Scotland kept its own church, legal system, and universities, which gave it distinctive institutions. Universities at Glasgow, Edinburgh, Aberdeen, and St Andrews employed professors who lectured in English, taught moral philosophy and natural science, and published. Francis Hutcheson at Glasgow influenced Hume and Smith, Thomas Reid taught at Aberdeen, and Adam Smith held a Glasgow chair before leaving for private work and later official office. Clubs and societies structured conversation: the Select Society (1754), the Poker Club (1762), and the Philosophical Society, which became the Royal Society of Edinburgh in 1783. Edinburgh publishers produced the short-lived Edinburgh Review of 1755 and, between 1768 and 1771, the first edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica. David Hume, born in Berwickshire, wrote the Treatise of Human Nature (1739 to 1740), essays, and a six-volume History of England (1754 to 1762), and his literary success depended on London publishers as much as on Edinburgh friends. He served in the British embassy in Paris from 1763, was lionized in salons, and brought Rousseau to England in 1766, only to quarrel with him. Adam Ferguson, William Robertson, Smith, and others shared a concern with the history of society, commerce, and manners. This chapter reserves the substance of their economic and political theories for Chapters 83 and 85. The Scottish case shows how an Enlightenment could flourish within the structures of a Presbyterian church and a university system, and how Scottish clergy, the Moderates, joined learned society even as hostile critics objected to Hume's skepticism.

Ireland shows a different pattern. Dublin was a major printing center whose booksellers reprinted London titles for cheaper sale. The Dublin Society, founded in 1731, promoted agriculture, manufacture, and the arts, and the Royal Irish Academy followed in 1785. Writers such as Jonathan Swift and George Berkeley were widely read abroad. The penal laws, which restricted Catholics, who were a majority of the population, shaped access to education and to learned institutions, so that the intellectual public was largely Protestant. Irish Catholic scholars worked in continental colleges and in Irish language networks that left a different kind of evidence.

The Dutch Republic, a center in the previous century, remained crucial in the eighteenth. Its universities, particularly Leiden, trained physicians and natural philosophers, and Hermann Boerhaave's teaching drew students from across Europe. Its printers continued to publish French language works for export, including Rousseau's major books, which Marc-Michel Rey printed in Amsterdam, and many works unavailable elsewhere. Huguenot journalists and translators operated in The Hague, Amsterdam, and Rotterdam. The Dutch Republic's political economy, discussed in Chapter 77, had changed, and its relative economic decline in this century coincided with continuing influence as a publishing and intermediary center. Learned societies such as the Hollandsche Maatschappij der Wetenschappen at Haarlem (1752) and later reform societies promoted science and improvement, and Dutch moral weeklies and novels, including those by Betje Wolff and Aagje Deken, reached a local audience. The Dutch case also warns against equating enlightenment with secular liberalism: Reformed church authority remained strong.

The regional comparison becomes clearer when institutions rather than celebrated names are placed at the center. British writers could work within London print markets, universities, dissenting academies, professional societies, and metropolitan clubs, each with its own restrictions. In Scotland, a relatively compact learned community linked professors, lawyers, clergy, physicians, and merchants through repeated meetings and personal recommendations. Such connections facilitated the testing of arguments but could also make reputation fragile: a professor's criticism of a colleague might become a dispute within a small institutional world. The continued legal independence of Scottish church and education after union created conditions unlike those of England. It would be misleading either to regard Scotland as merely a northern outpost of London or to imagine it intellectually isolated from British and continental markets.

Scotland's engagement with history and social comparison was particularly consequential for the republic of letters. Narratives of commerce, manners, law, and historical development drew on ancient literature, travel reports, contemporary statistical observations, and conjectures about societies for which documentation was scarce. Writers debated whether wealth fostered virtue, whether commercial exchange softened manners, and how different systems of education shaped conduct. The central concern for this chapter is not the resolution of those arguments, but their circulation through lectures, manuscripts, club discussions, published books, and reviews. A work composed in Edinburgh could find its decisive publisher in London, its first prominent critics in another kingdom, and readers in French or German translation. The geography of intellectual production cannot be reduced to the address on a title page.

Ireland and the Dutch Republic display other patterns of participation. Dublin printers profited from reprinting books that circulated in Britain; the resulting trade made Dublin a center of supply rather than simply a place receiving English texts. Religious inequalities and the position of the Irish Catholic majority nevertheless constrained who entered prestigious educational institutions. In the Dutch Republic, local legal diversity and a longstanding foreign-language publishing trade made international exchange commercially important. Neither case should be described through an uncomplicated scale of freedom. The institutional routes available to a privileged scholar were not identical to those available to a dissenting writer, an artisan, or a minority religious reader. Comparative explanation must hold market opportunity and social exclusion in the same frame.

Section 82.14

The German Enlightenment: Aufklärung, Scholarship, and Public Debate

The German-speaking lands had no single capital. The Holy Roman Empire, examined in earlier chapters and again in Chapter 78, comprised hundreds of territories, from large states such as Prussia and Austria to free cities, ecclesiastical principalities, and tiny courts. This fragmentation shaped the Aufklärung: universities, courts, and cities each created its own intellectual environment, publishers had to work across many jurisdictions, and no one censor or patron controlled the whole. It also meant that religious plurality, Lutheran, Reformed, Catholic, and Jewish, was a daily condition rather than an abstraction, and that debates about toleration were part of ordinary politics.

Universities were prominent. Halle, where the Pietist August Hermann Francke built a major educational center and where the philosopher Christian Wolff taught, was a site of conflict: Wolff's rationalist philosophy and a lecture praising Chinese moral thought provoked Pietist opposition and Frederick William I of Prussia expelled him in 1723, until Frederick II recalled him in 1740. Göttingen, opened in 1737, built an exceptional library and a reputation for modern scholarship and published the influential Gelehrte Anzeigen. Königsberg's university, where Kant taught for decades, was far from the main cultural centers yet connected by the book trade and by the Baltic. In Leipzig, Gottsched promoted reform of German language and theater, and the Leipzig book fair provided distribution. Many Aufklärer were professors, pastors, and officials, so that a good part of the movement grew within church and state institutions.

Berlin became the focus of public debate. Friedrich Nicolai, a bookseller and writer, founded the Allgemeine deutsche Bibliothek in 1765, a review journal that surveyed German literature for decades. Nicolai, Lessing, and Moses Mendelssohn collaborated on the Letters on the Latest Literature from 1759. The Berlinische Monatsschrift (from 1783) and discussion groups such as the Wednesday Society, founded in Berlin in 1783, provided forums where officials, scholars, and writers debated. Mendelssohn, born in Dessau, became a leading philosopher, wrote the Phaedon (1767), and in Jerusalem (1783) argued for separation of religious authority from state coercion. As a Jew, he could reside in Berlin only through a protected status, which he obtained after influential patrons petitioned on his behalf in 1763, a reminder of the legal limits on even the most celebrated intellectuals. Lessing, who had written a play sympathetic to Jews in 1749, and Mendelssohn were friends, and their relationship showed that individual friendship could cross boundaries that law maintained.

The relationship of the German Enlightenment to the Prussian state should not be oversimplified. Frederick II corresponded with Voltaire, reorganized the Berlin Academy with French as its working language, and wrote in French, dismissing German as a language of literature in a pamphlet of 1780. Yet German writers built a literary culture in German, and many worked in other states: Weimar, Gotha, Hamburg, Vienna, and Mainz, and in the Catholic territories of Bavaria and Austria where a Catholic Aufklärung developed. The Bavarian Illuminati and Masonic lodges drew members across territories. Johann Gottfried Herder and others criticized universalizing reason and emphasized language and history, while Kant, whose Critique of Pure Reason appeared in 1781, examined the limits of knowledge itself. The German movement was thus a conversation among universities, journals, courts, and societies, and its critics were part of it.

The German-speaking lands offer a particularly clear demonstration of intellectual unity without political unity. Authors worked within a patchwork of principalities, free cities, universities, ecclesiastical states, and Habsburg territories. A journal printed in one city might be read in another jurisdiction, where the rules about religious orthodoxy and state criticism were different. University appointments could provide income and status but require adherence to confessional expectations. Book fairs and private correspondence joined scholars whose rulers had different political interests. The shared literary market made arguments travel across territorial borders, while those borders created opportunities to seek alternative printers or patrons when a controversy became dangerous locally.

The choice of language was itself significant. Latin continued to serve specialist communication, and French was important at some courts, but the expansion of German-language periodicals allowed wider participation by readers who lacked specialist university training. The effectiveness of a publication depended on style as well as content. Essays, dialogues, plays, biographies, and popular moral writings carried arguments that would have remained inaccessible in a technical Latin treatise. Lessing's work across dramatic criticism, literary journalism, and theological controversy illustrates the movement between genres. His disputants did not agree about how freely sacred history might be examined, but they recognized a common arena in which books could be answered with books and controversy could be extended through periodicals.

Jewish participation challenges an account built solely around Christian institutions. Moses Mendelssohn was recognized by Christian scholars and writers while facing restrictions that a socially comparable Christian intellectual might not encounter. His work on religion, education, and public life belonged to a German intellectual culture but was also informed by Hebrew learning and Jewish communal debates. The Berlin Wednesday Society, founded in 1783, joined officials and writers who privately debated questions that could later be aired through the Berlinische Monatsschrift. The difference between a selected discussion society and the journal's larger readership matters: the society's members did not constitute the public as a whole, even when their arguments entered public print. The German case therefore connects university learning, multilingual exchange, confessional inequality, and contested notions of public criticism.

Section 82.15

Italy, Iberia, and the Scandinavian Enlightenments

Italy was a patchwork of states, including republics, duchies, papal territories, and the kingdoms of Naples and Sicily, and its Enlightenment was correspondingly varied. In Naples, intellectuals including Antonio Genovesi, who held a chair of commerce and political economy from 1754, Ferdinando Galiani, and later Gaetano Filangieri addressed economic reform and legal critique, building on the earlier legacy of Pietro Giannone, whose history of the kingdom of Naples (1723) attacked ecclesiastical jurisdiction and who died in confinement in 1748. In the Habsburg-ruled Milan, Pietro and Alessandro Verri formed the Accademia dei Pugni (the Academy of Fists) in the early 1760s, and their journal Il Caffè (1764 to 1766), modeled in part on English periodicals, promoted clear writing, economic improvement, and legal reform. Tuscany and its port of Livorno, a free port with a diverse merchant population, hosted publishers, including those who printed an edition of the Encyclopédie. Catholic reformers such as Muratori showed that Italian reform thought drew on clerical as well as lay scholars, and the presence of the papacy meant that questions of church jurisdiction were never abstract.

Cesare Beccaria, a Milan aristocrat in the circle of the Verri brothers, published On Crimes and Punishments anonymously in Livorno in 1764. It criticized torture, secret accusations, and the death penalty and argued that punishments should be proportionate, public, and prompt. Its success was international: André Morellet's French translation of 1766 and an accompanying commentary attributed to Voltaire spread it across Europe, and English and other translations followed. Rulers cited it; Catherine II drew on it in her instruction for a law commission, and Tuscany under Grand Duke Leopold abolished the death penalty in 1786. Practical reforms elsewhere were partial, and executions continued in many states. The case shows an Italian work becoming a European text through translation, intermediaries, and princely attention.

The Iberian peninsula is often wrongly treated as a place untouched by enlightened reform. In Spain, the Benedictine Benito Feijoo's Universal Critical Theater (1726 to 1740) criticized popular superstitions and defended experimental science in essays that were widely read. Gregorio Mayans, Gaspar Melchor de Jovellanos, and Pedro Rodríguez de Campomanes advocated educational and economic reform, and the Bourbon monarchy sponsored royal academies, scientific expeditions, and botanical gardens. A distinctive institution was the Economic Society of Friends of the Country, beginning with the Basque Society in 1765 and the Madrid society in 1775, which brought together nobles, clergy, and officials to promote agriculture, industry, and education. The Inquisition and censorship remained, and reformers often worked cautiously and within Catholic frameworks. In Portugal, Luís António Verney's True Method of Studying (1746) criticized scholastic education, and the marquis of Pombal, chief minister from 1750 to 1777, reorganized the university at Coimbra in 1772, expelled the Jesuits, and expanded state control of censorship and education after the Lisbon earthquake of 1755. Both states had overseas empires whose scientific institutions and Atlantic networks are discussed in Section 82.17.

Scandinavia developed its own settings. Sweden's Royal Academy of Sciences (1739) and Uppsala's learned societies supported Carl Linnaeus, whose botanical classification system made him an international correspondent and who sent students abroad to collect specimens. The Swedish Freedom of the Press Act of 1766 widened public debate and access to official records, but the opening was short-lived: Gustav III’s 1772 coup was followed by a restrictive press ordinance in 1774. In Denmark-Norway, the playwright Ludvig Holberg, born in Bergen, wrote comedies and historical works, and the Royal Danish Academy was established in 1742, with a scientific society at Trondheim in 1760. Finland's Åbo Academy supported scholars such as Henrik Gabriel Porthan. These networks were oriented toward both German and French models, but they also worked in local languages and under Lutheran state churches that shaped educational and moral reform. They should not be treated as late imitations of France: their institutions, languages, and problems differed.

The Italian peninsula's fragmentation created several centers of reform that were joined by correspondence and translation but faced different political constraints. Naples had a large population, important courts, and an intellectual tradition in which questions of feudal jurisdiction, ecclesiastical property, and commercial improvement could become matters of public argument. Milan's circle around the Verri brothers used the short essay and collective journal as a vehicle for intervention in policy without holding sovereign power. Tuscan reformers operated within another administrative and legal environment, and Livorno's commercial connections provided access to foreign books that might be difficult to obtain elsewhere. Rather than narrating Italy as a unified intellectual territory, the chapter should show how separate states offered different conditions for a common debate about law, learning, and useful knowledge.

The success of Beccaria's treatise demonstrates the importance of translation in creating a European controversy. An argument written in Italian gained new readers in French and other languages, but translation also reordered emphasis and fitted the work to new disputes. Reform proposals addressed torture, penal publicity, procedure, and proportionality; endorsement of a book did not entail immediate transformation of judicial practice. In a court that adopted some arguments, older punishments and privileges could persist. The distinction between intellectual reception and legislative effect is crucial. It avoids making humanitarian reform appear automatic and gives students a way to ask how an idea traveled from a Milanese circle into the hands of administrators, writers, and readers elsewhere.

Spain's enlightened culture had its own institutional forms. Religious orders, royal academies, universities, botanical gardens, economic societies, and officials exchanged proposals about teaching, farming, craft production, and commerce. A Catholic friar who criticized a popular superstition did not thereby reject Catholicism; a royal official who promoted an academy might also support restrictions on political publication. The societies known as Friends of the Country organized improvement within existing corporate and monarchical structures. Their records are valuable because they show how educated elites defined local problems, raised subscriptions, offered prizes, and sought patrons. They should not be confused with a mass movement embracing every community under the Spanish monarchy. Language, distance, resources, and censorship affected their influence.

In Portugal, reforms under Pombal rearranged authority rather than removing it. The 1768 Real Mesa Censória transferred important supervisory powers over books toward a royal board, replacing parts of the earlier ecclesiastical system. The shift encouraged some new educational and scholarly initiatives while strengthening the state's ability to judge what could be printed. The Coimbra reforms likewise linked curriculum to governmental purposes. A comparative chapter should contrast these developments with more commercially autonomous book markets without treating either model as wholly free or wholly repressive. The presence of colonial commerce adds another layer: Iberian scientific expeditions and collections depended upon expertise and labor beyond Europe, even when their publications appeared under metropolitan patronage.

Scandinavia provides a different example of regional adaptation. Swedish learned societies connected universities, state offices, and economic improvement, and Linnaean correspondence joined local collecting with far-flung expeditions. Sweden's 1766 press law widened public access to certain official records and reduced prior restrictions, but political conditions quickly changed. The 1772 coup and restrictive 1774 ordinance sharply curtailed the earlier experiment, including by increasing the vulnerability of printers. Denmark-Norway also experienced a brief interval of exceptional print freedom followed by retrenchment. These reversals belong inside the history of enlightenment because the expansion of discussion could be reversed without ending scholarly exchange. In Finland under Swedish rule, scholarship at Åbo operated within the same northern intellectual geography while pursuing linguistic, historical, and regional subjects of its own. Eighteenth-century Nordic Enlightenments were shaped by local churches, languages, and administrative needs, not simply by the arrival of French books.

Section 82.16

Poland-Lithuania, Eastern Europe, Russia, and Southeastern European Intellectual Networks

In the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, a large elective monarchy with a powerful nobility, enlightened reform came from within the clergy, the nobility, and the court. The Piarist priest Stanisław Konarski founded the Collegium Nobilium in Warsaw in 1740, reformed teaching, and wrote on political reform. The Załuski brothers, bishops and collectors, opened a major public library in Warsaw in 1747. After his election in 1764, King Stanisław August Poniatowski patronized writers, theater, and artists, and hosted the Thursday dinners mentioned earlier. The journal Monitor (1765) was modeled on the Spectator, and the Commission of National Education, established in 1773 after the first partition of the Commonwealth, administered schools and is often described as one of Europe's first state education authorities. The partitions of 1772, 1793, and 1795 by Russia, Prussia, and Austria, discussed in Chapter 79, ended the state, and the later history of Polish intellectual life shows how enlightened culture became intertwined with loss and national memory. Historians avoid treating the Commonwealth's reformers as precursors of a modern national state, since it was a multiethnic polity of Poles, Lithuanians, Ruthenians, Jews, Germans, and others.

Habsburg territories east of Vienna, including Hungary, Bohemia, Transylvania, and Galicia (acquired in 1772), received enlightened reform from the monarchy and developed local movements in languages other than German, including Hungarian writers around György Bessenyei and Czech and Slovak scholars. Russia shows a state-led pattern. Peter the Great's Academy of Sciences (founded in 1724) recruited foreign scholars. Mikhail Lomonosov, a scientist and poet from a northern fishing family, helped found Moscow University in 1755. Catherine II corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot, purchased Diderot's library in 1765 while leaving it in his keeping, and sponsored the Free Economic Society (1765) and a law commission in 1767. In 1783 she allowed private printing presses, and Nikolai Novikov, who leased the press of Moscow University in 1779, built a major publishing enterprise before his arrest in 1792, which shows how quickly patronage could turn to repression. Russian intellectual history has its own dedicated MGU textbook, and this chapter treats Russia only where it intersects European networks.

Southeastern Europe participated vigorously. Greek-speaking scholars, merchants, and clergy, often connected through the Ottoman Empire's Orthodox Christian communities, formed a Greek Enlightenment. Eugenios Voulgaris taught at schools in Ottoman lands and translated Voltaire, and princely academies in Bucharest and Iași, under Greek-speaking Phanariot rulers of the Danubian principalities, taught philosophy and science. Greek printing presses in Vienna, Venice, and Leipzig distributed books across the Greek world. Adamantios Korais, who settled in Paris in 1788, edited ancient texts and argued for language reform, and Rigas Feraios, active in Vienna, published political tracts before his execution in 1798. Greek merchant communities in Trieste and Vienna supported educational and publishing networks. Odessa, founded in 1794, became a substantial Greek commercial and institutional center later, beyond this chapter’s principal chronology. This movement was part of Ottoman and European history at once. In the Ottoman capital itself, Ibrahim Müteferrika established a printing press with imperial permission in 1727, and its first book appeared in 1729, though print in Ottoman Turkish remained limited for decades.

Among Slavic and Romanian communities, Dositej Obradović, a Serbian writer who traveled widely, published works in a vernacular Serbian and advocated education, and the Transylvanian School (Școala Ardeleană) of Romanian Greek Catholic scholars, including Samuil Micu and Gheorghe Șincai, studied language and history within the Habsburg framework and petitioned for political recognition in 1791. In Bulgaria, Paisii Hilendarski wrote a history in 1762 at Mount Athos, which later generations treated as a founding text, though historians distinguish its religious and local aims from nineteenth century nationalism. Jewish intellectual life included the Haskalah, a movement of Jewish enlightenment that began in Berlin around Mendelssohn, produced the Hebrew periodical Ha-Meʼassef (from 1783 in Königsberg), and responded to Habsburg reforms: Naphtali Herz Wessely's 1782 pamphlet welcomed Joseph II's toleration edict while urging educational change. The Haskalah moved into Galicia and the Russian partitions of Poland and met resistance from traditionalists and from Hasidic and rabbinic leaders, so it was one current among many in Jewish life. Across these regions, intellectuals were not passive recipients of western instruction but selected, translated, and reshaped ideas for their own communities and languages.

In Poland-Lithuania, the relationship between intellectual reform and political fragility is especially important. The Commonwealth's elective monarchy, noble political privileges, religious communities, and regional diversity formed a setting unlike a centralized western monarchy. Educational initiatives could be defended as strengthening the Commonwealth while encountering resistance from groups that feared a loss of local or corporate influence. The Załuski Library and the Commission of National Education illustrate different institutions: the former attempted to assemble knowledge and widen scholarly access, while the latter reorganized schooling through public authority after the suppression of the Jesuit order. Both depended on particular political choices and resources. Their achievements should not be treated as precursors that made a later Polish nation-state inevitable, since the Commonwealth encompassed multiple languages, faiths, and political identities.

The Habsburg monarchy likewise contained overlapping intellectual worlds. A reform promulgated in Vienna did not circulate uniformly among Czech, Hungarian, Romanian, Slovak, German, Italian, and other language communities. Translators, teachers, clerics, and printers interpreted policies for different audiences, sometimes endorsing administrative improvement while disputing the cultural assumptions attached to it. Local scholarship in history and language could serve imperial institutions or become a basis for demands for recognition. Those outcomes were contingent. A learned society or school operating under dynastic rule was not automatically a national movement, and intellectual exchange did not erase disagreements over religion, jurisdiction, and rank.

Russian participation was also more complex than a ruler writing to famous French authors. Academies, court libraries, university teaching, and publishing initiatives connected Russian writers to German, French, and other European learned markets. Foreign scholars recruited to institutions in Saint Petersburg relied on translators and local assistants, while Russian writers adapted imported genres to their own audiences. Catherine II's patronage made international intellectual contact visible at court, but the experience of a provincial reader or a critical publisher could differ sharply from that of a favored correspondent. The later prosecution of Novikov illustrates the vulnerability of commercial and educational projects to political reversal. This chapter should register those exchanges while leaving the independent history of Russian intellectual institutions to the separate Russian History textbook.

The Greek-speaking world illustrates how Europe cannot be mapped solely through the boundaries of its modern states. Ottoman subjects, merchants in Habsburg ports, Orthodox clerics, teachers in the Danubian principalities, and printers in Venice or Vienna formed routes of communication across imperial jurisdictions. A Greek book might be printed abroad, funded by merchants, and carried to readers in Ottoman towns. Its contents could combine religious instruction, ancient literature, mathematics, and contemporary philosophy. Such combinations should not be treated as contradictions: educational reform and Orthodox learning could coexist, even when particular authors challenged inherited practice. The same evidence demonstrates why a west-to-east diffusion model is insufficient. Translators chose and modified works in response to local educational and linguistic demands.

The chronological boundary requires care. Greek merchant communities active in Vienna and Trieste belonged to the chapter's eighteenth-century discussion; the growth of Odessa as a port and major Greek commercial center began after its foundation in 1794 and is a subsequent development, not a pre-1790 institution. Likewise, later nationalist interpretations of Serbian, Bulgarian, Romanian, or Greek writers should not determine how their eighteenth-century projects are presented. Their arguments arose from religious, linguistic, dynastic, educational, and commercial problems specific to their worlds. Jewish Haskalah debates introduce an additional dimension: Hebrew periodicals and reformist educational programs traveled across the same broad geography, but their communities debated religious practice and social position according to their own traditions. Multiple Enlightenments overlapped without becoming one uniform European political or cultural campaign.

Section 82.17

The Atlantic World, Colonial Knowledge, Slavery, and the Global Circulation of Ideas

European enlightened culture was never only European. Books, letters, specimens, instruments, and information moved through Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Asian networks created by commerce, empire, and missions, examined in Part XI and especially Chapter 73. Colonial towns had their own institutions. Benjamin Franklin helped found the Library Company of Philadelphia (1731) and the American Philosophical Society (1743), and in 1776 to 1785 he served as an American envoy in Paris, where he was celebrated in salons and academies. In Spanish America, expeditions such as the Royal Botanical Expedition to New Granada, begun in 1783 under José Celestino Mutis, gathered natural knowledge, and periodicals such as Mexico's Gazeta de Literatura (1788) and Lima's Mercurio Peruano (1791) built local publics. In Saint-Domingue, a plantation colony, planters and officials formed a Cercle des Philadelphes at Cap-Français in 1784, whose learned activity depended on enslaved labor. These institutions connected colonial elites to metropolitan networks, and they also served imperial extraction, since collected plants, minerals, maps, and knowledge fed European science and commerce.

Knowledge also came from non-European informants. Missionaries, particularly Jesuits, sent descriptions of China that filled the Edifying and Curious Letters (published 1702 to 1776) and shaped debates about morality and government, and Voltaire and Wolff drew on them while admiring and misreading Chinese thought. Antoine Galland's French translation of the Arabian Nights (1704 to 1717) shaped European imagery of the Islamic world, and Montesquieu's Persian Letters used a fictional Persian viewpoint. Naturalists depended on local knowledge: Maria Sibylla Merian's Metamorphosis of the Insects of Surinam (1705) relied on enslaved and Indigenous people's expertise, though they are scarcely named. Voyages such as those of Bougainville and James Cook produced accounts of Pacific societies read as evidence about human nature and attracted writers who argued about civilization. In every case, European authors controlled the printed record, and it is difficult to recover the views of those who supplied the information.

The question of slavery tests Enlightenment claims directly. Many writers condemned slavery in principle, and some did so forcefully. Condemnation came from varied sources: Quaker antislavery activism, Protestant evangelicals, moral philosophers, and Abbé Raynal's History of the Two Indies (1770, expanded 1774 and 1780 with contributions by Diderot), which attacked colonial brutality. Condorcet published a pseudonymous critique of slavery in 1781. Yet other writers accepted or defended slavery, or wrote racial hierarchies into natural history. Hume's added note of 1753 and 1754 doubted that non-Europeans were equal in capacity, Linnaeus and later Blumenbach classified human varieties, and Kant lectured and wrote on race in this period. Many Enlightenment readers were slaveholders or profited from the Atlantic economy, whose consequences for Europe are explored in Chapters 70 and 73. The same networks that circulated critiques of slavery also circulated plantation manuals, colonial science, and racial classification.

Black writers entered the printed conversation. Ignatius Sancho, a former enslaved man who became a shopkeeper in London, corresponded with writers, and his letters were published in 1782 after his death. Phillis Wheatley, enslaved in Boston, published Poems on Various Subjects in London in 1773, with the support of patrons including the Countess of Huntingdon. Anton Wilhelm Amo, born in what is now Ghana, studied and taught philosophy in German universities in the 1730s. Olaudah Equiano's autobiography of 1789 became the most widely read narrative of this kind. These authors worked in Enlightenment genres, and their presence challenged claims about racial incapacity, though their opportunities depended on patrons and audiences who could also doubt or limit them.

The imperial circulation of information depended on unequal systems of collection. A botanical expedition might be celebrated in metropolitan publications as a triumph of observation while relying upon local guides who recognized plants, laborers who carried supplies, and translators who made consultation possible. Maps and natural histories converted situated knowledge into printed forms that could be exchanged in European academies, but those transformations often detached information from the communities that produced it. The historian should ask who organized a collection, who performed its labor, and who possessed the final manuscript or specimen. A European author's name on a title page cannot establish that the knowledge originated exclusively with that person. Yet it would be equally misleading to portray all non-European contributors as passive sources. They chose what to explain, interpreted the travelers they encountered, and sometimes challenged the accuracy of European assumptions.

Atlantic correspondence also created competing publics rather than one undifferentiated enlightened world. A Philadelphia scientific society, a Caribbean plantation club, an abolitionist circle in London, and an administrator's reading room in a Spanish American city were connected by shipping and books but differed in membership, purpose, and political circumstances. A colonial elite might use the language of improvement to advocate agriculture, infrastructure, or botanical classification while denying literacy and autonomy to enslaved workers. An abolitionist, by contrast, might use the same transatlantic publishing channels to expose violence within the system that sustained the trade. These differences make it important to identify the institutional interests behind a published report, especially when a colonial observer claimed to speak for an entire population.

Black authors demonstrate that intellectual participation was not limited to being observed by Europeans. Wheatley's poems, Sancho's letters, Equiano's narrative, and Amo's university philosophy entered distinct genres and audiences. They should not be collapsed into a single model of resistance or imagined to have held identical views. The forms of publication mattered: subscription lists conferred social credibility, testimonials sought to secure readers' trust, and booksellers determined access to the market. Hostile readers often subjected Black writers to demands for authentication that they did not impose uniformly on white authors. Those demands themselves are evidence of the hierarchies embedded within the expanding print public. The debate about authorship and authenticity belongs to the history of the republic of letters just as much as the contents of the works do.

The global history of enlightenment also requires attention to what European writers misunderstood. Reports about China, the Ottoman Empire, Indigenous American peoples, or Pacific island societies entered debates about law, religion, and human nature, but were often filtered through missionaries, colonial officials, traders, and literary conventions. An author could use an admired foreign example to criticize Europe without accurately representing the society being invoked. Fictional Persian observers in a European satire are particularly different from documented Persian intellectual voices. Distinguishing fiction, travel reporting, translation, and testimony prevents a literary technique from being mistaken for direct cultural encounter. Intellectual exchange occurred across the world, but its printed record remained highly unequal in authority and preservation.

Chronology also shapes the limits of this section. Some colonial newspapers and societies became especially influential after 1790 and belong chiefly to the wider history of the revolutionary and nineteenth-century Atlantic. Their antecedents may be mentioned to establish continuity, but they should not be folded into a settled eighteenth-century conclusion. The central finding is that European intellectual culture was materially connected to empire, forced labor, and intercultural exchange, not that those connections produced a single political outcome.

Historical Debate

How Should Universal Intellectual Claims Be Evaluated Alongside Exclusion, Slavery, and Empire?

The question: Enlightenment writers frequently spoke of human reason and rights in universal terms, while many accepted slavery, colonial rule, or social and gender hierarchy. Was the Enlightenment fundamentally complicit in empire and racial thought, or did it also supply the concepts later used to challenge them?

Interpretation A: Universalism was compromised, and often enabled domination. Scholars such as Emmanuel Chukwudi Eze have emphasized how racial classification, hierarchical theories of civilization, and justifications of tutelage developed in the same intellectual milieu as universal claims. In this view, the Enlightenment's language of reason helped legitimate empire and racial hierarchy.
Interpretation B: The Enlightenment contained powerful resources against empire and slavery. Others, including Sankar Muthu, have argued that major writers criticized imperialism and defended the dignity of non-European peoples, and Jonathan Israel has argued that the more radical strand was consistently opposed to slavery. Critics of slavery used Enlightenment arguments, and enslaved and formerly enslaved people used the same language to demand freedom.

Evidence: Both sides use published works, correspondence, lectures, subscription lists, and the practices of institutions and merchants. They differ on which writers represent the movement and how to weigh private conduct, public statements, and later uses of ideas.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the heritage of the Enlightenment is judged as liberating, oppressive, or both, and how modern debates about universal rights relate to their origins. Students should resist both celebration and blanket condemnation, since the evidence supports a more complicated account.

Student task: Select two authors from this section, one who criticized slavery or empire and one who defended or ignored it, and explain how their differing positions complicate any single judgment of the Enlightenment.

Section 82.18

Women, Religious Minorities, Translators, and the Unequal Republic of Letters

The republic of letters claimed to be a community of merit, but its prestigious institutions were socially restricted. The Académie française did not admit a woman until 1980, and the Royal Society elected its first women fellows only in 1945. Universities generally excluded women, though exceptions existed in Italy, where Laura Bassi received a doctorate at Bologna in 1732 and held a university position, and Maria Gaetana Agnesi published a textbook on analysis in 1748. Women nevertheless participated as writers, translators, experimenters, hosts, correspondents, and patrons. Participation, recognition, and institutional admission were distinct: a woman could contribute substantially and be denied the title, the salary, or the seat that a man with similar work might obtain.

Émilie du Châtelet (1706 to 1749) illustrates the possibilities and the limits. Born into the nobility, she studied mathematics with tutors including Maupertuis and the mathematician Alexis Clairaut, shared a household at Cirey with Voltaire from the 1730s, and wrote independently. Her Institutions de physique (1740) presented principles of physics for her son and combined Newtonian and Leibnizian ideas. She entered an essay on the nature of fire in the Paris Academy's competition in 1738, was elected to the Bologna Institute of Sciences in 1746, and produced the French translation of Newton's Principia with commentary, published in 1759 after her death in childbirth. In Voltaire's 1738 exposition of Newton, the frontispiece depicted her as a light reflecting Newton's ideas to him, an image that acknowledged her role but placed her as intermediary. She disputed publicly with the academician Dortous de Mairan over the measurement of motion, showing that she could contest an established scientist in print.

Émilie du Châtelet, Institutions de physique (Paris, 1740)

Context: Du Châtelet wrote the book for her son's instruction and to present a systematic account of physics that drew on Newton, Leibniz, and Wolff. Analysis: The Institutions shows a woman making an original intellectual intervention in a form, the textbook, that was socially acceptable for her position. Other women authors and translators shaped intellectual life. Elizabeth Carter, a member of the bluestocking circle, translated the Stoic philosopher Epictetus (1758), Françoise de Graffigny's Letters of a Peruvian Woman (1747) was a best seller, and Catherine Dashkova became head of the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences in 1783. Translators, women and men, functioned as intellectual intermediaries who determined what crossed language frontiers and in what form, though their names were often omitted or secondary. Their labor made the international republic possible even when it did not confer equal recognition.

Religious minorities also faced barriers. Jewish intellectuals could achieve fame but faced legal restrictions on residence, occupation, and education. Mendelssohn's rise depended on exceptional protection, and he argued publicly for Jewish civil improvement and against forced conversion. Henriette Herz, wife of the physician Marcus Herz, and other Jewish women hosted Berlin salons beginning in the 1780s that brought together Jews and Christians, an unusual space of social contact that nevertheless coexisted with legal inequality. Sephardic and Ashkenazi merchants in Amsterdam, London, Livorno, and elsewhere took part in learned and commercial networks. Protestants in Catholic France and Catholics in Protestant Britain and Ireland faced limits of their own. In all cases, inclusion in the republic of letters depended on the combination of talent, patronage, wealth, and the willingness of institutions to admit them.

The distinction between access and recognition can be followed through each stage of intellectual production. A woman might read a draft, translate a manuscript, calculate tables, or finance publication without appearing as its author. A bookseller's widow could operate a firm or maintain its credit relationships yet receive little attention in histories centered on philosophical writers. A salon host might help introduce an author to a patron, while an assistant in an academy might gather observations credited to a senior member. To reconstruct these contributions, historians combine title pages with correspondence, contracts, family papers, laboratory notebooks, and institutional records. Absence from membership lists is important evidence of formal exclusion but is not evidence that excluded people did no intellectual work.

Du Châtelet's career illustrates how editions make recognition visible. The first Paris printing of her Institutions de physique in 1740 was anonymous, although her authorship was known within relevant circles. A substantially revised Amsterdam edition in 1742 explicitly named her and reflected continuing engagement with the philosophical questions she discussed. Treating both as identical publications conceals an important development in the author's control of her work and public recognition. Nor was her contribution simply the interpretation of a male scientist for an untrained audience. She analyzed problems in natural philosophy, engaged with competing accounts of force and motion, and revised her position in response to debate. Her Newton translation was issued posthumously with substantive commentary. Attention to editorial history thus enlarges rather than diminishes the historical account of intellectual authorship.

Religious minority participation likewise cannot be measured by admission to elite associations alone. Jewish scholars and writers used Hebrew and vernacular print, participated in commercial networks, and contested the relationship between religious education and broader learning. Some readers welcomed programmes of reform; others feared that externally defined improvement would undermine communal autonomy. For Protestants in Catholic jurisdictions or Catholics in Protestant-majority settings, the social possibilities of correspondence might differ sharply from their legal standing. Toleration, private friendship, conversion, equality before the law, and full civic participation were not interchangeable accomplishments. An individual could be celebrated as a scholar while lacking rights available to less accomplished neighbors.

The labor of translation is an especially revealing case because translators brought texts across languages while changing their arguments in the process. Selection, abridgment, explanatory annotation, and the choice of familiar examples could reshape how a foreign work was received. Translators had to understand two reading publics and often negotiate censorship in the destination market. Some were praised as cultural ambassadors; others were unnamed, underpaid, or treated as secondary to the original author. The familiar image of great thinkers speaking directly to a unified Europe hides the work of these intermediaries. Recovering them makes the historical network more accurate and shows why a text could acquire different meanings in different places.

Intellectual exclusion was neither static nor uniform. An Italian university could make exceptional appointments unavailable elsewhere; a metropolitan club might exclude women even while periodicals marketed essays specifically to them; certain Jewish and non-Christian writers might publish internationally while facing local restrictions. Such variations do not invalidate the existence of structural inequalities. They explain how participants maneuvered within them. The Enlightenment's claims about merit can therefore be assessed against actual institutional practices rather than accepted as a description of social reality.

Section 82.19

Was There One Enlightenment? Criticism, Contradictions, and Historical Legacies

The preceding sections show that enlightened cultures shared practices and concerns but diverged on much else. They shared a reliance on print, correspondence, and sociability; an interest in criticism, improvement, and natural explanation; and a habit of comparison across societies. They differed in language, institutions, relation to religion and the state, and in who participated. Scottish Presbyterian clergy, Neapolitan jurists, Berlin Jewish merchants, French philosophes, Spanish Benedictines, and Greek merchants did not form a single party. Contradictions also ran through the movement: calls for toleration coexisted with prejudice, praise of liberty with defense of hierarchy, universal humanity with racial classification, and sociable conversation with exclusion.

Contemporaries debated the meaning of the term as it was being used. In 1783, as noted earlier, Zöllner questioned the project in a footnote, and in 1784 Mendelssohn answered that enlightenment was bound up with culture and education, which together shaped the vocation of human beings as individuals and as citizens, and he warned that excessive enlightenment could damage society if not balanced by culture. In December Kant answered that enlightenment was emergence from self-incurred immaturity, urged the courage to use one's own understanding, and distinguished between the public use of reason, which must be free, and the private use in a civil post, which could be restricted. He argued that his age was one of enlightenment in progress. The two replies, written within months for the same journal, differ in emphasis and show the contested character of the term.

A defensible answer to the question of one Enlightenment must distinguish three kinds of coherence. There was a shared set of problems, including the credibility of knowledge, the authority of inherited institutions, the value of useful learning, and the obligations of civilized society. There were shared means of communication, including periodicals, correspondence, academies, salons, and translations. And there was a language of reason, criticism, and improvement recognizable across numerous intellectual communities. None of these requires agreement about God, monarchy, property, religious toleration, or the meaning of freedom. The historian can describe a common intellectual infrastructure without inventing a common program. Conversely, the existence of local differences does not mean every writer or city constituted an entirely separate movement. Common controversies connected the participants even when they disagreed.

The relationship between commercial publication and criticism illustrates this balance. The publishing system enlarged the number of people who could encounter arguments from outside their immediate community, yet it also favored subjects and formats that booksellers believed customers would purchase. Censorship could restrict circulation while inadvertently making a prohibited text more attractive. Translation broadened reach while sometimes altering a source. Patronage provided time and resources but could constrain independence. These arrangements made intellectual exchange effective and uneven at once. They explain why neither a triumphalist story of inevitable liberty nor a purely cynical account of elite domination adequately captures what contemporaries were doing.

The concept of a public sphere offers a useful but contested framework. In one influential interpretation, print and sociable institutions created arenas in which private persons discussed matters of common concern and increasingly evaluated public authority. Critics point out that those arenas often depended on privilege, excluded many people, and coexisted with older institutions rather than replacing them. Different kinds of publics emerged, including religious, commercial, civic, scholarly, and local groups, and they did not always cooperate. A historical inquiry should therefore ask which people discussed which problems under what conditions, and whether discussion affected decisions, rather than assume that participation in an eighteenth-century club was equivalent to modern democratic citizenship.

The Enlightenment's moral contradictions are equally central. Writers could condemn religious persecution while repeating prejudices against Jews or Muslims. Arguments about a shared human nature could coexist with schemes for classifying people into unequal racial groups. Critiques of Atlantic slavery were published in societies whose wealth and institutions were intertwined with coerced labor. A philosopher might defend intellectual freedom for trained authors while warning that uninstructed readers should be restrained. These differences are not a reason to deny the force of antislavery campaigns or demands for toleration; those interventions had consequences and deserve specific investigation. But they prevent later readers from using the achievements of selected authors as proof that an entire age held a coherent doctrine of equality.

The paired 1784 replies by Mendelssohn and Kant crystallize the chapter's principal argument. Two thinkers in different cities used the same Berlin periodical to respond to a question about the movement's meaning. Both valued intellectual development; they emphasized different relationships among education, criticism, social obligations, and public argument. Their disagreement appeared through editorial decisions and subscription networks that reached more readers than the participants of any single learned society. The episode therefore teaches a method for reading the Enlightenment historically: examine the question posed, identify the institutional setting, compare what each author argued, and avoid turning either answer into the definition of an entire century.

The chapter's transition to political thought should remain precise. The communication networks described here carried arguments about rights, representation, and sovereignty, but they also carried religious defense, literary criticism, agricultural experiments, and schemes for more effective administration. The next chapter concentrates on particular theories of political legitimacy and their disputes. It does not follow that the existence of salons and journals caused the French Revolution or established a single road toward representative government. Part XIV will examine revolutionary crises through their own institutional, fiscal, social, and political causes. Chapter 82 has a distinct conclusion: European intellectual life acquired more extensive, multilingual, and internationally connected means of criticism, while older inequalities and constraints continued to structure who could use them.

Creators: Moses Mendelssohn, Jewish philosopher in Berlin; Immanuel Kant, professor at Königsberg · Date and place: Mendelssohn, September 1784; Kant, December 1784; both in the Berlin monthly Berlinische Monatsschrift · Type: Two short essays in German responding to a question posed in the same journal

Context: Zöllner's footnote of December 1783 asked what enlightenment is. The editors welcomed answers, and several writers replied. The essays by Mendelssohn and Kant are the best known. They appeared in a periodical read by officials, professors, pastors, and educated townspeople.

Paraphrase of both essays, with one famous motto Mendelssohn treats enlightenment as one aspect of a broader process of education, alongside culture, concerned with theoretical knowledge and the capacity for rational reflection on human life. Kant defines enlightenment as emergence from self-imposed immaturity, urges readers to adopt the Latin motto sapere aude ("dare to know," a phrase from Horace), and argues that freedom to make public use of reason is the essential condition. This paraphrase summarizes the two essays; students should read verified translations of both.

Analysis: Read together, the essays show that thinkers writing from different cities, Mendelssohn in Berlin and Kant in Königsberg, used the same Berlin journal to conceive enlightenment differently. They also show the form of the debate: a question posed in a periodical, answered by readers, circulated beyond Berlin. Kant's essay, composed under Prussian censorship, balanced criticism and obedience. Mendelssohn, a Jew writing in a Christian state, had particular reasons to link enlightenment to civil conditions. The sources cannot show how ordinary readers understood the terms, nor do they represent the Enlightenment outside German-speaking Europe.

Historical Debate

Was There One Enlightenment or Several?

The question: Should historians describe a single, international Enlightenment, or a set of distinct regional and confessional Enlightenments?

Interpretation A: A recognizable, unified movement. Some historians, including Peter Gay in his classic account and, in a different way, Jonathan Israel, have argued for a coherent core, whether in a shared critical outlook or in a radical tradition tracing back to Spinoza that defined the movement's central struggle with religious and political authority.
Interpretation B: Plural Enlightenments. Others, including J. G. A. Pocock, and the contributors to comparative studies of national contexts, argue that Enlightenments developed from specific religious, political, and institutional settings, such as Protestant and Catholic, monarchical and republican, so that the singular term misleads. They emphasize the importance of local conditions and of conservative and religious Enlightenments.

Evidence: Both draw on publications, correspondence, institutional histories, and the vocabulary used by contemporaries. They differ in what level of abstraction they find most illuminating and whether shared practices outweigh differences of content.

Why it matters: If the Enlightenment was unified, one can ask about its general causes and consequences. If it was plural, explanations must be local and comparison becomes central. A plural view also makes space for Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish, and eastern European actors in the story.

Student task: Select three regions from Division IV and identify one feature they share and one that distinguishes them, then argue whether the shared or distinguishing features better justify the term.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Learned Republic of 1650 to the Reading Public of 1789

What changed?

  • The audience expanded from scholars to a wider educated public of professionals, merchants, officials, and some women
  • Vernacular languages, especially French and later German and English, overtook Latin for much cultural exchange
  • New forms, such as the moral weekly, the encyclopedia, and the review journal, joined the book
  • Voluntary associations, salons, and coffeehouses added settings beyond courts and universities

What remained continuous?

  • Dependence on patronage, rank, and personal introduction
  • The need to negotiate with states and churches that controlled printing and teaching
  • The central role of correspondence, academies, and universities
  • Exclusion of most women, the poor, and most colonized people from prestigious institutions

Who experienced change most directly? Urban and educated readers, booksellers, and writers who could live by publication. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural populations, the poor, serfs, and enslaved people, for whom reading and learned societies were largely remote. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against assuming that new communication forms immediately transformed the whole of society.

Section 82.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has argued that the Enlightenment is best understood as a culture of communication. Its ideas traveled through letters, books, periodicals, translations, salons, clubs, and academies, and these channels made intellectual life more public, more commercial, and more contentious than it had been. They were built from older materials: the humanist republic of letters, the learned journals and scientific societies of the seventeenth century, Huguenot exile, and a commercial book trade with international reach. They were also shaped by constraints. Censors, churches, and rulers regulated print, patrons and rank governed access, and the audience remained educated, urban, and disproportionately male.

Regional variety was essential. Scottish universities, Dutch printers, Berlin journals, Milanese reformers, Iberian economic societies, Warsaw schools, Greek diaspora presses, and Jewish scholars of the Haskalah all took part, each within different political and religious circumstances. The movement's claims to universality coexisted with exclusions of women, religious minorities, and enslaved and colonized peoples, though members of those groups sometimes used its genres to speak. The question of whether the Enlightenment was one movement or many remained contested then and remains so among historians.

These networks supplied the setting for the political ideas examined in Chapter 83, where Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, and others are analyzed in detail, and for the reforming efforts of rulers discussed in Chapter 84. The social and material conditions of readers' lives are developed in Chapters 85 and 86. How and whether these networks contributed to the political upheavals beginning in 1789 is a question for Part XIV, and this chapter has deliberately not treated revolution as their inevitable outcome. The review materials that follow consolidate the chapter's argument.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Enlightenment as a transregional culture of exchange, criticism, and publication rather than as a list of philosophers and doctrines. Contemporaries used images of light and used different words in different languages. They argued about what enlightenment required, as the 1784 responses of Mendelssohn and Kant show. Historians have also disagreed, with older accounts stressing a unified movement and recent scholarship emphasizing regional variety and social history.

The foundations lay in the republic of letters of the seventeenth century, sustained by correspondence, learned periodicals, and scholarly exchange across confessions. The Revocation of the Edict of Nantes dispersed Huguenot printers and writers, and the Dutch Republic became a major center of French language publishing. Pierre Bayle's journal and Dictionary turned critical erudition into a public instrument, while showing that critics could be punished by Protestant as well as Catholic authorities.

The book trade supplied the material means. Booksellers financed editions, relied on privileges and exchange at fairs, and used foreign presses and false imprints to avoid restrictions. Translators determined what crossed language frontiers and in what form. Literacy and reading practices expanded unevenly, through subscriptions, circulating libraries, reading societies, and newspapers, but most readers consumed devotional and practical works, and the reading public remained a minority.

Settings of sociability gave intellectual life its social form. Salons, led by women such as Geoffrin, du Deffand, and Lespinasse, brokered reputations and patronage. Coffeehouses, clubs, and Masonic lodges offered more open or associational spaces. Academies conferred prestige and organized prize competitions, as with Rousseau's Discourse. Correspondence and postal networks linked scholars who rarely met, within limits set by cost, surveillance, and war.

The printed Enlightenment generated controversy. Periodicals and satire popularized criticism. The Encyclopédie, financed by booksellers and edited by Diderot and d'Alembert, produced seventeen volumes of text and eleven of plates by 1772, withstood suppression and papal condemnation, and was widely reprinted. Religious criticism ranged from deism and skepticism to atheism and reformist belief, and toleration was debated in cases such as Calas and in Lessing's Nathan the Wise. Regulation varied across Europe, and neither prohibition nor toleration alone decided what was read.

Regional Enlightenments developed distinctive forms: Scottish universities and clubs, Irish reprinting and Dutch publishing, the German Aufklärung of universities, journals, and religious pluralism, Italian reform culture in Milan and Naples, Iberian economic societies, Scandinavian academies and press experiments, Polish educational reform, Russian state patronage, Greek and Romanian movements in the Ottoman and Habsburg worlds, and the Jewish Haskalah. Eastern and southeastern Europe took part in making these exchanges.

Atlantic and imperial networks carried books, correspondence, and specimens, and colonial knowledge drew on non-European informants whose roles were seldom acknowledged. The treatment of slavery tested Enlightenment universalism: some writers condemned it, others defended it or developed racial hierarchies, and Black authors such as Equiano, Wheatley, and Sancho used print to speak. Historians debate whether the movement's universal language primarily enabled domination or resisted it.

Women, Jews, and other minorities participated unequally. Women were excluded from the major academies but wrote, translated, and hosted, as the career of du Châtelet shows. Jewish intellectuals gained fame through exceptional protection while living under legal restrictions. The distinctions among participation, recognition, and admission help explain how a universal rhetoric coexisted with narrow institutions.

The chapter's larger significance is that it explains the infrastructure through which knowledge became public and contested. It prepares the ground for political thought in Chapter 83 and for reform and resistance in later chapters, without treating later events as inevitable. The Enlightenment emerges as an interconnected but plural, ambitious but unequal, and international but locally rooted set of cultures.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Enlightenment
A cluster of intellectual and cultural movements of roughly the late seventeenth to late eighteenth century that stressed reason, criticism, and improvement; the singular label is a later historical construction.
Aufklärung
The German term for enlightenment, used by Kant and Mendelssohn in 1784 and applied to the German movement centered on universities, journals, and religious debate.
Philosophe
A French term for writers, not always professional philosophers, who criticized prejudice and authority and promoted reform.
Republic of letters
The community of scholars and writers linked by correspondence, books, and criticism across borders, sustained by reputation rather than law.
Public sphere
Habermas's term for a space in which private persons discuss common concerns apart from the state; used by historians as an analytical tool and contested.
Sociability
The habits and settings of organized social interaction, including conversation, visits, clubs, and dinners, through which intellectual work was shared.
Salon
A regular private gathering, usually led by a woman host, at which invited guests conversed, read, and made connections.
Coffeehouse
A commercial establishment, open to paying customers, offering coffee, newspapers, and conversation, and serving as a center for news and business.
Academy
A chartered body of scholars or writers that conferred membership, published proceedings, and often organized prize competitions.
Learned society
A voluntary association for study and discussion of science, agriculture, history, or letters, often less formal than an academy.
Prize competition
An academy's contest in which a question was posed and a medal awarded for the best essay, creating a forum beyond the academy's members.
Periodical
A publication issued at regular intervals, including learned journals, reviews, newspapers, and moral weeklies, that sustained continuing public discussion.
Moral weekly
An essay periodical modeled on the Spectator that promoted manners, reading, and reform and spread across Europe in local versions.
Encyclopédie
The reasoned dictionary of sciences, arts, and trades edited by Diderot and d'Alembert and published in Paris from 1751, with text and plates completed by 1772.
Privilege
An exclusive royal or official right to print or sell a work, protecting investment and implying approval.
Permission tacite
In France, an unofficial toleration of a book that was not formally approved, allowing circulation without a privilege.
Censorship
Control of printing and speech by prior examination, indexes of banned works, prosecution, and seizure, practiced unevenly by churches and states.
Index of Forbidden Books
The Roman Catholic Church's list of prohibited books, administered by a papal congregation.
Clandestine literature
Works printed or copied without permission, often anonymously or under false imprints, including philosophical, scandalous, and pornographic texts.
False imprint
A title page naming a fictitious publisher or place to disguise where a book was printed.
Subscription publishing
A financing method in which buyers pledged payment in advance, producing lists of subscribers that show patronage and audience.
Circulating library
A commercial library lending books for a fee, important for reading novels and periodicals in Britain and elsewhere.
Reading society
A club, as in German lands, in which members pooled subscriptions to journals and books.
Freemasonry
A fraternal movement with ritual and moral teaching, organized in lodges from the early eighteenth century, drawing members across rank and region.
Deism
The belief in a creator accessible to reason, without reliance on revelation or church authority.
Toleration
Forbearance of religious difference, varying from informal coexistence to legal recognition, and debated rather than universally endorsed.
Haskalah
The Jewish Enlightenment, a movement beginning in German lands around Mendelssohn that promoted secular education, Hebrew writing, and civil improvement.
Cosmopolitanism
An orientation toward membership in a wider human or European community beyond locality or state, often claimed by Enlightenment writers.
Correspondence network
A web of letter exchange linking people across distance, studied through surviving letters and subject to biases of archival survival.
Sapere aude
A Latin phrase from Horace meaning "dare to know," adopted by Kant as the motto of enlightenment.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that ideas do not move on their own. Between an author's desk and a reader's room lay printers, patrons, translators, censors, couriers, and hosts, and their decisions determined who could think in public, about what, and with whom. Understanding that infrastructure changes how the Enlightenment is understood: it becomes a historical process involving institutions and commerce as well as arguments, and its participants appear as people with interests, constraints, and rivalries.

The chapter also connects regions that older narratives separated. Edinburgh and Königsberg, Naples and Warsaw, Amsterdam and Athens appear as parts of one communicating but unevenly connected Europe, and Atlantic and imperial networks tie that Europe to the wider world. This breadth supports the argument of Volume IV: that the transformations of this era arose from interaction among states, markets, societies, and empires, not from a single national or philosophical source.

Several continuities extend beyond the chapter. Questions of who may speak, what may be printed, and who counts as a member of the public remain central in later chapters on revolution, liberalism, nationalism, and mass politics. The practices examined here, periodicals, associations, academies, and international correspondence, were inherited and transformed by later movements. Yet nothing in the chapter suggests that these developments led inevitably to particular political outcomes.

Finally, the chapter leaves open the question with which it began. Kant and Mendelssohn answered what enlightenment is in 1784, and historians have been answering it ever since. The persistent disagreement is part of the history, and a sign that the Enlightenment was more a conversation than a doctrine. Chapter 83 now turns to the political ideas debated within that conversation.
