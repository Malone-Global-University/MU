Division, Vikings, Magyars, and a Europe under Pressure

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 845, a Scandinavian fleet ascended the Seine to Paris, and the Frankish king Charles the Bald, according to the Annals of St-Bertin, bought its departure with a very large payment of silver. Three years earlier, the king's own brother and his army had sworn oaths of alliance in two different vernacular languages. Between these episodes lies the central problem of this chapter: how an imperial order built by Charlemagne came apart along family lines while new forms of mobile warfare, raiding, and settlement forced European societies to invent new ways of organizing defense and authority.

The chapter begins where Chapter 31 ended, in 814, when Charlemagne died at Aachen and his surviving son Louis inherited an empire that stretched from the Baltic and North Seas to central Italy and from the Atlantic to the Elbe and the Danubian frontier. That empire was not, in 814, a failing structure. It possessed a functioning court, a network of counts, bishops, and monasteries, a shared ecclesiastical culture, and a prestigious imperial title. Yet it also depended on personal loyalty, on the goodwill of regional aristocracies, and on a Frankish custom of dividing royal inheritance among sons, a custom that sat uneasily beside the idea of a single Christian empire. The first half of this chapter traces how those tensions produced civil war, the partition of 843, and a long series of further divisions that left the Carolingian family ruling several kingdoms rather than one empire.

The second half of the chapter turns to two forms of external pressure that intensified during the same decades. Scandinavian seafarers, whom contemporaries called Northmen, Danes, or heathens and whom modern writers call Vikings, raided, traded, extorted tribute, and eventually settled across the Frankish lands, the British Isles, Ireland, and the North Atlantic. In the late ninth and early tenth centuries, the Magyars, a confederation of mounted groups that had entered the Carpathian Basin around 895 or 896, launched campaigns into central and western Europe and Italy. These pressures did not affect a healthy and united Europe, nor did they strike a Europe that was already dead. They interacted with the political divisions already described, and their consequences depended heavily on how local leaders, bishops, and kings responded.

The chapter therefore avoids two familiar stories. The first is that Charlemagne's weak successors squandered his achievement and thereby invited barbarian invasion. The second is that a violent age of Viking and Magyar catastrophe interrupted an otherwise stable civilization. Both stories oversimplify. Carolingian fragmentation grew from inheritance practice, competing ideas of unity, aristocratic interests, and family conflict, and external attacks exploited and accelerated it. The attackers, in turn, were not simply destroyers. Scandinavians were also merchants, mercenaries, colonists, and founders of new political communities, and Magyar raiding was one dimension of a society with its own leadership, economy, and diplomacy.

Language and terminology require care throughout. This chapter uses West Francia and East Francia for the Carolingian kingdoms that emerged from the partitions, rather than France and Germany, because those later identities and states were not created in 843 and took shape through processes examined in Chapter 33. It uses "Viking" cautiously, as an activity and modern category rather than the name of one people, and it speaks of the Magyars, or Magyar confederation, rather than the Kingdom of Hungary, which belongs to a later political transformation. It also observes the chronological distinction between raiding, tribute, conquest, and settlement, because these were different situations with different consequences.

Historians reconstruct these events from evidence that is abundant but partial. Frankish annals, chronicles, capitularies, charters, and letters record raids and political crises from the viewpoint of ecclesiastical and court writers who often had personal reasons to emphasize suffering. Insular sources such as the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and the Irish annals offer another perspective. Archaeology, coin finds, place names, ship burials, and cemetery evidence add material that written accounts cannot supply, and for the Magyars, Byzantine and Arabic writers supplement Latin sources that were frequently hostile. Numbers of ships, warriors, and dead in these sources should be treated as impressions, not counts.

Within Part V, this chapter forms the hinge between the construction of Carolingian imperial order in Chapters 29 to 31 and the consolidation of durable kingdoms in Chapter 33. Within Volume II, it shows the political conditions from which the medieval kingdoms, principalities, and Christian frontiers of the later chapters emerged. Its governing claim is that the ninth and early tenth centuries were a period of severe violence and political decentralization, but also of institutional adaptation, negotiated settlement, fortified defense, and the emergence of new regional centers of power.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the empire Louis the Pious inherited in 814 was substantial, and identify the structural tensions within it.
  2. Analyze the Ordinatio Imperii of 817 and the succession conflicts of Louis's reign as products of competing ideas of unity and partition rather than of personal weakness alone.
  3. Trace the sequence from Louis's death through Fontenoy, the Oaths of Strasbourg, and the Treaty of Verdun, and explain why Verdun divided dynastic shares rather than creating France and Germany.
  4. Describe the repeated partitions, recombinations, and extinctions of Carolingian branches between 843 and the early tenth century.
  5. Distinguish raiding, tribute, conquest, trade, and settlement in Scandinavian activity, and explain why the term "Viking" requires careful use.
  6. Assess Frankish responses to Scandinavian pressure, including tribute, fortified bridges, regional commands, and negotiated settlement.
  7. Compare Scandinavian settlement in England, Ireland, the North Atlantic, and Normandy as different historical situations.
  8. Contextualize the Magyars within the Carpathian Basin and the wider steppe world, avoiding the projection of later Hungary backward.
  9. Interpret the Oaths of Strasbourg as evidence for dynastic politics, language, and the limits of later national narratives.
  10. Evaluate the interpretations of Carolingian fragmentation as collapse or as regional transformation, using specific evidence.
  11. Connect political fragmentation and frontier violence to the strengthening of regional aristocratic, episcopal, and ducal authority, without anticipating the full analysis of lordship reserved for Chapter 34.

Historical Context

By 814, the Frankish world had undergone a century of expansion under the Pippinid and Carolingian dynasty, traced in Chapters 30 and 31. Charlemagne and his predecessors had extended Frankish rule over Saxony, Bavaria, Lombard Italy, the Breton and Spanish marches, and Frisia, and had defeated the Avars on the middle Danube in the 790s. His coronation as emperor at Rome in 800 gave this accumulation of kingdoms an imperial title and a Christian vocabulary of unity. Rule was exercised through the king's court, through counts who administered districts and led local military levies, through bishops and abbots who held enormous landed endowments and provided counsel, and through royal envoys, the missi dominici, who supervised local officials. Written capitularies expressed royal commands and reforms, though their actual enforcement varied widely.

This system had real strengths, but it rested on limited institutional foundations. There was no standing bureaucracy in the later sense, and communication moved at the speed of horses, boats, and messengers. Counts were typically members of powerful local families with their own estates and followings, and the king's ability to reward them depended on land, offices, and the proceeds of war. Historians such as Timothy Reuter have argued that plunder and tribute from successful expansion helped underwrite the system, so that the end of large conquests in the early ninth century placed new strain on royal patronage. Whether this argument fully explains later developments is debated, but it draws attention to the material and political basis of Carolingian rule.

Succession practice supplied a second structural problem. Frankish kings had long treated the realm as a family patrimony to be shared among legitimate sons, and Charlemagne's own accession in 768 involved division with his brother Carloman. In 806, Charlemagne drew up a plan to divide the empire among three sons, but two of them died before him, in 810 and 811, leaving Louis as the sole surviving legitimate heir. Louis's inheritance therefore did not reveal weakness. It reflected the chance survival of one son, and it postponed rather than resolved the question of what would happen when the next generation had several heirs.

The geography discussed in Chapter 2 shaped both the empire and the pressures placed on it. Navigable rivers such as the Rhine, Meuse, Scheldt, Seine, Loire, and Garonne connected the interior to the North Sea and Atlantic, and Frankish wealth in monasteries, market towns, and river ports lay close to water. The same routes offered access to shallow draft ships. To the east, the open Pannonian Basin and the Eurasian steppe corridor examined in Chapter 2 formed a route by which mounted peoples could enter central Europe. Frankish rulers had defeated one such power, the Avars, but had not eliminated the geographic openness that had allowed steppe peoples to appear in the region.

Scandinavia, finally, was not an isolated backwater in 814. The Baltic and North Sea trading system already linked emporia such as Ribe, Hedeby, and Birka to Frisian merchants and to the wider exchange of silver, furs, slaves, and weapons. Danish kings had grown powerful enough to confront the Franks, and in 810 the Danish king Godfred, according to the Royal Frankish Annals, sent a fleet against Frisia and exacted tribute. Scandinavian raiding of Britain and Ireland had already begun in the 790s. What changed during the ninth century was the scale, frequency, and geographic range of these activities, and their entanglement with Frankish political conflict.

Section 32.1

The Empire after Charlemagne, 814

Charlemagne died at Aachen on 28 January 814. His son Louis, born in 778 and king of Aquitaine since 781, had been associated with the imperial title in a ceremony at Aachen in 813, and he succeeded without opposition. Charlemagne's other legitimate sons, Pippin of Italy and Charles the Younger, had died in 810 and 811. Pippin's son Bernard held the kingdom of Italy under imperial oversight, a position that would later create difficulties. In 814 the transfer of power was smooth, and contemporaries did not experience it as the beginning of collapse.

The empire Louis inherited was a complex assemblage of regions with different histories: the Frankish core between the Rhine, Meuse, and Loire, Aquitaine and the Spanish March in the southwest, Brittany on a contested western edge, Saxony and Frisia in the north, Bavaria and Alemannia in the southeast, and the kingdom of Italy, together with the papal territories around Rome. Each had its own aristocratic families, ecclesiastical institutions, and legal customs. The unity of the whole was expressed in the person of the emperor, in shared oaths of fidelity, in the movement of the court, and in an ideology of Christian rule that bishops and scholars helped articulate. It was not expressed through a uniform administrative system.

Three features of this inheritance mattered for what followed. First, the scale of the empire made direct supervision impossible, so rulers depended on counts and bishops whose loyalty had to be maintained through favor, marriage alliances, and reward. Second, the system had grown through conquest, and the political economy of royal generosity was easier to sustain when military campaigns produced land, tribute, and captives. By 814 the great expansion had largely ended, although fighting along the Saxon, Slavic, Breton, Spanish, and Danish frontiers continued. Third, imperial ideology and Frankish inheritance custom pointed in different directions: the first emphasized one Christian empire, the second the equal claims of several royal sons.

Historians therefore treat 814 as a moment of inherited strength and inherited tension rather than a point of decline. The empire still commanded resources and prestige, and its institutions, from the court chapel at Aachen to the network of royal monasteries, remained active. Yet the same institutions would be tested by succession disputes, and the external environment, especially along the North Sea coasts and rivers, was beginning to change. To read the later ninth century as the working out of a predetermined failure would obscure the contingent choices, chance deaths, and shifting alliances that shaped events.

Section 32.2

Louis the Pious and the Problem of Imperial Succession

Louis's reign, from 814 to 840, is often described as a failure, but the evidence supports a more careful view. Louis inherited a court that was already deeply involved in church reform, and he continued that program, supporting monastic reform associated with Benedict of Aniane and emphasizing the emperor's responsibility for the moral welfare of the Christian people. His contemporaries, including the biographical writers known as Thegan and the Astronomer, saw him as a pious and legitimate ruler, though both wrote with particular perspectives shaped by the later conflicts. The question is not whether Louis was personally strong or weak, but why the succession arrangements of his reign proved so unstable.

The central document is the Ordinatio Imperii of 817, issued after a serious accident at Aachen that reminded Louis and his advisers of mortality. It designated his eldest son Lothar as co-emperor and principal heir, gave his younger sons Pippin and Louis the subordinate kingdoms of Aquitaine and Bavaria, and required the younger brothers to remain subject to the emperor. The plan was an ambitious attempt to reconcile the ideal of a single empire with the practice of sharing royal power among sons, and it had the support of prominent churchmen and lay magnates who valued imperial unity. It also placed enormous weight on the willingness of the younger sons and their followers to accept permanent subordination.

The arrangement was immediately tested. In 817, Bernard of Italy, Charlemagne's grandson, rebelled, apparently because the Ordinatio subordinated the kingdom of Italy to Lothar. Louis suppressed the revolt, and Bernard was blinded, a punishment that led to his death in 818. In 822, at Attigny, Louis performed a public penance that included acknowledgment of his treatment of Bernard, an episode that shows both the moral authority claimed by the church and the unusual political vulnerability of a ruler who accepted it. The event later became part of arguments over how far bishops could judge a king.

The crisis deepened after Louis's second marriage, in 819, to Judith of the Bavarian Welf family. Judith bore a son, Charles, in 823, and Louis wished to provide for him. Every provision for Charles reduced the shares of the older sons or required revision of the Ordinatio. In 829, Louis gave Charles a territorial portion in the southeast, prompting the rebellion of 830 by Pippin and Lothar, supported by important churchmen and courtiers who opposed Judith's influence and that of certain advisers. Louis recovered his position, but the underlying problem, the absence of an accepted way to provide for a fourth son, remained.

In 833, at a place near Colmar later called the Field of Lies, Louis confronted his rebellious sons and their supporters, and his own following dissolved, an outcome that later sources attribute in part to betrayal or deception, though the details come from partisan accounts. Lothar and the bishops forced Louis into a public penance at Soissons and confined him, but the humiliation of the emperor by his sons and by bishops provoked a reaction. Louis was restored in 834, and further adjustments followed in 837 to 839, including new shares for Charles after Pippin's death in 838. When Louis died on 20 June 840, on an island in the Rhine near Ingelheim, no arrangement enjoyed the acceptance of all parties.

Louis's difficulties therefore reflect structural tensions more than personal incompetence. The Ordinatio attempted an ideal solution but did not reflect the balance of aristocratic interests. Courtiers and regional magnates aligned with different sons because their own estates, offices, and marriages depended on the outcome. Bishops asserted a role as guardians of Christian order and were themselves divided. The conflicts of the 830s belong to the same political world as the raids on Frisia, which resumed with force in the mid 830s and are discussed in Section 32.6, and the strains on royal attention and military resources cannot be separated from one another.

Historical Vocabulary
Partible Inheritance and the Ordinatio Imperii

Partible inheritance refers to the practice of dividing a kingdom or property among several heirs, rather than passing it whole to one. Frankish kings from the Merovingian period onward generally treated the realm as a family possession in which legitimate sons all had a claim. The Ordinatio Imperii of 817 modified this custom by naming one son, Lothar, as co-emperor and principal heir, while giving the others subordinate kingdoms. It was a compromise between an imperial ideal of unity and dynastic custom.

The term matters because it prevents students from treating division as an aberration or failure of nerve. Division was an expected outcome of Frankish kinship politics. The modern idea of a single, indivisible state, and the later rule of primogeniture, should not be projected onto ninth century assumptions.

Section 32.3

Civil War and the Treaty of Verdun

Lothar, already crowned co-emperor, expected to inherit imperial authority over the whole empire under the Ordinatio of 817. His brothers Louis, who ruled Bavaria and adjacent lands, and Charles, whose claims rested on the arrangements of 837 to 839, did not accept a settlement that left them subordinate. Between 840 and 843, the brothers fought over the extent of their shares and the meaning of Lothar's imperial position. The war was not a struggle between nations. It was a contest among royal brothers for territory, legitimacy, and the loyalty of aristocratic followers, many of whom held lands in more than one region.

The decisive battle was fought at Fontenoy, near Auxerre, on 25 June 841. Lothar and his nephew Pippin II of Aquitaine faced the combined forces of Louis and Charles. Lothar lost, and contemporary writers described enormous losses, though the numbers given in the annals cannot be verified and probably reflect horror at fighting among Christians and kinsmen. Contemporary writers also worried about the moral status of a battle that they interpreted as a judgment of God. Fontenoy did not end the war, but it established that Lothar could not impose his claims by force.

The following winter produced one of the most important documents of the ninth century. On 14 February 842 at Strasbourg, Louis the German and Charles the Bald swore mutual oaths of alliance in the presence of their armies, and each army then swore in its own language, as recorded by Nithard, who was a participant and a grandson of Charlemagne. The oaths are discussed in the Primary Source Window below. They show that the brothers' cooperation depended on public ritual, that armies were expected to bind themselves, and that the languages of the two followings were different enough to require separate versions.

After further negotiations, the three brothers reached a settlement, generally dated to August 843 and associated with Verdun. According to contemporary annals, commissioners were charged with assessing the resources of the empire so that shares could be divided fairly. Charles the Bald received the western portion, including Aquitaine, Septimania, and the western Frankish lands between the Atlantic and the Scheldt, Meuse, and Rhône. Louis the German received the eastern portion east of the Rhine, together with bridgeheads on its west bank around Speyer, Worms, and Mainz, including Bavaria, Saxony, Thuringia, Alemannia, and Franconia. Lothar retained the imperial title and received a central realm running from the North Sea coast of Frisia through Aachen and the Rhine and Rhône corridors into Provence and the kingdom of Italy, while his imperial position also gave him an important relationship with Rome and the papacy.

The shares were designed to be roughly equal in economic and political value, and they were not designed to create linguistic or ethnic states. The western realm included Romance speaking populations but also Breton speakers and Germanic speakers in the north. The eastern realm included Germanic speakers but also Slavic populations along its frontiers. The middle kingdom contained Romance, Germanic, and Italian speaking populations, and contained major royal palaces and monasteries, including the especially important Carolingian palace complex at Aachen. It was also the most difficult realm to defend and to hold together, since it was long, narrow, and lacked a single regional identity.

Verdun is therefore best understood as a dynastic partition. The brothers continued to regard themselves as members of one family and one Christian people, and they met at conferences in the 840s and 850s to affirm brotherly concord and coordinate responses to common problems, including Scandinavian raids. Later historians and national traditions, especially in France and Germany, presented 843 as the starting point of their own histories, but the shape of West Francia and East Francia in 843 was not the shape of later France or Germany. Their territories, dynastic fortunes, and identities changed repeatedly, and it is Chapter 33 that explains how durable kingdoms eventually consolidated.

Primary Source Window

The Oaths of Strasbourg, 14 February 842, as recorded by Nithard, Histories, Book III

Creator: Nithard, aristocrat, lay abbot, and grandson of Charlemagne, writing at the request of Charles the Bald during the conflict · Date: written c. 841 to 843 · Type: A contemporary narrative history containing the earliest substantial texts in a Romance vernacular and in an early form of Old High German

Context: After Fontenoy, Louis the German and Charles the Bald met at Strasbourg to reaffirm their alliance against Lothar. Nithard, who was present in Charles's circle, reports that each king addressed the assembled armies, and that the oaths were sworn in the language that the other side's followers would understand. Louis swore in the Romance vernacular, so Charles's followers could understand him, and Charles swore in the Germanic vernacular, so Louis's followers could understand him. Each army then swore its own oath in its own language. Nithard's account is written in Latin, and the oaths themselves appear in the vernaculars.

Paraphrase of the sworn content The kings each pledged, out of love for God and for the salvation of the Christian people and their common welfare, that from that day forward, so far as God gave them knowledge and power, they would support the other brother and would not make any agreement with Lothar that would harm him. The armies swore separately that if either king broke the oath, they would not be obliged to support him. This is a paraphrase of the content, not a translation of the vernacular texts.

Analysis: The source is important for several reasons. It shows that an alliance among kings was not simply a private arrangement but was made public before armies, whose consent and support were needed. It shows that the language communities within the Frankish world included Romance and Germanic speakers, and that their leaders recognized the need to speak in the vernacular for political purposes. The text is significant in the history of the Romance and Germanic languages, since it preserves early written forms of speech that are otherwise unattested in this period. At the same time, the oaths should not be treated as a "birth" of French and German, since the followings of both kings included speakers of multiple varieties, and later national languages developed through long processes.

The limitations are substantial. Nithard was a partisan writing for Charles the Bald, and his account of Lothar's conduct is hostile. The vernacular texts survive only in a later manuscript copy, and scholars debate the precise linguistic form of the words. The document says nothing about the peasants, women, and clergy who lived in the regions affected by the war, or about the interests of Lothar and his followers.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the obligations of kings and their armies to one another?
  2. Who created the source, and what purpose might Nithard have had in recording the oaths in vernacular form?
  3. What can historians learn from it about politics, language, and military organization in 842?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone, including the linguistic and political identities of ordinary people?
Section 32.4

From Partition to a Fragmented Carolingian Political World

Verdun did not end the process of division. It initiated a long series of further partitions, deaths, inheritances, and recombinations that left the Carolingian family ruling a shifting collection of kingdoms. The middle kingdom, in particular, could not remain intact. When Lothar I died in 855, having retired to the monastery of Prüm, his realm was divided among his three sons: Louis II received Italy and the imperial title, Lothar II received the northern portion between the North Sea, the Rhine, and the Meuse, a region later called Lotharingia after him, and Charles received Provence and part of Burgundy. Charles of Provence died in 863, and further redivisions followed.

The end of Lothar II's line, in 869, without a legitimate heir recognized by the church, initiated a struggle over his territory. Lothar II's attempt to end his marriage to Theutberga in favor of his partner Waldrada had involved Pope Nicholas I, and became one of the major legal and political disputes of the era, showing how papal, episcopal, and dynastic issues intertwined. After his death, Charles the Bald and Louis the German negotiated the Treaty of Meerssen in 870, dividing Lotharingia between the western and eastern realms. This treaty did not settle the borderland permanently, and control of Lotharingia would remain disputed for generations, a pattern typical of contested borderlands between West and East Francia.

Between the 870s and the 880s, the family's fortunes continued to fluctuate. Louis II of Italy died in 875 without sons, and Charles the Bald was crowned emperor at Rome on Christmas Day 875, a prestigious achievement that did not translate into durable control. Louis the German died in 876, and his realm was divided among his sons Carloman, Louis the Younger, and Charles the Fat. Charles the Bald died in October 877 after crossing the Alps. Through a series of deaths and inheritances, Charles the Fat, the youngest son of Louis the German, gathered several realms, including Italy, East Francia, and West Francia, and was crowned emperor in 881. His control of all these regions in 884 briefly recalled Charlemagne's dominion. Yet in November 887, a group of magnates at Tribur deposed him, and he died in January 888.

The events of 887 and 888 are frequently seen as a turning point. They show that the Carolingian family could still command loyalty and the attention of aristocrats, but that it could no longer guarantee unified rule. After 888, aristocratic assemblies and factions in several regions chose kings, in many cases of non Carolingian origin, and in some cases Carolingian relatives. Odo, count of Paris, was elected king in West Francia. Arnulf of Carinthia, an illegitimate Carolingian, took power in East Francia and later received the imperial crown at Rome in 896. In Italy, rival claimants such as Berengar of Friuli and Guy of Spoleto contended for kingship, and in the Rhône and Alpine regions the new kingdoms of Upper Burgundy and Provence appeared.

Even so, Carolingian legitimacy did not vanish. West Frankish nobles later accepted Charles the Simple, a grandson of Charles the Bald, as king, and the East Frankish line continued through Louis the Child, who died in 911, the last Carolingian ruler in East Francia. Aristocratic families sought marriage ties with the dynasty, and royal charters and coronation rituals maintained a language of continuity. The picture is therefore one of repeated political recombination. No clean three way division generated modern states, and the kingdoms of the period were fluid political arrangements shaped by dynastic accidents, aristocratic choice, and external pressure.

The consequence was a Carolingian world in which kingship was still central but no longer capable of fully organizing a single empire. Kings continued to hold assemblies, issue charters, and grant lands, but they increasingly did so within regions, and they relied on local leaders whose resources and followings were crucial. This regionalization shaped how the Franks confronted their external enemies, as the sections that follow show.

Section 32.5

Scandinavian Expansion: Raiders, Traders, Migrants, and Settlers

Scandinavia in the eighth and ninth centuries was a region of interconnected but politically distinct communities, not a single kingdom. In what are now Denmark, southern Norway, and central Sweden, chieftains and kings competed for followers, land, and access to exchange. Archaeology reveals a landscape of farms, halls, cemeteries, and, at key coastal locations, trading emporia such as Ribe on the Danish North Sea coast, Hedeby at the base of the Jutland Peninsula, Birka in Lake Mälaren, and Kaupang on the Norwegian coast. These centers connected Scandinavia to Frisian merchants, to the Rhine and Frankish markets, and, through the Baltic and eastern rivers, to Slavic and steppe regions and the Islamic world, from which silver coins, or dirhams, reached northern hoards in substantial quantity.

Maritime technology was central. Scandinavian shipbuilders developed clinker built vessels, with overlapping planks, that were light, flexible, and shallow in draft, and by the eighth century they were using sails effectively. Ships ranged from cargo carriers to long, narrow warships that could be rowed and sailed, that could operate in the open sea, and that could also travel far up rivers and be beached on shores. The ship burials at Oseberg, in Norway, from the early ninth century, show both the skill of craftsmen and the ritual importance of ships. Mobility gave Scandinavian groups the ability to move along coasts and rivers faster than most land forces could respond, a fact central to the pressure discussed in the next section.

The reasons for Scandinavian expansion remain debated. Older explanations emphasized overpopulation or the flight of chieftains from the consolidation of royal power, but neither has gained general support as a full explanation. Historians now see multiple interacting factors: the growth of long distance trade and the demand for silver and slaves, competition among Scandinavian leaders who required wealth to reward followers, familiarity with Frankish and insular coastal regions through trade, the availability of ships and navigational skill, and the political circumstances of the Franks and English, including the weakening of coastal defenses and internal conflict. The Saxon Wars of Charlemagne, which brought Frankish power to the borders of Denmark, and Danish responses such as the construction of the Danevirke earthwork, indicate that the North had long been politically entangled with the Frankish world.

Monasteries appear prominently in surviving accounts because clergy wrote them, and because monastic houses did hold portable wealth, such as precious metalwork, and could be reached by sea. The raid on Lindisfarne in 793, followed by raids on Jarrow, Iona, and other houses in the 790s, shocked contemporary observers, and the scholar Alcuin, then at the Frankish court, interpreted them in a moral and providential framework. But monasteries were not the only targets. Raiders attacked towns, ports, estates, and travelers, and they also seized captives, who could be ransomed or sold into slavery. The prominence of religious houses in the record partly reflects the fact that monks wrote, preserved, and copied the annals.

Scandinavians were also active as participants in Frankish politics. The Danish leader Harald Klak received baptism at Ingelheim in 826 with Louis the Pious as his sponsor, an episode connected with the mission of Ansgar, later archbishop of Hamburg and Bremen, and with an alliance intended to shape Danish politics. Later, Lothar I granted territories in Frisia, including Dorestad, to the Danish leader Rorik, who thereby became a Frankish ally and an authority in the region. Such episodes show that the boundary between raider and ruler's partner could be permeable, and that Frankish kings sometimes sought to use Scandinavian leaders against rivals, including their own relatives.

The people involved are named differently in different sources. Frankish sources commonly wrote of Northmen or Danes, English writers of Danes or heathens, Irish annalists of Gaill, meaning foreigners, and distinguished groups called "dark" and "fair" foreigners, and Slavic, Byzantine, and Arabic writers used terms such as Rus or Varangians for Scandinavians active on the eastern rivers, a subject that connects to Chapter 39. Modern historians commonly gather all these under the term Viking, but this word requires careful use, as the following feature explains.

Historical Vocabulary
Viking

The Old Norse word víkingr appears in sources with the sense of a person who went on a seaborne raiding expedition, and in some cases the related word víking denotes the activity itself. It was not a general name for Scandinavians as a people, and most Scandinavians were farmers, craftspeople, merchants, or leaders who never went raiding. Contemporary Latin and English sources rarely used the word for the whole population.

Modern writers use "Viking" for convenience to describe Scandinavians active abroad in the period roughly from the late eighth to the eleventh century, and the term now carries strong popular associations with horned helmets and barbarism that are not supported by evidence. In this textbook, "Viking" is used sparingly, mostly as a modern label for raiding activity, and more specific terms such as Scandinavians, Danes, Norwegians, or Northmen are used whenever the evidence allows.

Section 32.6

Scandinavian Pressure on the Frankish World

In the Frankish lands, Scandinavian attacks became more frequent and more organized from the 830s. The trading center of Dorestad on the Rhine was raided in several successive years in the mid 830s, at a time when Louis the Pious was dealing with rebellion. After Louis's death and during the war among his sons, fleets attacked Rouen in 841, Quentovic in 842, and Nantes in 843, where the annals report violence against the cathedral community and its bishop. In 845 a fleet attacked Hamburg, the base of Ansgar's northern mission, and Ansgar's see was later moved and joined with Bremen. In the same year a fleet reached Paris, and Charles the Bald, according to the annals, paid a substantial sum in silver for its withdrawal.

These events fit a pattern. Fleets used the Seine, Loire, Garonne, Scheldt, Meuse, and Rhine to reach towns, monasteries, and estates far from the coast. They often wintered at fortified camps, usually on islands or riverbanks, from which they could operate over months. The forces could be small or large, and the figures given in the annals for fleets, sometimes in the hundreds of ships, cannot be checked and probably exaggerate. Franks, too, contributed to the violence: rival kings and aristocrats sometimes hired Scandinavian groups, and fleets could shift allegiance or attack one another. The raids were thus embedded in Frankish politics rather than external to them.

Rulers responded in several ways. The first response was tribute. Paying raiders to leave, as at Paris in 845, could spare a town or region in the short term, although it also encouraged further demands and drained silver. Tribute is easily condemned in retrospect, but for a ruler with limited forces, no ready fleet, and multiple enemies, it was a pragmatic option. A second response was fortification, and here the record is more impressive. In the Edict of Pîtres of 864, Charles the Bald ordered the construction of fortified bridges on the Seine and other rivers, regulated the mobilization of men, and restricted the sale of horses and weapons to Scandinavians. The same edict also ordered the destruction of unauthorized fortifications, showing that royal officials worried that local strongholds might challenge royal power. Fortified river crossings hindered fleets, and later evidence indicates that they influenced the course of the raids in the region.

A third response involved regional command. Charles and other rulers gave military responsibility to trusted magnates, among them Robert the Strong, whose family was later called Robertian and became a major power in the Paris region. Robert was killed in 866 at Brissarthe in a fight against Scandinavian forces and Bretons, and his sons Odo and Robert would later hold prominent positions. This delegation was an effective way to respond to attacks quickly, but it also created powerful aristocratic families with regional bases. The political effect of the raids was therefore not only destruction but redistribution of military authority.

The later ninth century saw a second phase of intensification. A large force sometimes called the Great Army on the Continent operated in the Frankish lands from about 879 to 892. It attacked the Rhine and Meuse regions, and in 881 raiders reached Aachen, the site of Charlemagne's palace and chapel, and Cologne. Frankish forces won important victories at Saucourt in 881, a triumph for Louis III celebrated in vernacular verse, and at the Dyle near Leuven in 891, where Arnulf of East Francia defeated a Scandinavian army; these successes did not by themselves end Scandinavian activity. Charles the Fat, in 882, recognized a Scandinavian leader named Godfred in Frisia, an example of settlement as strategy discussed in Section 32.8.

The siege of Paris in 885 to 886 is the best documented episode. A large Scandinavian force, according to the account of the monk Abbo of Saint Germain des Prés, who was present, besieged the island city, which controlled river crossings on the Seine. The defense was led by Odo, count of Paris, and Bishop Gauzlin. The defenders held out for months, and the siege ended after Charles the Fat arrived and negotiated an arrangement in which, according to the sources, the raiders were allowed to sail upriver toward Burgundy and received payment. Abbo's poem celebrates the defenders, but the arrangement made by the emperor drew criticism, and Odo's prestige rose. In 888, the magnates chose Odo as king. The events at Paris thus illustrate how local defense could generate political capital, and how the distance and the compromises of imperial rulers could weaken them.

The overall impact of these attacks should be assessed carefully. Some regions suffered severe damage. Monasteries were abandoned or relocated, including the community of Noirmoutier, whose monks carried the relics of Saint Philibert on a long series of moves that ended at Tournus. Town life suffered in areas near navigable rivers, and the trading center of Dorestad declined. Other regions were less affected or recovered quickly, and some monasteries, such as those in the Loire valley, restored their fortunes after periods of disruption. Charters and archaeological evidence suggest that the scale of destruction varied greatly from place to place.

Cause and Effect

Why Raids Had Strong Political Consequences

Long-term causes

Navigable rivers and coasts placed wealthy monasteries, ports, and towns within reach of shallow draft ships. Royal government depended on slow communication and on regional magnates for military response. Scandinavian trade contacts and shipbuilding skill gave raiders knowledge and mobility.

Short-term causes

Civil conflict among Louis the Pious's sons diverted royal attention and resources, and rival Carolingians sometimes employed Scandinavian forces. Divided command after 843 meant that neighboring rulers often had to respond separately.

Immediate effects

Tribute payments, destruction of some monasteries and towns, movement of relics and communities, capture and ransom of captives, and the building of fortified river crossings and refuges.

Long-term consequences

Growth in the authority of counts, bishops, and regional commanders who organized defense, the rise of families such as the Robertians, negotiated settlements such as those in Frisia and the lower Seine, and a changing relationship between local communities and distant kings.

Historical complexity: The raids did not by themselves destroy the Carolingian empire, and fragmentation did not by itself cause the raids. The two developments reinforced each other in ways that depended on particular rulers, regions, and circumstances, and on decisions to pay, fight, fortify, or bargain.

Section 32.7

Scandinavian Settlement in Britain, Ireland, and the North Atlantic

Scandinavian activity in the British Isles followed a different trajectory from that in the Frankish lands, with an earlier start and, in England, a larger scale of territorial settlement. In 865, a large army, described in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle as the "great heathen army" and now known to historians as the Great Heathen Army, landed in East Anglia. Over the next decade it moved through the English kingdoms of East Anglia, Northumbria, and Mercia. It took York in 866 or 867, defeated Northumbrian claimants, and in 869 to 870 attacked East Anglia, where King Edmund was killed. In 873 to 874 it wintered at Repton in Mercia, where archaeological work has revealed a fortified camp and burials of Scandinavians, and Mercia's king Burgred was driven out.

In 871, the army moved against Wessex, and Alfred became king that year during a campaign of repeated battles. In 875 the army divided. One part under Halfdan settled in Northumbria in 876, and according to the Chronicle, the leaders shared out the land and began to plow and to support themselves. Another part under Guthrum campaigned against Wessex, and after Alfred's victory at Edington in 878, Guthrum accepted baptism with Alfred as godfather and later settled in East Anglia. A later agreement between Alfred and Guthrum, the date of which is uncertain, defined a boundary and set out legal arrangements between English and Scandinavian populations. It is important to observe that the Chronicle was compiled at Alfred's court in the 890s, and presents events from a West Saxon perspective. The dating of some entries can differ by a year, as annalistic years were not always reckoned from the same starting point.

The regions where Scandinavians settled are often called the Danelaw, a term that requires careful handling, as the following feature explains. Evidence for settlement includes Scandinavian place names, especially those ending in -by and -thorpe, stone sculpture that combines Scandinavian and English motifs, coin evidence, and the presence in eastern and northern England of legal and administrative terms of Scandinavian origin. The size of the settling population is disputed. Some historians believe in a large migration of farmers and families, while others emphasize the adoption of Scandinavian names and customs by existing populations and a smaller elite. The evidence from place names, burials, and, in recent years, scientific analysis of human remains has suggested that settlement included women and children, not only warriors, but the extent remains an area of research.

The consequences for English politics were profound. Of the four major English kingdoms of the mid ninth century, only Wessex survived under an English dynasty, and it survived partly through military reorganization, including the building of fortified places called burhs and the improvement of the military levy. The subsequent expansion of Alfred's successors into areas of Scandinavian settlement, and the formation of a larger English kingdom, belongs to Chapter 33. For present purposes, Alfred's kingdom is significant because it demonstrates adaptation: a ruler faced with severe military pressure reorganized defenses, negotiated with Scandinavian leaders, and used baptism and treaties to incorporate them into a shared political order.

Across the Irish Sea, Scandinavian activity began with raids in the 790s and by the 830s included fortified naval bases, called longphuirt in Irish sources. The base at Dublin, founded in 841 according to the annals, became one of the most important Scandinavian towns in the Atlantic world. Its rulers took part in Irish politics, fought and allied with Irish kings, and controlled a market that included the trade in enslaved people. Dublin's rulers were expelled in 902 and returned in 917. The resulting society is often described as Hiberno-Norse or Norse-Gaelic because of its mix of cultures, and its links to the Isle of Man, the Hebrides, and York created an Irish Sea network in which people and goods traveled between Ireland, Britain, and beyond.

Farther north and west, Scandinavian seafarers settled in the Orkney and Shetland Islands, the Hebrides, and the Faroe Islands, and, from about 870, in Iceland. The written sources for Icelandic settlement, the Landnámabók and Íslendingabók, were composed in the twelfth century and combine memory with later tradition, so historians rely also on archaeology, including a settlement level in Reykjavík dated by volcanic ash layers to about 871. The settlers came from Norway and from the Norse-Gaelic Atlantic region, and their households included Irish and Scottish people, some of whom were free and some enslaved. Iceland's later assembly and legal order, and the further movement to Greenland, lie beyond this chapter, but the initial settlement shows that Scandinavian expansion could create new societies in North Atlantic islands that had no established indigenous or permanent population when large-scale Norse settlement began.

Historical Vocabulary
Danelaw

The term Danelaw derives from an Old English expression for the area in which Danish law or custom prevailed, and it is attested in English legal writing of the early eleventh century, well after the settlements of the 870s. Historians use it to describe the regions of northern and eastern England where Scandinavian settlement was concentrated, but it did not name a unified state, and its borders shifted.

The term matters because it can suggest a single Scandinavian territory, with sharp boundaries, existing alongside an English one. The reality was more varied: settlement patterns differed among regions, English and Scandinavian populations interacted, and English kings gradually incorporated the area, a process treated in Chapter 33.

Section 32.8

Settlement, Negotiation, and Normandy

One of the most important developments of the period was the transformation of some raiders into politically recognized settlers. This process was neither uniform nor automatic. In some cases rulers defeated their attackers, in others they paid them to leave, and in a smaller but significant number they granted territory or recognized existing control in return for oaths, baptism, and military service. Such settlements could be defensive strategies. A ruler who could not expel a Scandinavian force might reduce its threat by converting its leader into an ally responsible for defending the region against others.

Earlier examples in the Frankish world include the arrangements with Scandinavian leaders in Frisia, mentioned above, and Alfred's baptism of Guthrum in England. These precedents show that Frankish and English rulers understood settlement as a political tool. The Church supported the process by offering baptism, which formalized alliances through a sacramental bond and integrated newcomers into Christian society. Baptism did not necessarily reflect deep conversion, but it altered the legal and diplomatic position of the newcomers and gave rulers a language of legitimacy.

The best known example is the settlement associated with Rollo on the lower Seine. Scandinavian forces had operated on the Seine since the 840s. Later Norman tradition, especially Dudo of Saint-Quentin writing around 1000 for the ruling family of Normandy, connects Rollo with a Frankish victory near Chartres in 911 and a subsequent agreement with Charles the Simple at Saint-Clair-sur-Epte, by which Rollo and his followers received territory around Rouen, accepted baptism, and were expected to defend the region against other raiders. No text of such a treaty survives, and contemporary evidence for the precise events of 911 is extremely limited. Details of Dudo's narrative, including the terms and ceremonies, are difficult to verify.

What historians can say with confidence is that a Scandinavian polity on the lower Seine is securely evident by the following decade, since the first secure documentary witness to Rollo's recognized position is a royal charter of 918, and that the West Frankish king and regional lords accepted this presence. The territory expanded over subsequent decades, as Charles the Simple's successors recognized grants of additional lands, including the Bessin in 924 and the Cotentin and Avranchin in 933. The political community of the region later became known as Normandy, land of the Northmen. The identity of its inhabitants changed as well. Scandinavian settlers and local populations intermarried and interacted; over succeeding generations the ruling community adopted the local Romance vernacular, Christianity, and many Frankish political and legal practices, while Scandinavian influence remained visible in place names and vocabulary. The eventual Norman conquest of England, which built on this new political community, belongs to Chapter 45.

The example of Normandy should not be treated as typical or inevitable. It depended on the weakness of royal power in the region, the interests of neighboring magnates, the flexibility of Scandinavian leaders, and the availability of Frankish institutions, including the church, that could absorb newcomers. Other regions, such as Brittany, followed different paths, with a resurgence of Breton rule in the 930s. The lesson for this chapter is that settlement and negotiation were part of the range of adaptive responses to external pressure, and that they created new political forms, such as regional principalities, whose later history became central to medieval Europe.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with Insecurity: Households, Communities, and Sacred Space

For most people in ninth century Europe, political fragmentation and raiding were felt through household economy and local community. Peasant families cultivated small holdings within estates held by lay lords, bishops, and monasteries, and they owed rents and labor services. Raiding forced them to make choices: to flee, to hide stores, to seek refuge in fortified places, or to negotiate with attackers. The capture of persons was a major risk, since captives could be ransomed by kin or church, or enslaved and sold in networks that reached Dublin, Hedeby, and beyond. Contemporary sources record ransoms for prominent churchmen, but the fate of ordinary captives is seldom documented.

Religious communities adapted in visible ways. Monks moved relics and treasures to safer locations, and some houses relocated permanently. Bishops and abbots had to divert resources to defense, and church lands sometimes supported military service. Bishops such as Gauzlin at Paris emerged as leaders of local defense. Local assemblies, both in villages and in towns, negotiated tribute and organized labor for fortifications, and the building of bridges and walls affected the rhythm of agricultural work. At the same time, life continued: markets operated, harvests were gathered, marriages were arranged, and monastic scriptoria produced manuscripts, so that the Carolingian cultural inheritance was preserved even in disturbed regions.

Gender and family also shaped experience. Women, particularly aristocratic women, acted as landholders, abbesses, and political players, as Judith did at the Carolingian court, while less privileged women and children faced particular vulnerability to capture. In settlement regions, Scandinavian and local families formed mixed households, whose everyday interactions are recorded mainly in archaeological and linguistic evidence, not in narrative sources.

Section 32.9

The Magyars and the Carpathian Basin

The Magyars entered the Carpathian Basin, the lowland region also called the Pannonian Basin and introduced in Chapter 2, around 895 to 896, a movement that changed the political situation of central Europe. Hungarian is a Uralic language, traditionally classified in the Ugric branch alongside Khanty and Mansi, but linguistic relationship should not be treated as a direct map of Magyar ethnic origins or as evidence for one simple migration route. Magyar ethnogenesis developed over a long period through interactions among communities of the Eurasian steppe. By the ninth century the Magyar confederation was established in the steppe zone north of the Black Sea and was interacting with the Khazar Khaganate, Byzantium, the Bulgars, Pechenegs, and neighboring peoples.

The Byzantine emperor Constantine VII, writing around 950, described a Magyar confederation of seven groups, joined by three groups called Kabars, and named early leaders including Álmos and his son Árpád, and Arabic and Persian geographers such as Ibn Rusta and Gardizi described a dual leadership, in which a sacral ruler and a military commander held different functions. The Hungarian tradition of the conquest, recorded in later medieval chronicles, elaborates these accounts but was written centuries afterward and reflects later concerns, so historians use it with caution. The dating of the entry into the basin, around 895 to 896, rests primarily on Byzantine and Frankish evidence and archaeological research.

The Magyars did not appear suddenly from nowhere. Frankish annals mention Hungarians in the region as early as 862, and in 881 groups appear near Vienna. In the 890s, various powers used them as allies or mercenaries. Arnulf of East Francia reportedly employed Magyar forces against Great Moravia around 892, and the Byzantine emperor Leo VI used them against Bulgaria in 894 to 895. These episodes show that Magyars were already participants in the diplomacy of the region before they settled in the basin, and that they could be recruited by rulers who sought military help.

The basin they entered was not empty. It had been part of the Avar Khaganate, which had declined after Frankish attacks in the 790s and internal crisis, and its population included Slavic communities, remnants of the Avar population, and others. To the northwest lay Great Moravia, a Slavic polity that collapsed in the first decade of the tenth century, partly under Magyar pressure. To the east and south, Bulgaria under Symeon was a major power. The Franks maintained a frontier zone in Pannonia. The Magyars therefore entered a region of overlapping claims, and they became one of several contenders for power. The geography of the basin, with its open plains, river systems such as the Danube and Tisza, and grassland suitable for herds, favored a pastoral economy supplemented by agriculture, while its position between the steppe and central Europe gave it strategic importance.

Sources for the Magyars are uneven. Latin writers, such as Regino of Prüm around 908, described them as ferocious and compared them with earlier steppe peoples, drawing on stereotypes, and clerical authors emphasized suffering and paganism. Byzantine and Arabic writers offer more detail on organization but had their own interests. Archaeology, especially the numerous tenth century cemeteries in the basin, shows burials of mounted warriors with weapons, horse gear, and ornaments, and women's graves with jewelry, which suggest a society that combined mobility with settled elements. These sources indicate a society of considerable complexity, in which raiding was one of several activities.

It is important to avoid describing this society as the Kingdom of Hungary. The Magyars of the tenth century were a confederation of groups led by chiefs, who took part in raiding and diplomacy. The formation of the Christian kingdom of Hungary, associated with the ruler Stephen around the year 1000, occurred after a long transformation, including conversion and consolidation, that is discussed in Chapter 35. Terms such as Magyar confederation or Magyar groups better reflect the political situation of the period covered here.

Section 32.10

Magyar Raids and the Changing Politics of Defense

From 899, Magyar forces conducted a series of campaigns across central and western Europe. In 899 they entered Italy and defeated King Berengar of Friuli at the Brenta River, after which they devastated parts of northern Italy and extracted payments. In 900 they raided Bavaria, and over the next decades they attacked East Francia, Italy, Burgundy, and at times regions of West Francia. In 907 they destroyed a Bavarian army near Pressburg (modern Bratislava), killing the margrave Luitpold, and in 910 they won victories at the Lechfeld and on the Rednitz against East Frankish forces. Raids reached Saxony and Bremen in 915, Alsace and the Rhineland, Pavia in 924, and, at intervals, regions as distant as Burgundy, Provence, and southern Italy.

Historians offer several explanations for why these campaigns were effective. Magyar forces relied heavily on highly mobile mounted archers and remounts, allowing them to cover long distances, avoid unfavorable engagements, raid dispersed targets, and use tactics such as feigned withdrawal. Campaigns were typically conducted in spring and summer, when pasture was available. Frankish and Italian forces also included substantial mounted contingents, but assembling royal and regional armies from dispersed aristocratic followings and levies generally took more time, making rapid interception difficult. Defeat of an army in open battle could leave a region exposed. The Magyars also exploited divisions among Frankish and Italian rulers, and some, such as Berengar, hired Magyar forces against their rivals.

The objectives of the raids were varied. They included plunder, captives who could be sold or ransomed, and tribute from communities and rulers, and there is evidence that some raids were undertaken at the request of rival powers. The Magyar leadership was not unified in a single command, and different groups organized different expeditions, so that peace with one did not guarantee safety from another. This diffusion made negotiation difficult, but also sometimes made it possible to purchase truces, as Henry, duke of Saxony and later king, did in 926, when he obtained a nine year truce in exchange for tribute after capturing a Magyar leader, according to the chronicler Widukind of Corvey, who wrote decades later.

Defense developed in stages. At first, local leaders and bishops built or repaired walls and refuges, and some regional rulers sought to gain resources by unconventional means. Arnulf, duke of Bavaria from 907, appropriated or redirected substantial ecclesiastical resources to support his political and military following, actions condemned by later clerical writers and reflected in his hostile epithet ‘the Bad.’ Bavarian rulers also sometimes relied on negotiation as well as warfare in managing Magyar pressure. Bishops, including Ulrich of Augsburg, strengthened fortifications and played leading roles in organizing and sustaining urban defense. In Saxony, Henry I, according to Widukind, organized garrisons in fortified places and required a share of the population to support them, and trained cavalry. The details of Widukind's account are debated, since it was written in the 960s and favors the Saxon dynasty, but archaeological evidence for new or improved fortifications in the region suggests that defensive building did take place.

The changing military situation contributed to shifts in political authority in East Francia. After the death of Louis the Child in 911, the magnates elected Conrad of Franconia, and after Conrad's death in 918, Henry of Saxony was chosen in 919. Regional leaders, sometimes called dukes, in Bavaria, Swabia, Franconia, Saxony, and Lotharingia, played major roles, and their status was tied to their ability to defend their regions. Historians call these polities stem duchies, a term with a long history that has been criticized for implying ethnic bases that may have been weaker than older scholarship assumed. The point relevant here is that repeated attacks made regional military leadership more important. The subsequent consolidation of the East Frankish kingdom under the Saxon dynasty is the subject of Chapter 33.

In 955, at the Lechfeld near Augsburg, a coalition army led by Otto I, son of Henry I, defeated a large Magyar force, and several Magyar leaders were executed. The battle is often treated as a decisive end to Magyar raids in the west, and later tradition made it a symbol of Christian victory. Raiding did diminish sharply after 955, but that change also reflected a broader transformation in Magyar society, including greater emphasis on settlement, diplomatic relations with neighbors including Byzantium, and internal political change that led toward the formation of the Hungarian kingdom. The Lechfeld is used here only as a closing chronological marker, since the political consolidation associated with Otto I and the Saxon dynasty is reserved for Chapter 33.

Section 32.11

Frontier Violence and Political Adaptation

Scandinavian and Magyar pressures, though different in form, produced comparable political dynamics. In both cases, attackers used mobility to strike beyond the reach of slow moving levies. In both cases the political fragmentation of the Carolingian world limited the ability of any king to organize a coordinated response, and in both cases the local actors who could respond quickly, including counts, bishops, and regional dukes, gained in authority. The Mediterranean pressure belongs more fully to the histories of Italy and Islamic Sicily. Later chapters return to Christian-Muslim interaction in the Mediterranean in different political settings, especially during the crusading era, but the ninth-century conquest of Sicily and attacks on Italian and Provençal coasts deserve brief notice here because they show that Latin Christian societies faced military and political pressures from several directions. Muslim forces from North Africa conquered Sicily over the ninth century, raided the coasts of Italy and Provence, and attacked Rome in 846. It would be inaccurate to describe these activities only as raiding, since they included commerce, political alliance, and the establishment of new societies, as in Sicily, but they added to the sense of insecurity in the coastal regions of the Latin west.

On land frontiers, the Franks and their successors faced continuing conflict with Slavic communities, including the Obotrites and Sorbs east of the Elbe and the polities of Bohemia and Moravia, while the Breton frontier and the Spanish March remained contested. Alongside warfare, these frontiers involved trade, tribute, alliances, and missionary activity, and their peoples were not simply enemies of the Franks. Borderlands such as Frisia, Lotharingia, Brittany, and Pannonia were places where authority was negotiated, and where lords with local followings managed the demands of distant rulers.

Adaptation took several forms. The first was the growing role of regional aristocrats. In West Francia, the Robertians, the counts of Flanders, and the rulers of Aquitaine, Burgundy, and Brittany built power on regional defense and royal grants. In East Francia, dukes such as those of Saxony and Bavaria acted as regional war leaders. The second was the role of bishops and abbots, who owned land, could mobilize men, and had the resources to build walls and support garrisons, as at Paris and Augsburg. The third was fortification, in the form of fortified bridges, walled towns, and refuges. The fourth was diplomacy, including tribute, truces, baptism, and the granting of territory. Fifth, kings continued to matter, as sources of legitimacy, arbiters between magnates, and leaders of the coalition armies that won major battles, including the Lechfeld.

These developments contributed to what historians describe as regionalization. The word does not mean that kings disappeared or that a single new system replaced them. It means that political life increasingly took place at levels below the empire, in counties, duchies, dioceses, and principalities, where local leaders exercised judicial, military, and fiscal authority. The relationship of these leaders to kings and to the peasants who worked the land is the subject of Chapter 34, which examines lordship, dependency, castles, and the debated concept of feudalism, and this chapter does not attempt to anticipate that analysis. It is sufficient here to observe that violence and fragmentation encouraged local organization, and that this organization would shape later medieval political structures.

Finally, the effects of the pressures should not be reduced to the language of loss. Population movement and settlement created new communities in Normandy, the Danelaw, Ireland, and the North Atlantic. Trade continued, and in some places grew, as Scandinavian merchants linked northern Europe to wider networks. Ecclesiastical culture was preserved and, in places, expanded, with missions to Scandinavia and Slavic lands continuing, as will be examined in Chapter 35. Political creativity, in the form of new alliances, treaties, and institutions of defense, was an equally important feature of the age.

Historical Debate

Did the Carolingian Empire Collapse, or Fragment and Transform?

The question: Should the political history of the later ninth and early tenth centuries be understood as the collapse of an imperial order, brought on by dynastic weakness and external invasion, or as a process of fragmentation in which new regional forms of authority emerged?

Interpretation A: Collapse and crisis. Older narratives, influenced by national historiography and by the language of the annalists themselves, emphasized decline: the loss of central authority, the ravages of Vikings and Magyars, and the end of a unified Carolingian project. In this view, the raids and divisions produced a long period of political disorder, from which new structures arose only later, in response to insecurity.
Interpretation B: Fragmentation and transformation. Much recent scholarship, including work by historians such as Janet Nelson and Timothy Reuter, emphasizes that Carolingian institutions continued to operate at regional levels, that kings and aristocrats remained engaged in negotiated politics, and that the raids, though destructive, also prompted adaptation and produced new political communities. In this view, the empire did not vanish so much as change into a set of kingdoms and principalities in which Carolingian ideas, ecclesiastical structures, and elite families continued to shape events.

Evidence: Support for the first view comes from contemporary laments of ecclesiastical writers, records of destruction and relocation, and the deposition of Charles the Fat. Support for the second comes from continuing charters, assemblies, and church councils, the endurance of Carolingian legitimacy into the tenth century, archaeological evidence of regional economic activity, and the fortification and settlement patterns of the period. Historians also discuss how the emphasis of the annals may reflect the perspective of clerics who suffered disproportionately from raids.

Why it matters: How historians frame the period affects how they explain the later medieval kingdoms. A collapse model tends to portray them as recoveries, while a transformation model traces them to adaptations already in progress. The debate also affects how students understand the roles of Vikings and Magyars, whether as external destroyers or as participants in a shared political world.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence from this chapter that supports each interpretation. Then explain what additional evidence would help determine how severe the disruption was in a particular region, and why regional variation might complicate any single answer.

Multiple Perspectives

Living under Pressure: Perspectives on Raiding, Settlement, and Political Change

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions synthesizing patterns found across many sources. They are not quotations from any named individual, and the groups did not possess equal power or freedom of action.

A monastic community facing attack: Monks, whose houses held land, relics, and portable treasures, saw raiding as both a physical and a spiritual crisis, and frequently interpreted it in providential terms. Communities responded by hiding treasures, moving relics, appealing for royal protection, and sometimes relocating, and the writing of annals and saints' lives preserved their view of events.
A Frankish count or bishop organizing defense: Regional leaders faced immediate demands for protection from communities, while contending with rival aristocrats and remote kings. Building fortifications, negotiating tribute, and leading troops offered a way to demonstrate effectiveness and to gain followers, and could enhance their political standing.
A Scandinavian household in a settlement region: For families who settled in eastern England or the lower Seine region, the move meant acquiring land, forming relationships with local populations, and adapting to unfamiliar legal and religious environments. Archaeological and linguistic evidence suggests a range of experiences, from continued use of Scandinavian names and customs to gradual assimilation.
An English community in a region of Scandinavian settlement: Local populations experienced conquest, tribute, and the reallocation of land, but also new markets and, over time, shared legal practice. Their perspective survives mostly in English sources written from a West Saxon standpoint, which tend to emphasize resistance and later reconquest.
A Magyar mounted warrior group: For leaders and warriors of the confederation, campaigns provided wealth, captives, and prestige, and diplomacy with Byzantium and Frankish rivals offered alternatives to fighting. Their own account of these events survives only through later tradition and through the writings of outsiders, so historians rely heavily on archaeology.
A peasant household subject to requisition and insecurity: Rural families faced the demands of lords, church institutions, and military levies, as well as the risk of capture, loss of stores, and forced labor on fortifications. Their perspective is rarely recorded directly, and historians infer it from charters, estate records, and archaeology of villages.
Section 32.12

Europe after the Carolingian Imperial Order

By the middle of the tenth century, the political landscape of western and central Europe differed markedly from that of 814. The imperial order envisioned by Charlemagne, with a single emperor exercising authority over a vast Christian empire, no longer functioned. The imperial title itself lapsed after 924, and it was not revived until Otto I received it at Rome in 962, an event connected to developments in Chapter 33. What remained was a set of kingdoms and principalities, often ruled by rulers who claimed Carolingian legitimacy or sought to replace it.

In West Francia, kingship alternated between Carolingian and non Carolingian rulers, including Odo, Robert I, and Rudolf, and Carolingian kings such as Charles the Simple and Louis IV, while power increasingly rested with regional principalities such as Normandy, Flanders, Burgundy, Aquitaine, and the Robertian lands around Paris. In East Francia, kingship passed from Conrad I to Henry I of Saxony and Otto I, and the duchies of Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia, and Franconia acted as centers of regional power. The middle kingdom had dissolved into contested zones, including Lotharingia, Upper Burgundy, Provence, and the Italian kingdom, where rulers such as Berengar I competed for power. The West and East Frankish political worlds had diverged, though their rulers still met and their churches remained connected.

Scandinavian settlers were embedded in several regions. In England, areas of Scandinavian settlement were being contested by the kings of Wessex, a story taken up in Chapter 33. In Normandy, a new principality had emerged. In Ireland and the Irish Sea, Norse-Gaelic towns and dynasties continued to play a role, and in Iceland a new society was taking shape. In Scandinavia itself, rulers and chieftains continued to compete, and Christian missions had begun, with consequences examined in Chapter 35. Magyar pressure had reshaped central European politics, contributing to the collapse of Great Moravia, the strengthening of regional leaders in East Francia, and the decline of Bavarian and Italian defenses, and after 955 Magyar political society increasingly moved toward consolidation, changing relations with neighboring Christian powers, and the developments that eventually produced the Christian Kingdom of Hungary around 1000.

Continuities remained. The Church was a major landholder and a source of administrative skill, and its bishops, abbots, and councils continued to shape politics. Carolingian ideas of Christian kingship, including anointing and the ruler's duty to protect the Church, survived and were adopted by new dynasties. Latin literacy and manuscript culture, rooted in Carolingian reform, persisted in monasteries and cathedral schools. Many of the aristocratic families that had risen in the ninth century continued to be important, and the landscape of counties and dioceses remained the framework of local life.

The chapter's account therefore points not to Europe falling apart but to an imperial political order fragmenting while warfare, mobility, settlement, and local adaptation created the conditions for a different political Europe. It remained uncertain in the mid tenth century which of the emerging structures would prove durable. The next chapter asks how some of these fragmented and adaptive political structures consolidated into more durable kingdoms in England, West Francia, and East Francia, and how their rulers built on the foundations laid in the era of division and pressure.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Authority in 814 and c. 900

What Changed

  • A single emperor claiming authority over a vast realm was replaced by several kingdoms and regional principalities.
  • Effective military and judicial authority became more localized, with counts, dukes, and bishops taking greater responsibility for defense.
  • Scandinavian and Magyar groups became active participants in the politics of several regions, and new communities appeared in Normandy, the Danelaw, Ireland, and Iceland.
  • Kings were increasingly elected or accepted by aristocratic assemblies, and non Carolingian rulers appeared in several kingdoms.

What Stayed the Same

  • Imperial ideology and Carolingian dynastic legitimacy remained influential, even when rulers could not exercise broad control.
  • Bishops, abbots, counts, and aristocratic families remained central to political life, and church institutions preserved learning and landholding.
  • Christian kingship, assemblies, oaths, and personal ties remained the basis of political relations.
  • Peasant agriculture and the rhythm of rural labor continued, though with local disruption.

Who experienced change most directly: Communities near navigable rivers, coasts, and the eastern and southern frontiers experienced the most direct disruption, as did monastic houses and towns that were attacked or forced to pay tribute, and regional aristocrats who gained new power. Who experienced greater continuity: Many inland rural households and communities in less exposed regions saw fewer immediate changes in daily routine, although demands from lords and churches could shift.

Why the pattern matters: The comparison shows that the period cannot be reduced to either total collapse or seamless continuity. Authority was reorganized, and this reorganization shaped the later medieval polities.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Carolingian imperial order that Louis the Pious inherited in 814 fragmented over the following century, and how Scandinavian expansion and Magyar raiding interacted with that fragmentation. Its central argument is that the period was one of political decentralization and severe violence, but also of institutional adaptation, negotiated settlement, and the emergence of new regional centers of power.

In 814 the empire was substantial, but it rested on personal loyalty, aristocratic cooperation, and the proceeds of conquest, and it contained a tension between an ideal of imperial unity and a Frankish custom of dividing royal inheritance among sons. Louis's attempt in the Ordinatio Imperii of 817 to reconcile these principles was an ambitious effort that failed to secure lasting agreement, particularly after the birth of Charles the Bald and the opposition of aristocratic and ecclesiastical factions, and Louis's difficulties should be understood in structural, not merely personal, terms.

After Louis's death in 840, his sons fought a war culminating in the battle of Fontenoy in 841, the Oaths of Strasbourg in 842, and the Treaty of Verdun in 843, which divided the empire into three realms of roughly comparable resources. These were dynastic shares, not nations, and the later identification of Verdun with the birth of France and Germany reflects national memory, not ninth century reality.

Further partitions, deaths, and recombinations followed, including the divisions of the middle kingdom in 855 and 870, the imperial coronation of Charles the Bald, the brief reunification under Charles the Fat, and his deposition in 887. From 888, kings were chosen in several regions, and although Carolingian legitimacy remained valuable, no single ruler could organize the whole. Repeated political recombination, and not a clean three way division, characterized the period.

At the same time, Scandinavian expansion, based on shipbuilding skill, trading networks, competition among leaders, and opportunity, took the form of raiding, tribute taking, trade, mercenary service, and settlement. The term "Viking" describes an activity, not a unified people. Attacks along the Frankish rivers, including Paris in 845 and 885 to 886, forced rulers to respond with tribute, fortified bridges, delegation of command, and diplomacy, and the responses strengthened regional leaders such as the Robertians and bishops such as Gauzlin.

Scandinavian activity in Britain, Ireland, and the North Atlantic produced different results, including the Great Heathen Army's campaigns and settlements in England, the Norse-Gaelic society of Dublin and the Irish Sea, and the settlement of Iceland. The Danelaw, a term of later origin, should be used with care, and Alfred's defensive adaptations, including burhs and negotiation with Guthrum, illustrate responses that would become important for the consolidation of England in Chapter 33.

Settlement could serve as a strategy, as in the arrangement associated with Rollo in 911 and the development of Normandy, where rulers and magnates incorporated newcomers into existing political systems. The evidence for this episode is later and partly legendary, but the establishment of a Scandinavian led principality on the lower Seine is well supported, and its eventual importance was considerable.

The Magyars, a mounted confederation that entered the Carpathian Basin around 895 to 896, had already participated in regional diplomacy and conducted major raids from 899. Their mobility, tactics, and the divisions of their opponents made them formidable, and repeated attacks encouraged fortification and regional military leadership in East Francia, Italy, and Bavaria. Their history should not be reduced to raiding or equated with the later Hungarian kingdom, and the battle of the Lechfeld in 955 marks a closing point for the main phase of raiding in the west.

Frontier violence and fragmentation encouraged regionalization, with counts, dukes, bishops, and local communities taking on greater responsibility for defense and order, while kings retained roles as sources of legitimacy and coalition leaders. The interpretation of these developments as either collapse or transformation is a central historical debate, and the evidence supports a view that emphasizes regional variation, severe local suffering, and creative adaptation together.

By the mid tenth century, the imperial order of Charlemagne no longer functioned as he had envisioned. West and East Frankish worlds had diverged, Scandinavian settlers were embedded in several regions, Magyar pressure had reshaped central Europe, and regional rulers exercised growing authority. The next chapter asks how some of these adaptive political structures consolidated into more durable kingdoms.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a large political order can change without simply ending. The Carolingian empire fragmented, but its ideas of Christian kingship, its church institutions, its aristocratic families, and its written culture continued to shape the successor kingdoms. Understanding that continuity guards against the simple picture of a dark age, and understanding the fragmentation guards against the opposite mistake of treating medieval Europe as a seamless extension of Charlemagne's achievement.

The chapter also reveals the interaction of internal and external forces. Dynastic partition, aristocratic ambition, and the personal relations of rulers were not separate from the arrival of ships on the Seine or riders on the Danube plain. Each shaped the consequences of the other. Scandinavian and Magyar groups appear here not merely as attackers but as participants in a wider political world, capable of alliance, trade, service, and settlement. Their histories connect Frankish Europe to the North Atlantic, the Baltic and eastern river systems, and the steppe.

The developments examined here shaped much that follows. The regional principalities and kingdoms that emerged from the period became the frameworks for the medieval political order examined in Chapter 33. The local lordship and fortified settlement that grew from regional defense would become central to the discussion of Chapter 34, and the expansion of Latin Christianity into Scandinavia, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary, examined in Chapter 35, followed the settlement and stabilization of the peoples who had first appeared as raiders. Normandy and the Danelaw show how communities created in this era would later influence England, France, and southern Italy.

Within Volume II, the chapter helps explain why medieval Europe did not take the form of a single empire, and why its political landscape would be marked by a diversity of kingdoms, duchies, and principalities, and by continuing interaction with peoples beyond Latin Christendom. The period shows that violence and adaptation could go together, that fragmentation could strengthen local organization, and that later outcomes were contingent on decisions made in circumstances of uncertainty.
