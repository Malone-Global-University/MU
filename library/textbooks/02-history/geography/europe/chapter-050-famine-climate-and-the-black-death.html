Famine, Climate, and the Black Death

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

For roughly three centuries, much of Europe had grown: more people, more fields, more towns, more markets. In the fourteenth century that growth met a sequence of shocks, unusually wet and unstable weather, a devastating famine in the north, and then a plague that moved across Eurasia and killed a very large share of the people it reached. The result was not the end of Europe, but the end of the demographic world described in the chapters before this one.

In the town of Ypres, in the county of Flanders, municipal accounts recorded the corpses collected at public expense in 1316, and from them modern historians have been able to estimate how many people the town lost in a single summer. The town was one of the great cloth-making centers of northern Europe, dependent on wool, on merchants, and on the grain that fed thousands of workers who did not farm. When the harvest failed in 1315 and again in 1316, the price of bread rose beyond what many households could pay, and the town's accounts list 2,794 corpses collected at public expense, a figure that, set against an estimated population near 28,000, would represent about 10 percent. Neither the municipal record nor the population estimate is a complete demographic count. Ypres was not typical of all Europe, and no single place could be. But it shows what this chapter studies: the point at which a densely connected, commercially active society discovered how thin its margins had become.

A generation later, in the autumn of 1347, ships from the Black Sea world reached the Sicilian port of Messina, and within a few years a disease that contemporaries called simply the pestilence, or the great mortality, had crossed the Mediterranean, entered Atlantic and Baltic Europe, and moved on toward the Russian principalities. The disease is now identified by scientists as plague caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, and it was not only a European event. It affected the Black Sea region, Constantinople, the Mamluk lands of Egypt and Syria, North Africa, and the Islamic territories of Iberia, and it had earlier roots deep in Central Asia. Europeans experienced it as one regional chapter of a Eurasian catastrophe.

This chapter therefore studies three connected but distinct subjects: environmental stress and climate variability, the Great Famine of 1315–1317 and its aftermath, and the Black Death of 1346–1353 with its recurrences. They were not a single crisis. The famine did not cause the plague, and the plague did not strike a Europe uniformly weakened by hunger. They were separate catastrophes, with different mechanisms and different geographies, whose effects overlapped in the memory of survivors and in the long demographic history of the continent.

The chapter also takes seriously the human response. People fled, prayed, fasted, bought grain at ruinous prices, wrote wills, buried relatives in trenches, imposed municipal controls, accused neighbors, and in some cities killed them. Jewish communities in many parts of western and central Europe were falsely accused of poisoning wells and were massacred or expelled. That violence belongs to the history of the plague years, but it must be understood as the work of people with existing hatreds and political interests, not as a consequence of the disease itself.

The evidence for these events has changed greatly in recent decades. Historians have always had chronicles, wills, tax records, manorial accounts, and municipal ordinances. They now also work alongside climate scientists who read tree rings and ice cores, archaeologists who excavate plague cemeteries, palynologists who count fossil pollen, and geneticists who recover ancient pathogen DNA from teeth. These sources do not replace documents, and they raise as many questions as they answer, but they have transformed how confidently the Black Death can be described and how carefully claims about its scale and uniformity must be qualified.

Within Part VIII, Chapter 50 supplies the demographic and environmental shock that makes the rest of the Part intelligible. Chapter 51 turns to the Hundred Years' War, Chapter 52 to labor, wages, revolt, and social change, Chapter 53 to the crisis of the western church, Chapter 54 to the European peripheries, and Chapter 55 to the technological and political transformations around 1500. This chapter deliberately stops short of those subjects. It explains how the crisis happened, how Europeans lived and died through it, and why the population of Europe took generations to recover, while leaving the political and social consequences to the chapters that follow.

Within Volume II, the chapter marks the break between the expansive High Middle Ages studied in Chapters 42 to 49 and the late medieval world of contraction, adaptation, and uncertainty. Students should read it as a hinge, not as a conclusion. The fourteenth century was a time of catastrophe, but it was also a time in which survivors rebuilt households, adjusted prices and wages, altered landholding, and reworked their religious and civic lives. None of those later outcomes was predetermined by the mortality itself.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why High Medieval population growth created vulnerability to harvest failure without making famine or epidemic inevitable.
  2. Distinguish among the kinds of evidence, documentary, archaeological, environmental, and genetic, used to reconstruct famine, climate, and plague, and assess the limits of each.
  3. Describe the fourteenth-century climatic transition with appropriate regional and chronological caution.
  4. Analyze the Great Famine of 1315–1317 as the product of weather, agrarian structure, markets, and politics, rather than climate alone.
  5. Explain what modern science can and cannot say about Yersinia pestis, forms of plague, and the transmission of the Black Death.
  6. Trace the plague's movement from Central Asia and the Black Sea through the Mediterranean and across Europe between 1346 and 1353.
  7. Evaluate competing estimates of mortality and explain why no single Europe-wide figure can be given.
  8. Compare how plague affected towns and countrysides, regions, and different social groups, using evidence cautiously.
  9. Interpret religious responses to plague, including penitence and charity, and explain the persecution of Jewish communities as scapegoating rooted in prior antisemitism.
  10. Assess the long demographic crisis created by recurrent plague, and distinguish what the mortality made possible from what it did not by itself determine.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a society that had expanded for centuries prove so vulnerable to a few years of bad weather, and why should historians resist explaining the Great Famine by climate alone?
  2. How do historians combine chronicles, tax records, tree rings, cemetery archaeology, and ancient DNA, and what happens when these kinds of evidence disagree?
  3. In what sense was the Black Death a European event, and in what sense was it a Eurasian one?
  4. Why do estimates of Black Death mortality vary so widely, and what would count as good evidence for choosing among them?
  5. Was plague socially indiscriminate, or did wealth, occupation, nutrition, and location shape who lived and who died?
  6. How did contemporaries explain the plague, and why did some of those explanations lead to violence against Jews and other groups?
  7. Why did Europe's population fail to recover quickly after 1351?
  8. How can a historian describe the consequences of mass mortality without treating later developments as automatic results?

Historical Context

The preceding chapters of Volume II described a Europe that, between roughly the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, had become more populous, more commercial, and more institutionally elaborate. Chapter 42 traced land clearance, new agricultural techniques, and the expansion of the countryside. Chapter 43 examined towns, merchants, fairs, banking, and regional markets, while Chapter 49 described the everyday lives of nobles, peasants, townspeople, women, children, Jews, and other communities within that expanding society. This chapter begins at the point where that expansion encountered limits, though historians disagree sharply over how those limits should be understood.

Estimating medieval population is difficult, because no state conducted modern censuses. Historians work from tax assessments, hearth counts, manorial surveys, parish and cemetery evidence, and settlement archaeology, and then extrapolate. For that reason, population totals for c. 1300 are best treated as scholarly estimates with wide margins of error. Most specialists agree, however, that population in many regions of Europe was substantially higher around 1300 than it had been around 1000, that densities varied greatly between, for example, Flanders, northern Italy, and the Paris basin on the one hand and Scandinavia, much of the Iberian interior, and the eastern frontier lands on the other, and that in several regions growth had slowed or stalled before 1315.

Chapter 2 introduced the climatic background: the relatively warm conditions in parts of the North Atlantic region often grouped under the label Medieval Climate Anomaly, the later cooler and more variable period sometimes called the Little Ice Age, and the warning that neither label describes a uniform, precisely dated event. That caution governs this chapter. Historians now speak more often of a transition during which seasonal variability, wet summers, and cold episodes became more frequent in many parts of Europe, rather than of a single moment at which the climate changed.

The wider Eurasian setting also matters. In the thirteenth century the Mongol conquests had linked the steppe, China, Central Asia, Iran, the Black Sea, and the eastern Mediterranean within a series of related polities and trade routes. By the 1340s the Golden Horde controlled much of the steppe north of the Black Sea, Italian merchants, especially Genoese and Venetians, maintained trading posts on its shores, and goods, travelers, and animals moved along caravan and maritime routes between Asia and Europe. Chapter 47 and Part VI examined Mediterranean and Black Sea relationships among Latin, Byzantine, and Islamic powers, and those relationships form part of the transmission story told below.

The evidence base for this chapter is unusually varied. English manorial accounts and bishops' registers, the tax and notarial records of Italian communes, municipal ordinances, chronicles such as those of Giovanni Villani and Gilles li Muisis, the literary work of Giovanni Boccaccio, Arabic and Byzantine accounts, physicians' treatises, and later legal records survive alongside scientific evidence unavailable to earlier historians. Their quality is uneven by region: England, northern Italy, parts of France and the Low Countries, and Catalonia are comparatively well documented, while Scandinavia, much of eastern Europe, and many rural areas have far thinner records. This unevenness shapes what historians can claim, and it is worth remembering that a region with few documents is not a region that was spared.

Section 50.1

Europe around 1300: Growth, Vulnerability, and the Problem of Crisis

By the opening of the fourteenth century, Europe was the product of long expansion. Forests had been cleared, marshes drained, and new villages founded, from the polders of Flanders to the settlements established by German-speaking colonists in the lands east of the Elbe, and from the irrigated plains of Lombardy to the frontier territories opened by the Iberian kingdoms. Towns had multiplied and some, among them Paris, Florence, Venice, Genoa, Milan, Ghent, Bruges, and Barcelona, had grown to sizes that required grain to be imported over long distances. Rents, prices, and land values had generally risen across the thirteenth century, and in many districts the number of people with little or no land had grown alongside the number of prosperous households.

That growth had produced real achievements, but also a more tightly bound society. Many households depended on markets for part of their food, and urban workers, especially in cloth towns, depended on grain brought from the countryside or from abroad. Landholdings in some regions had been divided among heirs until many families held plots barely large enough to feed them in a normal year. Lords and governments, for their part, drew revenue from rents, tolls, and taxes calibrated to the productivity of a growing economy. When the weather, harvests, or prices shifted sharply, the consequences travelled quickly through these linked systems.

It would be a mistake, however, to describe Europe in 1300 as simply overpopulated. That older view, often associated with Malthusian reasoning, imagined that population inevitably outran food supply until famine and disease restored a balance. Modern historians are more careful. They note that regions differed greatly, that productivity continued to improve in some places, that poverty depended on who owned land and how it was distributed, that markets sometimes moved grain to where it was needed and sometimes failed to do so, and that political decisions, such as export bans, taxation, and warfare, shaped how a bad harvest became a famine. Population pressure was one factor among several, and it mattered most where institutions and inequality left households without reserves.

The fourteenth-century crisis should therefore be approached as a question rather than a conclusion. Why did growth give way to contraction, and how much of that was environmental, how much agrarian, how much epidemiological, and how much accidental? This chapter treats the Great Famine and the Black Death as separate events with different causes, and then asks how their effects accumulated. The central caution of the chapter is that vulnerability is not destiny: a society that is vulnerable may be struck by disaster or may escape it, and which happens depends on events that cannot be inferred from the society's structure alone.

Section 50.2

Reconstructing Famine, Climate, Disease, and Mortality

Historians of famine and plague face an evidence problem that is partly the reverse of the one faced by political historians. Governments and churches generated records, but the poor, who suffered most, rarely did. A chronicler such as a monk in a Flemish abbey or a notary in an Italian city could write vividly about death, yet he usually saw only part of the picture, often exaggerated for religious or rhetorical effect, and rarely offered numbers that can be trusted. For this reason historians use documentary sources as one line of evidence among several.

Documentary evidence includes chronicles, annals, and letters; manorial accounts recording harvests, rents, and the deaths of tenants; tithe and tax records; grain price series from markets and institutions; wills and probate records, which spike during epidemics; bishops' registers recording institutions to benefices when clergy died; municipal ordinances; and physicians' treatises. Their strengths are specificity and the ability to link events to dates and places. Their weaknesses are patchy survival, institutional bias, and the fact that many deaths, especially in the countryside, went unrecorded. Manorial records, for example, usually count only tenants who owed a payment to the lord when they died, so they do not measure the entire population.

Scientific evidence adds a second layer. Dendrochronology, the study of tree rings, allows annual variation in growth to be dated precisely, and reconstructions of European summer temperature and precipitation show that the years around 1315 were exceptionally wet in many regions. Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica preserve traces of volcanic eruptions. Pollen preserved in lake sediments and peat bogs shows how vegetation and farming changed, and can reveal, for example, where cereal cultivation retreated and woodland regrew. Speleothems, lake and marine sediments, and other proxies contribute additional evidence about temperature and precipitation. Each of these is a proxy, an indirect measure that must be calibrated and interpreted, not a direct record of weather.

Archaeology and biomolecular science have transformed the study of plague. Excavations of cemeteries such as the East Smithfield burial ground in London, opened in 1348 and 1349 to handle the dead, and other mass and individual graves in France, Germany, Italy, and elsewhere have shown how burials were managed, and in some cases have allowed researchers to extract pathogen DNA from teeth. Genomic analysis has shown that the bacterium responsible for the Black Death can be identified with high confidence and placed in the evolutionary history of plague. At the same time, a skeleton cannot reveal how many others died unrecorded, and genetic evidence cannot by itself describe social experience.

The most reliable historical reconstructions therefore ask how independent lines of evidence converge or conflict. Where tax rolls, bishops' registers, and cemetery archaeology all suggest severe mortality in a region, confidence rises. Where documentary evidence suggests catastrophe but pollen evidence shows little agricultural change, the discrepancy is itself a historical problem. This chapter returns to such discrepancies in Sections 50.12 and 50.13, and the Historical Debate feature examines them directly.

Section 50.3

Environmental Change and the Fourteenth-Century Climate Transition

As Chapter 2 explained, Europe's climate has fluctuated across the Holocene, and the high medieval centuries were, in many regions of the North Atlantic world, comparatively favorable for agriculture. Specialists increasingly describe the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries as a period of transition toward a climate that was, on average, cooler and more variable in many European regions, with more frequent episodes of cold winters, wet summers, and shortened growing seasons. That is not the same as saying that a Little Ice Age began in 1300. The timing varied by region and by the climate variable being examined, and different reconstructions disagree on dates and magnitudes.

Several possible drivers have been proposed. A large volcanic eruption in 1257, now widely attributed to Samalas in present-day Indonesia, injected sulfur into the atmosphere, and ice cores suggest a series of further eruptions in the later thirteenth century. Volcanic aerosols can cool surface temperatures for a few years, and some scientists argue that repeated volcanic forcing, interacting with ocean and atmospheric circulation and possibly with reduced solar activity, helped push the Northern Hemisphere toward cooler conditions. Others emphasize that the link between these events and specific European weather in the 1310s is plausible but not demonstrated, and that internal variability in the climate system can produce wet and cool spells without a clear external cause.

What evidence does firmly show is that the years from about 1314 to 1317 were extraordinarily wet in much of northwestern, central, and parts of eastern Europe. Tree-ring evidence, documentary reports of rain and flooding, and records of failed harvests, rotting hay, and fields too wet to plow or sow agree in broad outline. Reports describe wet springs and summers, delayed harvests, grain that sprouted in the sheaf, hay that could not be dried, and difficulty producing salt, which depended on evaporation. Other parts of Europe, particularly the Mediterranean south, experienced different conditions, and their agricultural troubles in the same years were less severe or took different forms.

Climate matters to the argument of this chapter because it altered the probability of harvest failure, not because it determined its human consequences. A wet season would have been a hardship in any century. In 1315 it fell on a society where a large share of households had little margin, where grain markets were tightly linked, and where political authorities had limited capacity to coordinate relief. The same weather in a differently organized society might have caused a smaller crisis. The reverse also holds: climate variability alone would not have produced the Black Death, which required a pathogen, a route of transmission, and human networks that carried it.

Section 50.4

Land, Population, and Food Supply before the Great Famine

Chapter 42 described how medieval agriculture expanded through clearance, drainage, new crops and rotations, and the founding of new settlements. By the early fourteenth century, that expansion had in many regions reached the poorer soils. Cultivation had moved onto heavy clays, thin uplands, sandy heaths, and reclaimed marshland, which could be productive in favorable seasons but were prone to failure in wet or cold years. Pasture and woodland had in some places been reduced, with consequences for fuel, building timber, and the manure that sustained arable yields.

Inequality shaped vulnerability as much as soil. In many regions, holdings had been subdivided among heirs, or recently settled land had been granted in small units, producing a growing class of smallholders and landless laborers who bought part of their food. In England, for example, manorial surveys show many tenants holding only a few acres, while a minority of larger tenants and the lords themselves held much more. Such households could survive in good years by combining farming with wage labor or craft work, but they had little to sell in a bad year and had to buy dear grain with little income. Peasants of different regions faced different legal and tenurial conditions, including free and unfree tenures, and these affected obligations, mobility, and access to lords' help in crisis.

Markets both reduced and increased risk. Trade could move grain from surplus areas to deficit ones, and Italian cities such as Venice and Genoa organized imports from the Black Sea and Sicily. Yet markets also transmitted shortages as merchants and speculators sought higher prices, and local authorities sometimes restricted exports or requisitioned grain for armies. Livestock, a source of food, draught power, fertilizer, wool, and savings, formed another point of vulnerability, because wet weather encouraged diseases of sheep and cattle that, as the next sections show, spread widely in the same years.

Population pressure interacted with this structure rather than acting alone. There is evidence from several regions that real wages were falling, that land was becoming scarcer relative to people, and that food prices had been rising before 1315, although historians dispute how general this was, and some regions, such as parts of northern Italy and Flanders, continued to be commercially dynamic. The best summary is that Europe around 1300 contained many households living near the margin of subsistence, in an economy that offered few ways of cushioning them. That condition made catastrophe possible. It did not make it certain.

Section 50.5

The Great Famine of 1315–1317

The Great Famine began with weather. In the spring of 1315, heavy rain fell across much of northern and northwestern Europe, delaying sowing and spoiling crops. Summer was cold and wet, the harvest was poor, and grain was difficult to dry and store. The next year was not better. Seed had been eaten or was of poor quality, and a second failed harvest in 1316 exhausted reserves that many households had hoped would see them through. Conditions began to improve only in 1317, and full recovery in many places took until the early 1320s. The geographic reach of the famine was extensive, affecting the British Isles, the Low Countries, northern France, the German lands, Scandinavia in some regions, the Baltic coasts, and parts of Poland and the east, though evidence for the east is thinner.

Prices tell part of the story. Grain, the basis of the diet, rose sharply in markets across the region, in some English and Flemish markets reaching several times their ordinary level. Because the poor spent most of their income on bread and other cereals, such increases pushed households into hunger quickly. Chroniclers reported people eating grass, roots, seed grain, and animals normally not eaten, and, in a few accounts, resorting to cannibalism or abandoning children. Such reports must be read cautiously, since chroniclers used conventional images of famine and may have repeated rumor, but the pattern of rising prices, malnutrition, and mortality is confirmed by independent records.

Mortality is hard to measure, but the evidence is sufficiently consistent to establish severity. In Ypres, municipal accounts record 2,794 corpses collected at public expense during 1316. Compared with an estimated population near 28,000, this would represent about 10 percent, though neither the municipal record nor the population estimate constitutes a complete demographic census. In other towns and regions, such as parts of England, mortality estimates range more widely, and many scholars suggest that the Great Famine killed a substantial minority of the population in the worst-hit districts and far fewer in others. These are estimates with real uncertainty, and the famine is best understood as a major regional catastrophe, not a continent-wide collapse. The infants, the elderly, the poor, and migrants who flooded into towns seeking food were especially vulnerable, and malnourished people also became more susceptible to disease.

Livestock disease deepened the crisis. Sheep suffered widespread epidemics in the middle of the decade and cattle disease followed later in the 1310s and early 1320s, reducing wool production, milk, meat, manure, and plowing power. A shortage of salt, which was essential for preserving food and difficult to produce in wet weather, also contributed. Meanwhile, politics intensified hardship. The French king Louis X's campaign in Flanders in the late summer of 1315 stalled in mud, and military activity, requisitioning, and conflicts such as the Scottish invasions of northern England and Ireland strained supplies. Some authorities tried to regulate prices or restrict exports; such measures were often ineffective and sometimes counterproductive.

The social effects extended beyond the death toll. Marriage and baptism records, where they survive, suggest that marriages were delayed and births fell. People migrated in search of food, crime and desperate sales of land rose, and charity strained to the limit, although religious houses and some town governments organized alms and grain distributions. The famine also shaped belief. Preachers and chroniclers often interpreted the disaster as divine punishment, and processions and prayers were organized. These responses prefigured, on a smaller scale, those that would follow the plague a generation later.

Cause and Effect

The Great Famine of 1315–1317

Long-term causes

Population growth and agricultural expansion onto marginal soils; subdivision of holdings and growing numbers of smallholders and landless households; dependence on markets; limited public capacity to store or distribute grain; the shift toward a more variable climate.

Short-term causes

Exceptionally wet and cold weather from 1314 to 1317; consecutive failed harvests; exhaustion of seed and reserves; livestock epidemics; warfare and requisitioning in some regions; ineffective price and export controls.

Event or transformation

Severe harvest failure, soaring grain prices, hunger, and elevated mortality across northern and northwestern Europe from 1315 to 1317.

Immediate effects

High urban and rural mortality in the worst-hit areas; delayed marriages and reduced births; migration and begging; livestock losses; strain on charity and municipal government.

Long-term consequences

A population weakened but not collapsed; continuing poverty and slow recovery; lessons, often unlearned, about the fragility of grain supply; a demographic stagnation that preceded the plague.

Historical complexity

Weather did not cause the famine alone. The same rainfall would have had different consequences in a society with greater reserves, better distribution, and less inequality. The famine also did not cause the Black Death.
Historical Vocabulary
Subsistence Crisis

A subsistence crisis is a period in which a large share of a population cannot obtain enough food to survive normally, typically signaled by sharply rising grain prices, hunger, and increased mortality. Historians distinguish it from a simple bad harvest. A bad harvest becomes a subsistence crisis when low incomes, limited reserves, constrained markets, or political decisions prevent people from obtaining food. The term is useful in this chapter because it directs attention from weather alone to the social conditions that determined who went hungry.

Section 50.6

After the Famine: Incomplete Recovery, Poverty, and Continuing Stress

The decades between the Great Famine and the Black Death are sometimes passed over, as though Europe waited unchanged for 1347. In fact they were a period of uneven recovery and continuing difficulty. Harvests improved after 1317 in many regions, grain prices fell, and some districts regained their earlier productivity. Yet livestock losses took years to replace, and the cattle epidemic of the late 1310s and early 1320s compounded those losses. Poor harvests and local famines returned in several regions during the 1320s, 1330s, and 1340s, and the Mediterranean south and parts of central Europe experienced their own subsistence crises, including a serious famine in Italy in 1346 to 1347 on the eve of the plague.

Demographic evidence for this period is ambiguous. In some regions, population seems to have stagnated or begun to decline before 1347, because of the famine's direct deaths, reduced fertility, and migration. In others, notably parts of Italy and the Mediterranean, growth had been slowing earlier. Historians disagree about how far population was recovering when plague struck. What can be said is that Europe in the 1340s was not simply the prosperous Europe of 1280, and that many households, especially smallholders and urban poor, had less margin than before.

Political and economic strain accompanied environmental stress. The Hundred Years' War, examined in Chapter 51, began in 1337, and its taxes and military demands, together with warfare in Italy and the German lands, weighed on populations. Banking houses in Florence failed in the 1340s, including the major Bardi and Peruzzi firms, amid a broader financial crisis involving sovereign lending, commercial losses, war, and structural weaknesses in the firms themselves. Edward III's unpaid or delayed obligations contributed to their difficulties, but historians no longer treat the English crown's debts as a sufficient explanation for the collapse. The failures reduced credit in some trading networks. Such developments illustrate that Europe on the eve of the plague faced financial, political, and agrarian difficulties, yet it remained a complex, commercially active, and in many places productive society.

Historians therefore reject two simple narratives. One portrays the plague as a bolt from the blue striking a thriving Europe. The other presents famine as having weakened the population so that the Black Death was bound to be devastating. The first neglects the earlier strains. The second confuses vulnerability with causation: the plague was caused by a pathogen and its transmission, and its mortality in regions that had experienced little famine, such as parts of the Mediterranean, was also very high. Prior hardship may have affected how many people died in some places, but the famine did not cause the plague.

Section 50.7

What Was the Black Death? Yersinia pestis and the Evidence

For much of the twentieth century, some scientists and historians doubted that the Black Death had been bubonic plague at all, suggesting instead that it might have been anthrax, a viral hemorrhagic fever, or another disease. The question has now been largely settled. Beginning in the 2010s, researchers extracted ancient DNA from the teeth of victims buried in plague cemeteries, including East Smithfield in London, and from burials in other European sites, and reconstructed the genome of the bacterium Yersinia pestis. Their findings, confirmed by many later studies, showed that this pathogen was present in Black Death burials and that the strain was an ancestor of many later plague lineages. Most specialists now accept that Y. pestis caused the Black Death.

Plague takes several forms. In bubonic plague, the most common, bacteria infect lymph nodes, producing painful swellings called buboes, typically in the groin, armpit, or neck, along with fever, chills, and weakness. Untreated, bubonic plague has a high but not total mortality rate, often cited in a wide range from about one third to well over half, with survival possible. In septicemic plague, bacteria enter the bloodstream and can cause death rapidly, sometimes before buboes form. In pneumonic plague, the lungs are infected, and the disease can spread directly between people through respiratory droplets, with very high mortality if untreated. Contemporary descriptions mention buboes, dark spots on the skin, fever, and, in some accounts, coughing and spitting blood. Historians should not attempt to diagnose individual medieval cases, but the broad clinical picture matches plague.

How the disease spread is more complicated than a familiar textbook formula suggests. The older model held that infected black rats carried infected fleas, which bit humans, and that rat populations therefore drove epidemics. This model is based on observations of plague in twentieth-century Asia, and it is clearly part of plague ecology, but it does not fit every aspect of medieval epidemics. In northern Europe, for example, the speed of spread, the low density of rat populations in some regions, and the seasonal pattern of outbreaks have led researchers to propose other routes. Recent modeling suggests that human ectoparasites, the human flea and the body louse, may have transmitted the bacterium between people in some outbreaks, and pneumonic transmission, while probably not dominant everywhere, would have contributed in cold seasons and crowded settings. The best present position is that the Black Death spread by more than one route and that no single vector account has gained universal acceptance.

The second major issue is the origin and recurrence of plague in Europe. Plague belongs to a long evolutionary history. Earlier pandemics include the Justinianic Plague that struck the Mediterranean world beginning in 541, which affected the Byzantine and wider Mediterranean world and is now also attributed to Y. pestis. The Black Death opened what scholars call the Second Plague Pandemic, which persisted in Europe in recurring waves until the eighteenth century, with the last major Western European outbreak at Marseille in 1720 to 1722. A Third Pandemic began in China in the nineteenth century. The study of ancient genomes continues to refine this picture, and findings should be treated as strong but evolving.

Historical Vocabulary
Pestilence, Great Mortality, and "Black Death"

People living through the epidemic did not call it the Black Death. Contemporaries in Latin and the vernacular languages used words such as pestilentia (pestilence), mortalitas (mortality), and equivalents meaning the great death, and writers in Arabic used terms associated with plague and epidemic. The name Black Death became widely used in English and other European languages much later, with antecedents in earlier Scandinavian and Latin usage, and it became the standard historians' label only in the modern period. Historians use it as a convenient label for the plague of 1346 to 1353, but students should remember that it is a retrospective name and that "plague" in medieval usage could refer to any serious epidemic, not only to disease caused by Y. pestis.

Section 50.8

Plague before Europe: Eurasian Origins and Routes of Transmission

The Black Death entered Europe from the east, but it did not begin there. Plague is a disease of wild rodents and their fleas, and the bacterium persists in natural reservoirs, populations of animals in which it circulates without necessarily causing epidemics among humans. Modern reservoirs exist in parts of Central Asia, including the Tian Shan region, and genetic evidence has long suggested that the lineage ancestral to the Black Death arose in that region. In 2022, a study reported ancient plague DNA from burials in the Chu Valley, in present-day Kyrgyzstan, dated to 1338 and 1339. Gravestones from the same cemeteries carry inscriptions recording deaths from a pestilence, but the surviving inscribed stones do not correspond to the individuals whose DNA was analyzed, so the two lines of evidence are complementary, not identical. This provides strong evidence that the strain existed in Central Asia shortly before the Black Sea outbreaks, though the specific path from there to the west remains uncertain.

How the disease moved from reservoir to human epidemics is debated. Some researchers argue that climatic fluctuations affected rodent populations in Central Asia, triggering spillover into human communities, and that trade and military movements then carried the disease across long distances. Others have proposed that the plague established reservoirs in Europe itself that produced later outbreaks, rather than repeated reintroductions from Asia, though most evidence for the first arrival still points eastward. Both questions remain active areas of research, and students should treat reconstructions of the precise routes as provisional.

The wider historical conditions matter. The Mongol-era Eurasian system, with the Golden Horde, the Ilkhanate, the Yuan dynasty, and Mamluk Egypt, had increased the movement of merchants, armies, envoys, and goods across the steppe and the Silk Road networks, and along the sea lanes of the Black Sea and Mediterranean. Plague could be carried in grain shipments, in the clothing or goods of travelers, on animals and their fleas, and in the bodies of people incubating the disease. Armies and caravans offered conditions for transmission, as did ships, whose crowded holds often contained rodents, fleas, and lice. None of this made the epidemic inevitable, but it meant that a local outbreak in Central Asia could reach the Black Sea in a few years.

A Eurocentric narrative that begins with the plague appearing at Messina in 1347 misses the point. By then the disease was already established across a very wide area. Reports and later accounts describe epidemic mortality in the steppe and in the Black Sea lands before 1347, and Arabic sources record the plague in Egypt and Syria in 1347 to 1349, where it killed many thousands in Cairo and other cities and was described by scholars such as Ibn al-Wardi, who died of it in Aleppo in 1349. Constantinople, the Black Sea ports, and the Islamic Mediterranean were as central to the story as Italian and French cities. The European Black Death was one regional chapter of a Eurasian and North African pandemic, and to study it as such is to see more clearly both what was distinctive about European responses and what was shared.

Section 50.9

From the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, 1346–1348

The plague's arrival in the Black Sea world is associated with the Crimean port of Caffa (Kaffa, now Feodosia), a Genoese trading colony on the coast controlled by agreement with the Golden Horde. In 1345 and 1346, tensions between the Genoese and the Horde's ruler, Jani Beg, led to a siege of the town. Plague seems to have been present among the besiegers and within the colony. The most famous account, written years later by Gabriele de' Mussi, a notary from Piacenza, claims that the Mongol army catapulted infected corpses into the city, and that Genoese ships then carried the disease westward. Historians treat this story with great caution. The text was composed after the events and by an author who may not have been present, the scenario of deliberate biological warfare is not supported by independent evidence, and plague can be transmitted by many routes that do not require such an act. The usual conclusion is that the siege illustrates how war, trade, and disease met in the Black Sea, not that the corpse story is established fact.

Whatever happened at Caffa, ships and traders moved between Black Sea ports and the Mediterranean throughout 1346 and 1347. By 1347 plague was present in Constantinople, where it killed, among others, a son of the emperor John VI Kantakouzenos, who later described the epidemic in his history. It reached Alexandria and other Egyptian ports in the same period, and spread through the Mamluk lands in 1347 to 1349. Muslim and Christian merchants, pilgrims, and soldiers moved along the same networks, and the pandemic therefore struck the Byzantine, Mamluk, and Latin worlds in close succession and with comparable ferocity. The disease did not respect religious or political frontiers.

In the autumn of 1347, a fleet of Genoese galleys reached Messina in Sicily, and chroniclers described crews already sick or dying. Authorities reportedly ordered the ships to leave, but the disease had landed and spread through Sicily and southern Italy. By November, plague had appeared in Genoa and in Marseille, and in January 1348 in Pisa and Venice. Mediterranean ports, linked by regular shipping, became the first points of entry into western Europe, and from them the epidemic moved inland along roads and rivers. The Italian peninsula, the south of France, and the Catalan and Aragonese coast were struck in the winter and spring of 1348. Castile and Portugal followed, and the Muslim kingdom of Granada also suffered, an episode recorded by Ibn al-Khatib, who argued from experience that the disease passed by contagion even though some religious scholars resisted this view.

The first wave of mortality in Mediterranean cities was severe and rapid. Florence, one of the largest cities in Europe, may have lost around half of its population or more in 1348, according to many but not all estimates. Venice, Genoa, Pisa, and Siena were hit hard, and records from Catalan towns and Marseille tell of high mortality and flight. Because Italian cities kept relatively good records and produced literary witnesses, the Mediterranean experience has shaped popular images of the plague, but it was only one regional variant. The Black Death in the north and east followed a different rhythm.

Section 50.10

The Spread across Europe, 1348–1351

From the Mediterranean ports, plague spread across the continent in a pattern that depended on routes, seasons, and local conditions. In France it moved up the Rhône valley and from the Atlantic coast, reaching Paris in the summer of 1348. In Iberia it traveled through the interior, and Alfonso XI of Castile died of plague in March 1350 while besieging Gibraltar. In the Alpine regions and the German lands, it arrived in 1348 and 1349 and continued through 1350. The disease moved most rapidly where roads and rivers were busy, and more slowly, or in some cases not at all, in regions with low population density or poor connections, though historians debate how far apparent exceptions reflect real differences or gaps in the evidence.

England received plague in the summer of 1348, with the traditional landing point recorded at Melcombe in Dorset, and the epidemic reached London in the autumn. It spread through much of England during 1349 and then to Wales and, later that year, to Scotland, while Ireland was infected from the Irish Sea ports in August 1348 and the epidemic spread over the following year. Norway was struck in 1349, according to tradition by a ship arriving at Bergen from England, and Sweden and Denmark followed in 1349 and 1350. The Baltic ports and Hanseatic cities carried plague to the Baltic world, and by 1351 to 1353 the epidemic reached the Rus' principalities, appearing in Pskov and Novgorod in 1352 and in Moscow in 1353. Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary have sometimes been described as lightly affected, but the evidence is thin and recent research suggests that their experience was more complicated than older assumptions allowed.

Timing and intensity varied. Some towns were struck early and severely and others later, and a village could suffer terrible mortality while a neighboring one escaped. The epidemic often peaked in a given locality over a period of months and then declined, but in some regions it lasted more than a year because it spread from household to household or returned. Seasonal patterns also mattered, since reports indicate that plague mortality was often highest in warm months in the Mediterranean and that winter pneumonic spread contributed in some northern outbreaks. Historians therefore treat the Black Death less as a single wave sweeping across Europe at constant speed than as a series of regional epidemics linked by travel and trade.

Not every place was equally affected, and some areas apparently suffered less. Milan, according to some accounts, saw lower mortality in the first wave, perhaps as a result of the severe measures taken by its rulers, but that interpretation is contested and the evidence limited. Parts of the Low Countries, central Europe, and Poland have been proposed as comparatively lightly hit. Such cases remind us that the plague's geography depended on contact and local ecology, and that we should be cautious about assuming uniform devastation, a theme developed in the Historical Debate in Section 50.12.

Section 50.11

Experiencing the Plague: Symptoms, Medicine, Care, and Public Response

Contemporary descriptions of the disease emphasize its suddenness and horror. People sickened with fever, chills, headache, and exhaustion, developed swellings in the groin, armpit, or neck that could grow to the size of an egg or larger, and sometimes dark spots on the skin; death could come within days. In cases where the lungs were affected, coughing and bloody sputum were reported, and chroniclers noted that the disease seemed to pass rapidly within households and among those who nursed the sick. Some recovered, and writers described survivors whose buboes burst. Such accounts confirm the broad clinical picture, though they are shaped by literary convention and by what observers could see.

Learned medicine, examined in Chapter 48, offered explanations but little effective treatment. In October 1348, the medical faculty of the University of Paris issued a report, written at the request of the French crown, attributing the epidemic to a conjunction of planets in 1345 that corrupted the air, a view consistent with the dominant theory that disease arose from corrupt air, or miasma. Physicians recommended bloodletting, diet, regimen, purging, aromatic substances, and avoidance of crowds and of certain foods. Municipal authorities employed physicians, and some, such as Guy de Chauliac, physician to the papal court at Avignon, wrote about the disease and recognized that it was contagious. Medicine did not cure plague, but it was not irrational given contemporary knowledge, and physicians in many places continued to attend the sick at risk to themselves, though others fled.

Most care was provided by families, neighbors, clergy, and religious communities. Households tended the sick with whatever remedies were available, and in many places the burden fell on women, who served as nurses and attended deathbeds. Hospitals, religious houses, and confraternities provided care and burial where they could. Death rates among those who cared for the sick were high, and some communities lost entire convents or hospital staffs. The scale of mortality overwhelmed customary burial. Churchyards filled, new cemeteries were opened, and mass graves were used in many cities, though contemporaries and historians alike emphasized that burial practice varied and that, in many places, individual burials continued.

Authorities responded in ways that foreshadowed later public health. Italian city governments, Venice and Florence among them, appointed commissions or officials to oversee sanitation, burial, and the movement of goods and people in 1348. Some towns restricted entry, regulated markets and the sale of meat and fish, and ordered the cleaning of streets and the removal of waste. In Milan, rulers reportedly took stern measures against households with plague. Over the following decades such measures developed into systems of isolation, and in 1377 Ragusa ordered a period of isolation for arrivals from affected areas, an early step toward the quarantine and lazaretto systems of the fifteenth century and later. These institutions, which appeared first in the Adriatic and Italian city-states, arose from experience of contagion even as official theory continued to emphasize bad air.

Flight was the other common response. Wealthy residents and officials left cities for the countryside, as Boccaccio's literary frame depicts, while the poor often could not. Flight spread disease in some cases and left cities short of officials, notaries, and physicians, provoking criticism and, in some places, legislation. Many ordinary people stayed because they had no alternative or out of religious duty or loyalty. The experience of the plague was thus differentiated by wealth, office, family responsibility, and chance, and the record is shaped by those who could write.

Historical Vocabulary
Miasma and Contagion

Miasma refers to the theory, inherited from ancient medical writers and dominant in medieval learned medicine, that disease was caused by corrupted air, often thought to arise from putrefying matter, stagnant water, or astrological influences. Contagion refers to the idea that disease passes from person to person or via objects. Medieval thinkers did not hold the two ideas in strict opposition. Many accepted that corrupt air caused the epidemic and also that proximity to the sick was dangerous, and practical measures such as flight, isolation, and cleaning could be justified under either view. Students should avoid assuming that medieval people were ignorant of contagion: Ibn al-Khatib and others argued from observation that it was real.

Section 50.12

How Many Died? Mortality and the Problem of Measurement

Students frequently encounter the statement that the Black Death killed one third of Europe, or half, or more than half. These figures are not interchangeable, and none can be treated as established for the continent as a whole. Contemporary writers often gave impressionistic estimates, some claiming that nearly everyone had died in their city, and these have little statistical value. Later writers even attributed to Pope Clement VI an implausibly precise worldwide death toll of 42,836,486. Whatever the provenance of that figure, it is not usable demographic evidence. Modern estimates are indirect reconstructions based on local records, projected to regions and the continent.

The local evidence is often strong. English bishops' registers show that in many dioceses perhaps four in ten of beneficed clergy died during the epidemic. Manorial records allow estimates of tenant mortality, often in the range of 30 to 60 percent, though these vary and are complicated by migration and by the incomplete coverage of the sources. Wills and tax lists in Italian and Catalan towns show very high mortality, and cities such as Florence, Siena, Orvieto, and Barcelona are often said to have lost around half or more of their populations. Cemetery excavations suggest rapid, massive mortality in some places. Yet extrapolating from such records to the continent requires assumptions about regions where documentation is poor.

Estimates for large regions reflect this uncertainty. Some historians, working from English evidence, have argued for mortality in England of between about 40 and 50 percent. Ole Benedictow, in an influential study, has argued for a Europe-wide mortality of approximately 60 percent, higher than most. Others, drawing on more cautious readings of the evidence, suggest overall mortality of roughly one third to one half, with large regional variation. A 2022 study of fossil pollen from many sites across much of Europe found that the long-term effect of the Black Death on agriculture and land use varied regionally, with signs of agricultural decline and woodland regrowth in some regions and continued or expanded farming in others. That research does not deny the plague's severity, but it challenges assumptions that all regions suffered equally.

For students, the practical lessons are these. First, there is a difference between local and regional mortality, and a village or city could lose a very large share of its inhabitants while a region lost less. Second, the figures depend on the baseline population, which is itself uncertain. Third, the Black Death was only the first of many plague epidemics, and some of the demographic effects ascribed to it came from recurrence. Fourth, most scholars agree that the Black Death was among the most severe demographic catastrophes in recorded European history, even though they disagree about how severe.

Historical Debate

How Large and How Uniform Was Black Death Mortality?

The question: Did the Black Death kill a very large and relatively uniform share of Europe's population, perhaps well over half, or was mortality lower overall and sharply variable by region?

Interpretation A: Very high and widespread mortality. Some historians, notably Ole Benedictow, argue that the best local sources, when carefully assessed, show mortality of 50 to 60 percent or more across most regions, and that apparent exceptions result from poor documentation or from underestimated mortality. On this view the plague's high lethality reflects the nature of the disease, and cautious estimates have underestimated it.
Interpretation B: High but regionally variable mortality. Other scholars argue that the evidence supports mortality of roughly one third to one half overall, with substantial variation. They point to the incomplete coverage of the sources, to the difficulty of extrapolating from records that mostly concern tenants, clergy, and townspeople, and to recent environmental evidence, such as pollen records showing little agricultural change in some regions, as indications that some areas were less severely affected.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same kinds of sources: manorial and tax records, bishops' registers, wills, cemetery data, and now environmental proxies. They differ in how they weigh local evidence, how they correct for missing data, and how much weight they give to non-documentary evidence, whose relation to mortality is indirect.

Why it matters: The debate does not concern whether the Black Death was a catastrophe, since both sides agree that it was. It concerns how to explain regional differences in later social and economic change, how to model plague transmission, and how far later developments can be attributed to the scale of mortality versus other factors.

Section 50.13

Unequal Death: Town, Countryside, Wealth, Gender, and Region

Plague could kill anyone, but it did not strike everyone equally. Exposure depended on where people lived and worked, and cities and densely populated villages offered more opportunities for transmission than isolated hamlets. Households with many members, especially those living in crowded, poorly ventilated housing, faced greater risk, and occupations that brought people into frequent contact with others, such as priests, physicians, notaries, innkeepers, and caregivers, involved greater exposure. Merchants and sailors, who moved between infected places, carried and encountered infection. The wealthy often fled to the countryside, though this did not always save them, and the poor stayed in town and suffered heavily.

Whether wealth gave protection remains a subject of research. Some evidence suggests that poorer people died at higher rates, in part because malnutrition and crowded housing increased susceptibility and exposure, while other evidence shows that the rich, including nobles, clergy, and officials, died in large numbers when they could not or did not flee. Archaeological studies of plague cemeteries have in some cases found that victims included people of varied health, rather than only the frail, which supports the claim that plague was indiscriminate in some respects. Other bioarchaeological work suggests that people with prior frailty were at higher risk. These findings depend on particular sites and should not be generalized without caution.

Evidence on age and sex is also mixed. Documentary sources sometimes suggest that men died in greater numbers than women in certain records, but those records, for example of tenants who held land, may reflect who is recorded, not who died. Contemporary chroniclers remarked that the 1361 to 1362 epidemic, discussed in Section 50.15, struck children and young people especially heavily. Historians have proposed prior exposure or immunity among some survivors of the first pandemic as one possible explanation, but the cause of the apparent age pattern remains uncertain. In general, historians avoid sweeping claims about sex- or class-specific mortality and emphasize that results depend heavily on the dataset.

Regional differences were also real. The densely populated Mediterranean cities, Florence, Venice, Barcelona, and others, suffered heavy losses, as did many English and French towns. Some rural districts lost most of their inhabitants, and others a smaller fraction. In some places, the departure of survivors, the abandonment of marginal farms, and the consolidation of holdings changed the settlement pattern, a process examined in Section 50.15.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household in a Plague Year

For a household, plague meant sudden decisions. Families chose whether to leave or stay, who would care for the sick, and whether to seal doors or take in kin. In many towns, notaries were overwhelmed by wills as people attempted to arrange inheritance, and the number of wills drawn up rose sharply and then fell as notaries themselves died or fled. Marriages occurred hastily, and remarriage among survivors was often rapid, as widows and widowers sought to rebuild households and to protect property. Guardianship of orphaned children, who were numerous, became an urgent issue for relatives, guilds, and civic authorities.

In villages, the death of tenants left holdings vacant and animals untended, and in some places crops stood unharvested for lack of labor. Lords, whose revenues depended on rent and service, were affected too, and many lost tenants and officials. Parish priests died in large numbers and were replaced, sometimes by inexperienced clergy, which affected pastoral care. The burden fell on women as nurses, mourners, and sometimes as heads of households and tenants of land. These ordinary experiences, recorded in wills, court rolls, and parish documents, remind us that demographic catastrophe was lived household by household.

Section 50.14

Religion, Fear, Scapegoating, and Violence

Most contemporaries interpreted the plague in religious terms. Many understood it as a sign of divine anger at human sin and responded with penitence, processions, special masses, fasting, and prayer to saints associated with protection from disease, especially Saint Sebastian; in later outbreaks Saint Roch also became one of Europe's most important plague saints, with his cult becoming widespread in the fifteenth century. Wills show increased charitable giving and bequests to churches, hospitals, and confraternities, and survivors founded chantries and chapels. Priests and religious worked to provide the sacraments, and many died doing so. Some people gave up religious practice, according to critics, but documentary evidence suggests that, for most, the epidemic intensified rather than weakened devotion. Apocalyptic interpretations circulated, though they should not be treated as universal.

Among the most striking responses were the flagellant processions that spread in 1349, especially in the German lands and the Low Countries. Groups of penitents marched from town to town, publicly scourging themselves in disciplined rituals, and attracted large crowds. Such movements had earlier precedents in the thirteenth century. In 1349 they developed a more radical character, some groups claiming spiritual authority independent of the clergy, and in several places flagellant processions were associated with attacks on Jews. Pope Clement VI condemned the movement in October 1349, and secular and church authorities suppressed it, although similar practices recurred.

The most tragic response was the persecution of Jewish communities. Rumors spread, beginning in 1348 in the western Alps, that Jews had poisoned wells and water sources to spread the disease. In Savoy, in September and October 1348, Jews were arrested at Chillon and elsewhere and tortured, and confessions extracted under torture were circulated as evidence. Similar accusations and trials spread across Switzerland, southern Germany, and the Rhineland, and in 1349 Jewish communities were destroyed in towns including Basel, Freiburg, Erfurt, Cologne, Mainz, and many others. At Strasbourg, in February 1349, a large number of Jews, reported in later accounts as around two thousand, were burned, though such figures are uncertain. Jews were also expelled, their property seized, and their debts cancelled, and communities across a wide region ceased to exist, though some resettled later.

These accusations were false. Jews died of plague like their neighbors, and no evidence exists that they poisoned wells. The violence cannot be explained by the epidemic alone. It drew on long-standing Christian antisemitism, on earlier accusations including the so-called lepers' plot of 1321 in France, in which lepers and Jews were accused of poisoning wells, on economic resentments, since Jews were often moneylenders and debtors saw an opportunity to cancel obligations, and on political rivalries within towns, where factions used the persecution to advance their interests. Some rulers and magistrates tried to protect Jews, and Pope Clement VI issued statements denying that Jews caused the plague and urging protection, but papal authority was limited, and in several towns councils yielded to popular pressure or participated. In other regions, such as Poland under Casimir III, Jewish communities were treated differently, and some Jewish refugees found shelter there, though this should not be exaggerated.

Other groups were also suspected in some places, including foreigners, beggars, pilgrims, and lepers, though the evidence for these cases is thinner and varies by region. The persecutions reveal how fear and misinformation, in the hands of people with their own interests, turned a natural catastrophe into organized violence. They also show that the plague did not simply produce solidarity. For Jewish communities, the years 1348 to 1351 marked one of the most destructive episodes of the Middle Ages, with consequences for settlement, migration, and the geography of Jewish life in Europe.

Multiple Perspectives

Responding to the Plague: Differing Experiences

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns found in many sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and these groups did not have equal power, resources, or responsibility.

From the perspective of a town council: Faced with mass death, dwindling officials, food supply problems, and fear, councils sought to maintain order, regulate burial and markets, and show that they were acting. Some adopted sanitary and movement controls, and, in some towns, councils under pressure from guilds and crowds took part in violence against Jewish residents, while in other towns they tried to prevent it.
From the perspective of a Jewish community in the Rhineland or Alps: Residents were themselves dying of the epidemic when they were accused of causing it. Community members faced arrest, torture, forced conversion, expulsion, or death, and had to decide between flight, appeals to rulers and papal protection, and, in some cases, desperate acts. Their experience was one of persecution layered on top of the same disease that struck their neighbors.
From the perspective of a parish priest or monk: Clergy were expected to provide the sacraments, bury the dead, and comfort the dying, which exposed them to infection and produced high mortality in many dioceses. They interpreted the epidemic as a call to repentance, organized processions and prayers, and sometimes struggled with the shortage of priests and the pressure to administer the last rites quickly.
From the perspective of a poor urban household: Without resources to leave town, people faced crowded housing, loss of work, and dependence on charity. They buried their dead as best they could and weighed the risk of caring for sick relatives against the risk of abandoning them, while survivors sometimes found sudden opportunities in vacated houses, tenancies, and workshops.
Section 50.15

Recurrent Plague and the Long Demographic Crisis

The Black Death did not end in 1351. Plague returned repeatedly in the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, sometimes in widespread epidemics and sometimes in local outbreaks. In 1361 to 1362, a major epidemic struck much of Europe, in England sometimes called the children's plague because contemporary chroniclers repeatedly remarked that it struck children and young people especially heavily. Prior exposure or immunity among some survivors of the first pandemic has been proposed as one explanation for this age pattern, but its causes remain uncertain. Further epidemics occurred in 1369, in the mid-1370s, and in many later years, and from the late fourteenth century onward plague was a recurring presence in European towns. Historians debate whether new waves came from reintroduction from outside Europe or from local reservoirs, and different outbreaks may have had different origins.

Recurrent epidemics prevented population recovery. In an ordinary demographic regime, a population that has lost a large share of its members may recover within a generation or two, as survivors marry earlier, have more children, and migrants fill gaps. Plague's repeated return, together with other diseases and episodes of famine and war, repeatedly removed new cohorts of children and young adults, and population in many regions remained low or continued to decline into the fifteenth century. In England and in many other regions, historians commonly find that population did not begin to recover clearly until the later fifteenth or early sixteenth century, although the timing and pace differed greatly, and some parts of the Mediterranean recovered earlier.

The result was a changed landscape. Across much of Europe, villages shrank or disappeared. In England, thousands of deserted medieval villages have been identified, though many were abandoned over a long period and for varied reasons, including the consolidation of holdings and the conversion of arable land to sheep pasture. In the German lands, historians use the term Wüstungen for deserted settlements, many of which date from the later Middle Ages. Marginal land opened in the thirteenth century was often abandoned, and cultivation retreated to the better soils. Towns also lost population, though some recovered more rapidly through migration from the countryside.

Regional trajectories diverged. Some areas, for example parts of Italy and Iberia, saw recurrent epidemics and population stagnation, while others, with lighter or later mortality, recovered more rapidly. Eastern and northern regions followed different timelines, and the pattern of settlement across Europe became more uneven. The long demographic crisis explains why the fifteenth century was not a simple bounce-back. It was a century in which survivors and their descendants lived in a society with fewer people, adjusting institutions and expectations, while the threat of epidemic remained.

Section 50.16

Catastrophe and Transformation: What Changed, and What Did Not?

The most immediate consequence of mass mortality was a change in the ratio of people to land and to labor. With fewer workers, landlords and employers found it harder to obtain labor at old rates, while survivors could often find better holdings and, in many places, higher wages. Prices of grain, which depended on the number of consumers, tended to fall relative to those of labor-intensive goods, and the diet of many people shifted, with more meat and ale in some regions. Abandoned marginal land and vacant tenancies altered patterns of landholding. These changes created new pressures on lords and governments and new opportunities for tenants and workers.

But the chapter must stop at this point. How those pressures and opportunities worked out depended on law, lordship, government policy, markets, and collective action, and they differed dramatically across Europe. In some regions, lords and rulers attempted to restrain wages and mobility by legislation, in others tenants successfully bargained for better terms, and in some areas lords tightened their control over peasants. Peasant resistance, urban unrest, changes in serfdom, and changing relations between elites and common people belong to Chapter 52. The war between France and England, which interacted with plague in complex ways, belongs to Chapter 51, and the crisis of the church and renewed demands for reform belong to Chapter 53. This chapter's task is to explain the demographic and environmental shock, not to narrate all that followed.

Equally, students should avoid attributing to the plague outcomes it did not automatically produce. The Black Death did not by itself end serfdom, since serfdom declined in some regions and intensified in others. It did not create capitalism, cause the Renaissance, or produce modern Europe. Such claims treat a catastrophe as a machine for producing history. A more careful view is that the plague altered the conditions within which people made choices, and the later outcomes arose from those choices, from institutions, and from other events, including wars, technological developments, and recurrences of disease.

Nor was the plague the end of the older world. Many structures continued: the manorial system in its various forms, the church and its institutions, towns and guilds, trade networks, and dynastic states. Europeans rebuilt, adapted, and sometimes learned. Municipal health boards, the early quarantine systems of Adriatic and Italian cities, and the growth of charitable and medical institutions are examples of adaptation. The fourteenth century thus presents a mixture of rupture and continuity, which later chapters of Part VIII explore.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Europe's Population and Landscape, c. 1300 to c. 1450

What Changed

  • Population fell sharply in many regions after 1347 and, because of recurrent epidemics, stayed low for generations
  • Marginal land was abandoned in many areas, and some villages disappeared
  • The balance between labor, land, and prices shifted, creating new pressures and opportunities explored in Chapter 52
  • Public authorities, especially in Italian and Adriatic cities, developed new health measures

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture remained the basis of the economy and the livelihood of most people
  • The church, the manor, the town, and dynastic monarchy continued as central institutions
  • Trade networks across the Mediterranean, North Sea, and Baltic persisted, and indeed carried later epidemics
  • Learned medicine continued to rely on humoral and miasmatic frameworks

Who experienced change most directly? Survivors who inherited vacant holdings or rose in wages experienced the changes as opportunities; lords whose revenues fell, clergy who died in large numbers, and Jewish communities that were destroyed experienced them very differently. Rural smallholders in regions of continued lordship experienced greater continuity in obligations. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a demographic shock does not have a single social meaning.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the environmental, agrarian, and epidemic crises that ended the demographic expansion of the High Middle Ages. Europe around 1300 was more populous, more urbanized, and more commercially integrated than it had been three centuries earlier, and in many regions cultivation had spread onto marginal land while holdings had been subdivided and many households depended on markets for food. These conditions created vulnerability, but the chapter has insisted that vulnerability was not destiny, and that explanations based on simple Malthusian reasoning neglect inequality, institutions, markets, and chance.

The chapter first considered how historians know what they know. Chronicles, manorial accounts, tax records, wills, and bishops' registers are now combined with tree rings, ice cores, pollen, cemetery archaeology, and ancient DNA. Each source has strengths and limitations, and the most reliable conclusions emerge where independent lines of evidence converge, while discrepancies among them define live scholarly problems.

Environmental change formed the backdrop. Europe was entering a more variable and, on average, cooler climate regime, but the transition was gradual, regionally uneven, and not a single event. Volcanic eruptions, ocean and atmospheric variability, and possibly solar changes have been proposed as contributing factors, but the exceptionally wet years of 1314 to 1317 are far better documented than their causes.

The Great Famine of 1315 to 1317 was the first great catastrophe. Repeated harvest failures, soaring grain prices, livestock epidemics, and war combined with the fragility of an unequal agrarian society to produce mass hunger and elevated mortality, especially in northern and northwestern Europe. Municipal accounts from Ypres record 2,794 corpses collected at public expense in 1316, which against an estimated population near 28,000 suggests a loss of about a tenth, though this is not a complete demographic count. The famine was a regional catastrophe, not a uniform continental collapse. Recovery was incomplete, and subsistence crises, livestock losses, financial failures, and warfare continued to strain Europe in the following decades.

The Black Death was a distinct disaster. Ancient DNA has established Yersinia pestis as its cause, and the epidemic belonged to a Eurasian pandemic that originated in the plague reservoirs of Central Asia and moved through the Mongol-era networks of the steppe and Black Sea to Constantinople, the Mamluk lands, and the Latin Mediterranean. Its transmission within Europe was more complicated than the old rats-and-fleas formula, probably involving several routes. The story of siege and corpses at Caffa is a later and uncertain account, not established fact.

From the autumn of 1347, the disease spread through Mediterranean ports, across France, Iberia, and the British Isles, and into the German lands, Scandinavia, the Baltic, and the Rus' principalities by 1353, with varying timing and intensity. People experienced sudden illness, painful swellings, and death within days. Learned medicine, based on miasma and astrology, could not cure it, and families, clergy, and municipal authorities improvised care, burial, and early public health measures that developed into the institutions of quarantine.

Estimates of mortality range from roughly one third to well over half of the population, depending on region and author, and no single Europe-wide figure can be defended. Local mortality could be extreme, and recent evidence suggests that regional effects varied. Death was not wholly indiscriminate: exposure and vulnerability differed by place, occupation, wealth, and household, though the evidence on class, age, and sex remains dataset-dependent.

Religion shaped responses, from processions, penitence, and charity to flagellant movements. The same fear that produced devotion also produced violence. Jewish communities, falsely accused of poisoning wells, were tortured, massacred, and expelled in Savoy, Switzerland, and the German lands, drawing on existing antisemitism and on political and financial motives, and not on anything the disease itself required.

Plague returned repeatedly, and recurrent epidemics, together with other pressures, kept population low through the fifteenth century in many regions. Villages were deserted, marginal land was abandoned, and settlement patterns shifted. The immediate consequence was a change in the ratio of people to land and labor, but how that change played out depended on law, lordship, government, markets, and collective action. These subjects belong to Chapters 51 through 55, and this chapter has avoided treating the plague as an automatic cause of the end of serfdom, the rise of capitalism, or the Renaissance.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Great Famine
The subsistence crisis of 1315 to 1317 in northern and northwestern Europe, caused by persistent wet weather and repeated harvest failure interacting with agrarian vulnerability.
Subsistence crisis
A period when many people cannot obtain enough food, marked by high grain prices, hunger, and increased mortality, and shaped by incomes, reserves, markets, and politics as well as harvests.
Harvest failure
A crop yield low enough to threaten food supply, which becomes a famine only when social and economic conditions prevent people from obtaining food.
Murrain
A general term for epidemic disease in cattle and other livestock, which in the early fourteenth century reduced food, traction, and manure.
Marginal land
Land of poor or unreliable productivity, such as heavy clay, thin upland soil, or reclaimed marsh, cultivated during medieval expansion and vulnerable to abandonment in bad years.
Malthusian explanation
The argument that population grows until it outruns food supply and is then reduced by famine or disease, now treated by most historians as insufficient to explain fourteenth-century crisis.
Proxy evidence
Indirect evidence, such as tree rings, ice cores, or pollen, from which past climate or vegetation is inferred.
Dendrochronology
The dating and study of tree rings, used to establish annual chronologies and reconstruct past climate.
Ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from archaeological remains, used to identify pathogens such as Yersinia pestis in victims of past epidemics.
Yersinia pestis
The bacterium that causes plague, identified through ancient DNA in Black Death burials.
Bubonic plague
The most common form of plague, marked by swollen lymph nodes called buboes and fever.
Septicemic plague
A form in which bacteria enter the bloodstream, often producing rapid death.
Pneumonic plague
A form infecting the lungs that can spread directly between people through respiratory droplets.
Reservoir host
An animal population in which a pathogen persists without necessarily causing epidemics among humans.
Vector
An organism, such as a flea or louse, that transmits a pathogen between hosts.
Second Plague Pandemic
The long series of plague outbreaks beginning with the Black Death in the 1340s and continuing in Europe until the eighteenth century.
Justinianic Plague
The earlier plague pandemic beginning in 541, also attributed to Y. pestis and affecting the Mediterranean world.
Pestilence
The usual contemporary word for the epidemic, referring to serious epidemic disease in general.
Miasma
The theory that disease arose from corrupted air, the dominant explanation in learned medicine.
Contagion
The idea that disease passes from person to person or through objects, recognized by some medieval writers such as Ibn al-Khatib.
Quarantine
Enforced isolation of people or goods from affected places for a set period, developed in Adriatic and Italian ports after the Black Death.
Flagellants
Penitents who publicly scourged themselves, whose processions spread in 1349 and were condemned by Pope Clement VI.
Scapegoating
Blaming a vulnerable group for a catastrophe, as when Jews were falsely accused of poisoning wells.
Well-poisoning accusation
The false charge that Jews, lepers, or others spread disease deliberately, which preceded and drove massacres in 1348 to 1351.
Pogrom
A violent attack on a minority community, here applied to the massacres of Jews during the plague years.
Recurrent epidemic
A later outbreak of plague after the first wave, which prevented population recovery.
Deserted village
A settlement abandoned in the later Middle Ages, evidence of demographic and agrarian change, though abandonment had varied causes.
Golden Horde
The Mongol-successor state controlling the steppe north of the Black Sea, central to Black Sea trade and plague routes.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a society that had grown for centuries could be reshaped by events that were partly environmental, partly biological, and partly the product of human organization. The famine and the plague were different catastrophes, but each exposed how closely Europeans were linked to one another and to the wider Eurasian world, through grain markets, shipping, armies, and trade routes.

It also shows how historical knowledge is made. The Black Death is now studied through ancient DNA, tree rings, and pollen, as well as chronicles and wills, and the most important lesson of that work is humility. Scientific evidence has settled some questions, such as the identity of the pathogen, and has opened others, such as regional variation in mortality and the routes of transmission. Students should carry that habit of weighing evidence into later chapters.

The chapter's argument for Volume II is that the High Middle Ages ended not in a single collapse but in a series of shocks whose effects unfolded over generations. Many institutions endured, and others were tested. The persecution of Jewish communities, the early development of public health, the strain on clergy and lords, and the abandonment of villages all belong to this transition, and each shaped how Europeans experienced the century that followed.

Chapter 51 now turns to the Hundred Years' War, in which dynastic conflict, military change, and taxation interacted with the demographic world described here. Chapter 52 will examine the labor, wages, and revolts that followed mass mortality. What this chapter has tried to provide is the foundation: a clear account of how the shock occurred, and a caution that what came next was shaped by human choices, not by the catastrophe alone.
