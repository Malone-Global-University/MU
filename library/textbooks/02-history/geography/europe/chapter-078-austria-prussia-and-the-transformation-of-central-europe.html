Austria, Prussia, and the Transformation of Central Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 1648 no observer could have predicted that a scattered collection of sandy, war-damaged Hohenzollern territories would, within a century, fight the Habsburg monarchy to a standstill. Central Europe's transformation between the Peace of Westphalia and the Treaty of Hubertusburg was not the triumph of a destined German state. It was the result of wars, loans, recruiting districts, negotiated privileges, and the persistence of an imperial constitution that most contemporaries still took seriously.

Consider the Imperial Diet, which after 1663 no longer assembled and dispersed in the old way but sat permanently at Regensburg on the Danube, its delegates trading drafts, precedence disputes, and procedural objections for generation after generation. Consider, at roughly the same time, a very different scene in the territories ruled from Berlin and Königsberg, where a ruler known to later generations as the Great Elector was assembling a permanent army, collecting taxes that provincial estates had not freely voted, and trying to hold together lands that lay hundreds of kilometers apart. Consider, finally, the court in Vienna, where Leopold I governed a monarchy that was not a single state at all but a dynastic union of Austrian duchies, the Crown of Bohemia, and the Kingdom of Hungary, each with its own laws, assemblies, and privileged elites. These three settings, the Empire, Brandenburg-Prussia, and the Habsburg lands, together form the subject of this chapter.

The chapter's responsibility, within the Authoritative Chapter Outline, is to trace Habsburg recovery, Brandenburg-Prussia, military reform, bureaucracy, dynastic rivalry, and the changing Holy Roman Empire. It asks how the Austrian Habsburg monarchy and Brandenburg-Prussia became competing great powers between 1648 and 1763. The answer requires attention to war and finance, but also to law, dynastic inheritance, confessional politics, the bargaining power of landed elites, and the ordinary burdens borne by peasants, townspeople, soldiers, and religious minorities who lived inside these expanding systems of government.

The chapter also insists on several distinctions that modern vocabulary tends to blur. The Holy Roman Empire was not the Austrian monarchy, although the Habsburg dynasty held the imperial title almost continuously. Austria was not Germany, and Prussia was not Germany. Brandenburg lay inside the Empire, while the Duchy of Prussia lay outside it, and that legal fact shaped the royal title Frederick I assumed in 1701. The Habsburgs governed some territories inside the Empire, some outside it, and some, like Hungary, under a constitutional arrangement that no Austrian or Bohemian official could override at will. Avoiding the retrospective assumption that these two states were embryonic components of a later German nation is one of the central methodological tasks of the chapter.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Rulers wrote political testaments intended to instruct their successors. Treaties and imperial recesses survive in printed and archival form. Fiscal and military records, regimental lists, recruitment ledgers, and the registers of provincial estates allow historians to reconstruct how states extracted men and money. Court artists, engravers, and chroniclers produced representations of monarchy meant to impress rival powers and subjects alike. Against these official sources historians must set the records of peasants' petitions, urban complaints, confessional disputes, and Jewish community negotiations, which reveal how policies felt to those who were governed rather than those who governed.

Within Volume III, this chapter belongs to the movement from confessional conflict to the consolidation of states. Chapter 67 explained how the Thirty Years' War ended in 1648 and why the settlement preserved the Empire. Chapter 74 introduced the general concept of the early modern state, and Chapter 75 followed the Habsburgs through their dynastic empire. Chapter 78 applies those frameworks to a single region and shows that state formation took divergent forms. Chapter 79 will place the northern, eastern, and Ottoman powers within the same European order, and Chapter 80 will provide the comprehensive account of the wars of the balance of power in which Austrian and Prussian armies fought.

The chapter is organized in five divisions, comprising nineteen historical sections and a final review section. Division I establishes the Empire's institutions and the beginning of Habsburg recovery. Division II examines the Austrian Habsburg monarchy, its Hungarian frontier, and its dynastic and administrative reconstruction. Division III traces the rise of Brandenburg-Prussia. Division IV explains the wars through which the two powers became rivals. Division V widens the lens to the other German states, to society, and to a comparative evaluation of Austrian and Prussian state capacity.

Historical Context

The Thirty Years' War, examined in Chapter 67, left Central Europe materially damaged and politically reconfigured. Population losses varied sharply by region. Some districts of Pomerania, Brandenburg, Württemberg, and the Palatinate suffered severe depopulation, while others escaped relatively lightly, and modern scholarship rejects both the older image of uniform catastrophe and the opposite claim that damage was negligible. The Peace of Westphalia of 1648 confirmed the territorial rights of the imperial princes, recognized Calvinism alongside Catholicism and Lutheranism, regulated confessional relations through a principle of parity in imperial institutions, and preserved the Empire as a constitutional framework. It did not create a centralized monarchy. It also left the Habsburgs, who held the imperial title, with their hereditary lands largely intact but with their ambitions to impose confessional and political uniformity on the Empire checked.

The Habsburg dynasty ruled a cluster of territories that fell into three broad groups. The Austrian hereditary lands, including the archduchies of Upper and Lower Austria, Styria, Carinthia, Carniola, and Tyrol, formed the dynastic core. The Crown of Bohemia, comprising the Kingdom of Bohemia, the Margraviate of Moravia, and Silesia, had been reorganized after the Bohemian defeat at the White Mountain in 1620, when the Habsburgs confiscated rebel estates, reinforced Catholicism, and issued a revised provincial ordinance in 1627 that strengthened the ruler's hand. The Kingdom of Hungary, partly under Ottoman occupation, retained its own diet, laws, and noble liberties. Beyond these lay other Habsburg possessions, whose later history will be addressed in the relevant sections of this chapter. Chapter 75 examined the wider dynastic empire and its Spanish branch, which ended in 1700, and the Austrian branch is the focus here.

Brandenburg, an electorate within the Empire, had been ruled since the fifteenth century by the Hohenzollern dynasty. Through inheritance and marriage the dynasty acquired additional territories, including the Duchy of Prussia, which had been a fief of the Polish crown, and the western lands of Cleves, Mark, and Ravensberg. These possessions did not form a continuous territory. Each retained its own estates and customs, and the Hohenzollerns could not simply command them. At the same time, the dynasty's ruler held an electoral vote in the imperial election, a status that gave Brandenburg diplomatic weight out of proportion to its poor soils and limited population.

The wider international environment shaped both powers. France under Louis XIV, examined in Chapter 76, pressed on the Empire's western frontier and exploited the divisions among imperial princes. The Ottoman Empire, treated in its wider European setting in Chapter 79, remained a formidable military power on the Hungarian frontier. Sweden controlled territories on the southern Baltic coast after 1648, and Poland-Lithuania remained a major regional power until the later seventeenth century. Russia, which will become increasingly important in the later sections, was not yet a regular participant in Central European diplomacy in the way it would become by the 1750s.

The sources for this chapter include imperial constitutional texts and recesses, treaty texts, diplomatic dispatches, the records of provincial estates, fiscal and military ledgers, rulers' political testaments, memoirs, engravings, and court art. Historians must read these with attention to the audiences for which they were produced. A political testament, for instance, was a private instruction written by a ruler to his successor, and it expresses what the ruler hoped to achieve, not necessarily what his government actually accomplished. Fiscal statistics from this period are often incomplete, calculated in different currencies, and complicated by the distinction between nominal and effective strength. Wherever the chapter uses numbers, it identifies their limitations.

Section 78.1

Central Europe after the Peace of Westphalia, 1648

The treaties of Osnabrück and Münster, concluded in October 1648, ended thirty years of war in the Empire, but they did not return Central Europe to a prewar condition. Chapter 67 narrated the course of that war and the negotiation of the peace. This section takes the settlement as a starting point and asks what kind of political world it left behind. The answer is a world defined less by decisive centralization than by the entrenchment of legal pluralism: an elected emperor, several hundred territorial rulers and corporate bodies with recognized rights, and a set of confessional arrangements that gave Catholics, Lutherans, and Calvinists a place in the imperial order.

The war's material consequences must be described with care. Contemporary lamentation, later nationalist memory, and nineteenth century historiography all encouraged an image of total devastation in which a third or more of the population vanished everywhere. Modern demographic research instead shows pronounced regional variation. Some districts of Pomerania, Mecklenburg, Brandenburg, Thuringia, and the southwest suffered heavy mortality from violence, famine, flight, and epidemic disease, with losses locally well above a third. Other districts, especially in the north and northwest and in some Alpine territories, recovered quickly or were scarcely affected. Estimates of aggregate loss are therefore uncertain and depend on the region and the method of calculation. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that recovery in the most heavily damaged areas, including much of Brandenburg, became a political problem for rulers who needed taxpayers and recruits.

The settlement strengthened the position of territorial rulers within the Empire. The imperial estates, meaning the electors, princes, and cities with seats in the Diet, were confirmed in their rights of territorial lordship, and they received the right to conclude alliances with foreign powers, provided these were not directed against the emperor or the Empire. This provision has often been read as granting sovereignty, but historians generally emphasize that it was a limited authorization within a constitutional order, and that rulers who exercised it continued to be bound by imperial law and by the jurisdiction of the imperial courts. Territorial rulers governed their lands with considerable autonomy, but they remained embedded in an imperial legal system that offered subjects and rival rulers avenues of appeal.

Religious arrangements were similarly consequential. The Peace extended the Peace of Augsburg of 1555 to Calvinists, adopted 1624 as the normative year for determining the confessional status of ecclesiastical properties, and instituted procedures of parity and consensus on religious matters within imperial bodies. In practice the Diet divided into two confessional bodies, the Corpus Catholicorum and the Corpus Evangelicorum, when confessional matters arose, and such matters could not be settled by simple majority. The Habsburgs, however, retained the right to impose Catholicism in their hereditary lands, with limited exceptions such as three "peace churches" later allowed for Silesian Protestants. This asymmetry, with greater confessional pluralism in the Empire than within Habsburg territories, shaped relations between the dynasty and Protestant princes for the rest of the period.

Territorial change also mattered. Sweden received Western Pomerania, Bremen and Verden, and Wismar, giving it a presence in the Empire as an imperial estate as well as a Baltic power. France acquired rights in Alsace whose precise legal meaning remained disputed. Brandenburg received Farther Pomerania, along with the secularized bishoprics of Halberstadt, Minden, and Kammin and the reversion of Magdeburg, which it took up in 1680. Bavaria retained the electoral dignity previously held by the Palatinate, and a new eighth electorate was created for the restored Palatine line. The Habsburgs lost little territory in the settlement but gained no constitutional supremacy.

Section 78.2

The Holy Roman Empire: Emperor, Imperial Diet, Courts, and Territorial Princes

The Holy Roman Empire after 1648 is easier to misunderstand than almost any other political structure of early modern Europe. It had no capital in the sense that Paris was the capital of France, no standing imperial army paid from imperial revenue, and no bureaucracy that reached ordinary subjects. Observers from the seventeenth century onward, notably the jurist Samuel von Pufendorf, who wrote under the pseudonym Severinus de Monzambano in 1667, found it difficult to classify, and his much quoted description of it as resembling a monstrous body reflected a frustration with categories borrowed from Aristotle's typology of states. Yet it would be a serious error to conclude that the Empire simply did not function. It governed disputes, coordinated defense, regulated confessional relations, and gave smaller political entities a legal shelter that they would not otherwise have had.

At the head of the structure stood the emperor, elected by the electors, who numbered eight after 1648 and nine after the elevation of Hanover in 1692, a status finally confirmed in 1708. At each election the electors imposed an electoral capitulation on the candidate, binding him to respect the rights of the estates. From the fifteenth century the imperial title had been held almost continuously by the Habsburg dynasty, but it was an elective office, and imperial authority depended on negotiation. The emperor could grant titles, confirm privileges, convene the Diet, and exercise judicial authority through the Reichshofrat, the Imperial Aulic Council, a court which sat in Vienna and heard cases from across the Empire. The Habsburgs' possession of the title gave their other territories a legal and symbolic prestige, but the Empire was never merely their patrimony.

The Imperial Diet, or Reichstag, was organized in three colleges. The college of electors, the college of princes, which included secular princes, prelates, and counts grouped in collective votes, and the college of imperial cities each deliberated and voted, and a decision of the Diet required agreement among the colleges and the emperor's consent. After the Diet of 1653 to 1654, whose recess strengthened the territorial rulers' ability to levy contributions from their subjects for fortifications and garrisons, the Diet convened again in 1663 and did not dissolve. It remained in permanent session at Regensburg until the end of the Empire in 1806, its members increasingly represented by envoys rather than attending in person. The permanent Diet can be read as a sign of paralysis, and contemporaries sometimes mocked its slowness, but it also provided a continuous forum through which princes and cities negotiated imperial taxes, military contributions, and policy toward foreign powers.

Justice was a second pillar. The Reichskammergericht, the Imperial Chamber Court, sat at Speyer until its destruction in 1689 and reopened at Wetzlar in 1693, with judges nominated by the emperor and by the estates and with confessional balance among its assessors. The Reichshofrat in Vienna offered a second jurisdiction, more directly responsive to the emperor. Subjects could appeal against their princes, cities could sue other cities, and minor lords could seek protection against their more powerful neighbors. The courts were notoriously slow, and their decisions were sometimes difficult to enforce, but they made the Empire a rule governed polity in which claims were litigated rather than settled only by force. Executing judgments fell to the Imperial Circles, regional associations of estates that also organized defense and coinage, and some circles, such as those of Swabia and Franconia, proved more effective than others.

Territorial rulers thus operated in two overlapping systems. Within their own lands, princes governed through their courts, councils, and estates. At the imperial level, they sat as members of an assembly, sued and were sued in imperial courts, and contributed to imperial defense. The relationship between these levels changed over the period. Large territories such as Bavaria, Saxony, Brandenburg, and Hanover increasingly acted as independent powers in foreign policy and sometimes defied imperial authority. Smaller territories, including ecclesiastical principalities, free cities, and imperial knights, depended heavily on the imperial order for their survival. The Empire therefore served distinct constituencies in different ways.

Historical Vocabulary
Imperial Constitution: Estates, Diet, Courts, and Circles

Imperial estates (Reichsstände) were the rulers and corporate bodies with an immediate relationship to the emperor and a vote in the Diet: electors, princes, prelates, counts, and free imperial cities. The Reichstag was their assembly, permanently seated at Regensburg from 1663. The Reichskammergericht and the Reichshofrat were the Empire's two supreme courts, the first dominated by the estates and the second by the emperor. The Imperial Circles (Reichskreise) were regional administrative units responsible for military organization and the enforcement of imperial law. Together these institutions made the Empire a constitutional order in which authority was shared, contested, and litigated. Modern readers should not mistake it for a failed nation state, since it was never designed to be one, and should avoid using the term "Germany" for it without qualification, because it included non German speaking territories and excluded some German speaking ones.

Primary Source Window 1

The Imperial Diet at Regensburg, 1653 to 1654: Contemporary Representation and Imperial Recess

Creator: Contemporary engravers and imperial chancery officials · Date: 1653 to 1654 · Type: A seventeenth century pictorial representation of the Diet in session, together with the legal text of the concluding Recess (the "Most Recent Recess of the Empire," Jüngster Reichsabschied, 1654)

Context: During the imperial meetings of 1653, the prince-electors elected Ferdinand IV, son of Emperor Ferdinand III, as King of the Romans at Augsburg, and he was crowned at Regensburg in June. The Imperial Diet at Regensburg dealt with outstanding questions left by the Peace of Westphalia. Ferdinand IV died in 1654, and the Habsburg succession to the imperial title passed instead to his brother Leopold, elected in 1658. The Recess of 1654 was the last concluding document issued by an imperial Diet before the Diet became permanent. The 1653 engraving and an English translation of the 1654 Recess are separately available through German History in Documents and Images; they are two distinct sources.

Verification record (1653 pictorial source and 1654 legal text): GHDI, engraving of the Diet, June 1653; GHDI, “Youngest Recess,” 17 May 1654, §§178–180. Source descriptions are paraphrases; any verbatim transcription or precise article claims still require direct edition-level collation.

Labeled paraphrase, not a quotation The pictorial representation shows the emperor and the three colleges arranged according to rank, an image that expresses constitutional order through ceremonial hierarchy. The Recess, in a provision often cited by historians, required subjects to contribute to the cost of fortresses and garrisons that territorial rulers considered necessary for defense, and this provision was subsequently used by princes to justify levying taxes against the resistance of their territorial estates. The defense-and-garrison authorization is treated in section 180 of the 1654 Recess; students can examine its edited translation before drawing conclusions from the paraphrase.

Analysis: The image is valuable because it reveals how the Empire understood itself: as a hierarchy of ranks in which the emperor presided and the estates participated, not a monarchy in which the emperor commanded. The Recess is valuable because it shows a concrete way in which imperial law could strengthen princes against the estates in their own territories, which contributed to the territorial state building described later in this chapter. Both sources represent official perspectives. The engraving idealizes order and conceals the procedural disputes and precedence quarrels that characterized actual sessions, and the Recess shows what the dominant participants agreed upon but not how subjects experienced the resulting burdens.

Historical Debate 1

Did the Holy Roman Empire Become Politically Ineffective after Westphalia?

The question: Was the Empire after 1648 a decaying shell, overshadowed by its territorial states, or a functioning constitutional order that continued to provide legal protection and political coordination?

Interpretation A: A weak and obsolete structure. Influenced by the nineteenth century national narrative, many older accounts treated the Empire as a barrier to German political unity whose institutions were slow, fragmented, and incapable of defending its borders against France and the Ottomans. In this view, the future belonged to territorial states such as Prussia and Austria, which had the military and fiscal capacity that the Empire lacked.
Interpretation B: A functioning constitutional order. Many recent historians, drawing on research into the imperial courts, circles, and Diet, argue that the Empire effectively regulated disputes, protected smaller territories, balanced confessions, and mobilized armies in major crises, as at Vienna in 1683 and in the wars against France. In this view, the Empire's lack of centralization was a feature of a federal and legalistic order, not evidence of failure, and it offered smaller states a security that centralizing monarchies could not.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same material: the records of the Reichskammergericht and Reichshofrat, the proceedings of the permanent Diet, the military performance of imperial contingents, and the political behavior of large territorial rulers.

Why it matters: If the Empire was a failure, the rise of Prussia and Austria looks like a natural replacement. If it functioned, then the two powers developed within a constitutional order whose rules they both used and sometimes violated, and their rivalry had to be conducted through its institutions. The scholarly consensus has moved toward the second view while acknowledging genuine structural limits.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that could be used to measure the Empire's effectiveness and explain what each would show and what it might conceal.

Section 78.3

Leopold I and the Reconstruction of Habsburg Dynastic Power

Leopold I (born 1640) became ruler of the Habsburg hereditary lands in 1657, was crowned King of Hungary in 1655 and King of Bohemia in 1656 as a younger son destined for a church career whose elder brother's death changed his prospects, and was elected emperor in July 1658 after a contested election in which French diplomats tried to prevent Habsburg continuity. He reigned until 1705, longer than any other Habsburg ruler of the period. Cautious, devout, musical, and attentive to ceremony, he was long judged by historians as an indecisive monarch, but modern scholarship credits his reign with laying the foundation of the Habsburg great power through a combination of dynastic legitimacy, institutional development, and persistent, if often reluctant, war making.

The monarchy Leopold inherited was institutionally decentralized. Each of the main territorial groups had its own estates, which voted taxes, administered justice through their own officials, and defended provincial privileges. The ruler governed through a court centered on Vienna, a council system including the Secret Council, and several central offices. The Hofkriegsrat, or Court War Council, established in the sixteenth century, supervised military administration, and the Hofkammer, the Court Chamber, managed crown finances. Alongside these were chanceries for the Bohemian, Austrian, and Hungarian lands. Their jurisdictions overlapped and their relations with provincial estates were negotiated rather than prescribed.

Leopold's central strategic problem was the pressure of two simultaneous threats. To the west, Louis XIV's France sought to extend its frontier and to weaken Habsburg influence in the Empire, and war with France repeatedly diverted imperial and Habsburg resources. To the southeast, the Ottoman Empire, which had occupied much of Hungary since the sixteenth century, remained a formidable opponent. The Habsburg response relied on the creation of a standing army, partly as a result of the pressures of the Turkish war of 1663 to 1664, which ended with the Habsburg victory at St. Gotthard (Mogersdorf) in August 1664 and an immediate truce, the Peace of Vasvár. Many Hungarian nobles regarded the settlement as a betrayal, since it left Ottoman gains intact despite a victory, and their discontent led to a conspiracy among leading magnates, whose suppression in 1670 and 1671 with executions and confiscations opened a period of harsh Habsburg rule in Hungary. Subsequent Hungarian resistance, led by Imre Thököly in the 1670s and 1680s with Ottoman support, reflected both confessional grievances among Hungarian Protestants and constitutional objections to Habsburg measures.

Finance was a persistent constraint. The Habsburgs depended on grants from provincial estates, on loans from court bankers, and on subsidies from allies. Prominent Jewish financiers such as Samuel Oppenheimer, who supplied the army and lent to the court during the wars of the 1670s through the 1690s, played an important role in military finance, even though Leopold had expelled the Jews of Vienna in 1670 and 1671 in a measure combining religious, economic, and political motives and then allowed prominent financiers to return in a privileged capacity. The Vienna City Bank, founded in 1705, later offered a mechanism for managing public debt. The contrast between the dynasty's dependence on Jewish and Christian financiers and the formal exclusion of most Jews from Habsburg cities illustrates the contradictions of early modern fiscal practice.

Dynastic politics were also essential. Leopold married first a Spanish Habsburg princess, Margaret Theresa, and later others, including Eleonor Magdalene of Pfalz-Neuburg, with whom he had the sons Joseph and Charles who would succeed him. The ambiguous question of the Spanish succession, which hung over the entire reign because the last Spanish Habsburg, Charles II, was childless, shaped his diplomacy and eventually led to the war that began in 1701. In the Empire, Leopold used the imperial title to mobilize princes, and in 1692 he elevated the Duke of Hanover to the rank of elector in exchange for military support, a pattern in which titles and dignities were exchanged for service. As Section 78.11 will describe, the creation of a Prussian royal title in 1701 followed the same logic.

Leopold's court also developed a distinctive culture. The Viennese court of Leopold's reign was devout, ceremonial, and musically rich, and the dynasty cultivated a Catholic baroque political theology that presented the Habsburgs as defenders of Christendom against the Ottomans and as guardians of the true faith. This image, reinforced by victories in Hungary, helped the dynasty claim legitimacy in territories whose elites had once been Protestant. Historians should treat that image as a representation, not as a description of the actual religious diversity of the monarchy's subjects, among whom Protestants, Orthodox Christians, Jews, and, in the Ottoman borderlands, Muslims were numerous.

Section 78.4

The Ottoman Challenge and the Siege of Vienna in 1683

The Ottoman Empire was not an external intruder into the history of Central Europe but one of its established powers. It ruled much of the Kingdom of Hungary as the provinces of Buda and Temesvár, maintained a vassal relationship with the Principality of Transylvania, and kept a heavily garrisoned frontier with the Habsburgs along a line running from the Adriatic to the Carpathians. Chapter 79 will treat the Ottoman Empire's wider European and regional position, and Chapter 41 traced its expansion to the fall of Constantinople. In the later seventeenth century the Ottoman state, under the leadership of the Köprülü family of grand viziers, enjoyed a period of administrative recovery and military confidence. The Ottomans seized Crete from Venice after a long war that ended in 1669, took Podolia from Poland in 1672, and in the 1680s turned again to the Habsburg frontier.

The immediate background to the Ottoman campaign of 1683 was Hungarian discontent. Imre Thököly, a Protestant magnate, rebelled against Habsburg rule in the early 1680s with Ottoman support and was recognized by the Ottomans as ruler of a portion of Upper Hungary. The Habsburgs, meanwhile, had concluded a truce with the Ottomans in 1664 that was due to expire in 1684, and in 1683 the grand vizier Kara Mustafa Pasha led a large army toward the Habsburg lands. The size of the army is uncertain, with older sources claiming well over 150,000 men and modern estimates generally lower, and the aims of the campaign are debated, with historians divided over whether Kara Mustafa intended only to take the frontier fortress of Győr and extend Ottoman control in Hungary or to capture Vienna itself. What is clear is that, by mid July 1683, the army had encircled Vienna, and Leopold and his court had fled westward.

The defense of Vienna was led by Count Ernst Rüdiger von Starhemberg, the city's military commander, with a garrison that numbered in the low tens of thousands, including citizens and students who were enrolled as auxiliaries. The siege began in the middle of July. The Ottomans proceeded by mining and digging approach trenches rather than by an immediate assault, and by early September the defenders, who suffered from dysentery and shortages, were in desperate condition. Leopold, working through diplomacy that had long prepared for the possibility of war, had concluded an alliance with Poland in March 1683. John III Sobieski, the king of Poland-Lithuania, brought a substantial Polish force south, and the imperial relief army was assembled from Habsburg troops and contingents sent by Bavaria, Saxony, Franconia, and Swabia, under the overall coordination of Sobieski, as the highest ranking monarch present, with Charles V, Duke of Lorraine, directing the imperial forces.

The relieving armies attacked from the Kahlenberg heights on 12 September 1683, and the Ottoman forces, divided between the siege lines and the defense against the relief army, were routed. The battle was won by a coordinated advance of Polish, imperial, and German forces, including a famous cavalry charge led by Sobieski, and the Ottoman army withdrew in disorder. Kara Mustafa was later executed on the order of the sultan. The relief of Vienna should therefore be understood as a multinational achievement. Later Austrian and Polish national memories each tended to emphasize their own contribution, and Habsburg commemoration emphasized the dynasty's role as defender of Christendom, but the contemporary record shows a coalition in which Polish leadership was crucial and German princes contributed substantial contingents.

The consequences were strategic and political. The Ottoman defeat before Vienna shifted initiative to the Habsburgs and their allies. In 1684 the Holy League was formed, uniting the emperor, Poland-Lithuania, and Venice, under papal sponsorship, and Russia joined in 1686. Habsburg prestige increased substantially, and the dynasty's claim to leadership of the war against the Ottomans enhanced its position inside the Empire, where princes had demonstrated that they could be mobilized for imperial defense. At the same time, the victory did not end the war, which continued for sixteen years, nor did it make future Habsburg success certain. Section 78.5 follows the campaigns through the Treaty of Karlowitz and beyond. The response of Louis XIV, who took advantage of imperial preoccupation to seize territories in the west, a policy that led to the Nine Years' War beginning in 1688, also demonstrated how Habsburg success in the southeast depended on conditions in the west.

The historical memory of 1683 deserves comment. Subsequent generations in Austria, Poland, and elsewhere have invoked the siege as a symbolic confrontation between Christian Europe and the Ottoman world. Historians should distinguish this later symbolic reading from the contemporary situation, in which the Habsburgs and the Ottomans had both fought and negotiated for more than a century, in which Protestant Hungarian magnates had accepted Ottoman backing against Catholic Habsburg rule, and in which France, a Catholic power, had maintained an understanding with the Ottoman state. The contest was political and imperial, and its confessional dimension was real but not exclusive.

Section 78.5

Habsburg Expansion in Hungary, the Balkans, and Southeastern Europe

The Holy League's war against the Ottoman Empire, which lasted from 1683 to 1699, transformed the Habsburg position in southeastern Europe. Imperial forces advanced down the Danube and recaptured Buda, the old Hungarian capital, on 2 September 1686, after a siege of about two months that ended a century and a half of Ottoman rule there. The garrison and many civilians were killed when the city fell, and Buda's Muslim and Jewish inhabitants suffered heavily, a reminder that reconquest was experienced very differently by different communities. Subsequent campaigns recovered most of the Hungarian plain. The second battle of Mohács in August 1687, fought near the site of the Ottoman victory of 1526, was a significant defeat for the Ottomans, and it contributed to political upheaval at Constantinople, where the sultan Mehmed IV was deposed later that year.

Military success reshaped Hungarian politics. At the Diet of Pressburg (Pozsony) in 1687, the Hungarian estates accepted the hereditary succession of the Habsburg male line to the Hungarian crown, giving up the elective principle which had been central to the Hungarian constitution, and agreed to abandon the nobility's formal right of resistance asserted in the Golden Bull of 1222. Leopold's eldest son Joseph was crowned King of Hungary in December 1687 at the age of nine. These concessions were won in part because the Habsburg army was now the dominant military force in Hungary, but the Hungarian estates retained their diet, their county administration, their legal exemptions, and their separate treasury and chancery. The Habsburgs governed the reconquered territories through a special commission, the Neoacquistica Commissio, which assessed claims to land and resolved the status of lands previously held by Hungarian nobles who could not prove their titles, a process that produced resentment among many noble families.

The war continued with changing fortunes. Prince Eugene of Savoy, a French born commander who had entered Habsburg service after Louis XIV rejected him, won a decisive victory at Zenta on the Tisza River on 11 September 1697, where the Ottoman army was caught during a river crossing. The Treaty of Karlowitz, concluded in January 1699 under the mediation of Britain and the Dutch Republic, gave the Habsburgs most of Hungary, excluding the Banat of Temesvár, along with Transylvania, Croatia, and Slavonia. Poland recovered Podolia, Venice gained the Morea and some Dalmatian territory, and Russia retained Azov under a separate settlement. Karlowitz is traditionally seen as the first major Ottoman territorial loss in Europe, and it marked a shift in the balance of power on the frontier.

The outbreak of the War of the Spanish Succession in 1701, narrated in Chapter 80, drew Habsburg forces westward and gave the Hungarian opposition an opportunity. From 1703, Francis II Rákóczi, a magnate from a prominent family, led an uprising that drew on peasant discontent, noble grievances over taxation and the suppression of privileges, and Protestant resentment. The insurgents, called kuruc, controlled much of Upper Hungary and Transylvania for several years, and Rákóczi was recognized as prince of Transylvania. The rebellion finally ended with the Peace of Szatmár in 1711, negotiated by the imperial general János Pálffy, which offered amnesty, confirmed noble privileges and the Hungarian constitution, and committed the Habsburgs to rule Hungary according to its laws. Szatmár is a central example of how Habsburg authority in Hungary rested on a settlement with the Hungarian nobility, not on the extension of Austrian or Bohemian administrative practice.

The final phase of expansion came during the war of 1716 to 1718. Prince Eugene captured the fortress of Temesvár in October 1716 and then took Belgrade in August 1717 after defeating an Ottoman relief army. The Peace of Passarowitz (Požarevac) of July 1718 gave the Habsburgs the Banat of Temesvár, northern Serbia including Belgrade, and Lesser Wallachia (Oltenia), producing the largest extent of Habsburg territory in the southeast in the entire century. These gains were not permanent. After a further Austro-Ottoman war, in which Austria entered the Russo-Ottoman conflict in 1737, the Peace of Belgrade in September 1739 returned Belgrade, northern Serbia, and Lesser Wallachia to the Ottomans, while the Banat remained Habsburg. The loss demonstrated both the limits of Habsburg military capacity, which had declined under the pressure of simultaneous commitments and weaker leadership after Prince Eugene's death in 1736, and the continuing power of the Ottoman state.

The reconquered areas required new forms of settlement and defense. The Habsburgs expanded the Military Frontier, a belt of territory along the Ottoman border settled by peasant soldiers, many of them Orthodox Serbs and others, who held land in exchange for military service and were governed directly through military administration instead of by provincial estates or nobles. In the Banat and Hungary, the dynasty encouraged colonization by German speaking, Hungarian, Romanian, and Serbian settlers, a policy that altered the ethnic and confessional makeup of regions depopulated by war and long Ottoman rule. Modern national histories often treat these movements retrospectively as the origins of nationalist claims, but contemporaries thought in terms of dynastic loyalty, confession, and legal privilege. Students should resist reading later Hungarian, Serbian, Romanian, or Croatian national identities back into these populations.

Section 78.6

The Habsburg Composite Monarchy: Government, Finance, Armies, and Provincial Privilege

The Habsburg territories were not a state in the sense that later centuries would understand the term. They formed a composite monarchy, a set of territories linked principally by their common ruler, each retaining its own laws, privileges, and institutions. Chapter 74 introduced this concept as a general feature of early modern Europe. This section applies it to the specific machinery of the Habsburg court, treasury, and army and explains why the dynasty's powers varied so greatly from one territory to another.

At the center was the ruler's court in Vienna. The court was a political institution as well as a household, where noble families competed for offices, favor, and influence, and the ruler's confessor, his chief ministers, and members of the high nobility exercised important roles. Advisory bodies such as the Privy Conference (Geheime Konferenz) offered counsel on foreign policy and war, and aristocratic families, including the Schwarzenbergs, Lobkowitzes, Liechtensteins, Kinskys, and Trauttmansdorffs, held high office while also being major landowners in the provinces. The court therefore tied provincial elites to the dynasty by offering them careers and honors, which was one of the principal mechanisms of Habsburg integration.

Specialized central offices managed war and finance. The Hofkriegsrat supervised recruitment, supply, and fortifications, and its presidents, who included major commanders, were among the most powerful figures in the monarchy. The Hofkammer administered the dynasty's domain revenues and its customs and other regalian income, and it negotiated loans. These bodies were hampered by corruption, inadequate information, and competition among officials. They also shared authority with the territorial chanceries, the Bohemian Court Chancery, the Austrian Court Chancery, and the Hungarian Court Chancery, which represented the legal interests of their provinces to the ruler. The relationship between central organs, which pursued the dynasty's strategic priorities, and the provincial chanceries, which defended local law, was a continuing source of tension.

Provincial estates were the most important institutional counterweight to the ruler. In the Austrian hereditary lands and in Bohemia and Moravia, the estates of nobles, prelates, and in some cases towns met in diets, voted taxes, and administered collection through their own officials. After 1620 the Bohemian estates had been severely weakened, with confiscations of rebel property and a reordering of the nobility, but they continued to meet and vote taxes. In Hungary the diet and the county assemblies held the nobility's rights to tax exemption and to resist unconstitutional measures. The Hungarian crown, symbolized by the Crown of Saint Stephen, represented a distinct legal and political community whose constitution the king swore to uphold at his coronation. For this reason, Hungary was never merged administratively with the Austrian and Bohemian lands during this period.

Taxation was therefore a negotiation. The estates of Austria and Bohemia granted funds, known as contributions, usually for fixed periods, and they allocated the burden among the provinces and within them among the different social groups. Nobles' own demesne lands were generally taxed lightly or not at all, and most of the burden fell on peasant holdings, which also owed rents and labor services to their lords. Towns paid through separate assessments and indirect taxes. Because the estates controlled collection, a significant share of revenues was absorbed by their own administration, and the ruler lacked precise knowledge of what could be extracted. Hungary contributed far less to the central military budget than its size would suggest, since the nobility's tax exemption and the diet's resistance limited what could be extracted.

Military organization combined older and newer elements. Regiments were raised by colonel proprietors, officers who recruited, equipped, and sometimes partly financed their units, and who held rights over appointments within them. Soldiers were generally volunteers, impressed men, and recruits drawn from the provinces by a system of assigned quotas, with substantial foreign enlistment. Supply relied on contractors, on provincial magazines, and on contributions extracted from occupied territory in wartime. The Hungarian light cavalry, the hussars, and the irregular troops of the Military Frontier, the Pandurs and Croats, became prominent in Habsburg armies, and they gave the monarchy a fighting capacity that relied on its distinctive frontier societies. The standing army, which had begun to take shape in the wars of Ferdinand III and Leopold I, was a lasting presence but not an instrument that the ruler could use free of the constraints of financing and provincial consent.

Historical Vocabulary
Composite Monarchy and Provincial Estates

A composite monarchy was a state formed of several territories under one ruler, each with its own laws, customs, and political institutions, so that the ruler acted in different capacities in different places: archduke in Austria, king in Bohemia, and king in Hungary. Provincial estates were assemblies of privileged groups, usually nobles, clergy, and in some cases townspeople, who represented the province's interests, granted taxes, and enforced local law. The concept helps explain why the Habsburg monarchy could not simply impose uniform policies and why even a victorious ruler had to bargain with local elites. It also warns against using the term "Austria" for the whole monarchy before the nineteenth century, a usage that obscures the legal separateness of Bohemia and Hungary. Historians sometimes use the phrase "Habsburg monarchy" for the whole as a convenient label, while recognizing that contemporaries did not use a single name for it.

Section 78.7

Charles VI, the Pragmatic Sanction of 1713, and Dynastic Continuity

Joseph I, who succeeded Leopold in 1705, died of smallpox in April 1711, leaving two daughters and no sons. The succession passed to his younger brother Charles, who had been fighting for the Spanish crown in Spain since 1703 and who was elected emperor as Charles VI in October 1711. The settlement that ended the War of the Spanish Succession, concluded at Utrecht in 1713 and at Rastatt and Baden in 1714, denied Charles the Spanish throne, which went to Philip V of the Bourbon line, but gave the Austrian Habsburgs the former Spanish Netherlands, Milan, Naples, and Sardinia, the last of which was exchanged for Sicily in 1720. The monarchy thereby acquired Italian and Netherlandish territories, expanding its geographic range but also its strategic commitments, and extending the number of different legal regimes under its rule.

The central dynastic concern of the reign was succession. By the 1710s it was clear that Charles might have no surviving sons. His only son, born in 1716, died within months, and subsequent children were daughters. A family agreement concluded by Leopold and his sons in 1703 had provided that if the male line failed, the daughters of Joseph I would have priority over those of Charles. In April 1713 Charles issued the Pragmatic Sanction, a family law that revised this arrangement. It declared the hereditary lands to be indivisible and inseparable, established succession by primogeniture, and provided that, in the absence of male heirs, Charles's daughters would inherit before the daughters of Joseph. The measure was initially announced to a small circle of councillors and only later presented to the estates and foreign powers.

It is important to understand what the Pragmatic Sanction did and did not do. It is sometimes summarized as a measure permitting women to inherit, and it did regulate succession through the female line. But its central purpose was dynastic indivisibility: it was meant to guarantee that the entire collection of Habsburg territories would pass as a single inheritance and could not be partitioned among heirs, as earlier Habsburg possessions had sometimes been divided. Its effectiveness depended on recognition by those who had a stake in the outcome. The provincial estates were asked to accept the arrangement, and during the 1720s the diets of Bohemia, Austria, and Hungary and the estates of other territories gave their assent, in Hungary in 1722 and 1723 in exchange for confirmations of privileges. The consent of the estates gave the document a constitutional foundation, but it also demonstrated that the ruler needed provincial agreement.

The more difficult task was gaining recognition abroad. Charles VI spent considerable diplomatic capital securing guarantees from the other powers, sometimes at the cost of concessions. Prussia recognized the Sanction in 1726 in exchange for Habsburg support for Hohenzollern claims in the Rhineland duchies of Jülich and Berg, a promise that Vienna did not keep, and Britain did so in the Second Treaty of Vienna in 1731, in exchange for the dissolution of the Ostend Company, an Austrian Netherlands trading venture that rivaled British and Dutch commerce. France accepted it in 1738 as part of the settlement that ended the War of the Polish Succession, under which Francis Stephen, Duke of Lorraine, who had married Charles's daughter Maria Theresa in 1736, gave up Lorraine and received Tuscany. Russia, Saxony, Bavaria and others were drawn into the arrangement on varying terms. The accumulated guarantees made the Sanction a major element of the European diplomatic order, but guarantees were promises that could be reinterpreted when circumstances changed.

By the end of Charles VI's reign the monarchy was in a weaker position than the extent of its territory suggested. The war with the Ottomans in 1737 to 1739 ended in the losses of the Peace of Belgrade. Finances remained strained, the army was reduced and underfunded, and the aging Charles had not systematically prepared his daughter for rule. When Charles died on 20 October 1740, Maria Theresa, then twenty three years old, was left to govern with a treasury and army in poor condition, with ministers inherited from her father, and with the legitimacy of her succession reliant on promises from rulers who now had interests to pursue. The Sanction guaranteed her succession in principle, but it could not alter the fact that the monarchy lacked the military and financial strength to deter predators.

Section 78.8

Maria Theresa, the Defense of the Monarchy, and Administrative Reconstruction

Maria Theresa's accession in October 1740 was immediately challenged. Frederick II of Prussia invaded Silesia in December, and within months other rulers, among them Charles Albert of Bavaria, who claimed the Habsburg inheritance through his wife and an older family claim, and the courts of France and Spain, moved to take advantage of the monarchy's apparent weakness. Section 78.13 and Section 78.14 narrate the war itself. This section examines how the young ruler and her advisers responded, and how wartime pressure produced an administrative reconstruction that would last long after the peace.

Maria Theresa was never Holy Roman Emperor, a distinction that must be maintained. She ruled the Habsburg lands as archduchess of Austria, queen of Bohemia, and, after her coronation at Pressburg in June 1741, queen of Hungary, a title she held in her own right and not as a consort. The imperial title, which could not be inherited by a woman, passed in January 1742 to Charles Albert of Bavaria as Charles VII, the first non Habsburg emperor in three centuries, and after his death in January 1745 to her husband Francis Stephen, who was elected in September 1745 as Francis I. Francis was Holy Roman Emperor, and Maria Theresa was his consort in that capacity, but real authority over the Habsburg lands remained with her. The dual arrangement gave the dynasty the imperial dignity without subordinating its territorial government to imperial office.

The Hungarian diet was the first crucial test. In the summer of 1741, with her position in Austria and Bohemia threatened, Maria Theresa traveled to Pressburg to be crowned and to seek military aid from the Hungarian estates. Later tradition emphasized a dramatic scene in which the assembled nobles pledged their lives and swords to her, but contemporary records are less theatrical, and the diet's support came after negotiation that confirmed noble privileges, including tax exemption, and obtained Hungarian troops for the war. The episode shows the pattern of Habsburg rule in Hungary: loyalty was secured through a bargain preserving the constitution and the nobility's fiscal immunity, not through the extension of central control.

The war exposed the inadequacy of the existing administrative system. Revenues were too low and unevenly collected, and the multiplicity of authorities made coordination difficult. After the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle, Maria Theresa and her advisers undertook reforms directed principally by Friedrich Wilhelm von Haugwitz, a Silesian nobleman who had entered Habsburg service after the Prussian conquest of his homeland and who had studied Prussian practices. In 1748 the estates of the Austrian and Bohemian lands agreed, under pressure, to a ten year settlement providing a fixed level of military funding, set to support a peacetime army of about 108,000 men, a major departure from the earlier practice of annual negotiation. The recess ensured a predictable income and reduced the estates' leverage, though not their other privileges.

In 1749 a new central body, the Directorium in publicis et cameralibus, merged the previously separate Austrian and Bohemian chanceries into a single administrative authority responsible for domestic administration and finance in those lands. The judicial functions were separated from administration, with a Supreme Judicial Office created for the purpose, and district officials, the Kreishauptleute, were given strengthened roles in collecting taxes and overseeing local conditions, reducing the role of the estates' own officials. A new tax assessment attempted to tax noble demesne lands, though exemptions persisted in many areas. Subsequent adjustments followed, including the creation of the State Chancellery for foreign affairs under Wenzel Anton von Kaunitz in 1753 and reorganizations of the central offices in the 1760s. Revenues increased substantially, though precise figures remain uncertain and are discussed in Section 78.15.

Equally important were the reforms' limits. The Hungarian kingdom, protected by its constitution and by the bargain of 1741, was not integrated into the new system. Hungary retained its own diet, county administration, treasury, and chancery, and the nobility retained its immunity from direct taxation. The Habsburg lands outside the Austrian and Bohemian core, including the Netherlands and Italian possessions, were also governed separately. Reforms in the Austrian and Bohemian lands, moreover, did not eliminate noble influence. Nobles continued to dominate the new offices, and local administration continued to depend on cooperation between officials and landlords. The reconstruction transformed the monarchy's capacity without turning it into a unitary state.

Section 78.9

Brandenburg-Prussia after the Thirty Years' War: Territory, Dynasty, and Fragmentation

The name Brandenburg-Prussia is a modern convenience that describes the cluster of lands ruled by the Hohenzollern dynasty from the early seventeenth century, not a state recognized by contemporaries. In 1648 the Elector of Brandenburg ruled a set of territories which differed in legal status, confession, and social structure and which were separated from one another by the lands of other rulers. Understanding the later Prussian state requires first grasping how improbable such a state seemed, and how much the dynasty had to overcome in order to govern its possessions as a coherent whole.

The core was the Electorate of Brandenburg, a sandy and thinly populated territory centered on the Havel and Spree rivers, with Berlin and its twin city Cölln as the residence of the court. Its soils were poor compared with the loess lands of Saxony or the Rhineland, and the war had inflicted severe damage, with villages abandoned, livestock lost, and population greatly reduced, though regional variation was considerable. The Hohenzollerns had been margraves and electors of Brandenburg since 1415, and the Brandenburg elector held one of the seven, later eight, electoral votes, which gave him standing in imperial politics. Brandenburg lay within the Empire and was subject to imperial law and, in principle, to the jurisdiction of the imperial courts.

East of Brandenburg lay the Duchy of Prussia, the former territory of the Teutonic Order, which had been secularized in 1525 when Albert of Brandenburg Ansbach, the last grand master, became duke and a vassal of the Polish crown. The Brandenburg Hohenzollerns inherited it in 1618, and until 1657 the Duke of Prussia owed homage to the king of Poland. The duchy was not part of the Empire. Its capital was Königsberg, a major Baltic port and university city, and its estates, dominated by landowning nobles, along with the city of Königsberg, had a strong tradition of defending their privileges. Because Ducal Prussia lay outside the Empire, the dynasty's later claim to a royal title could be based there.

In the west, the Hohenzollerns held the duchy of Cleves, the county of Mark, and Ravensberg on the lower Rhine and in Westphalia, acquired after the Jülich Cleves succession crisis of 1609 and partitioned between Brandenburg and the Wittelsbach Palatinate branch of Neuburg in 1614 and 1666. These territories were economically more advanced, with a more developed urban sector and a Dutch influence, and they lay on the opposite side of the Empire from Brandenburg, with the lands of Hanover, Hesse, and other rulers intervening. In 1648, the dynasty received Farther Pomerania, the bishoprics of Halberstadt and Minden, and the reversion of Magdeburg, extending its holdings across northern Germany, though Swedish Pomerania, including Stettin, remained in Swedish hands until 1720.

The result was a territorial agglomeration with no natural defensive frontier, no common estates, and no unified administration. Each province had its own diet, its own customary law, and its own officeholders, and the Hohenzollern ruler was prince, duke, or count by different titles in different places. Communications between the Rhenish lands and Brandenburg or Prussia required passing through foreign territory, and the long, exposed frontiers of Brandenburg and Prussia invited invasion from Sweden, Poland, or Russia. The dynasty's religious position added complications: the electors had converted from Lutheranism to Calvinism in 1613, while most of their subjects remained Lutheran, and the dynasty had therefore to govern a Lutheran majority through a Calvinist court, relying on a policy of limited toleration for religious diversity.

The estates, dominated by landed nobles, were the principal institutional obstacle to consolidation. They controlled the voting of taxes and, in many provinces, their collection, and they defended their privileges, including exemption from most direct taxation and control over serfs on their estates. The Junkers, the landholding nobility of Brandenburg and Prussia east of the Elbe, held estates worked by dependent peasants subject to hereditary subjection and labor services. The nobles were not simply opponents of the ruler. They supplied the dynasty's military leaders, its administrators, and its court, and the dynasty's later success depended on binding them to royal service while preserving their social dominance. Section 78.10 explains how the Great Elector began that process, and Section 78.12 shows how it culminated under Frederick William I.

Section 78.10

Frederick William the Great Elector: Army, Taxation, and Political Consolidation

Frederick William, who became Elector of Brandenburg in 1640 at the age of twenty and ruled until 1688, inherited territories occupied or threatened by Swedish and imperial forces and a small, unreliable army. His reign is conventionally regarded as the beginning of the Hohenzollern state, and the later designation "the Great Elector" reflects the way subsequent generations of Prussians, especially in the nineteenth century, retrospectively emphasized his role. Historians today stress both his achievements and the extent to which he governed through compromise and opportunism, rather than by following a consistent plan of centralization.

The army was his principal instrument. Early in his reign he reduced the force he inherited, which had been tied to the obligations of the war, and rebuilt it as a permanent body under his own command, financed by regular taxation. By the end of his reign it numbered about 30,000 men, a very large force relative to the population and resources of his lands, though the figure is an approximation and varied with the circumstances of campaign. The army needed supply, equipment, and officers, and the elector created a military administration, the General War Commissariat, to oversee recruitment, supply, and finance. The commissariat gradually acquired civil functions, since taxation to support the army required administration of the towns and countryside, and it became the nucleus of a broader bureaucracy.

Money came through bargains with the estates. In Brandenburg the elector negotiated a recess with the estates in 1653, in which the nobility granted a substantial sum over six years in return for confirmation of their privileges, including their authority over their dependent peasants, a settlement that reinforced noble control of the countryside while enabling the elector to collect revenue. In the towns he introduced the excise, an indirect tax on consumption and trade, collected by officials in the pay of the state, from the 1660s, first in Brandenburg and then in other provinces. The excise was applied to townspeople and not to nobles' own estates, a distinction that reflected the political balance: the ruler taxed the towns directly and left the countryside largely to the landlords, who collected a land tax, the contribution, from their peasants. The social division of fiscal burdens therefore reflected, and perpetuated, the alliance between ruler and nobility.

Resistance was strongest in Ducal Prussia and in Cleves. The elector took advantage of the Northern War between Sweden and Poland in the 1650s to obtain recognition of his sovereignty in Ducal Prussia. By the Treaty of Wehlau of September 1657, the Polish king John Casimir relinquished feudal overlordship over the duchy, and the Treaty of Bromberg of November 1657 ratified the arrangement with additional territorial provisions. The sovereignty of the Hohenzollerns in Ducal Prussia was confirmed by the Treaty of Oliva in 1660, which ended the war. This did not remove Brandenburg or the other Hohenzollern lands from the Empire, and the elector remained an imperial prince and an elector. The estates of Ducal Prussia, whose rights had depended on Polish suzerainty, fought to preserve their privileges, and Frederick William overcame their resistance by force and negotiation, including the arrest of Christian Ludwig von Kalckstein, an opponent who was executed in 1672.

The elector's diplomacy was opportunistic. He shifted alliances among Sweden, Poland, the Dutch Republic, France, and the emperor according to circumstances. His army defeated the Swedes at Fehrbellin in June 1675, a victory later celebrated in Prussian tradition, although its strategic significance was modest. He cooperated with Habsburg and Dutch interests against Louis XIV, but was dissatisfied with the results and turned for some years toward France. After the revocation of the Edict of Nantes by Louis XIV in 1685, he issued the Edict of Potsdam, inviting French Protestants to settle in Brandenburg, and offered them privileges. Around 20,000 Huguenot refugees came to Brandenburg-Prussia, a small share of the total emigration from France, but significant relative to local population and important for Berlin's artisan, commercial, and cultural life. The policy combined confessional solidarity with calculation about repopulating war damaged lands. Similarly, the elector admitted a limited number of Jewish families expelled from Vienna in 1670 and 1671, who were permitted to settle under conditions of payment and restriction.

The achievements must be kept in proportion. The Great Elector created a standing army, a more productive revenue system, and a nucleus of central administration. But he did so by bargaining with the nobility, and the resulting state remained an assemblage of provinces in which central officials worked alongside provincial estates and noble landlords. His successor inherited both his institutions and the limitations of his achievement.

Section 78.11

From Electorate to Kingdom: Frederick I and the Royal Title of 1701

Frederick William died in 1688 and was succeeded by his son Frederick III, who ruled Brandenburg until 1713 and became the first Hohenzollern king. Often depicted by older writers as an extravagant ruler whose pursuit of prestige weakened the state, Frederick is now viewed more evenhandedly, as a ruler who inherited a demanding strategic situation and who used ceremony and dynastic status as instruments of power in a society in which rank mattered greatly. The royal title he obtained in 1701 shaped the identity of the dynasty, though its immediate practical effects were limited.

The acquisition of a royal title was a matter of rank within a hierarchy of princes. In the seventeenth century a king stood above an elector in precedence, and rulers sought royal titles to elevate their houses and to secure better treatment in diplomatic ceremonial. Two obstacles stood in the way of a Brandenburg royal title. Within the Empire, only the emperor could confer kingship, and there was no precedent for a king of Brandenburg, since the emperor was the feudal superior of the electors. The Hohenzollerns, however, possessed Ducal Prussia, which lay outside the Empire and was no longer a Polish fief. A royal title based on Prussia would not conflict with imperial law, provided the emperor assented.

Leopold I agreed because he needed Brandenburg's support in the approaching War of the Spanish Succession. In the Crown Treaty of November 1700, Frederick undertook to provide troops, about 8,000 men, to support the emperor, to vote for the Habsburg candidate in imperial elections, and to support Habsburg interests in the Empire, while Leopold recognized Frederick's right to assume the royal title in Prussia. The wording was careful. The new title was "King in Prussia," not "King of Prussia," because the western part of historic Prussia, known as Royal or Polish Prussia, remained under the Polish crown, and because the title applied to the duchy, not to the entire Hohenzollern territory. Frederick crowned himself in Königsberg on 18 January 1701 in an elaborate ceremony, taking the name Frederick I, and the use of the title "King in Prussia" for the dynasty continued until 1772, when the acquisition of Polish Prussia in the First Partition allowed Frederick II to adopt the title "King of Prussia." This legal precision is important, since the title is often misleadingly rendered in later literature.

The crown's legal geography is significant. Brandenburg, Magdeburg, Cleves, and other Hohenzollern territories west of the Elbe and within the Empire remained part of the Empire and subject to imperial law, and Frederick continued to sit in the imperial Diet as Elector of Brandenburg. The monarchy therefore had a dual character: an independent kingdom in Prussia and a collection of imperial territories under the Elector of Brandenburg. This duality lasted through the eighteenth century and helps to explain the later complex relation of Prussia to the Empire, in which Prussian rulers could claim independence as kings while exercising influence within the Empire as electors. It also explains why the Hohenzollern state is more accurately called Brandenburg-Prussia than simply Prussia for much of the period, though contemporaries and later historians increasingly used "Prussia" for the whole.

Frederick I's reign saw cultural and institutional developments. The court at Berlin was expanded and embellished, with the construction of Charlottenburg palace for his wife Sophie Charlotte of Hanover, an intellectual who corresponded with the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Leibniz was instrumental in founding the Academy of Sciences at Berlin in 1700. The University of Halle, founded in 1694, became a center of Pietism, a movement of devotional renewal that would influence the dynasty and the officer corps, and of enlightened jurisprudence associated with Christian Thomasius. The court also cost heavily, and critics, including some of his own officials, complained about expenditure and favorites. The Prussian contingent fought effectively in the War of the Spanish Succession, adding to the dynasty's military reputation, and at the Peace of Utrecht in 1713 Prussia's royal title was recognized by the major powers and the dynasty gained Upper Guelders on the Meuse.

The royal title carried benefits and costs. It strengthened the dynasty's prestige, assisted in diplomacy, and provided a rallying symbol for a monarchy of dispersed territories, but it also incurred expenses and obligations, including a larger army to support royal dignity. The title did not alter the constitutional position of the Brandenburg estates, the structure of provincial government, or the distribution of social privilege. It was a political resource, whose significance grew only as the later military and administrative strength of the state made it credible.

Section 78.12

Frederick William I, the Soldier King, and the Prussian Fiscal-Military State

Frederick William I, who reigned from 1713 to 1740, is the figure most often credited with creating the distinctive Prussian combination of a large army, disciplined administration, and fiscal prudence. He cut the expenses of the court, reduced luxuries, and devoted the state's resources to the army and to the improvement of royal finances. His personality, which contemporaries and later writers depicted as irascible, frugal, and devout, has often dominated accounts of his reign, and historians now emphasize that his policies built on earlier institutions and depended on cooperation with the nobility. His harsh treatment of his son, the future Frederick II, has also overshadowed the structural significance of his achievements.

Army growth was the central fact. At his accession the army numbered perhaps 38,000 men, and at his death in 1740 it numbered around 80,000, a figure that varies by source and by the distinction between paper strength and effective strength. Given a population in 1740 of about 2.2 million, the Prussian army was proportionally larger than that of any other major European power. Prussian infantry were trained in rapid, disciplined fire, aided by the iron ramrod and by drill methods promoted by Leopold of Anhalt-Dessau, the "Old Dessauer," a Prussian field marshal, and the army attained a reputation for drill and cohesion. The famous regiment of tall grenadiers, the Potsdam Giants, was the king's personal indulgence, and its recruitment, often by coercion or purchase from abroad, caused diplomatic friction.

Recruitment was organized through the canton system, formalized in 1733, which assigned each regiment a district in which it recruited and in which young men were liable for service. Many categories of people were exempt, including nobles, many townspeople, some skilled craftsmen, and those in certain occupations, so the burden fell chiefly on peasants and the poorer inhabitants of rural districts. Soldiers recruited in the cantons served for long terms, but, to reduce the economic burden on agriculture, they were allowed leave to work on the land for much of the year, returning for training and during campaigns. A substantial share of the army, probably a third or more, consisted of foreign recruits, often enlisted by persuasion or compulsion, and desertion was a persistent problem. The system gave the state a large force at relatively modest cost, but it depended on an agrarian society in which peasants were bound to their landlords, and the exemptions reflected the privileged position of other groups.

The officer corps was the point at which the monarchy and the nobility were bound together. Frederick William I encouraged young nobles to serve, established cadet schools in Berlin, and reserved most officer commissions for the nobility, using military service as a way of harnessing the Junkers to the monarchy. Service offered status, income, and a career within the king's service, and many families found it profitable. The relationship was not without conflict: nobles resented royal interference with their estates, and officers' independence in local matters remained substantial. But the integration of the nobility into the army made possible a social alliance that gave the Prussian state unusual cohesion, one that would persist into the nineteenth century.

Administrative coordination advanced. In 1723 the king merged the military and financial administrations, the General War Commissariat and the General Finance Directory, into the General Directory, a central body, supervising provincial war and domains chambers. The General Directory, organized in departments by geography and subject, coordinated taxation, domain revenue, supply, and economic policy. Royal domains, crown lands leased to tenants, were the largest source of revenue, and the king insisted on careful management, audits, and leasing arrangements. The monarchy also promoted resettlement and economic development, notably in East Prussia, which suffered a severe plague epidemic in 1709 to 1711, where the king settled immigrants, including some 15,000 or more Protestants expelled from the archbishopric of Salzburg in 1731 and 1732. By his death the state's annual revenue was about 7 million thalers, and the king left a substantial reserve treasury.

The social costs were considerable. Peasants bore the burden of recruitment, taxation, and labor services, and the canton system tied them to the land and to military obligations. Soldiers faced brutal discipline, including harsh corporal punishment. Townspeople were subject to billeting, to the excise, and to the control of royal officials. Nobles received privileges but were also pressed into service. The Prussian system should not be mistaken for efficient rationality. It rested on coercion, on exemptions that protected elites, and on the close management of resources by an exacting ruler, and its stability depended on the king's personal supervision.

Historical Vocabulary
The Fiscal-Military State, the Canton System, and the Junkers

A fiscal-military state is a state organized to raise taxes and credit for the maintenance of armies, a concept introduced in Chapter 74. In Prussia it took the form of a standing army supported by the excise, the land contribution, and royal domain revenue. The canton system was the arrangement, formalized in 1733, by which each regiment recruited from an assigned district whose young men were liable for service, with extensive exemptions and periods of leave. The Junkers were the landed nobility of Brandenburg and Prussia east of the Elbe, who held estates worked by dependent peasants, enjoyed fiscal privileges, and supplied most of the army's officers. The term later acquired polemical connotations in nineteenth and twentieth century debates, and students should use it for the early modern landowning nobility, avoiding the assumption that all Prussian nobles shared the same wealth or outlook.

Historical Debate 2

What Drove the Rise of Brandenburg-Prussia?

The question: Was Brandenburg-Prussia's rise driven mainly by military reforms, dynastic diplomacy, administrative institutions, or negotiations with landed elites?

Interpretation A: Military and administrative innovation. Some accounts emphasize the army and the central administration that supported it, from the Great Elector's commissariat to Frederick William I's General Directory, arguing that Prussia's strength came from the efficient mobilization of limited resources through rational institutions and disciplined rulers.
Interpretation B: Negotiation with elites and opportunistic diplomacy. Other historians stress that rulers governed through compromises with the Junkers and provincial estates, that nobles supplied the officer corps and enforced rural order, and that dynastic diplomacy and favorable international conjunctures, such as the Northern War and the Spanish Succession, created opportunities that the rulers exploited. In this view, Prussian strength depended less on bureaucratic efficiency than on a durable alliance of crown and nobility.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the records of the commissariat and directory, the recesses with the estates, army lists, and the diplomatic correspondence of the period.

Why it matters: If efficiency explains Prussian success, the model looks like a general path of modern state building. If social bargains explain it, the Prussian state appears historically specific, dependent on a particular agrarian and aristocratic society. Most recent scholarship combines both, emphasizing the interaction of institutions and elites.

Student task: Explain how the exemption of nobles from the excise and the recruitment of officers from the nobility illustrate the interplay between institutional development and social bargaining.

Section 78.13

Frederick II's Accession in 1740 and the Seizure of Silesia

Frederick William I died on 31 May 1740, and his son became Frederick II. Born in 1712, the new king had been educated in a regime that sought to make him a soldier and a frugal administrator, and had resisted, to the point of attempting to flee in 1730, a father who disapproved of his interest in French literature, music, and philosophy. After the flight attempt his companion Hans Hermann von Katte was executed, an episode that left a deep mark on him. As crown prince he corresponded with Voltaire and wrote a treatise criticizing Machiavelli's political thought, and many contemporaries expected a cultured, reform minded ruler. The expectation was partly borne out, and Chapter 84 will treat his reform program and the question of enlightened monarchy. In the first months of his reign, however, the most consequential decision he made was military.

He inherited an army of about 80,000 men, a full treasury, and an administrative apparatus built by his father, and he could now use them. The death of Charles VI on 20 October 1740 created an opportunity. The Habsburg monarchy was led by a young and inexperienced woman, its army and finances were in poor condition after the Ottoman war, and the guarantees of the Pragmatic Sanction had been given by powers with their own interests. Frederick later wrote that ambition, interest, and the desire to make people talk about him decided him, a retrospective admission that scholars treat with caution but which reflects the opportunism of the decision. On 16 December 1740 Prussian troops crossed the frontier into Silesia without a declaration of war.

Silesia was a valuable prize. A province of the Crown of Bohemia, it lay on the Oder river between Brandenburg, Poland, Bohemia, and Moravia, and had a population of roughly a million or somewhat more, with a developed linen industry, mining, and agriculture. Its strategic position, extending along the northeastern flank of Bohemia, gave Prussia a base from which to threaten Habsburg territories and Saxony, and its capture removed a substantial source of Habsburg revenue and recruits. Most of the population of the province was Catholic, but there was a large Lutheran minority, protected after 1707 by the Convention of Altranstädt imposed by Sweden, and Silesia also had Polish speaking and Czech speaking communities in addition to its predominantly German speaking inhabitants. Prussian propaganda sometimes appealed to Protestant solidarity, though religion was not the stated basis of the claim.

Frederick advanced legal justifications, based on old Hohenzollern claims to several Silesian duchies. The most prominent concerned Liegnitz, Brieg, and Wohlau, where a treaty of inheritance concluded in 1537 between the Piast dukes and the Elector of Brandenburg had been annulled by Ferdinand I in 1546, and a further claim concerned the Duchy of Jägerndorf, which had been confiscated from a Hohenzollern margrave during the Bohemian rebellion. Legal scholars of the period, and modern historians, have generally judged these claims weak, and Habsburg jurists rejected them. Frederick knew that a legal argument would not carry the day, and he offered Maria Theresa a package: Prussia would defend her against other claimants, support the election of her husband as emperor, and pay a sum of money, in exchange for the cession of Silesia. When she refused, the matter was left to the sword.

The seizure of Silesia must not be described as a peaceful or uncontested inheritance. It was a conquest, justified after the fact by legal claims that few rulers accepted, and it was recognized by the Habsburgs only through war and treaty, first provisionally in 1742 and then definitively in 1748 and again in 1763. It also shocked contemporaries, who regarded a surprise invasion by a ruler who had guaranteed the Pragmatic Sanction as a breach of the conventions of dynastic politics. Other states followed with their own claims, producing the war narrated in the next section. The episode shows how thin the protection of legal guarantees could be when an ambitious ruler possessed a ready army.

Section 78.14

The War of the Austrian Succession, 1740 to 1748, and the New Central European Balance

The Prussian invasion of Silesia opened a general war. Chapter 80 will provide the comprehensive European narrative of the eighteenth century's wars. This section concentrates on the Central European dimension: how the Habsburg monarchy survived, how Prussia retained its conquest, and how the war altered the balance of power in the Empire. The conflict is conventionally divided into the first and second Silesian Wars, in which Prussia fought Austria, and the wider War of the Austrian Succession in which France, Spain, Bavaria, Saxony, and Britain took part.

The Prussian army demonstrated its quality at Mollwitz on 10 April 1741, where the Prussian cavalry was routed and Frederick himself left the field, but the infantry's disciplined fire carried the day under Field Marshal Schwerin. Silesia's fortresses fell progressively into Prussian hands. Meanwhile, other powers moved against Maria Theresa. The Franco Bavarian alliance, with French encouragement, put forward the claim of Charles Albert of Bavaria, and an allied army invaded Upper Austria and Bohemia in late 1741, with Prague falling in November. Charles Albert was crowned King of Bohemia in December 1741 and elected emperor as Charles VII in January 1742, the first non Habsburg emperor since the fifteenth century. Maria Theresa's position appeared desperate, and the monarchy's dissolution seemed conceivable.

The Habsburg recovery depended on negotiation and on the strengths of the composite structure. The Hungarian estates provided troops, and Hungarian and frontier light troops raided deep into Bavaria. Britain, concerned about French power, provided subsidies and eventually an army, the so called Pragmatic Army, which fought in the Rhineland. Maria Theresa made concessions to the one enemy she could buy off. Under pressure from the military situation, and by secret agreement at Klein Schnellendorf in October 1741 and then formally after the Prussian victory at Chotusitz in May 1742, she ceded most of Silesia and the county of Glatz to Prussia in the preliminary Peace of Breslau of June 1742, confirmed at Berlin in July. The concession freed Habsburg forces to fight Bavaria and France, and the Austrians recovered Bohemia and occupied Bavaria itself.

Prussia re entered the war in 1744, fearing that a Habsburg recovery would threaten the Silesian settlement, in the Second Silesian War. Frederick invaded Bohemia, but the campaign went badly, and he withdrew to Silesia. The death of Charles VII in January 1745 removed the Bavarian claimant, and his son Maximilian III Joseph made peace with Austria at Füssen in April 1745, renouncing claims to the Habsburg inheritance in exchange for the restoration of his lands and a promise to support Francis Stephen's election as emperor. Prussian victories at Hohenfriedberg in June 1745, at Soor in September, and at Kesselsdorf in December, the latter against the Saxons, who had allied with Austria, forced Maria Theresa to the Peace of Dresden of 25 December 1745, in which Frederick again recognized Francis as emperor and Austria confirmed the cession of Silesia. Francis had already been elected in September 1745.

The wider war continued in the Low Countries and Italy until the Peace of Aix la Chapelle in October 1748, which restored most conquests, with the main exceptions of Silesia, which Prussia kept except for a southeastern strip around Teschen, Troppau, and Jägerndorf that remained Austrian, and of the Italian duchies, where a Bourbon prince received Parma and Piacenza. The settlement guaranteed the Pragmatic Sanction and Maria Theresa's succession, confirming the monarchy's survival. But it also left Habsburg leaders bitter at the loss of Silesia, and convinced that they had been poorly served by their allies, notably Britain, which had pressed Vienna to make concessions to secure peace. In Berlin, the settlement confirmed that Prussia could defend its gains against a larger power but also made clear how dependent such defense was on fortunate circumstances, on the diplomatic isolation of Habsburg Austria, and on Frederick's military skill.

The war transformed the Central European balance. Prussia emerged as a state able to challenge Austria militarily, adding perhaps half again to its population at a stroke, and Habsburg leaders reached the conclusion that the monarchy's survival required reform of its military and fiscal system, a conclusion that produced the Haugwitz program. The imperial title had briefly left the Habsburg line, demonstrating that it was not a permanent Habsburg possession, and Francis's election restored it, but the experience contributed to a reassessment of the relationship between Habsburg dynastic interests and imperial office. The Empire itself had functioned as a legal and political forum throughout, with imperial princes choosing sides according to their interests, a demonstration that it provided a framework for conflict rather than an instrument for preventing it.

Cause and Effect

The Growth of Austro-Prussian Dynastic Competition

Long-term causes

The rise of standing armies and tax systems to support them, the dispersal of Hohenzollern territory and its demand for consolidation, the Habsburgs' loss of dominance in the Empire after 1648, and the weakness of legal constraints on ambitious princes.

Short-term causes

The deaths of Charles VI and Frederick William I in 1740, the youth and inexperience of Maria Theresa, the full Prussian treasury and ready army, and the uncertain value of guarantees given to the Pragmatic Sanction.

Event or transformation

The Prussian invasion of Silesia in December 1740 and the wars that confirmed it, in which two major powers within the Empire became open rivals.

Immediate effects

Prussian possession of most of Silesia, the Habsburg administrative reforms of 1748 to 1749, the diplomatic reorientation of Austria under Kaunitz, and new tensions within the imperial constitutional order.

Long-term consequences

A durable dualism in Central European politics, repeated war in 1756 to 1763, and long term competition over leadership among German states, though not a predetermined outcome.

Historical complexity

The rivalry cannot be reduced to one ruler's ambition, to Silesia alone, or to geography. It also reflected the legal weakness of guarantees, the ability of each state to mobilize resources, and the strategies of France, Britain, Russia, and smaller German states.

Historical complexity: Historians debate how much weight to give Frederick's personal ambition relative to structural pressures on a vulnerable state, and no single factor suffices.

Section 78.15

Military Reform, Administrative Competition, and the Preparation for Renewed Conflict

The eight years between the Peace of Aix la Chapelle and the outbreak of the Seven Years' War in 1756 were a period of intense preparation in both Vienna and Berlin. Each government drew its own lessons from the previous war. For Frederick, the lesson was that Prussia's survival depended on keeping an army and treasury ready for rapid action and on preventing a coalition from forming. For Maria Theresa and her advisers, the lesson was that Austria's military and fiscal organization needed reform and that its diplomatic alignment needed rethinking. Comparing these preparations clarifies the relationship between state capacity and war making.

On the Habsburg side, reform proceeded along the lines established by Haugwitz. The peacetime army was fixed at around 108,000 men under the ten year recess of 1748, funded by regular contributions from the Austrian and Bohemian lands, with Hungarian forces supported separately. Military reform was directed above all by Field Marshal Leopold von Daun, with Franz Moritz von Lacy a prominent collaborator by the later 1750s, and they reorganized regiments, standardized drill and equipment through new regulations, improved artillery under the direction of Prince Liechtenstein, and established the Theresian Military Academy at Wiener Neustadt in 1751 to train officers. The aim was to professionalize the officer corps, which had previously relied heavily on aristocratic proprietors and on foreign adventurers, though noble dominance of the higher ranks persisted. Austrian forces also made use of the Military Frontier and Hungarian hussar regiments, giving them a large contingent of light troops that Prussia could not easily match.

On the Prussian side, Frederick maintained the canton system and army organization developed by his father, adding to the army's size and refining its tactics. His own regulations stressed rapid maneuver, the oblique order of battle by which a weighted flank attacked while the other held back, and the integration of cavalry and artillery with infantry. The officer corps was increasingly restricted to nobles, with commoners being dismissed from commissions in many regiments after the war, a policy motivated by Frederick's belief in the loyalty and honor of the nobility. The administrative structure, headed by the General Directory, continued to supply the army, and the king supervised the system directly, reading reports and issuing orders from his cabinet. His reliance on personal supervision gave the system flexibility but also made it dependent on a single will.

Financial capacity was the decisive underlying variable. Both states relied on regular taxation and on credit, but their financial systems differed. Prussia relied on the domain revenues, the contribution, and the excise, on a substantial treasury reserve built by Frederick William I, and, in wartime, on contributions extracted from occupied territory such as Saxony and on British subsidies from 1758 and later on debasement of the coinage. Austria relied on contributions from its provinces, subject to negotiation with the estates, on loans from bankers, on the Vienna City Bank and other credit institutions, and on British subsidies and French support, though the latter ended up costing Austria some of its autonomy. The Habsburg monarchy's larger territory and population could in principle generate greater revenue than Prussia, but the diversity of its institutions and the privileges of its elites limited how much could be extracted.

Section 78.16

The Diplomatic Revolution, the Seven Years' War, and the Treaty of Hubertusburg, 1763

The Seven Years' War, which in Central Europe is also called the Third Silesian War, was fought on a larger scale than the preceding conflict and tested the competing monarchies more severely. Chapter 80 narrates the war's global dimension, including the colonial conflicts between Britain and France. Here the focus is on its Central European theater, where Austria sought to recover Silesia and Prussia fought for survival against a coalition of larger powers.

The war was preceded by a realignment of alliances, known as the Diplomatic Revolution. Wenzel Anton von Kaunitz, the Habsburg ambassador to France and from 1753 the State Chancellor, argued that the monarchy's true enemy was Prussia and that the traditional alliance with Britain was of little use, since Britain's priorities lay overseas and in the defense of Hanover. He worked to bring about an understanding with France, Austria's historical rival. The Anglo French colonial conflict in North America, which began in 1754, and Britain's concern for Hanover, led to the Convention of Westminster of January 1756, in which Britain and Prussia agreed to neutralize Germany. France, which regarded this agreement as a betrayal by its Prussian ally, concluded the First Treaty of Versailles with Austria in May 1756, a defensive arrangement that was expanded in 1757. Russia, whose Empress Elizabeth feared and disliked Frederick, also moved toward the Austrian side. The reversal of alliances of the previous century surprised contemporaries and has been discussed ever since, with historians arguing over how far it was planned by Kaunitz and how far it resulted from the logic of the Anglo French conflict.

Frederick, aware that a coalition was forming, struck first. In August 1756 he invaded Saxony, whose elector Augustus III was also King of Poland, seeking to forestall an attack and to obtain Saxon resources, and he forced the Saxon army to surrender at Pirna in October. This pre emptive attack had the effect of bringing the Empire's Diet to declare imperial war against Prussia in January 1757 and encouraged France and Russia to join the coalition more actively. The occupation of Saxony by Prussia, which was exploited for money and men throughout the war, is a significant example of the way in which warfare was financed, with the burdens falling heavily on occupied populations.

The war's course was a series of dramatic reversals. In 1757 Frederick invaded Bohemia and won a costly victory at Prague in May but was defeated by Daun at Kolín in June, forcing him to withdraw. France and the imperial Army of the Empire advanced from the west, and Russia from the east. In November Frederick destroyed a larger Franco Imperial army at Rossbach, a victory that made him a hero in Protestant Germany, and in December defeated the Austrians at Leuthen in Silesia, famous for his use of the oblique order. In 1758 he fought the Russians at Zorndorf, a bloody battle that left both sides exhausted. The year 1759 brought disaster at Kunersdorf in August, where a combined Russian and Austrian army routed the Prussians, and the loss of an entire corps at Maxen in November. Berlin was briefly occupied by Russian and Austrian troops in October 1760. Frederick won costly victories at Liegnitz and Torgau in 1760, but by 1761 Prussia's resources were nearly exhausted, and its army depleted.

The war was also a struggle of finance and endurance. Prussia relied on British subsidies, on contributions from Saxony and Mecklenburg, and on the debasement of its coinage, contracted to private mint lessees, which provoked inflation and was a source of long term financial strain. Austria struggled with a growing public debt and, after 1762, issued paper money in the form of the Bancozettel to ease the financial burden. Neither side could easily replace losses of trained men, and desertion and disease weakened armies on both sides. Civilians, in Saxony, Silesia, Pomerania, and Brandenburg suffered requisitions, plunder, and billeting, and the war devastated some regions.

Prussia was saved by the death of Empress Elizabeth of Russia on 5 January 1762 (New Style; 25 December 1761 in the Julian calendar then used in Russia). Her nephew and successor, Peter III, an admirer of Frederick, immediately made peace and returned occupied territory, and even briefly allied with Prussia. Although Peter was deposed in July 1762 by his wife Catherine II, who withdrew from the alliance but did not renew the war, Russia's departure left Austria unable to continue. Frederick won further victories in Silesia and Saxony, while Britain and France negotiated peace. The Treaty of Paris between Britain, France, and Spain was signed on 10 February 1763, and five days later, on 15 February, Austria, Prussia, and Saxony concluded the Treaty of Hubertusburg, named for a Saxon hunting lodge.

The settlement restored the territorial situation before the war, apart from the question of Silesia, which remained in Prussian hands, together with Glatz. Prussia evacuated Saxony, and Frederick promised to support the election of Archduke Joseph, the eldest son of Maria Theresa and Francis, as King of the Romans, which occurred in 1764. The war had thus ended in a stalemate in territorial terms, after a loss of life and treasure that scholars estimate in the hundreds of thousands of military deaths, with precise figures uncertain, and in an even greater number if civilian mortality from disease and hardship is included. Prussia's survival confirmed its status as a major European power, but the outcome did not resolve its underlying vulnerabilities: a dispersed territory, a small population, dependence on a large army, and strategic exposure to larger neighbors, as the experience of Kunersdorf had shown. Habsburg leaders concluded that further reform was needed, and Maria Theresa and her son Joseph II would continue it. The later reform programs of both monarchs will be addressed in Chapter 84.

Section 78.17

The Other German States: Saxony, Bavaria, Hanover, Imperial Cities, and Political Alternatives

A history of Central Europe that follows only Vienna and Berlin would misrepresent the political world of the period. Most inhabitants of the Empire lived under rulers who were neither Habsburgs nor Hohenzollerns, in territories whose size, constitution, and prospects varied enormously. These states offered alternatives to the Austrian and Prussian paths and shaped the context in which the two great powers operated. They also remind students that political fragmentation was not simply a defect but a condition that sustained a variety of political forms.

The Electorate of Saxony was among the wealthiest and most culturally brilliant territories in the Empire, with a developed textile and mining economy, the commercial city of Leipzig, whose fairs were among the largest in Europe, and the court city of Dresden. Elector Frederick Augustus I, known as Augustus the Strong, ruled from 1694 to 1733 and converted to Catholicism in 1697 in order to be elected King of Poland, though his subjects remained overwhelmingly Lutheran and the Saxon estates secured guarantees for the Protestant church. The union of Saxony and Poland, which lasted under his son Augustus II (as elector, Frederick Augustus II, king of Poland as Augustus III) until 1763, brought prestige but also vulnerability, since the two territories were not contiguous, and Saxony's exposed position between Prussia and Bohemia made it a recurrent theater of war. The Saxon court's patronage of the arts, including the Dresden collections and Meissen porcelain, was extravagant, and the heavy debts of the electorate were a continuing problem. Under the minister Heinrich von Brühl, Saxon policy tried to balance among the great powers but failed to protect the country from Prussian invasion in 1756.

Bavaria, a Catholic electorate with a strong tradition of estates, was ruled by the Wittelsbach dynasty. Max Emanuel (ruled 1679 to 1726) distinguished himself against the Ottomans at Vienna and Belgrade, then allied with France in the War of the Spanish Succession and lost his lands for a time after the Habsburg victory at Blenheim in 1704. His son Charles Albert became emperor as Charles VII from 1742 to 1745, in an episode that showed both the possibilities and the limits of a non Habsburg imperial candidacy, since he lacked the resources to defend himself and died as a ruler whose own capital had been occupied. His son Maximilian III Joseph made peace with Austria in 1745 and pursued a policy of cautious neutrality. Bavaria illustrates how a territorial prince might use imperial office as an instrument of dynastic ambition and how an intermediate power could be overshadowed.

Hanover, formally the Electorate of Brunswick Lüneburg, offers a different example. The Guelph dynasty obtained electoral status in 1692, in exchange for military support for Leopold I, and when Queen Anne died in 1714, the elector George Louis became King George I of Great Britain under the terms of the English Act of Settlement. The personal union of Hanover and Britain lasted until 1837. The two states remained legally and institutionally separate, with Hanover governed by its own ministers and estates, but the connection shaped diplomacy, since British policy in Germany was shaped by concern for the electorate's security, as shown by the Convention of Westminster in 1756. The University of Göttingen, founded in 1737 by George II, became a center of Enlightenment scholarship. Hanover's dynastic link to Britain, made possible by the British constitutional settlement examined in Chapter 77, gave it a unique position among German states.

Beneath the larger territories was a dense array of smaller entities. The ecclesiastical principalities, headed by the three Rhenish archbishop electors of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier, and including numerous prince bishoprics and abbeys, were governed by clergy chosen by cathedral chapters dominated by the nobility, and families such as the Schönborns accumulated several bishoprics and promoted a flourishing baroque culture. The free imperial cities, including Frankfurt, Nuremberg, Augsburg, Hamburg, and Regensburg, were self governing urban republics with an immediate relationship to the emperor, with their own councils, courts, and in many cases a confessionally mixed population. The imperial knights held small estates with direct imperial status, and numerous counts and lords governed tiny territories. These smaller entities depended on the Empire's courts and circles for protection against their larger neighbors, and they had a strong interest in the maintenance of imperial law.

Many other territories deserve mention. Württemberg's duke, Charles Alexander, converted to Catholicism in the 1710s, and the duchy's Lutheran estates defended their rights in the imperial courts. The landgraves of Hesse Kassel, among others, rented soldiers to other powers, a practice that gave their small states revenue and a military role. The duchies of Mecklenburg witnessed prolonged struggles between dukes and estates that were adjudicated by imperial authorities. Such examples show how imperial institutions arbitrated constitutional conflicts within territories, a function that helps explain why the Empire retained legitimacy among many of its members. They also show that the territorial states varied in the strength of their estates, the autonomy of their rulers, and the degree to which they imitated Prussian or Habsburg models.

The significance of this political fragmentation for Austria and Prussia was twofold. On the one hand, the smaller states constrained the two powers, since they could appeal to the imperial courts, form associations such as circles, or ally with one great power against the other. On the other hand, they offered opportunities, as Habsburg and Hohenzollern rulers sought support in the Diet and from imperial princes for their policies. Many smaller states leaned toward Vienna in the mid eighteenth century as protector of imperial law, a tendency reflected in the Diet's vote against Prussia in 1757, though a few, particularly Protestant ones, sympathized with Frederick. The existence of this third Germany complicates the later narrative of a simple Austrian and Prussian duel.

Section 78.18

Nobility, Peasants, Towns, Religion, and Everyday Life in Central Europe

The states described in this chapter were built on top of agrarian societies in which most people were peasants and in which social status, legal rights, and obligations differed sharply by region and by estate. The administrative reforms and wars that produced the rise of Austria and Prussia reached ordinary people through landlords, tax collectors, recruiting officers, and clergy, and their consequences depended on local circumstances. This section examines the main social groups and the ways in which state development affected them.

The nobility held a privileged position in all these states, though noble society was highly stratified. In Brandenburg and Prussia, the Junkers were landowners east of the Elbe who exercised judicial and police authority over their peasants, enjoyed exemption from the excise and many other taxes, and provided most army officers. Their estates were not all large, and many Junkers were of modest means, dependent on royal service. In Bohemia and the Austrian lands, a small number of great aristocratic houses, often owning vast territories and holding the highest offices at court, stood above a larger body of lesser nobles. The Bohemian aristocracy was largely the product of the confiscations and redistributions that followed the White Mountain, in which many older Czech families were replaced by loyalist Catholic families of diverse origins, who combined estate management with court service. In Hungary, nobles made up a far larger share of the population than in Western Europe, perhaps around five percent, a figure that includes a numerous class of poor nobles, and magnates such as the Esterházy, Pálffy, and Batthyány families wielded immense wealth and political influence. All Hungarian nobles claimed tax exemption and the right to attend the diet, and defended these privileges tenaciously.

Peasants constituted the great majority of the population and bore most of the burdens of taxation, rent, and labor service. The conditions of peasant life varied. In the lands east of the Elbe, including Brandenburg, Pomerania, and Prussia, the manorial system of Gutsherrschaft bound peasants to estates through hereditary subjection, imposing labor services, often several days per week in the case of larger holdings, and restricting movement and marriage. In Bohemia and Moravia, serfdom and labor service, the robot, were also heavy, and following the plague of 1679 and 1680 and a peasant rebellion in 1680, the Habsburg government issued a patent in 1680 attempting to limit the robot to a maximum of three days per week, a limit often ignored. Maria Theresa's government would later issue further patents regulating labor services, though that program belongs mainly to the following decades. In western and southwestern Germany, peasants more often held land on hereditary or leasehold terms with lighter obligations and sometimes greater legal protection, and in parts of the Alpine lands they enjoyed considerable local autonomy.

For peasants, the state's growth meant new burdens. Taxes increased, recruitment drew young men away or exposed them to danger, and the movement of armies through the countryside brought requisitions, billeting, plunder, and epidemic disease. The canton system in Prussia gave peasants some protection by regularizing recruitment, but it also meant that rural young men were tied to service. In Habsburg lands, recruitment was a heavy burden on the Austrian and Bohemian peasantry, and desertion and flight were common responses. Peasants responded through petitions, through litigation in imperial and territorial courts, and occasionally through revolt, though large uprisings were rarer in the eighteenth century than in the sixteenth and seventeenth.

Towns varied from great commercial centers to small agricultural market towns. Vienna, with a population approaching 150,000, Berlin, which approached and then passed 100,000 inhabitants in these decades including the garrison, Prague, Dresden, Leipzig, Breslau, and Königsberg were major urban centers, in which guilds regulated craft production, merchants organized trade, and royal and provincial officials exercised increasing control. In Prussia, townspeople paid the excise, were subject to billeting, and could be required to supply soldiers' lodging and supplies, and the king's officials supervised municipal governance. In Habsburg lands, the towns' political weight within the estates had declined, particularly in Bohemia, while commercial and financial life in Vienna benefited from the court's demand and the growth of the state. The encouragement of manufactures by both governments, including textiles in Silesia and Bohemia, silk and porcelain projects, linked state policy to urban economies.

Religion remained a central fact of social life and state policy. The Habsburgs pursued Catholic confessionalization in their hereditary lands, discouraging Protestant practice, though with variation by region. Protestants in Hungary and Transylvania retained certain rights, and hidden Protestant communities survived in Alpine regions of Austria, where in the 1730s and later the authorities deported thousands of Protestants, called Transmigranten and later Landler, to Transylvania. Prussia's dynasty was Calvinist, but its subjects were mostly Lutheran, and the state tolerated Catholics and, from the Great Elector on, welcomed persecuted Protestants as settlers, such as the Huguenots and the Salzburgers. Pietism, with its centers in Halle, influenced the monarchy's education, charity, and military chaplaincy. After 1740, Prussian rule in Silesia brought Protestant and Catholic communities under a state that granted formal toleration to both while favoring Protestant leadership in the administration.

Jewish communities lived under a patchwork of legal restrictions and privileges. In Habsburg lands, Jews were expelled from Vienna in 1670 and 1671, but court Jews such as Samuel Oppenheimer and Samson Wertheimer served the dynasty as financiers and suppliers, and sizeable communities lived in Bohemia, Moravia, and Hungary. The so called Familiants Laws of 1726 and 1727 restricted the number of Jewish families in Bohemia and Moravia by allowing only one son per family to marry and establish a household, and in 1744 and 1745 Maria Theresa ordered the expulsion of the Jews from Prague and Bohemia, a measure revoked in 1748 after intervention by foreign Jewish and Christian authorities and at a financial price. In Brandenburg Prussia, fifty families expelled from Vienna were admitted in 1671, forming the nucleus of the Berlin Jewish community, which later included prominent figures such as the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn, who arrived in the city in 1743. The General Privileged Regulation issued in 1750 by Frederick II classified Jews into ranks with different rights and imposed economic and residential restrictions. These measures show that Jewish communities were a visible part of Central European society and that their position was conditioned by fiscal calculations as well as by religious prejudice and sometimes by persistent hostility.

Refugees and migrants were another constant presence. Rulers sought settlers to repopulate war damaged lands, from the Huguenots to the Salzburgers to the colonists of the Banat. The military also created mobile populations of soldiers, camp followers, and deserters, and warfare displaced civilians. The varying social consequences of administrative reform meant that the same measures that strengthened the state could weigh differently on different groups: an officer career benefited the Junker, a recruitment district burdened the peasant, and the excise impinged on the townsman.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Households in the Age of Fiscal and Military Pressure

A Prussian rural household. On a Junker estate in Brandenburg or Pomerania, a peasant family farmed a holding for which it owed labor services, produce, and rent to the lord and a land contribution to the state. The household's sons were registered in the regiment's canton, and could be called up for training and for war, though they spent much of the year at home. Marriage and movement required the lord's consent, and the lord also held police and judicial authority in the village. The household's vulnerability to crop failure, to disease among livestock, and to the demands of recruiting officers shaped its decisions.

A Bohemian or Austrian rural community. In a village on a great aristocratic estate in Bohemia, peasants owed robot to their lords, often more than official limits allowed, and paid taxes assessed through provincial administration. Local officials of the estate collected dues and often mediated between peasants and the state. The Catholic parish priest was an important figure, and the pressure to conform confessionally was stronger than in many Prussian districts. Recruitment could draw sons into the army, and communities responded with petitions for exemption or by hiding young men.

An urban household affected by military demands. A craftsman's household in a garrison town such as Berlin or a Habsburg city lodged soldiers under billeting obligations, paid excise or other indirect taxes on goods, and sometimes supplied the army with its products. Wives and daughters often worked in the family trade, and the household's fortunes depended on guild regulations, state orders, and the movement of armies. Wartime brought requisitions and uncertainty, and in the Seven Years' War towns in Saxony and Silesia endured heavy exactions.

The evidence for these households comes from petitions, tax and estate records, parish registers, and court cases, which are imperfect sources. They record disputes and obligations more fully than ordinary contentment, and elite administrators wrote most of the surviving documents.

Section 78.19

Austria and Prussia Compared: State Capacity, Dynastic Ambition, and the Limits of Centralization

The preceding sections have followed two monarchies through more than a century of development. This section draws the threads together in a comparison. The aim is not to rank the monarchies by efficiency or to declare Prussia strong and Austria weak, a pattern common in older nationalist historiography, but to explain how each mobilized resources differently and why both remained dependent on legal, territorial, social, and dynastic arrangements that limited centralization.

In taxation, Prussia relied on a comparatively simple system, with a land contribution collected through noble landlords in the countryside, an excise collected by royal officials in the towns, and substantial domain revenue from crown lands. Austria's system was more complex, with the estates of each province granting and administering taxes, a difference that persisted despite the reforms of 1748 and 1749, which created a more regular system for the Austrian and Bohemian lands but left Hungary outside it. Public credit was more developed in the Habsburg lands, where bankers, the Vienna City Bank, and provincial credit institutions provided loans, while Prussia under Frederick William I and Frederick II relied on a treasury reserve and on wartime expedients. These contrasts reflect different political economies: Prussia's small size and relatively homogeneous elite allowed for direct supervision, while Austria's size and diversity favored negotiation and credit.

Military organization also differed. Both states maintained large standing armies, but Prussia achieved a higher ratio of soldiers to population through the canton system and the employment of foreign recruits, while Austria relied on a mixture of recruitment among its provinces, colonel proprietors, frontier forces, and Hungarian contributions. Prussian officers were largely noble and tied to the state, while Habsburg officers included nobles, foreigners, and increasingly graduates of the new military academy. Both armies depended on supply systems that limited their operational range, and both found that wars exhausted their resources more quickly than expected.

Bureaucracy and law showed contrasting patterns. The Prussian General Directory and the provincial chambers extended royal control through a hierarchy of officials, but nobles retained their local judicial and police powers, and the provincial estates continued to exist, though weakened. The Habsburg Directorium, the new district officials, and the central courts established after 1749 extended the reach of Vienna in the Austrian and Bohemian lands, while Hungary retained its own institutions and the Netherlands and Italian possessions their own. Religious policy diverged, with the Habsburgs enforcing Catholic uniformity in their core lands and Prussia practicing limited toleration for pragmatic reasons. Political legitimacy rested on dynastic right, on the sanction of the imperial title for the Habsburgs, and on military success and royal rank for the Hohenzollerns.

The settlement of 1763 left both monarchies in positions that reflected these differences. Prussia had survived a coalition of larger states, confirmed its conquest of Silesia, and acquired a reputation for military prowess, but its population was smaller, its territory still dispersed, and its treasury exhausted by the war. The monarchy's survival had depended on the death of Elizabeth of Russia and the limits of its enemies' coordination. Austria had failed to recover Silesia but had preserved the monarchy, strengthened its administration, and gained the alliance of France, though the war left it heavily indebted. Each state emerged with lessons: Prussia sought to preserve its gains by cautious diplomacy and further economic reconstruction, and Austria to continue reform while seeking compensation in other directions. Their rivalry would shape the politics of the Empire through the later eighteenth century, as Chapter 84 and the chapters on the French Revolution (Chapter 88) will show, but the pattern was not fixed.

Why was the later unification of Germany under Prussian leadership not inevitable in 1763? Several considerations support caution. In 1763 Prussia was a middling power whose survival had been narrow, and Austria remained the larger and, in the eyes of most contemporaries, the leading German power and holder of the imperial title. The Empire remained a functioning constitutional order with institutions valued by the smaller states. Neither Prussian nor Austrian rulers conceived of a German nation state, and their ambitions were dynastic and territorial. The later destruction of the Empire in 1806 under the pressure of Napoleonic France, the creation of the German Confederation in 1815, the revolutions of 1848, and the wars of the 1860s, discussed in Chapters 90, 92, 98, and 100, were contingent events that cannot be deduced from the situation of 1763. Reading the nineteenth century back into the eighteenth would mistake the outcome for a destiny.

Historical Debate 3

Two Absolutisms, or Two Negotiated Systems?

The question: Did Austrian and Prussian state building represent different versions of absolutism, or are both better understood as negotiated political systems in which rulers depended on privileged elites?

Interpretation A: Different versions of absolutism. Older and some textbook accounts treat both monarchies as variants of absolutism, in which rulers concentrated authority through standing armies, bureaucracies, and taxation, with Prussia representing a militarized, bureaucratic model and Austria a more dynastic, Catholic and court centered one. In this view, the concept helps compare the two with France and other states.
Interpretation B: Negotiated political systems. Many recent historians reject the absolutist label as misleading, arguing that rulers governed through bargains with nobles, estates, and local officials, that legal privileges and local autonomy persisted, and that the capacity of rulers to command was limited by custom, finance, and the need for cooperation. In this view, both monarchies are better understood as compromises, with the pace and form of negotiation varying by territory.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on recesses with the estates, the records of central offices, correspondence between officials and landlords, and legal sources defining the privileges of different groups.

Why it matters: If the monarchies were absolutist, their history can be written as the progressive concentration of power. If they were negotiated systems, the history must attend to local variation, resistance, and the persistence of privilege. Scholarship has moved toward the second view while retaining the term as a descriptive label for particular ideologies of rule.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Central European Political Order in 1648, 1683, 1701, 1740, and 1763

What Changed

  • In 1648 the Habsburgs were defensive, the Hohenzollern lands were devastated and fragmented, and the Empire's constitution had just been confirmed.
  • By 1683 the Habsburgs were facing the Ottoman crisis, but the relief of Vienna began a reconquest that would transform their southeastern frontier.
  • By 1701 the Hohenzollerns had a standing army, new revenue systems, and a royal title, while the Habsburgs were preparing for the Spanish succession war.
  • By 1740 the Prussian army had roughly doubled since 1713, the Habsburg succession was uncertain, and neither power's position was secure.
  • By 1763 Prussia held Silesia and had been confirmed as a major power, and the Habsburgs had reformed their administration and realigned their diplomacy.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Empire continued to provide a constitutional framework, with its Diet, courts, and circles.
  • Noble privilege and provincial institutions persisted in both monarchies, and peasants remained subject to heavy obligations.
  • The Habsburg monarchy remained a composite state, and the Hohenzollern territories remained dispersed.

Who experienced change most directly? Soldiers, recruits, taxpayers, and the populations of occupied or conquered regions such as Silesia and Saxony experienced change most directly. Nobles in the new administrations saw changes in careers and influence. Who experienced greater continuity? Many peasants and townspeople experienced continuity in their obligations and daily routines, even as taxes and recruitment intensified.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents the error of treating the period as a simple transition to modern states, and it highlights the persistence of old institutions within new structures.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how two dynastic monarchies, the Austrian Habsburg monarchy and Brandenburg-Prussia, became competing great powers within the political world of Central Europe between 1648 and 1763. The central argument is that their rise took place inside, and was shaped by, a constitutional and legal order that survived the Thirty Years' War: the Holy Roman Empire. The Peace of Westphalia confirmed the rights of the imperial estates, regulated confessional relations, and left the Empire as a framework of shared and contested authority, not as a centralized monarchy.

The Empire continued to function after 1648. Its permanent Diet at Regensburg, its two supreme courts, and its regional circles provided forums for negotiation, litigation, and defense, and they protected smaller territories against larger neighbors. Historians have moved away from the older view of the Empire as a decayed relic, but they acknowledge its limits, including the slowness of its institutions and the capacity of large territorial rulers to act independently of it.

The Habsburg recovery began under Leopold I, whose reign combined dynastic legitimacy, the gradual development of a standing army, and dependence on court financiers and provincial estates. The Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683 was ended by a multinational relief army under the leadership of John III Sobieski, and the war that followed brought the recapture of Buda in 1686, the victory at Zenta in 1697, and the Treaty of Karlowitz in 1699. The gains of Passarowitz in 1718 were partly reversed in 1739, a reminder that Habsburg expansion was limited and reversible. The Hungarian settlements, in particular the Peace of Szatmár in 1711, showed that Habsburg authority in Hungary rested on a bargain with the nobility.

The Habsburg monarchy was a composite monarchy, with different laws and institutions in the Austrian lands, the Crown of Bohemia, and the Kingdom of Hungary. Central offices such as the Hofkriegsrat and Hofkammer coexisted with provincial chanceries and estates. The Pragmatic Sanction of 1713 sought to preserve the indivisibility of this inheritance and to regulate succession through the female line, and it gained the consent of the estates and the guarantees of the powers. But the crisis of 1740 demonstrated that guarantees were no substitute for military and financial strength.

Maria Theresa, who was never Holy Roman Emperor, defended her inheritance with the help of the Hungarian estates and foreign subsidies, ceded most of Silesia in order to concentrate against other enemies, and then reconstructed the administration of the Austrian and Bohemian lands through the reforms associated with Haugwitz beginning in 1748 and 1749. These reforms increased revenue, reorganized central institutions, and enlarged the army, but they left Hungary largely outside, and they did not eliminate noble influence.

Brandenburg-Prussia emerged from a collection of dispersed Hohenzollern territories, damaged by war and governed through provincial estates. The Great Elector built a standing army, introduced new taxation through bargains with the nobility, secured sovereignty in Ducal Prussia, and began a central administration. Frederick I obtained the title King in Prussia in 1701, a legally precise status based on territory outside the Empire. Frederick William I created the General Directory, the canton system, and a large army supported by careful finance, binding the Junker nobility to the monarchy through officer service. The result was a fiscal-military state whose strength rested on social bargains as well as on administrative design.

The accession of Frederick II in 1740 and his invasion of Silesia opened an era of Austro-Prussian rivalry. The seizure of Silesia was a conquest justified by weak legal claims, and it was confirmed only after the wars of 1740 to 1745, the Peace of Aix la Chapelle in 1748, and the Treaty of Hubertusburg in 1763. In the Seven Years' War, Prussia survived a coalition of larger powers, helped by British subsidies, the exploitation of Saxony, and the death of Empress Elizabeth of Russia, while Austria failed to recover Silesia but preserved its monarchy and strengthened its institutions.

The comparison of the two monarchies shows different patterns of mobilization. Prussia concentrated resources through a simple fiscal system, a high ratio of soldiers to population, and a close alliance between crown and nobility. Austria relied on larger territory, negotiated taxation, credit, and diverse military forces. Both states depended on privileged elites, on legal and dynastic arrangements, and on the imperial framework within which they acted, and neither achieved uniform centralization. The terms absolutism and bureaucratic efficiency capture only part of what happened.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized that the outcome of 1763 did not make German unification inevitable. The Empire survived until 1806, the smaller German states retained distinct political choices, and neither Vienna nor Berlin imagined a German nation state. The later history of Central Europe, examined in subsequent volumes, would be shaped by revolution, war, and political choice in ways that could not be predicted from the situation at the end of the Seven Years' War.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Holy Roman Empire
A composite constitutional order of electors, princes, ecclesiastical lords, free cities, and other estates under an elected emperor, which continued to function after 1648 through its Diet, courts, and circles.
Imperial estates
Rulers and corporate bodies with immediate imperial status and a vote in the Diet, including electors, princes, prelates, counts, and free cities.
Reichstag
The imperial Diet, organized in three colleges and permanently seated at Regensburg from 1663.
Reichskammergericht
The Imperial Chamber Court, seated at Speyer and from 1689 at Wetzlar, which heard cases among imperial subjects and rulers.
Reichshofrat
The Imperial Aulic Council in Vienna, a supreme court and advisory body responsive to the emperor.
Imperial Circles
Regional associations of imperial estates responsible for defense, coinage, and the enforcement of imperial law.
Elector
One of the princes entitled to elect the emperor; there were eight after 1648 and nine after Hanover's elevation in 1692.
Free imperial city
A self governing city with an immediate relationship to the emperor and a vote in the Diet, such as Nuremberg or Frankfurt.
Composite monarchy
A state composed of several territories under one ruler, each retaining its own laws and institutions, as in the Habsburg lands.
Provincial estates
Assemblies of privileged groups that represented a province, voted taxes, and defended local privileges.
Hofkriegsrat
The Habsburg Court War Council, which supervised military administration and supply.
Hofkammer
The Habsburg Court Chamber, which managed crown revenues and credit.
Crown of Saint Stephen
The symbol of the Kingdom of Hungary as a legal and political community with its own constitution, to which the king swore at coronation.
Military Frontier
A belt of territory along the Ottoman border, settled by peasant soldiers under direct military administration.
Treaty of Karlowitz
The 1699 settlement that gave the Habsburgs most of Hungary, Transylvania, Croatia, and Slavonia.
Peace of Szatmár
The 1711 settlement ending the Rákóczi uprising and confirming Hungarian noble privileges and constitution.
Pragmatic Sanction
The 1713 Habsburg family law declaring the hereditary lands indivisible and regulating succession, including through the female line.
King in Prussia
The title adopted by Frederick I in 1701, based on Ducal Prussia outside the Empire; it did not apply to all Hohenzollern lands.
Fiscal-military state
A state organized to raise taxes and credit to support standing armies and war.
Canton system
The Prussian arrangement, formalized in 1733, assigning regiments recruiting districts, with extensive exemptions and periods of leave.
Junkers
The landed nobility of Brandenburg and Prussia east of the Elbe, who combined estate lordship with military service.
General Directory
The central Prussian administrative body created in 1723 by merging military and financial authorities.
Contribution and excise
Respectively, the Prussian land tax collected largely through landlords and the indirect urban tax collected by royal officials.
Robot
Compulsory labor service owed by peasants to their lords in Bohemia and neighboring lands.
Gutsherrschaft
The manorial system east of the Elbe in which landlords exercised economic, judicial, and police authority over dependent peasants.
Diplomatic Revolution
The 1756 realignment in which Austria and France allied and Britain and Prussia drew together.
Treaty of Hubertusburg
The 1763 settlement between Austria, Prussia, and Saxony confirming Prussian possession of Silesia and Glatz.
Dualism
A modern shorthand for the Austro-Prussian rivalry within the Empire, a term that must not imply that German unification was inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 78 matters because it shows how states grew in a world that was not designed to produce them. The Austrian and Prussian monarchies did not replace the Holy Roman Empire in 1648 or 1740. They developed within it, used its courts and ceremonies, exploited its divisions, and sometimes defied it. Their rivalry was real, but it unfolded alongside a constitutional order that continued to matter to many Central Europeans, from imperial cities to ecclesiastical princes.

The chapter also reveals how state power was constructed. Armies required money, money required bargains, and bargains required the cooperation of landlords, estates, bankers, and officials. In Brandenburg-Prussia the bargain bound a landed nobility to royal service, and in the Habsburg lands it combined negotiated taxation, dynastic loyalty, and the preservation of provincial and Hungarian privileges. Both systems bore heavily on peasants, soldiers, and townspeople, and both coexisted with religious diversity and minority communities whose status depended on the fiscal and confessional calculations of rulers.

These developments shaped later history. The acquisition of Silesia, the survival of the Seven Years' War, and the Habsburg reconstruction established the conditions in which both powers faced the partitions of Poland, the French Revolution, and the Napoleonic wars, examined in Part XIV. Yet the chapter's most important lesson for Volume III is one of contingency. The path from the fragmented Central Europe of 1648 to the confrontation of Austria and Prussia was not a straight line, and the later fate of the Empire and of German politics cannot be read from it.

Within Part XII, this chapter links the study of states and dynasties to the European balance of power. Chapter 79 will broaden the view to Scandinavia, Poland-Lithuania, Russia, and the Ottoman European order, and Chapter 80 will show how the wars of the period reshaped the continental system. Together these chapters ask students to understand the early modern state as a negotiated, uneven, and historically contingent form of political organization.
